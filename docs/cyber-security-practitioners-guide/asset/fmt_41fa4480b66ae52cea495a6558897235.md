
It has now become extremely important that every business should have a set of key information governance policies so that they can retain control of and ensure the safety of assets and information. It is also hoped that this will improve user confidence in how the assets are stored, protected and processed. Information governance is also an important part of an organisation’s structure as it tells the Executive Board what the risk treatment plan should be. The risk treatment plan should match the business risk appetite. There are valuable benefits to managing risk in this way.

There are also various frameworks that contain good practices that an organisation can use to deliver IT service management such as ITIL. These frameworks complement policies like ISO 27001 or CMMi. They help in training staff, improving the quality of IT services and provide a backbone to help monitor risk and improvements. This is all designed to assist in identifying vulnerabilities and taking note of lessons learned so that everything about how the current landscape is changing can be documented. Based on this information, new strategies can be developed and followed. There are different types of cyber attacks and a wide range of cyber attacks within each type, and this variety can lead to a multitude of issues both with regard to the organisations affected by ransomware and the users of services provided by those organisations. The possible consequences are unknown, but it is likely that the users’ data will be compromised in some way, as a cyber breach generally leads to a data breach. This is where the problems begin for the client.

Organisations can bring forth technical countermeasures to tighten security against attacks and highlight user awareness through education but all this still does not provide protection to the client once the cyber attack succeeds and data is taken. The awareness through education is needed to lessen the chance for an attack to succeed, but there is more thought being given to using blockchain as another added layer of security to protect the data. The data need not necessarily be held on-chain (as it can be stored off-chain) but the access and authentication would be initiated through blockchain and all fingerprint information would be stored on blockchain with all the attributes mentioned in earlier sections.

### 6.3.1.*Ransomware*

**What is Ransomware?**

There has been a significant increase in the volume and diversity of ransomware attacks over the last two years (see [Fig. 6.6](#chapter6.xhtml#fig6.6) for growth details). Attack formations have become more complicated, and the perpetrators of ransomware have progressed from script kiddies to crime-for-hire (ransomware-as-a-service) and sophisticated hackers. As Parent and Cushack (2016) mention, ‘a new layer cake in cybercrime’ has arisen. However, attack success relies on the victim acting as the catalyst to initiate the chain of events.

Ransomware is sophisticated and malicious software that blocks the victim’s access to their files or that threatens to publish or make available the victim’s data (based on cryptovirology). What usually happens next is the request for a ransom to be paid so that the victim can access their files again. Over the last five years the extent and complexity of ransomware has increased, and some organisations have taken to paying the ransoms through digital currencies such as Bitcoin. The victim dilemma is there is no sure way to know after paying a ransom whether the attacker will release the decryption key. There are some victims that have paid the ransom and were successful in accessing the data once again. Others have paid but the cyber attackers did not release the decryption keys. Yet others paid and received the decryption keys but they did not work. There is always the possibility as well that after the organisation pays the initial ransom, the cyber criminals may continue to ask for additional ransom.

Without doubt, attacks are on the increase. In this newspaper article (Hutchins, 2017), General Sir Richard Barrons also mentions that the NCSC is not as powerful or as integrated as the US Cybercom model.

**How does Ransomware work?**

The intrusions made by ransomware are usually carried out using a Trojan (to the user this seems a legitimate file but is the disguised malware). This can occur due to a vulnerability in the network or because the user opened the file (often as an email attachment). The attackers rely on the curiosity in human nature which often leads us to click on a link without thinking of the consequences, even when we are unsure of whom the sender is. This causes the payload to run a program that then either locks the system or appears to lock it (Scareware program). The consequences for the user depend upon the type of payload downloaded by the ransomware. The level of sophistication of the attack depends on the attacker’s capabilities but there are many ‘malware programs’ for hire on the dark web (Tor/similar network) so even a moderately skilled attacker can cause a massive impact on single or multiple organisations. These malware businesses selling cryptovirology tools are collectively known as ‘ransomware-as-a-service’, as noted by Young and Yung (2017). The payloads can vary from simple applications that appear with fake warnings to top-end programs using strong encryption such that the only way to deactivate the malware is with the attacker’s decryption key (cryptoviral). Young and Yung (2017) describe the explosion of cryptovirology and how formidable a threat it is, with victims that include individuals, hospitals, police precincts, universities, transportation systems and government offices making daily news.

**How is it spread?**

Social engineering is the key skill used by attackers to spread ransomware, as they know it is easy to trick a user into opening a malicious email attachment. The attackers use many ingenious ways to disguise the malware as fake PDF/Word files. The issue is that the victim may not know the sender of the email or the fake company but as the email is made to appear official, often will click the email attachment. Other methods include tricking the victim to confirm, for example, an online order. There may be ways the attacker can convince the victim to run an embedded program. As an example of the speed with which malware can spread, the WannaCry ransomware attack began on 12 May 2017 and in just a day had infected 230,000 computers in over 150 countries. This is covered in the article *Strategic Comments* (2017).

**How can we prevent Ransomware?**

With the increase in ransomware, various individuals and organisations have offered focused guidance on how to respond to incidents/attacks. McGoogan (2017) lists a number of core rules to follow in the Telegraph newspaper shortly after the WannaCry attack. There are quite a few basic rules that will stop most attacks occurring and some that will make paying the ransomware unnecessary. It has become evident that ransomware appears to be the most profitable malware and that the amount of ransomware will not decrease soon (Cisco, 2016). Organisations are starting to understand the risks and to take proactive measures such as those recommended by Kaspersky Lab and Intel Security. They have given advice on how to avoid attacks and on what to do if attacked. They have also shared actual known decryption keys with the public (No More Ransom, 2017).
