
An incomplete ransom attack is when the user gets to know that there is a possible ransomware attack due to appearances of `.KRAB` and/or `GDCB-DECRYPT.txt` files but the files are not yet encrypted. In order to recover from an incomplete ransom attack, the user is advised to automatically run the anti-virus application, which will do a virus scan of the system. It is also suggested that all devices infected with the ransomware should be disconnected from the network to protect cloud based or external back-up files (Brewer, 2016).

It is much harder to recover from a **successful** ransom attack. Organisations and researchers always ask a common question: **To Pay or NOT To Pay?** There is a good reason why researchers and organisations ask the question and it all has to do with the rate of success of file recovery and the eradication of the ransomware. Even when the ransom has been paid and the files decrypted, the user can never be sure that there are no more residual files from the ransomware left in the system (Brewer, 2016). The ransomware residual files can actually hide in mailboxes or directories without the user knowing and these files can be triggered at any time that the user is logged in to the computer. If the user opts to pay for the ransom, as previously stated, the ransomware **should** delete itself and hostage files should be returned without any alterations. The article (Brewer, 2016) finally suggests that buying a newer device is wiser especially if files are backed up online since this would help reduce the likelihood that the ransomware developer will produce more ransomware applications.

### 5.7.Conclusion

Although ransomwares are ranked as only the sixth most prominent malware in most cyber crime reports, ransomware is still a deadly malware. This is due to the fact that ransomware has the capability to do damage by means of infecting a user’s device and encrypting either all the files or the whole device. The damage will only stop when the victim has paid the ransom, and in most cases, the length of time given for the victim to pay the ransom is limited. If the deadline passes, multiple research studies have suggested that the victim would then be obliged to pay double the original ransom price. Multiple research studies also suggest that despite the victim being able to pay the ransom price, there is no guarantee of his files being returned and/or the ransomware being eradicated completely from the system.

Most of the prominent ransomwares such as WannaCry, CryptoLocker, TeslaCrypt, SimpleLocker, GandCrab, Petya and NotPetya use RSA 2048-bit encryption for the public key and AES 256-bit encryption to encrypt the victim’s data. Ransomware’s most prominent feature aside from encryption is the command and control feature which actually gave birth to a new type of malware. This step made Kaspersky predict that the new trend for 2018 is for a shift from ransomwares to ***cryptomining*** to occur. Cryptomining is a term that describes an attacker’s use of better ransomware.

**Human Involvement**

Human involvement in an attack is key since ransomware as a simple hard-coded software is **useless.** Ransomware cannot spread and infect devices **on its own.** Human involvement actually gives life to ransomware and the way ransomware infects devices. Humans create the ransomware software and humans spread the virus. As previously stated, most acquisitions of ransomwares occur due to a human falling for the spam email trick set by hackers. Social engineering, which was previously discussed, also plays a vital part since social engineers use psychology to achieve illegal ends. Humans are also responsible for activating the malware since most ransomware activation happens when .exe files disguised as PDF or Word files are executed. Cyber criminals are also responsible for the encryption of files inside the victim’s device. Finally, once the trap is set, humans are also responsible for choosing whether **to pay** or **not to pay.**

Human empowerment, along with the proper dissemination of information, is key, as it is important for the public to know what ransomwares are and how to fight the attacks. With proper education about this topic, humans can actually reduce the likelihood of these types of cyber attacks by means of early-stage prevention. Finally, ransomware attacks can only be prevented if the **humans** in the computing environment are willing to critically educate themselves about the digital advancements in different computing industries.
