
# Interoperability, transparency and longitudinal healthcare records — The data collected by an organisation is often trapped in data silos, and therefore not integrated. This can make it difficult to analyse and places restrictions on how it can be used. Ideally, there should be interoperability between the different systems in which different data sets are stored. In addition, those in charge of each system should be held accountable for how the data within that system is collected, stored and used. Other issues in this category include the limited control an individual has over their data, and the occurrence of data breaches which might result in data loss, or perhaps even identity theft. Blockchain technology can help by eliminating data silos and providing a more coherent and seamlessly integrated data model. Access to the data would be controlled through cryptographic methods, but all the relevant parties would be allowed access. These changes could lead to innovations such as longitudinal healthcare records for patients, which could be analysed to improve health outcomes.

## Privacy and Security — Confidentiality, integrity, availability and authenticity (CIAA) are four information security concepts that should ideally be adhered to when data is being dealt with. In reality, internal issues such as accidental data loss, and issues caused by external vectors such as targeted malicious behaviour for the purpose of identity and data theft, are widespread. As previously mentioned, industry is trying to tackle this by carrying out the day-to-day actions that will help mitigate risk and continually learning new methods to keep data secure. Blockchain can help by providing an additional layer of security with the use of encryption. The use of a decentralised and distributed ledger system would increase the integrity of the data. Data encryption, as well as more complex permission settings, will provide good controls for a securely authenticated data exchange.

## Time stamping and chronological ordering — If records and data are held in silos, there is more chance of corruption, fraud, the abuse of traceability and so on. One of the benefits blockchain can offer is its undeniable immutability. Even if blockchain is used only for authentication, perhaps to allow data to be stored in a ‘data lake’ (a centralised data storage solution which can be created in the cloud), the fingerprints associated with this type of activity would be time-stamped and kept in chronological order (as with smart contracts). No one would be able to tamper with the records. Depending on the type of blockchain, parties can then make use of smart contracts to help provide greater efficiency and integration.

## Procurement and Contract Process — Using the properties of blockchain that make it suitable for use in smart contracts will help relieve a lot of the complex processes, negotiations and supply chain issues currently faced. This will be done by streamlining the process to promote efficiency and reduce cost. It will then be possible to provide automated supplier contracts and analytics to maximise productivity and control.

### 6.2.5.*Potential security issues and limitations on blockchain* — Whilst support behind blockchain technology is growing, and whilst the benefits of blockchain technology to both clients and industry are becoming better known, one must not overlook the risk factors associated with blockchain technology. By knowing these risks and carrying out further research alongside blockchain use, one can contribute to the formation and development of alternative procedures that may make blockchain deployment safer or mitigate its risks.

### Quantum Computing — It has been much debated whether quantum computing poses a future threat to cryptography. In order to do so, quantum computing would need to be large and reliable so that it can perform millions of quantum bits (qubits) to break current cryptographic algorithms (Aumasson, 2017). As Aumasson mentions, it’s not yet known if quantum computing is scalable or fault-tolerant and general consensus believes it is not able to break current cryptographic algorithms yet. But it is good to know that cryptographers are looking at post-quantum cryptography (as the back-up plan for future-proofing).

### Irretrievable Data — Blockchain data access will be facilitated with a key. This use of private key security will allow a user to control their own data, but if the key is lost, data will become irretrievable. However, solutions can be put in place. It is possible to introduce a ‘back door’ concept, but that potentially may replace one issue with another (Engelhardt, 2017).

### Scalability and Performance — Further investigations must be carried out on the different types of blockchain and script execution. For example, the average transaction time on the Ethereum blockchain is approximately 14 seconds, while the average transaction time of Bitcoin is 10 minutes. It’s well known in cryptography that speed and security have an inverse effect on each other. Time is required to achieve consensus over the blockchain network, and to do the computation needed to verify a transaction. This will probably not be a show stopper, however. Over time, technology will improve and so too will speed and computation time.

### Malicious Attacks — Xu (2016) researched attacks (both malicious attacks and fraud) that one can be exposed to while using blockchain technology. He comments upon these attacks and recommends defensive and preventative measures.

### Google DeepMind Scenarios — The Google DeepMind project is an example of the mismanagement of data. In that particular case, patient data was recorded through an app. In order for the app to be created, the NHS allowed Google to access large amounts of sensitive medical information, not only from users of the app, but from the records of nearly 1.6 million NHS patients. Controversially, it was claimed that ‘implied consent’ had been given for the information to be released. This seems to suggest a lack of transparency and has muddied privacy and security concerns.

## 6.3.Threats to Mission Critical Data: Vulnerabilities and Cyber Attacks — The previous section in this chapter gave a formalised appraisal of how blockchain works, including its methods and key attributes. This section highlights the increasing number of vulnerabilities and the increasing scale of various types of cyber attacks, in order that the complexity of these attacks and the extent of the devastation caused can be understood. A high percentage of cyber attacks occur because a malicious outsider attempts to commit identity theft. There can be many types of malicious outsiders, ranging from users of low level malware-for-hire to hacktivists to state-sponsored attackers.

For example, the 2015 cyber attack on Ukraine’s power grid was initiated through phishing attacks, but it took six months for the cyber attackers to gather the security credentials they needed to compromise the SCADA network before the main thrust of the attack took place. Whilst 27 power distribution centres were knocked out of operation, the attack was made stronger by creating interference and inundating the call centres supporting the 225,000 customers that were affected with call traffic. During these chaotic types of attacks and potentially even during the build-up to the attacks, cyber attackers may be accessing core mission-critical data. This type of theft is masked by the chaotic nature of events. In essence, this also helps strengthen the case for blockchain, since it’s clear there are good use cases for blockchain technology that can be applied to various industries. However, there should also be another layer of security that offers protection to mission-critical data, since it is likely that cyber attackers will attempt to breach it. At least then if a cyber attack is successful, the core data remains uncontaminated.
