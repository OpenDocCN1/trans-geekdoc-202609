
If an attack is targeted then an attacker will generally know what they are looking for, otherwise the most likely focus of an attack will be to search for files with credit card details, usernames, passwords, product keys, cryptocurrency wallets (aka crypto-jacking) and other confidential information. The number of files that an attacker would find of interest will generally be small as an attacker would generally have no reason to download all of a company’s data unless it is all of interest. An attacker could examine emails for signs of a future large transaction that could be intercepted with a notification telling the company to transfer the money to the attacker’s bank account, or he could use stolen user credentials to access other websites and obtain further confidential information. If the amount of data stolen is small, then it will be more difficult to detect the attack.

If a rogue user sends out emails to a personal email account with the company’s data attached, how can this be detected when emailing out attachments is an everyday occurrence? Could a rogue user exfiltrate data to a web storage system such as Dropbox, or could a user accidentally launch a “command and control” data exfiltration attack by simply visiting a rogue website through opening an email attachment? These are real concerns not only for company directors but also security professionals.

1.1.1.*A brief look at the attack vectors in high profile leaks*

When leaks are reported, the method by which data is stolen is often glossed over. As far as the news reporter is concerned, the headline generally focusses on how many thousands or millions of personal records or user credentials have been stolen, how many credit card numbers have been taken, the subsequent risks to individuals and the damage to the company. Once confidential data is in the public domain, how it happened is often a secondary issue.

The following is a list of methods used to leak data from historical records accessed on or before March 7, 2019.

1.**Adultfriendfinder** — 412 million accounts were hacked in 2016 due to a Structured Query Language (SQL) injection vulnerability. Of the accounts hacked 103 million were apparently stored in a database in plaintext (Storm, 2016).

2.**Anthem** — The USA’s largest healthcare insurers were hacked in 2015. 80 million records were accessed using malware that gave the attackers access to a user’s workstation (Abelson and Goldstein, 2015).

3.**Appleby** (Paradise Papers) — 13.4 million documents were leaked. Appleby claimed that the German newspaper *Süddeutsche Zeitung* had obtained these because of a data theft. Appleby are a law firm, and sued newspapers such as the *BBC News* and the *Guardian* — which reported the story — for inaccuracies, arguing that the data was stolen, not hacked. Though they won this action and managed to prevent further publication, it was a hollow victory, as Appleby are still losing clients (Appleby, 2018).

4.**British Airways** (BA) — 380,000 credit card records were stolen for bookings made between 22 August and 5 September 2018 (FT, 2018). This was the first large theft of personal data in the UK national news since GDPR came into force. The transactions were believed to have been intercepted whilst being processed using an external partner's scripts. As the data controller, British Airways were responsible for reporting the breach even though they do not hold the CCV data. It is interesting to note that Air Canada’s data breach over the same period attracted far less publicity (Whittaker, 2018).

5.**Bupa** — 108,000 records were stolen in 2017 by a rogue employee who copied data and tried to sell it (Bupa, 2017). From the Bupa jobs advertised in 2015 we see that Bupa recognised the importance of compliance with security standards ISO27001:2013 and PCI DSS (Bupa, 2015).

6.**Citibank** — A hacker stole 200,000 customer details in 2011 (Packard, 2013). This was the second breach after the bank’s internet platform had been hacked (Kitten, 2011). The data was stolen after the hacker realized that once logged in a user could just alter the URL to access another person’s bank account, and automated the process (Wilson, 2011).

7.**Deloitte** was hacked through an account that did not have two-factor authentication (Daileda, 2017). This is despite Deloitte implementing ISO27001:2013 and being a company that promotes and assists companies with implementing the ISO27001 standard.

8.**Dixons Carphone** — 5.9 million payment cards and 1.2 million personal data records were stolen in 2017. The cards apparently have not been used maliciously as most of them were protected by their chips and PINs. The names, addresses and email addresses of more than one million people were also taken in the breach (Cellan-Jones, 2018).

9.**eBay** was hacked in 2014 due to the hacker obtaining access credentials from some eBay staff (Finkle, 2014). eBay itself does not process credit card transactions as these are done through a payment gateway provided by PayPal; however, eBay and Paypal would have been audited to the highest Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) standards due to the volume of transactions processed. The standard PCI DSS questionnaire D (PCI DSS, 2018) is very stringent and can be very challenging to deploy.

10.**Equifax** had their website hacked due to a security flaw in Apache Struts, an open source Apache Java add-on (Sweet and Liedtke, 2017). The vulnerability is explained by Sophos on the Naked Security website ([https://nakedsecurity.sophos.com/](https://nakedsecurity.sophos.com/)) (Ducklin, 2017).

11.**Facebook** — At least 87 million records were breached. An app was developed that scraped details about people’s personalities, social networks and engagement on the platform. The breach was disclosed on 17 March 2018 and Facebook were fined £500,000 (Hern and Pegg, 2018). Facebook lost over one million users as a result and its share price fell 20% over two weeks during the Cambridge Analytica scandal before recovering six weeks later, based on strong financial results.

12.**Facebook 2** — A further leak in September 2018 was due to problems with Facebook’s code. A quiz app apparently allowed attackers to take control of accounts. Astonishingly, about 50 million accounts were affected (Matsacis and Lapowsky, 2018).

13.**Google** was hacked from China in 2009 and 2011 (Packard, 2013). This was due to a spear phishing attack that gave the attackers access to a Google employee’s system. The attack lasted for about one year (Nakashima, 2013).

14.**Heartland Payment Systems** — 134 million accounts of the 6^(th) largest payment provider in the USA were hacked in 2008 using SQL injection (McGlasson, 2009; Vaas, 2018).

15.**JPMorgan Chase** was hacked by criminals who were not expert hackers. The attackers bought the tools to hack JPMorgan Chase (Economist, 2015). Personal data from 83 million accounts were accessed.

16.**Mossack Fonseca** (Panama Papers) — This leak was the result of the Outlook Web Access component of Exchange 2010 not being patched for three years (Wired, 2016).

17.**MyHeritage** — The hack was disclosed on 4 June 2018. A security researcher reached out to the Chief Information Security Officer of online genealogy platform MyHeritage and revealed they had found a file labeled “myheritage” on a private server outside the company. Upon inspection of the file, officials at MyHeritage determined that the asset contained the email addresses of all users who had signed up with MyHeritage prior to 26 October 2017.

18.**RSA** — 44 million account credentials were leaked in 2011. Two separate hacking groups launched a phishing attack against RSA employees. The leak cost the company $66 million (Armerding, 2018).
