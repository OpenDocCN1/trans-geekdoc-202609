
Sovereign states do not, technically, commit crimes, although their agents can be ordered to conduct activities that harm other states, organisations or individuals.

If one accepts that the points made above are correct, how can the perpetrators of these attacks be held legally or ethically accountable in the future? It is apparent that an international agreement is necessary.

There has been an attempt to implement an International Agreement to set acceptable parameters of cyber attacks. This followed the 2009 DDoS attacks against the Estonian government. This became known as the Tallinn Manual (named after the Estonian capital city where the Agreement was drafted, (NATO CCDCOE, 2013)).

The manual was drafted by member states of NATO and had a US and Western European bias. This led to the proposals in the manual being rejected by China, Russia and Middle Eastern states.

Lucas (2017) states that *‘emergent moral norms’ sufficient to guide and govern the choices and actions of moral agents offer a hopeful prospect for obtaining some greater cooperation and constraint of activities as these choices and actions unfold in the cyber domain. One must be cautioned, however, that if the desired end is the governance of the cyber domain by means of an authentic moral point of view that is internalised, self-imposed and widely shared then we are still confronted with a profound obstacle. The prospects for attaining anything like an authentic morality or common ethos may prove all but unattainable in so diffuse, diverse and divided an environment as the cyber domain represents.”*

This leads one to the assumption that only a truly international and non-geopolitical and ideologically neutral conference will be in a position to develop a globally acceptable set of laws or ethics pertaining to cyber security matters.

Given the identified issues relating to cyber security-based international laws and ethics, how then can organisations implement effective moral codes to ensure that their staff use cyber security tools and treat their colleagues, stakeholders and customers in an equitable manner?

Interested organisations are holding workshops specifically aimed at cyber security ethics. ACM SIGCOMM’s “Cyber-security Research Ethics Dialogue & Strategy Workshop” in May 2013 is one example. The “workshop” embraced the theme of “ethics-bydesign”, and aimed to:

•*`Educate participants about underlying ethics principles and applications;`*

•*`Discuss ethical frameworks and how they are applied across the various stakeholders and respective communities who are involved;`*

•*`Impart recommendations about how ethical frameworks can be used to inform policymakers in evaluating the ethical underpinning of critical policy decisions;`*

•*`Explore cyber security research ethics techniques, tools, standards and practices so researchers can apply ethical principles within their research methodologies;` and*

•*`Discuss speciﬁc case vignettes and explore the ethical implications of common research acts and omissions.”*` (Kenneally and Bailey, 2014)

The IEEE Standards Association is running “IEEE Project: 7000 — Model Process for Addressing Ethical Concerns During System Design”

The IEEE website has the following quote: *“Engineers, technologists and other project stakeholders need a methodology for identifying, analyzing and reconciling ethical concerns of end users at the beginning of systems and software life cycles. The purpose of this standard is to enable the pragmatic application of this type of Value-Based System Design methodology which demonstrates that conceptual analysis of values and an extensive feasibility analysis can help to refine ethical system requirements in systems and software life cycles.”* (IEEE SA, 2018)

Whilst the quote does not specify that such a methodology is needed in the field of cyber security, there is an obvious connection between security programming and ensuring that adequate security controls are embedded in systems during the development of software life cycles.

These workshops and projects indicate that interested global organisations are taking steps to develop and implement ethical processes. However, it is likely that these processes may take some time to be accepted and embedded into national and international organisations.

Another approach to embedding ethics which has been adopted by international and national organisations is for entities to publish “Codes of Conduct”. These are particularly deployed by vocational organisations that provide certification and training in Information Security.

These Codes of Ethics are high level aims that set out the organisation’s expectations of how their members/employees should act in relation to ethics — congruent to how organisations implement Tier 1 policies. If organisations combine these ethics codes with Corporate Responsibility processes and deliver training at induction and as part of an employee’s annual professional and personal development requirements, this should mean that ethical processes become part of the organisational culture.

To this point, the focus has been on implementing ethics as cyber security software, tools and processes are used. When teaching people ethical hacking, educational establishments demonstrate how offensive tools can be used to attack a target device or network. Then descriptions are given as to what “white-hat, black-hat and grey-hat” hackers are. The skills are already “out of the bag” so to speak. Surely there is a need to teach ethics before the tools and skills are made available, particularly when the student is a young child or an immature youth/adult or a non-neurotypical person, as in the case of autistic people.

Obviously, some organisations have a duty to conduct offensive hacking as part of their role (such as intelligence analysts). This would be made clear in contracts and terms of engagement and it is likely that crown immunity would apply. However, there is still a need to make students and employees aware of ethical guidelines.

Training people in ethics in advance of teaching the use of offensive tools will not necessarily deter criminal activity or those hacking for the challenge or the fun of it but it should give an ethical baseline to students and employees.

### 12.1.7.*Laws and ethics*

### 12.1.7.1.*Relevant UK laws*

Interestingly there are not many laws specific to cognitive and physical disabilities in the UK. The major law is the rather catchall Equalities Act 2010 (Equality and Human Rights Commission, 2010) wherein peoples’ rights to equality are guaranteed across the United Kingdom. Exceptionally, there is a law specific to autism in England. The Autism Act 2009 (UK Government, 2009) is the only conditionspecific law within the United Kingdom and demonstrates the level of importance that Parliament attaches to protecting the rights of people on the autistic spectrum. In Scotland, the Scottish Strategy for Autism was developed (Scottish Government, 2011).

In 2010 an Adult Autism Strategy “Fulfilling and Rewarding Lives” (NAS, 2010) was published. This was then superseded by “Think Autism” in April 2014 (NAS, 2014).

There are two further laws for consideration. The Children and Families Act 2014 “*supports young people in preparing for adulthood*” (UK Government, 2014) and The Care Act 2014 “*places a strong emphasis on preventing and delaying needs for care and support, making sure that there is appropriate information and advice for people, support for carers, and promoting integration between social care and health care services. It also places a duty on local authorities to promote a person’s well-being when carrying out any of their care and support functions in respect of that person.”* (UK Government, 2014)
