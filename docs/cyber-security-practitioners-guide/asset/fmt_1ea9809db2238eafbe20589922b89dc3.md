
These socialisation issues can have a detrimental effect on work relationships when autistic traits and behaviours are exhibited (`Test et al`., 2014). These barriers may be ritualised and focused behaviour, inattention to duties, hyperactivity, irritability and shouting, all of which are unacceptable in the workplace and could lead to dismissal. These behaviours can, however, be triggered by misunderstandings in the workplace. Triggers could be sensory overload, misinterpretation in communications and unexpected changes to the work schedule or office infrastructure and facilities (`Hurlbutt and Chalmers`, 2004).

The lack of secondary school educational qualifications and work experience could have a negative impact on employment prospects for autistic people and even if a job is offered there could well be a mismatch between a person’s qualifications and abilities and the duties required by the role (`Holwerda et al`., 2012).

This is indicative of a lack of understanding from employers as to the capabilities of autistic people and supports the findings expressed in the National Autistic Society’s employment survey.

Prospects improve after a university education, whether the qualifications earned are undergraduate or postgraduate qualifications. This is predominantly true where extra transitional support or autistic mentors have been provided by the university or government agencies (`Migliore et al`., 2014).

This does not mean that a university education will guarantee improved employment prospects. Reports indicate that there are still high levels of under-employment and unemployment for graduates with autistic spectrum disorder, with some researchers finding that nearly 50% of autistic people were overeducated for the jobs they hold (`Baldwin et al`., 2014).

Studies show that having mentors during summer school periods can greatly enhance achievement for autistic students in their academic pursuits; especially when the student is encouraged to act as a mentor in the following summer school intake (`Hotez et al`., 2018).

Continuing support from vocational service providers could improve employment success. Indeed, research shows that there are definite benefits when provision is consistent (`Howlin`, 2013). Occupational therapists could provide the necessary vocational training and help to prepare autistic people to transfer into employment through a combination of support and advice to potential employers about any workplace adaptations. However, there is an issue with the lack of availability of qualified and experienced vocational OTs in the UK at present.

Support workers are employed to provide continuous support to severely disabled people in either voluntary or employed positions. Evidence suggests that having a support worker provides enough specific and intensive support to be of benefit to people on the autistic spectrum despite the initial increased costs. The support worker delivers long term benefits to the employee, employer and the government (`Howlin et al`., 2005).

Support services have been shown to raise employment retention rates to nearly 90% for those autistic people who have access to them. This chapter will look at two approaches: TEACCH and Project SEARCH.

### 12.1.2.*TEACCH (Treatment and Education of Autistic and related Communication-handicapped Children)*

Mesibov and Shea (2010) state `TEACCH` “is a clinical service and professional training program, based at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, that has incorporated and contributed to the evidence base of autism interventions”. The late Eric Schopler, Ph.D., started the programme in 1972. The TEACCH approach is called ‘‘Structured Teaching.’’ Structured Teaching is based on evidence and the observation that individuals with autism share a pattern of neuropsychological deficits and strengths that we call the ‘Culture of Autism’, which includes the following characteristics:

1.`Relative strength in and preference for processing visual information (compared to difﬁculties with auditory processing, particularly of language).`

2.`Heightened attention to details but difﬁculty with sequencing, integrating, connecting, or deriving meaning from them`

3.`Enormous variability in attention (individuals can be very distractible at times, and at other times intensely focused, with difﬁculties shifting attention efﬁciently).`

4.`Communication problems, which vary by developmental level, but always include impairments in the initiation and social use of language (pragmatics).`

5.`Difﬁculty with concepts of time including moving through activities too quickly or too slowly and having problems recognizing the beginning or end of an activity, how long the activity will last, and when it will be ﬁnished.`

6.`Tendency to become attached to routines and the settings where they are established, so that activities may be difﬁcult to transfer or generalize from the original learning situation, and disruptions in routines can be uncomfortable, confusing, or upsetting.`

7.`Very intense interests and impulses to engage in favored activities and difﬁculties disengaging once engaged.`

8.`Marked sensory preferences and aversions.”` (`Mesibov and Shea`, 2010)

The support generated from this approach is aimed at the individual and involves the management of time, environment and sequences of events in order to facilitate clearer learning and making activities easier to perform.

The TEACCH program has proven to be effective and relatively easy to implement and has gained international support. In the UK, the National Autistic Society promotes its use and highlights that “*the TEACCH approach tries to respond to the needs of autistic people using the best available approaches and methods known so far, for educating and teaching autonomy. It is not a single method and can be used alongside other approaches. Our support uses techniques from the TEACCH approach, and we provide TEACCH training to professionals.*”

`T`eaching — sharing autism knowledge and increasing the skill level of professionals and practitioners.

`E`xpanding — increasing own knowledge to provide high-quality services to people on the autism spectrum and their families.

`A`ppreciating — appreciating the strengths and uniqueness of autistic culture.

`C`ollaborating and `C`ooperating with colleagues, other professionals, people on the autism spectrum and their families.

`H`olistic — adopting a holistic approach, looking at the person, their family and community.

Some of the TEACCH Autism Programme priorities are:

•`focussing on the person, their skills, interests and needs`

•`understanding the ‘culture of autism’ and identifying differences based on individualised assessments`

•`using visual structures to organise the environment and tasks when teaching skills`

•`being broad-based, i.e. support people at work, teach skills but also ensure that people are supported during leisure and/or social activities`

•`being flexible and teaching flexibility.`

`The TEACCH principles and techniques may always need to be in place for someone, but they may look very different at different stages of the person’s life.”` (`NAS`, 2018)

### 12.1.3.*Project SEARCH*

Project SEARCH is a transition programme developed to help youths with disabilities. It has been adapted to provide support to help young people with ASD to transition into employment and further education.

`"Key Project SEARCH programme components include setting a goal for employment, providing successive intensive internships in a community business, and assuring collaboration between school and adult services staff. Supports specific to students with ASD include providing intensive instruction in social, communication, and job skills; visual supports; and work routine and structure",` (`Wehman et al`., 2012).
