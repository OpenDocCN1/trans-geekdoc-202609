
As can be viewed in [Fig. 12.4](#chapter12.xhtml#fig12.4), "`If brain tissue is damaged or does not develop fully, the ventricles — vessels filled with cerebrospinal fluid, shown in blue in this image based on neuroimaging work — expand to fill the space`". Temple Grandin’s enlarged left ventricle is a sign of abnormalities in her left hemisphere, which typically handles language, and may account for the difficulties she has with processing words. To make up for this, the right hemisphere sometimes overcompensates, which can lead to special abilities in music, art, and visual memory. Savantism is not well-understood, but between a tenth and a third of people with autism may have some of these abilities. Cooperrider’s team also discovered that Grandin’s amygdala, the almond-shaped organ said to play an important role in emotional processing, is larger than normal. This was not a surprising finding because among other functions, this region processes fear and anxiety, affective states often affected by autism. Her fusiform gyrus is smaller than normal — also not a surprise, since this region is `involved in recognizing faces, a social skill that autism may disrupt`." (McGowan, 2013)

The two case studies seem to indicate that there are anatomical differences in brain structure in autistic people when compared to neurotypical brains as can be seen in the comparative brain scans shown in [Fig. 12.4](#chapter12.xhtml#fig12.4) above. Grandin’s brain scan shows marked differences in anatomical structure from the so-called normal brain. These differences could be caused by much more densely packed neurons in the highlighted area in Grandin’s brain scan. As the neurons are responsible for passing electrical impulses, this extra density may indicate that the electrical impulses occur faster and so allow for increased ability in particular areas. This can be called savantism, special isolated skills or perceptual peaks.

Also, the two subjects report that they experience their environment differently from how most people would experience it.

These differences could be advantageous in cyber security roles in that issues may be picked up more quickly. This may be particularly useful in combating an ongoing attack or in completing a forensic examination. Business-as-usual processes could also be more efficient and effective if the analyst was able to instantaneously spot errors or code in a particularly fast way.

If the supposition documented by McGowan (2013), "`Savantism is not well-understood, but between a tenth and a third of people with autism may have some of these abilities`", is correct, then there could be between 70,000 and 235,000 autistic people in the UK with savant skills that may be useful in cyber security roles. Obviously not all will be useful in cyber security roles but this could indicate that there is a sizeable relevant skill set waiting to be identified and utilised in the autistic demographic.

### 12.3.Analysis and Critical Discussion

### 12.3.1.**Matching traits with opportunities**

The National Autistic Society provides support, information and services for autistic people (including those people with Asperger’s Syndrome), and their relatives and guardians in the United Kingdom. It is a leading charity that campaigns for a better world for autistic people. The Society describes autism as "`a lifelong, developmental disability that affects how a person communicates with and relates to other people, and how they experience the world around them.`" (National Autistic Society, 2017)

The Society has been working with interested organisations, educational establishments and potential employees to identify specific traits that can be utilised in the workplace or act as a base for further education and training.

### 12.3.1.1.*Traits and strengths*

Listed below are some of the traits and strengths exhibited by autists (National Autistic Society, 2016):

•`Attention to detail`

•`Methodical approach`

•`Good memory for factual information`

•`Strong problem-solving skills`

•`Strong numerical skills`

•`Neuro-diverses will not be attached to this document.

A comparison of the traits and strengths and roles listed above does seem to indicate a useful correlation that could, with the implementation of appropriate support processes, lead to meaningful employment and mutually beneficial work relationships for both autistic people and employer organisations.

### 12.3.2.*Potential issues*

There are a number of traits that are exhibited by people on the Spectrum that should be considered by employer organisations to ensure an equitable relationship.

The National Autistic Society states `"Autistic people see, hear and feel the world differently to other people. If you are autistic, you are autistic for life; autism is not an illness or disease and cannot be ‘cured’. Often people feel being autistic is a fundamental aspect of their identity."` (National Autistic Society, 2017)

These potential issues could be seen as reasons to avoid employing autistic people but the research documented in this paper indicates that with some reasonable adjustments in the work environment and how communication is handled between the autistic employee and the organisation, a mutually beneficial working relationship is possible.
