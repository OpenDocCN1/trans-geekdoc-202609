### 2.2. Security Incident Response from an Organisation’s Viewpoint

Whenever an organisation comes under sustained or targeted cyber attack, it is the responsibility of the security team, working in conjunction with other operational or business teams, to mitigate the attack to the best of their abilities. Sometimes this will entail little input from other teams in the organisation; sometimes it may entail operational teams in the business working with other external thirdparty companies/agencies.

To enable the security team, and the organisation in general, to deal with cyber attacks successfully, there should be effective policies, procedures and guidelines in place that enable the employees in an organisation to be aware of what a cyber security incident is and what their actions should be when they detect a breach of policy or observe a suspicious event.

Procedures put in place by a security team are there to enable the security team or an operational team to better handle specific types of cyber attack, be they phishing campaigns against specific individuals in an organisation or virus/ransomware outbreaks right through to Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) attacks against an organisation’s internet-facing services.

This section will concern itself with policies and procedures that should be in place in an organisation that will enable it to react to and manage to a logical conclusion a cyber attack aimed at an organisation.

### 2.2.1.*Creating effective security policies*

Security policies are at the heart of an organisation’s security posture. Having effective and pertinent security policies defined for an organisation is the first port of call for any new security department.

Policies are in themselves security controls. A breach in a security policy by an employee could trigger a human-resources (HR) incident whereby the employee may have to take part in some sort of HR review process. In the worst-case scenarios, a breach of a security policy could lead to the termination of an employee.

Whenever a new employee starts with an organisation, as part of the induction process into the organisation, the new employee should acknowledge that they have read the relevant policies applicable to them. This then puts the onus on the employee to adhere to the policy and report breaches of the policy to the relevant party.

Security policies should be based on a standard template for the organisation. This ensures consistency of approach from the company and is also a good practice from an auditors’ point of view. If an auditor were to look at 20–30 policies from a company and they all had different layouts, then this may suggest to the auditor that there is no “joined up plan” in respect to the company’s policies. Conversely, if an auditor were to look at 20 policies and see that they all maintained a standard layout with a consistent approach, then that makes it easier for the auditor to audit and it also makes it much easier for an employee wishing to get to the heart of the policy statement itself.

### 2.2.1.1.*Format and layout of a policy*

The thought of creating a policy from scratch can be quite daunting. It was for the author when he had to create a new policy for the first time. What he soon realised though is that there are a lot of reference policies available freely on the internet. Though these may not fit your organization exactly as you would like, reading other policies gives you a general overview of what a policy is, and what it comprises. SANS Institute, for example, have multiple security policy templates. You just need to fill in the organisation’s name and you will have a working policy.

When creating policies from another person’s/organisation’s policy, it is important that you consider the wording of the policy. Does it “fit” with what you want your policy to say? If not, what can be changed to make it more applicable?

The wording of a policy is also important. The writer of the policy does not need to be a wordsmith, but word usage in policy is important. The word “SHOULD” is ambiguous in policy. You are telling someone that they should do something, but that it’s ok if they don’t. Replacing “SHOULD” with “MUST” changes the reading of the sentence and its applicability.

Consider the following control statement:

All company assets MUST be patched on a regular basis.

Reading this statement, the reader can take away two meanings. The first of these is that it is not mandatory that systems are patched regularly, and the second of these is that all assets are patched regularly.

If we rewrite the control statement as follows, it is much clearer:

All company assets MUST be patched on a regular basis.

Reading this, the only thing a reader can take away is that all systems MUST be patched regularly. You could argue that regularly is also ambiguous as it could mean weekly or annually since both are regular. Other wording in the policy could help you get around this ambiguity.

Brander (1997) in his RFC 2119 “Key words for use in RFCs to Indicate Requirement Levels” sets out to define the use of the above keywords and several others as they should be used in RFC documents. Such clarity is sometimes achievable in a good policy and it assists both the reader and the writer in the definition of the policy. The RFC states:

“The key words “MUST”, “MUST NOT”, “REQUIRED”, “SHALL”, “SHALL NOT”, “SHOULD”, “SHOULD NOT”, “RECOMMENDED”, “MAY”, and “OPTIONAL” in this document are to be interpreted as described in RFC 2119”
