
Articles 33 and 34 of GDPR explicitly outline requirements for reporting data breaches within a specific timeframe and for organisations to demonstrate reasonable steps have been taken to secure personal data (GDPR, 2016). GDPR promulgates the foundation for a risk-based approach to assess the risk to personal data, and for privacy protection measures to be introduced by default when collecting and processing personal data. This requires organisations who are either controllers or processors of personal data to assess the risks relating to the rights and freedom of data subjects and formulate appropriate policies and security measures to protect personal data (Garber, 2018). This indicates that complying with GDPR necessitates a clear and consistent approach towards interpreting and integrating the relevant articles and existing incident response and breach notification plans.

The aim of this research is to investigate compliance challenges by formulating the following questions:

1. What are the main challenges and enablers of implementing GDPR into an incident response and breach notification plan?

2. What decision support process or system could be developed to address the challenges identified?

3. What method could be adopted to assess the relevance and usability of the design for meeting the new requirements of incident response and breach notification challenges?

### 8.1.1.*Background*

In recent years, the upsurge in cyber attacks and data breach incidents has become a phenomenon that represents an existential threat for organisations who fail to formulate robust security policies and stringent measures to safeguard valuable data. Appropriate preparation plans are imperative for effectively responding to the evolving threats that could cause irreparable damage to business operations and reputation. This helped to precipitate a paradigm shift in recognising the value of personal and business information, as well as the importance of adapting to the changing legal and regulatory landscape.

Despite this recognition and various measures taken by organisations to implement security policies via existing incident handling frameworks, new requirements for breach notification and communication in GDPR have created potential gaps in compliance that need to be re-examined. Moreover, there are no specific recommendations in GDPR that organisations can check against to verify they are fully compliant. According to Hedley and Jacobs (2017), verifying compliance will be done through either external audits or a process of self-assessment by individual organisations who interpret and implement relevant areas of the regulation into their existing incident handling frameworks with policies and procedures.

This indicates that organisations will need to justify and demonstrate to their relevant supervisory authority that stringent security safeguards are in place for securing personal data. The research director of Gartner, Mark Horvath (2018), elaborated that, even if appropriate security policies are established, organisations could still be held accountable under GDPR if employees intentionally or accidentally precipitate serious data breach incidents.

In 2015, a reported cyber attack on telecommunications group Talk Talk precipitated several deleterious consequences that included a rapid decline in stock market value, a loss of revenue amounting to £80 million, and the alienation of many of the customers affected. The Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) conducted subsequent investigations and a £400,000 penalty was levied on the company for failing to adequately secure their customers’ personal data (Ico.org.uk, 2018). Fowler (2016) noted that, under the stringent edicts outlined in the GDPR, the fines imposed could have amounted to approximately £70 million. The new mandatory 72-hour deadline outlined in Article 33 for reporting instances of data breach has left some organisations behind the curve in their efforts to establish appropriate frameworks and security measures for meeting compliance under GDPR (Mansfield-Devine, 2016).

The sophistication of new threats and the absence of appropriate response approaches and security policies can greatly increase the chances of a threat exploiting vulnerabilities in an information system with the result that personal data is compromised. ### 8.2.1.*Changes in the legal and regulatory landscape*

The introduction of the GDPR denotes a significant inflection point in which a minimum tick box approach to compliance and a self-regulation paradigm will no longer be sufficient for complying with the new requirements. Various emerging threats and new attack vectors being employed by disparate threat actors have contributed to the profusion of reported cyber attacks and increased data breach incidents. The implementation of GDPR across the EU in 2018 introduced a radical far-reaching approach that brings data protection laws in line with the dynamic and evolving way in which personal and sensitive data is collected and processed. The pervasiveness of internet-ready devices within a highly data-driven interconnected world has led to the generation of a vast amount of personal data that needs to be protected. ### 8.2.2.*Exploration of a data breach and its wider impact*

The rise in reported data breach incidents has become a notable concern for businesses and consumers because of the deleterious impact it can have on a company’s reputation as well as on the individuals who are affected by the data breach. To strengthen the privacy and security of personal data, GDPR regulations outlined important mandatory stipulations for data controllers and processors. Articles (24) and (31) outline the responsibility of the controller requiring the implementation of technical and organisational measures to demonstrate stringent measures to protect data and develop accountability (EU, GDPR, 2016). In recent years, numerous publications about data breaches have shown a litany of security failures that had contributed to vast amounts of data being compromised, and the trend shows no sign of abating.
