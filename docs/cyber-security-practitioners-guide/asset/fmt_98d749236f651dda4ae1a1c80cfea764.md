
Where an autistic person is located could depend on the employer’s or their end clients’ requirements and this is something that needs to be taken into consideration.

The responses do indicate that employer organisations are considering some flexibility on deployment location.

The results seem to indicate that the organisations will rely upon existing relevant laws and their own policy document sets. The majority of the organisations highlighted their own Corporate Social Responsibility activities as being relevant. Only 25% reported that they had looked into ethical requirements, however, they did not stipulate how this was undertaken or what the results were. Less than half indicated that they were considering a mentorship scheme.

Overall, the small survey responses indicate that there are cyber security roles potentially available to autistic people.

Further consideration by the employer organisations needs to be given to ethical considerations on how the organisation will support the effective introduction of autistic people into the workforce and what adjustments need to be made.

Research shows that providing mentors is a positive step, particularly when mentors are themselves autistic and have been involved with a previous iteration of mentorship.

Change can be an issue and the employer organisations have indicated a willingness to allow flexibility in location. Combining this with effective communication on any planned changes in personnel, office environment or duties should lead to an effective business relationship for all parties.

### 12.3.4.*Autistic brain structural anomalies*

While basic brain anatomy has been discussed in previous sections, [Fig. 12.9](#chapter12.xhtml#fig12.9) in this section details the differences between a “normal” brain and an autistic brain.

![image](images/fig12.9.jpg)

**Fig. 12.9.**Anomalies in brain activity and anatomy — Autistic brain vs. control brain (Yamada *et al*., 2016).

Research indicates that an autistic brain may have more densely grouped regions at the cerebral cortex and the cerebellum. This extra density can manifest itself in more folds in these regions. These folds can increase the surface area of the brain and bring neurons into closer proximity to each other.

*“The insular cortex comprises multiple functionally differentiated subregions, each of which has different patterns of connectivity with other brain regions. Such diverse connectivity patterns are thought to underlie a wide range of insular functions, including cognitive, affective, and sensorimotor processing, many of which are abnormal in autism spectrum disorder (ASD).”* (Yamada *et al*., 2016)

The extra surface area and the close proximity of neurons could mean that neurological signals pass more effectively and efficiently in the areas that have anatomical abnormalities. This could then mean that the autistic person can utilise these differences in more focussed ways, leading to peak performances in, for instance, identifying patterns useful in checking network security baselines, penetration testing, configuring SIEM tools, writing software codes, etc.

Neurons process and transmit electrical-chemical signals throughout the nervous system and can be found in the brain, spinal column and peripheral nerves. The potential cognitive phenomena exhibited by autistic people can manifest across a range of subjects or areas, such as mathematics, exceptional memory, increased perceptual capacity, visual acuity, languages, music and art.

Mathematics can be directly associated with cyber security skills when one considers elements such as logic, cryptoanalysis, statistics, algorithms and computation. Similarly, memory skills, perceptual capacity — where there is an ability to process large amounts of information — and visual acuity can also be utilised in cyber security roles.

### 12.4.Conclusions

As evidenced in this research, there are traits and strengths exhibited by people on the Autistic Spectrum that can be utilised effectively by cyber security industry employer organisations.

There are some potential issues that should be taken into consideration to ensure an equitable relationship. There is the potential for anxiety and disruption for the new employee, the existing employees and the employer.

Although unlikely, there is the potential for either deliberate or accidental exploitation of the individual, particularly when cyber security tools are deployed in furtherance of national interests. The natural inclinations of some autists could also mean that they become focussed on discovering vulnerabilities in a given system or network and will keep at it regardless of the time it takes or the effect it could be having on them personally.

By developing ethical policies and support processes, these issues can be avoided or mitigated. There are existing laws that provide a level of protection but adopting ethical and support functionality will mean that both sides will understand their responsibilities and build an effective and fair working relationship.

The anatomical differences in brain structure and the denser collection of neurons in the rear of the brain (which function by passing electrical impulses) seem to indicate that in certain circumstances it is possible for the autistic person to exhibit special isolated skills, perceptual peaks or savant capabilities. These abilities can then produce some remarkable skills that can be effectively utilised in cyber security roles.
