
The voice requirements in wireless networks are different from those in wired networks. In the wireless networks we must carefully investigate and analyse the problems associated with this integration. The transmission of a voice is a real-time application that is sensitive to requirements in term of delay. For example, in phone call conversations, the voice should reach the destination quickly and efficiently. If the voice is delayed, the quality of the call will be affected. This quality of the call will also be affected if security rules are applied. Furthermore, this problem will become worse in wireless networks because of the nature of wireless networks. Many researchers have paid close attention to real-time application in wireless networks. They have tried to address some of the issues concerning security and delay. For instance, Binod and Hyuk (2010) and Emmanouis and Christos (2012) suggested a secure model for autonomous networks like mobile ad-hoc networks to create real-time communication during disaster rescue operations. Secure P2PSIP, intrusion detection and secure routing methods are a vital part of any secure model. The design of these extensions helps to meet the requirements of a mission-critical MANET where rescuers can create links to communicate with each other by using lightweight devices such as PDAs. Liang and Han (2011) designed a new security framework to enable different multimedia services in an IoT environment. Also, they presented a novel media-aware traffic security style based on the proposed traffic classification to enable the supply of various multimedia services to customers anywhere at any time. Furthermore, they proposed the scheme’s basis and approach, that is, to perform a good trade-off between system flexibility and efficiency.

However, there are still some flaws in the real-time application of security and cryptography schemes. So, the question is: how does one transmit a voice securely over a wireless network without affecting its quality? As previously mentioned, the voice needs to be transmitted with speed and security at the same time, and this should be addressed in this research.

### 11.3.Wi-Fi Vulnerabilities

Wireless Fidelity (Wi-Fi) is the most widely-used network in the information technology field. It can be used indoors and outdoors, and it follows the IEEE 802.11 standard. There are two possible topologies that could form this network. The two resulting types of network are the ad-hoc network and the infrastructure network.

`Vulnerability` is a cyber security term that refers to a flaw in a system that can leave it open to attack. A vulnerability may also refer to any type of weakness in a computer system itself, in a set of procedures, or in anything that leaves information security exposed to a threat. (Wolfram, 2018)

Security researchers¹ have discovered a major vulnerability in Wi-Fi Protected Access 2 (WPA2). WPA2 is a type of encryption used to secure the vast majority of Wi-Fi networks. A WPA2 network provides unique encryption keys for each wireless client that connects to it.

Think of encryption as a secret code that can only be deciphered if you have the “key,” and a vital technology that helps keep digital data away from intruders and identity thieves.

The vulnerability, dubbed ‘KRACKs’ (Key Reinstallation Attacks), is actually a group of multiple vulnerabilities that when successfully exploited, could allow attackers to intercept and steal data transmitted across a Wi-Fi network. Digital personal information that is transmitted over the Internet or stored on your connected devices — such as your driver’s license number, Social Security number, credit card numbers, and more — could be vulnerable. (Norton, 2017)

### 11.3.1.*Threats and attacks*

• `Routing Attacks`: The routing signal is modified by unauthorized nodes and rerouted through an incorrect route in order to provide unauthorized data access to the attacker. Such attacks include breaking the neighbour, black hole, routing table poisoning, worm hole, and replay attacks.

• `Eavesdropping`: This is an unidentifiable, passive attack with no impact on normal system processing. The attacker aims to passively intercept data by breaking encryption, without altering the normal routing protocol (Goodrich and Tamassia, 2011).

• `Denial of Service (DoS)`: This attack aims to disrupt routing in order to prevent the network from functioning and providing normal service. The attacker sends a massive volume of simultaneous requests to overwhelm the server and hamper its response time by jamming its mechanisms, ideally halting the complete system.

• `Sybil Attack`: Fake identities are deployed (i.e. in nodes) to gain access to wireless sensor networks in these attacks (Suriya and Rajamani, 2015).

• `Masquerading`: Similar to a Sybil attack, masquerading nodes (presenting themselves as legitimate nodes) intercept data during the neighbour acquisition process, and they either modify or replay these messages.

• `Alteration`: These attacks involve unauthorized alterations to data and codes, falsifying the system integrity.

### 11.3.2.*Wireless sensor networks*

Nowadays, the world is rapidly moving toward becoming a wireless environment. In 2020 most devices will connect wirelessly. The most significant part of this technology is wireless sensor networks (WSNs). According to Matin and Islam (2012), a WSN can be defined as “a self-configured and infrastructure-less wireless network to monitor physical or environmental conditions, such as temperature, sound, vibration, pressure, motion or pollutants and to cooperatively pass their data through the network to a main location or sink where the data can be observed and analysed”.

Currently, many researchers are focusing on real-time Wireless Multimedia Sensor Networks (WMSNs), because they support many application layer services in order to solve real life concerns, such as health monitoring, transport management, weather forecasting and safety and security investigation (Elhoseny and Hassanien, 2018; Ehsan and Hamdaoui, 2012). A traditional WMSN consists of a variety of sensor nodes, including both simple and multimedia sensor nodes, and a Base Station (BS), as shown in [Fig. 11.1](#chapter11.xhtml#fig11.1).

WMSNs support the practical applications of advanced technologies for remote control, including IPv6 over Low power Wireless Personal Area Networks (6LowPAN), and for surveillance, within the Internet of Things (IoT) (Usman *et al*., 2018). WSNs, like MANETs, have major security issues. The fundamental problem is that there is a trade-off between security and performance.
