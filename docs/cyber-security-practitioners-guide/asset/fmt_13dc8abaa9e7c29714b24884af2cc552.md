## Ethical Considerations and a Proposed Support Process When Employing People With Autistic Spectrum Disorder in Cyber Security Roles

`Northumbria University London, UK`

## ABSTRACT

There is an acknowledged global shortage in qualified and skilled cyber security practitioners, so much so that governments, employers and educational establishments are developing new routes and opportunities to encourage interest and applications from demographics that would not normally apply for cyber security roles. These demographics include women, young children and people on the autistic spectrum. The potential employment of people from this last demographic — people on the Autistic Spectrum — in cyber security roles will be the focus of this research. Two areas are of interest, the ethical considerations in employing people on the Autistic Spectrum and the identification of a strategy to support the employee and employer relationship. Both are discussed here. In Europe the shortfall is expected to be in the region of 350,000 employees by the year 2020 and in the United States the number is expected to be 1.2 million by the same date. This research looks at how specific autistic traits and strengths can be matched to cyber security vacancies and discusses ethical considerations and a potential support process. A qualitative research methodology was used to identify suitable traits and potential cyber security vacancies. Ethical principles and a proposed support process are put forward to allow potential employers and autistic employees to engage in equitable employment opportunities. The autistic demographic does indeed offer skilled and capable resources to help fill cyber security vacancies; however, work is needed to allow both sides to benefit from the opportunities.

*Keywords*: Autistic Spectrum Disorder, ASD, ethics, cyber security, training, employment opportunities, special needs, traits

### 12.1.Literature Review

Employers are competing against each other to attract potential employees who have the necessary qualifications, skills or aptitude to succeed in cyber security roles. This is due to shortages in the United States, where approximately 1.8 million vacancies are expected by 2020 (Frost and Sullivan Executive Brief, 2017), and in the European Union, where the expectation is that there will be unfilled vacancies totalling 350,000 until 2020 (Ashford, 2017). This means that employment opportunities in cyber security are excellent with benefits that include high salaries, work-life balance packages and further incentives.

Baker states, “It has been firmly established that the nation’s cyber workforce is lacking in numbers and in talented individuals needed to effectively operate in cyberspace. There is also undeniable evidence that the U.S. is trailing behind other countries in preparedness and sophistication in the cyber domain. While several directives, frameworks, programs, and initiatives exist that are designed to grow, train, and retain a skilled cyber security workforce, there are some gap areas where further focus could contribute to attaining measurable success.” (Baker, 2013)

Given the budgetary constraints that the government of the United Kingdom is currently experiencing due to the existing austerity measures, attracting qualified and skilled cyber security professionals could prove difficult. To alleviate this, various government departments have been and are running a number of initiatives aimed at identifying and encouraging potential employees. These initiatives include Cyber First (NCSC, 2018), Cyber Summer Schools (GCHQ, 2018) and Cyber Discovery (DCMS, 2017).

The cyber security industry is also conducting research aimed at identifying expertise within its own community to encourage professional development within the workforce.

Fontenele states, “One of the major challenges in current cyber security initiatives is to place skilled people wherever needed whilst reducing the overall knowledge gap. Thus, in order to orchestrate roles in such a complex and dynamic environment, a novel approach *to discover talent within the cyber security community is required.*” (Fontenele, 2017)

The Center for Cyber Safety and Education has recently completed a research paper *“Women in Cyber Security”*, which identified that 11% of the cyber security workforce are women. The paper concluded that women are underrepresented in cyber security roles and organisations needed to “take swift and considerable actions to engage, develop and retain women in the field or the global workforce gap will continue to grow year over year” (Frost & Sullivan, 2017).

Another demographic that is under consideration for providing potential cyber security employees is that of people with Autistic Spectrum Disorder.

The Israeli Defence Forces have used people on the Autistic Spectrum to good effect in intelligence analyst roles. Most notably, Unit 9900, one of Israel’s military intelligence units, has deployed male and female soldiers who have Autistic Spectrum Disorder in analysis roles where specific useful traits can be utilised to good effect (AIPAC, 2016).
