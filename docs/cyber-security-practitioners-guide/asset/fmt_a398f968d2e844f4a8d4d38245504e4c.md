
Ransomwares are not only built for the sole purpose of affecting regular citizens but are also built for a larger cause. Since malware development can be used for money making and has already become a booming business, malware developers have concentrated attacks on industries where more benefit could be derived. A report by Scientific American Magazine states that cyber criminals have been targeting and crippling important “institutions that everybody relies on” through different types of ransomware (Martin, 2017). According to a report by CSO, the three industries which have been most affected by ransomware attacks are the following: healthcare, government and education. Out of these, `healthcare` has been the most affected of all (Osborne, 2018). These industries are targeted more since they run 24/7 and thus need their updated files all the time. Serious threats can arise when institutional or organisational data from these industries is leaked or sold on the dark web. This is why these industries tend to pay for the ransom quicker than the other ransomware victims (Cylance, 2018).

Alarmingly, it has been made clear in a report by Cylance (called the 2017 Threat Report) that `healthcare` has obviously been targeted the most, and that law enforcement and/or ICT developers within the healthcare industry do not seem to have any methods to overcome these threats.

The pie charts below show the ransomware attacks performed on each type of industry over the span of a year (Kidron, 2017). Whilst there was a significant drop in the percentage of total ransomware attacks affecting almost every industry from 2016 to 2017, as shown in [Fig. 5.7](#chapter5.xhtml#fig5.7), the percentage of total ransomware attacks affecting the healthcare and food industries increased (by 24 percent for the healthcare industry). Scientific American Magazine (Martin, 2017) has reported that, aside from the Pennsylvania Senate incident, police stations such as the Swansea Police Department (2013) and Fire Departments in Maryland (2015) have been struck by ransomwares due to the simple fact that these organisations hold sensitive and important data about citizens which is vital for the proper functioning of the government. The police department of Swansea had to pay 750 USD just to recover their files but the Fire Department of Maryland opted not to pay in order to discourage further attacks.

Scientific American Magazine’s report (Martin, 2017), delivered a clear warning to those in charge of law enforcement and cyber security. These cyber attacks are happening to the health industry and the government now, but a day will come when these malicious attacks could become worse. Critical infrastructures such as dams, power grids or worse could be attacked. Supervisory Control And Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems, which must be frequent users of the internet in order to function, could also be attacked. A report suggests cyber criminals have coded a ransomware which is dedicated to infecting SCADA systems and this ransomware is named LogicLocker (Kidron, 2017). This ransomware uses socket APIs and manipulates weak credential-authentication mechanisms into infecting the workstation. After infection, the ransomware does not encrypt the files but instead deletes all current authentication passwords and lets the command and control centre change the password to one of the attacker’s choice. This way, the company affected needs to pay the ransom for the new authentication details (Turpin and Harmsworth, 2001).

### 5.6.Preventing Ransomware Attacks

Different companies, different researchers and different anti-virus brands have different suggestions for preventing ransomware infection and spreading. Although different research studies have yielded different and sometimes opposing opinions on how to prevent ransomware, the general approach to preventing ransomware is always the same. Prevention can be done in two ways, which we call human prevention and advanced prevention.

a)**Human Prevention**

**Information Dissemination:** With the advancement of technology, people are now expected to gather information, be vigilant and `educate` themselves about the latest news in the digital society (Ali, 2017). Information about harmful software, not just `ransomware`, should be disseminated on different platforms so that they reach a wider audience. These platforms are as follows (Carbon Black, 2018):

•`Mail` (Physical Mail or Email)

•`Newsletters`

•`Company or educational briefings`

•`Websites`

•`Reports`

•`Conferences`

•`Workshops`

•`Other media` (Social Media etc.)

Although people can learn plenty from these sources, they should be wise enough to choose credible sources like the government or IEEE. Websites accessed for information regarding malwares should ideally be from anti-virus company blogs such as Kaspersky, Avast, Esset, etc.

b) **Technical Prevention:** A few technical but simple steps are suggested by multiple research studies in the field of malware. These steps are subcategorised by different digital vectors and most of these steps can be found online.

**Back-up, Back-up, Back-up!** (Rajpal and Kotov, 2014):

•`Regularly Back-up Data`: Back-up could be done in two different ways. Since there is a minimal chance of being hit by ransomware on both your device as well as a selected cloud application, users are advised to use a cloud back-up for important and sensitive files. This is the same reason why mobile ransomware does not encrypt files inside the mobile device but just locks the whole system instead.

•`External Hard Disk Back-up`: An external hard disk can also be suggested as a back-up measure, but researchers suggest that after back-up, external hard disks should be unplugged from the computers (Ali, 2017).

•`Deploy User Account Control`: This will help limit the access of ransomwares since most ransomware procedures require administrative permissions.

**Email Security** (O'Brien, 2017):

•`Prevent downloads of unknown attachment files`.

•`Do not open spam mails` if the mail is sent by an unknown user.

•`Install special email defender applications` that have real-time link following.

•`Disable macro scripts` on the files received via emails (O'Brien, 2017).

**Anti-Virus:**

•`Anti-virus software` should always be updated (Ali, 2017).

**Other Important Preventive Measures:**

•`Disable remote services` if not in use.

•`Disable Bluetooth services` if not in use.

•`Limit access to unreliable websites` (i.e. porn sites).

•`Limit clicking on links` provided by colleagues; instead, type the full address into the link bar.

**Prevention for Mobile Users** (Ismail, 2018):

•`Apps` should only be installed from trusted application stores.

•`Check the permissions` of the application being downloaded to see if the permissions are appropriate for the use of the application.

•`Try as much as possible not to jailbreak` iOS platforms

c)Advanced Prevention

Multiple research studies online have given suggestions for different approaches that bigger companies can use in preventing ransomware attacks. A few of the most effective advanced prevention techniques are (Zetter, 2016):

•`Key back-up`

•`Process monitoring` on Android platforms

•`Using a connection monitor` and connection breaker

•`Honeypot techniques`
