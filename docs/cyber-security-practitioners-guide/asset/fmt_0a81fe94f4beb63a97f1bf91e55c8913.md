
—“Data concerning vulnerable data subjects”: the definition of a vulnerable data subject can change with time and space. Usually children, elderlies and people with physical or mental disabilities fall under this category. Most laws consider employees to be a group vulnerable to their employers, so organisations have to treat data related to work with extra precautions. Other situations must be considered case by case, with consideration of the sociological context of the data subjects.

—“Innovative use or applying new technological or organisational solutions”: the fact that the GDPR has isolated this type of processing is a sign of the times and of the concerns that new technologies are creating. There is a fear of the unknown, so whatever is new is deemed to be particularly dangerous. Machine learning or Artificial Intelligence, combined with Big Data, are definitely a growing concern in society and so are the privacy concerns related to the Internet-of-Things. At the same time, the general population seems to accept them as part of daily life and whatever comes next is for no one to forecast, so the legislators took the safe path. WP29 includes in the description of this item biometric technologies like fingerprint and facial recognition, but it also cites the GDPR to refer to the current state of the art as the main referential for the evaluation of what is innovative (Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, 2017c). Most experts in biometrics would not consider fingerprint authentication methods an innovative technology, but a new algorithm for fingerprint biometrical authentication would definitely fall into this category. Some recent biometric technologies, like cognitive biometrics, can be of even more concern, given that they include health related data (heart beat patterns, brain activity patterns, skin conductivity, etc.) (Revett and de Magalhães, 2010).

—Processing that “prevents data subjects from exercising a right or using a service or a contract”: this seems to be directed at preliminary analyses of clients, so whenever this is applied, the first item of this list (“Evaluation or scoring of data subjects”) is likely to also be applied.

It seems clear that the decision on the legal obligation to do a DPIA, like the DPIA itself, should be carried out by an exempt third party with technical and legal knowledge of information technology (databases, network communications, encryption, authentication/identification, operating systems, web programming, etc.) and privacy laws and without any conflict of interest. However, even if there is no legal obligation to do a DPIA, this is still a valuable tool for any organisation and it can prove itself priceless in case of a data breach.

### 15.8.The Fines and the Press

The GDPR has become most famous for the value of the fines that come with the failure to comply with it. All countries must adhere to these administrative fines, which must be “effective, proportionate and dissuasive”. The formal exceptions are Denmark and Estonia, which have legal systems that exclude them for the direct definition of administrative fines by an EU Regulation. However, these countries will have to apply them either through the criminal courts (Denmark) or through the supervisory authority in the framework of a misdemeanor procedure. Fines can reach 20 million Euros or, in the case of an undertaking company, 4% of the total worldwide annual turnover of the preceding year.

Following a lot of curiosity around the period when the GDPR started producing effects, the topic lost public attention ([Fig. 15.1](#chapter15.xhtml#fig15.1) and [Fig. 15.2](#chapter15.xhtml#fig15.2)), but, regardless of that, the second semester of 2018 brought the first GDPR fines and, as more countries create national regulations to the GDPR and empower their national authorities, more fines are expected. Therefore, media coverage will likely increase, which will, hopefully, raise awareness and promote compliance.

The first fine applied under the GDPR after May 25, 2018 was to a hospital in Portugal in July 2018. The national authority fined the hospital due to a lack of detail in the access control systems that protect health records, which allowed for any doctor or psychologist and even some social workers to access the medical records of any patient. The hospital didn’t have a policy for creation and/or deletion of account privileges, which allowed doctors who were no longer working there to continue accessing the files. The fine was of 400 thousand Euros and it is being disputed in court (“Portuguese Hospital Receives and Contests 400,000 € Fine for GDPR Infringement,” 2018).

Austria has also applied a fine under the GDPR, after accusations of trying to undermine the EU regulation by passing a law that would issue warnings to first time offenders (Nielsen, 2018). The Austrian authority issued its first GDPR fine in late September and it was quite modest: 4,800 Euros (Fritz, 2018). This fine was applied to a retail establishment that had a video surveillance system that captured the sidewalk. It remains to be seen how things will progress in this country.

In November 2018, Germany applied its first fine under the GDPR and, while this wasn’t of a high value it is an important case exactly because of that. The national authority fined the social media platform Knuddels.de 20 thousand Euros for a data breach of more than 300 thousand access credentials, including e-mail addresses and passwords. The fine didn’t result from the breach itself but from the lack of protection of the data, given that passwords were stored in plain text. The fine was especially low due to the consideration of two facts: the financial impact of the fine on the company and the level of cooperation showed by the company in their interactions with the national authority (Pressestelle, 2018).

It is important to notice that fines regarding data protection are not new in Europe. As discussed before, there was legislation in place that required companies to protect personal data and fines were applied quite frequently. Examples of both small and large fines can be found in many countries and in a variety of business areas.

The Italian authorities have applied several fines in the past years. Examples are:

—The 10,000 Euros fine issued to a hotel in San Felice Circeo (Federico Domenichelli, 2018);

—The 32,000 Euros fine applied to the celebrity blog *5-star movement* controlled by the Rousseau Association (“M5s, dal Garante della privacy 32 mila euro di multa al blog delle Stelle,” 2018);

—The 600,000 Euros fine for aggressive telemarketing applied to Fastweb (“Telemarketing aggressivo, 600 mila euro di multa per Fastweb,” 2018); and

—The 960,000 Euros fine applied to the telephonic company TIM (“Violazione della privacy, multa da 960 mila euro a Tim,” 2018).

Like Italy, the United Kingdom authority has issued several fines in the past years. Examples are:

—The £120,000 fine to the University of Greenwich due to a data breach that exposed almost 20,000 staff and students (“University of Greenwich fined £120,000 for breach of sensitive data,” 2018); and

—The £500,000 fines (the highest fine possible prior to GDPR) issued to Equifax (“Equifax Hit With Maximum UK Privacy Fine After Mega-Breach,” 2018) and to Facebook (Waterson, 2018).

Several factors should contribute to the definition of the values of fines, from the categories of the data in question to the level of collaboration with the authorities with the intent to mitigate the effects of the infringement. WP29 issued detailed but vague guidelines on this matter and the EDPB endorsed the document (Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, 2017a).
