
This section follows the Evaluation activity of the Peffers *et al*.’s DSRP (2006), that requires the artifact to be validated in order to determine: (1) how well it supports the solution to the stated research problem, and (2) whether it is necessary to iterate back to the Design and Development stage to improve the effectiveness of the artefact. Thus, to assess how the SDFIPM addressed the stated research problem, we involved the participation of the SDFIPM’s intended user community who could independently verify and validate the proposed model. This user community included DFPs operating within the three fields of relevance for this research, namely law enforcement, commerce and incident response, as well as experts in the domain of DF, legal practitioners and researchers in both academia and industry. Once the Evaluation Phase was completed, we were able to feed the results of the Evaluation activity back into the SDFIPM’s Design and Development Phase and amend the model accordingly. Similar approaches that involve using such an evaluation activity have been adopted in previous studies, for instance by Adams (2012) and Ciardhuáin (2004). The remainder of this section provides a summary of results in relation to the Evaluation activities performed on the SDFIPM.

### 9.6.1.*Methods of validation*

A major component of any forensic research is to demonstrate the validity of the results. The DSR studies are prone to the same types of errors as are any other qualitative studies, so ensuring validity is important. Therefore, to assess our model’s accuracy in meeting its intended outcomes, we employed a set of validation methods as opposed to relying on only one method. To ensure the credibility of the study results concerning the proposed model, we performed a triangulation technique to facilitate the validation of data. The triangulation consisted of combining five different methods to overcome the weaknesses or inherent biases and the problems that come from single-method, single-observer and single-theory studies. These methods enabling the validation of forensic artefacts included (Eddy *et al*., 2012):

1.Internal Validity — the model’s components are analysed and their accuracy is checked;

2.Cross Validity — results are compared with other models analysing the same problem;

3.External Validity — the model is used to simulate different scenarios;

4.Predictive Validity — the model results are compared with prospectively observed events; and

5.Face Validity — independent experts evaluate the model structure, its associated data and results.

In the context of this research, methods 1, 2 and 3 above have already been applied to the SDFIPM in the Design and Development stage of the SDFIPM, which is the middle part of the larger model, the CDFIPM, in Montasari’s study (Montasari, 2016a). See [Chapters 3](#chapter3.xhtml) and [4](#chapter4.xhtml) of Montasari’s research study (Montasari, 2016a) for more comprehensive details about the results of methods 1, 2 and 3. For instance, in terms of the Internal Validity method, the model’s components were analysed and justified in [Chapter 3](#chapter3.xhtml) (Design and Development), which addressed whether the model’s components behaved as intended and whether it had been implemented correctly. In relation to the Cross-Validation method, the model was mapped and compared with the existing models, which involved determining the degree to which they calculated the same results. Concerning the External Validation method, similar to [Section 9.5](#chapter9.xhtml#sec9.5) of this paper, in [Chapter 4](#chapter4.xhtml) (Demonstration) of Montasari’s research (Montasari, 2016a), the model was applied to different scenarios and a walk-through of the model was performed to simulate different types of real life investigations. Method 4, Predictive Validity, has been excluded from this research on the basis that this validation method would need to be carried out over a long period of time, thus being outside the timing scope of this research.

Face Validity (method 5), which is considered to be the strongest form of the 5 stated validation methods (Law, 2014; Eddy *et al*., 2012; Pace and Sheehan, 2002), is the topic of this section, where the external experts evaluate the model structure and its associated data, results and accuracy independently. This method is used to answer the following questions:

1.Does the model provide its users with both utility and usability?

2.Has the model been built right? and

3.Has the right model been built?

According to Pace and Sheehan (2002), the main validation method for models must include assessment by experts and peers. This argument is supported by other researchers in various scientific environments (Burgman *et al*., 2011; Czembor and Vesk, 2009; Ludwig *et al*., 2001). Moreover, in circumstances in which empirical data are scarce or unobtainable, expert knowledge and their input are often considered to be the best or only source of information (McBride and Burgman, 2012; Kuhnert, 2010; Sutherland, 2006). In these situations, experts can be requested to provide input for all the stages of the modelling process (Martin *et al*., 2012; Cowling and Pressey, 2003; Pearce *et al*., 2001). Thus, the remainder of this section draws upon the Face Validity method by recruiting external experts to answer the three questions above and to ultimately determine whether the SDFIPM has achieved both the ‘utility’ and ‘usability’ necessary for a model.

### 9.6.2.*Expert selection process*

Booker and McNamara (2004) define expert knowledge as the knowledge and expertise that qualified persons have acquired due to their technical practices, training and experience. Based on this definition, it is, therefore, reasonable to regard an individual as a ‘domain expert’ if he/she is familiar with the subject at hand and is accountable for the analysis of the issue and the provision of sound judgments. Moreover, according to McBride and Burgman (2012), the experts should be selected on the basis of explicit criteria in order to ensure transparency and establish that the results represent the full range of views in the expert field. The common metrics outlined in the literature for selecting experts include: experts’ qualifications, employment, memberships in professional bodies, publication records, years of experience, peer nomination, and perceived standing in their community (Czembor and Vesk, 2009; Drescher *et al*., 2008; Whitfield *et al*., 2008). Additional considerations that need to be made include the availability and willingness of experts to participate, and the possibility of conflicts of interest (McBride and Burgman, 2012).
