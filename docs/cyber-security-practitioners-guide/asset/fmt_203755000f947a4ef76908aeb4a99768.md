
In the UK, ‘Transport’ is part of the critical national infrastructure (CPNI, 2018). The government has identified DfT as the ‘Competent Authority’ for Transport and DfT have included TOCs under the sub-sector ‘Rail Transport’ (DfT, 2018) in the scope of the NIS Directive, (Directive (EU), 2016/1148). It therefore follows that the sensible option is to follow standards that will deliver the most cost-effective route to NIS compliance. NCSC (2018a) gives some guidance on this, also helpfully pointing towards both the 27000 and 62443 series of standards.

Whichever is the most appropriate security standard for this environment is far from clear. The diagram shown in [Fig. 3.7](#chapter3.xhtml#fig3.7) below shows some of the material that is available to the industry and how they interact.

As [Fig. 3.7](#chapter3.xhtml#fig3.7) shows, there is plenty of guidance relating to IACS and also to trains in particular but it is generally vague and simply offers possibilities. This is not just a problem in the rail industry. For example Leszczyna (2018) in a survey of standards appropriate to smart grids observed “the situation that operators may find it difficult to orientate themselves in this plethora of literature”.

The common theme of industry guidance documents is that they recommend the development of a management system driven by one of the requirement standards relating to cyber security. NIST (2018) is very helpful in this respect as it includes references to a variety of standards that satisfy the requirements of each subcategory in the framework it defines. This is possibly due to NIST providing a voluntary framework as opposed to a regulatory directive like NIS. RDG (2017) references both NIST (2017) (NIST is updated every year and so RDG referenced the version current at the time of its own publication) and (BSI, 2013) 27001. In turn NIST (2017) includes both (BSI, 2013) 27001 and a number of the 62443 standards (as IEC publications) in the standards it references. The question remains: which standard is most appropriate for Digital Trains and, importantly, will satisfy the requirements of NIS?

In a lecture at Stanford University, Weiss (2011) described the fundamental differences between IT security and the security requirements of IACS (he called it ICS). Much of what he said is still true today and illustrates why 62443 is different to 27001. The objectives of Information Security are often stated in terms of the triad CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability), listed in that order of criticality. Additionally, non-repudiation and other factors are considered as well but CIA is the baseline. In IACS the emphasis changes because the systems are concerned with making things happen rather than protecting intellectual property.

| CIA || IT Objectives || IACS Objectives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C || Confidentiality is the priority for IT as it is protecting information that is private to an individual or organisation either of which has entrusted it to the system. The responsibility for protecting this information is enforced through GDPR. || Confidentiality is generally unimportant to IACS. There may be proprietary information involved but largely data is in the form of control signals that are created and then consumed. Safety is the prime directive. |
| I || Integrity is important to IT as it is either storing or processing information and needs the confidence of its users that it can replay the information without corruption. || Integrity is also important to IACS as the system must be sure that the command received is the same as the one that was sent as corruption could have physically disastrous consequences resulting in injury or loss of life. |
| A || Availability in IT is about enabling access to information for the right user at the right time. Time criticality is not usually a factor. Business processes generally include a fair amount of latitude. Rebooting is the panacea for desktop support. || Availability in IACS is paramount as system failure could result in immediate and catastrophic consequences. A delay to allow for a reboot is unacceptable. If a valve is not opened or a brake applied at the right time then, as with integrity, it could be catastrophic. Time-criticality is also much more important especially where ‘real-time’ systems are involved. |
| N-R || Non-repudiation is probably equally important in IT as IACS. Where in IT it is important to know the source of information, so it is in IACS, and in both cases the source could easily be a user or a system component. |

Safety does not feature in 27001, yet in IACS it is even more important than either confidentiality, availability or integrity. In a cyber-physical environment the consequences of a system failure could result in loss of life or limb, which is why safety standards are part of industrial culture whereas security is not.

However, as Humphreys (2016) states, the ISO 27000 range of standards provides well-tried and well-tested processes that are wellestablished in over 100 countries. The first issue of BS ISO/IEC 27001 was in 2005 with the subtitle BS 7799-2:2005 in recognition of its development from that British Standard. As the guidance in BSI (2015) describes, 27001 was completely restructured in 2013 with changes that included alignment with BS ISO 31000:2009 for risk management and to strengthen management involvement in leadership and review. As BSI (2013) 27001 itself states, the standard defines the requirements for an Information Security Management System (ISMS) with the objective of preserving the triad of Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability. The ISMS is intended to be tailored to the organisation; the standard is generic and intended to apply to all types of organisation irrespective of their size or nature.

As an alternative, the emerging set of standards under the 62433 heading illustrated in [Fig. 3.8](#chapter3.xhtml#fig3.8) below doesn’t appear to offer such a stable platform.

A large part of the standard set is still unpublished and, oddly, although BSI has issued 62443-2-1 as a BS EN standard at the time of writing it has not done the same with 62443-1-1, which includes the terminology, concepts and models that underpin the rest of the set. There seems also to be some recognition from the authors of 62443-2-1 that it should have been more closely aligned to 27001 as it includes the note “The revision of this international standard will be initiated shortly after this standard is published. The next revision will be aligned more closely with ISO/IEC 27001, which addresses many of the same issues but without consideration of the specialized requirements for continuous operation and safety that are common in the industrial automation and control systems environment” (BSI, 2010) 62443-2-1. It is unfortunate that the statement was made such a long time ago especially as 62443-2-1 cross references the 2005 version of 27001.

Those observations aside, 62443 is making some headway towards establishing itself, particularly in industries where safety is the ‘prime directive’ and where the IEC version of the standard is placed alongside IEC 61508 and IEC 61511 which address functional safety. Sieber and Hildenbrandt (2016) note that “the IEC 61511 (safety) and IEC 62443 (security) standards coincide on the demand for independent layers of protection”. EN 50129 is the railway specific standard that includes IEC 61508 general requirements and so when IEC 61508 is referenced it can be assumed that for railways EN 50129 applies.
