
In recent years, there has been steady growth in the deployment of public networks, especially in metropolitan cities where users can connect their mobile devices to Wi‑Fi networks at many places. Attackers can take advantage of these facilities to perform attacks that could lead to information leakage, eavesdropping and MITM attacks including session hijacking, data tampering and extracting sensitive data that could be critical for organisations. Using a Virtual Private Network (VPN) service, one can establish an encrypted session between server and client to assure data privacy and confidentiality. A VPN service can be purchased in the marketplace, or a dedicated VPN service can be configured to allow users access to the corporate network.

### 7.4.7.*Download applications from trusted sources*

With the increased demand for smartphones and the tough competition in the market, especially between Android and Apple devices, users are always looking for applications that can improve their lifestyles, such as fitness apps, budgeting apps, etc. Attackers are always looking to gain unauthorized access to mobile phones, take control of them and steal information that could potentially harm users. For instance, this information could be used to perform fraudulent transactions or to impersonate a user during a transaction without their consent. One way to accomplish these objectives is by developing fake applications and uploading them in the marketplace, or uploading them to the internet so that users can download them and install them manually. For instance, attackers started developing mobile apps claiming to be the popular video game “Fortnite” in order to insert malicious software into mobile phones. This malware stole information from unencrypted HTTP sessions. This campaign was targeted at Android devices because of the decision made by the provider to make the software available for download from the official website.

### 7.4.8.*Update systems to latest patches*

One of the biggest implications of bringing innovative products to the competitive market is the lack of security or the presence of bugs in these products. Mobile devices are not the exception. Organisations should place as top priority the setting of appropriate patch management procedures pointing out which updates are critical to security. This is especially true if there are valid proofs of concept that the exploitation of bugs and vulnerabilities by attackers can cause significant damage to the user or the organisation in general. In BYOD environments, IT administrators cannot control the personal devices of members of the organisations unless they have installed dedicated software compatible with MDM architectures on those personal devices; however, users should take full responsibility for the security of their mobile systems. Users cannot blame organisations that develop mobile operating systems or applications for security bugs uncovered after the release of these operating systems or applications, because a culture of secure coding is already being encouraged, and besides, because of deadline constraints and marketing purposes, developers are focused on making their products innovative rather than providing built‑in security. Therefore, users should be trained to always download kernel, operating system or application‑level updates when they receive notifications about these updates.

### 7.5.Integration of a Multi-Layer Policy and the Information Governance Framework for a BYOD Security Framework

There are different security options for BYOD environments in various organisations, and each of them is designed for specific purposes. Most organisations, especially SMEs, are considered vague and unorganised according to security experts. This is because in SMEs cyber security is not fully considered due to financial issues or the lack of knowledge of personnel engaged with risk management procedures. Likewise, system administrators, who are commonly in charge of the security posture of the organisation, have frequently established few or no common policies and/or procedures to protect the personal data of employees, customers and third parties. Individuals should understand that cyber security does not only mean adding rigorous security technologies into the network infrastructure, it includes training employees to follow internal protocols to avoid risks caused by mistakes, internal threats and external attacks. Employees should be trained to respond properly to security breaches whilst assuring internal and regulatory compliance. The proposed BYOD security framework is based on the “Privacy by Design” principle, which considers the GDPR legislation along with the individual’s rights. It consists of two core elements, a multi‑layer security policy and the Information Governance (IG) Framework developed during previous research for England’s National Health Service (NHS). The multi‑layer security policy is formed by three layers of policies — Corporate Security, Application Security and Device Security policies — whilst this is supported by Information Governance according to [Fig. 7.6](#chapter7.xhtml#fig7.6).

**Layer 1 — Corporate Security**

Organisations must consider this layer in order to set the necessary terms and conditions with the employee prior to signing the respective policies regarding BYOD security. Policies and procedures are added to a checklist to be reviewed by executive management before they are implemented into the organisation. Implementation includes following protocols for internal compliance involving the protection of personal data during email usage, the usage of network services, communication privacy procedures and the usage of BYOD architectures. It is important to train users to back up their personal information and remove applications that could be considered a risk in the organisation. They should also be taught to avoid the usage of rooted or jailbroken devices and the consequences of policy violation should be explained to them.

The use of Access Control must be limited to a certain number of employees according to their roles and responsibilities, and it must be implemented in all the procedures established in the organisation (e.g., Incident Response, Business Continuity, Forensic Investigation, System Administration). Devices must be registered within the organisation and must not have access to the main corporate network due to confidential reasons. For network security purposes it is suggested that the implementation of isolated networks takes place to ensure sandboxing, meaning the isolation between personal and professional information (Zahadat *et al*., 2015). However, both personal and professional information can compromise the integrity of the organisation when involved in a breach.
