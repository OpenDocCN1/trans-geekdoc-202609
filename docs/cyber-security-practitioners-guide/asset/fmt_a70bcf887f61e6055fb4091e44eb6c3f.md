
Various United Kingdom government departments that have traditional information technology security and security service taskings are working with the National Autistic Society with the intention of identifying and developing opportunities for employing people on the Autistic Spectrum in cyber security roles within their organisations. The Cyber Security Challenge UK is a good example of this. It brings together the Information Assurance Advisory Council, CREST and the National Autistic Society to sponsor, develop and run “a series of national competitions, learning programmes, and networking initiatives designed to identify, inspire, and enable more people to become cyber security professionals. Established to bolster the national pool of cyber skills, it offers a unique programme of activities to introduce sufficient numbers of appropriately skilled individuals to learning and career opportunities in the profession.” (IAAC, 2017)

Evidently there is a potential resource available with people on the autistic spectrum. Research indicates that there are traits that may be suitable for use in fulfilling the requirements of cyber security roles. The following sections will identify these traits and potential opportunities.

### 12.1.1.1.*Basic brain anatomy*

Research shows that there are anatomical differences between neurotypical and non-neurotypical brains. The reasons and causes are not fully understood.

Autism may be the result of abnormal or damaged neurons (neural networks) in the brain. These neurons may be unable to establish synaptic connections or achieve neural synchronisation. A neuron is a nerve cell that specialises in sending signals or nerve impulses from one part of the brain to another or from the brain stem to the nervous system. This disruption may cause the cognitive, social and sensory issues exhibited by autistic people. Research to support this idea comes from anatomical (Courchesne *et al*., 2007), genetic (Geschwind and Levitt, 2007) and neuroimaging (Minshew and Keller, 2010) studies. Further research indicates that there may be much more densely folded grey matter in the rear regions of the brain, namely the left precuneus, left lateral occipital cortex and right temporal-occipital cortex (see [Figs. 12.1](#chapter12.xhtml#fig12.1) and [12.2](#chapter12.xhtml#fig12.2)).

People on the Autistic Spectrum and those with Asperger’s Syndrome have a range of potential issues ([Section 12.1.2](#chapter12.xhtml#sec12.1.2)), that could be exploited, either intentionally or unwittingly, by employer organisations or their employees. There are relevant laws, as documented below, that should protect autistic employees but unless there is an advocate or the autists themselves raise an issue, these laws may not protect the vulnerable employee. How likely it is that autists would raise any issues is not a matter that will be dealt with in this paper.

It should be noted that ethical research into autistic people tends to be conducted through parents, guardians or court-appointed deputies of the autistic person and is generally concerned with children (Mukaetova-Ladinska *et al*., 2012). Some autistic authors have highlighted this as an issue and suggest that more weight should be given to autonomy for autistic people, particularly when they have high-functioning autistic spectrum disorder.

### 12.1.1.2.*Support processes*

There are potential issues associated with employing autistic people but there has been extensive work carried out by researchers, clinicians and charitable organisations in discovering and developing support processes and assistive technology tools. Some of these support processes are described and referenced here.

There are no official statistics in the UK relating to employment rates for autistic people, although the National Autistic Society has produced some estimates from surveys of autistic people. They are summarised in a report entitled *“The autism employment gap: Too Much Information in the workplace”* (National Autistic Society, 2018).

*“The Government wants to halve the disability employment gap. This would mean that 64% of disabled people would be in work*.

*60% of employers would worry about getting support for an autistic employee wrong*.

*60% of employers say they do not know where to go for support or advice about employing an autistic person.”* (NAS, 2017)

Employment offers social, psychological and financial benefits to all, including autistic people — for whom the psychological benefits may have a greater positive impact. Not least of these is the increased self-worth and autonomy that having a job can bring, which can lead to a feeling of contributing positively to society. There is also a decrease in the reliance on social and welfare benefits. There may be some issues associated with anxiety around integrating into the workplace and creating a new social network, although this can lead to psychological benefits once the initial anxiety and acceptance of change is recognised (Roux *et al*., 2013).

Gaining employment is seen as a rite of passage from childhood into adulthood and gives a huge psychological boost to everyone, including autistic people (Bennett and Dukes, 2013; Wilczynski *et al*., 2013).

It can be seen then, that there are improvements in quality of life, psychological and physical health, social standing and independence (both financial and social) associated with successful employment (Fleming *et al*., 2013).

Another issue is the interview process. There is evidence that interviews can be a cause for concern for autistic people. Researchers have identified that social difficulties can mean that the job interview can represent an intense challenge (Smith *et al*., 2014). This can lead to poor interview performance which results in a rejection; even in cases where the autistic person has the capability to perform the job.

The National Autistic Society, in the UK, advises organisations to make adjustments to their interview process and to consider using the following steps:

•*“Keep job description in simple English and focused.*

•*Weigh the qualities to indicate the main focus of the role.*

•*Be absolutely sure about what is essential and what is desirable.*

•*State that applications from autistic people and other under-represented people are welcomed.*

•*Avoid overblown statements about the company and the ideal applicant.”*

(National Autistic Society, 2017)

By implementing the suggested adjustments to the interview process, organisations may see an improvement in applications from autistic people and may then benefit from the skills and capabilities that they could bring to the role.

Socialisation is important, so it is seen as vital that both employers and colleagues of autistic people are willing to interact with them and develop support processes to ensure successful employment. López and Keenan (2014) conducted a survey that demonstrated that *“lack of appropriate understanding from employers and co-workers”* is one of the biggest barriers to successful employment.

Research in the United States has identified that socialisation issues can be a serious barrier to employment for autistic people (Chiang *et al*., 2013).
