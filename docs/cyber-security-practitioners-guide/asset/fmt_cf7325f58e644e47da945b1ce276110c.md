
Mythen `et al`. (2016) discuss and critique the understanding of radicalisation and its connection with “home-grown” terrorism in the UK Government’s “Prevent” strategy. The paper focuses on how specific understandings of radicalisation are constructed and operationalised in “Prevent”, and how these understandings align with the world views of political parties. The authors hypothesise that a continuous focus on the role of religious ideology in the radicalisation process, as assessed in the “Prevent” strategy, blocks the recognition of other important grievances experienced by individuals involved in violent extremism.

### 14.3.Research Methodology

### 14.3.1.Datasets

The aim of this chapter is to analyse keyword metrics used on an open source media platform, and to assess any correlation. The analysis is used as part of a mechanism to attribute radical and exploitative behaviour to particular individuals, or to identify users that maybe show signs of exploitation or who risk being exploited by such behaviour through their social media posts. The use of keyword analytics is nothing new, and indeed is used by most social media platforms through either the identification of certain sentence structures and themes, or through the reporting mechanism on the media platform, whereby other users can flag posts and content to administrators of the platform for review and removal. However, there is little published work and research into the keyword analytics underlying the behaviour of users and the wider social landscape.

While such behaviour and psychological profiling can be attributed to an individual, individuals may utilise multiple accounts in the online space which may not relate to their actual real-world behaviour. Wider online activity and behaviour can build useful context in a larger intelligence profile, but for the purposes of this paper, the researcher will retain a narrow scope for the data analysis, in order to further explore and answer the question relating to the keyword analysis itself.

To create a control and baseline for the analysis, datasets containing social media posts from known/suspected extremist users/accounts have been analysed. Also, for the purposes of context, a dataset of known extremist published material, which would share the same ideological profile as or have a similar ideological profile to the social media posts, would be analysed as well. The researchers assert that the analysis of the keyword metrics from these two data sets could be compared with the trends identified through the wider research in the literature review. Any correlations drawn or anomalies found could then be used to direct further research, and eventually to sculpt a mechanism to help further profile thematic behaviour. Radical Islamist Extremist posts and published material were used for this data analysis; however, it is the researcher’s hope that the same research methodology can be utilised. By changing the type of datasets used for the baseline and control, other targeted behaviours could be more readily assessed and analysed.

Both datasets were compiled by a US Department of Defence Research contractor, and as such, the sets used as secondary data have been deemed to be verified from a reputable source.

The first dataset is a compilation of religious and ideological texts used in ISIS English-based magazines, namely “Dabiq” and “Rumiyah” (Fraser, 2014; Wright, 2016). Both publications have been referred to as ISIS recruitment propaganda, given their ideological narrative. The former is more ideological in nature, and the latter is more urgent and deliberate in its directives (McKernan, 2016). The dataset obtained contains texts from 15 issues of “Dabiq” published between June 2014 and July 2016, and 9 issues of “Rumiyah” published between September 2016 and May 2017; a total of 2685 texts. As a baseline source for extremist terminology and keywords, the researcher believes this dataset to be extremely valuable.

The second dataset, provided by the same organisation, is a compilation of 17,000 tweets scraped from 100+ pro-ISIS user accounts on Twitter. The tweets are sourced globally and have been published since the November 2015 Paris terrorist attacks. The researcher’s intention for this dataset is to provide a wider context for the keyword analysis. If the keyword analysis of the publication’s dataset provides a baseline of the terminology, words and phrases regularly used in such a publication, then this can be compared with the terminology, words and phrases used in the tweets of user accounts associated with the extremist group used for this research.

By running analyses of these datasets, and by comparing the results of the datasets to each other and to the keywords and themes lifted from the research and critical literature review; the researcher should be able to corroborate the assertions made and discover potential further research avenues. The analytics of these datasets can provide profiling methodologies in a wider intelligence landscape.

### 14.4.Keyword Analysis

### 14.4.1.Word count

A key aspect defining the scope of analysis in this research is determining the type of analysis to be used, and which could be the best fit for the type of data to be analysed.

*“Data analysis is the systematic search for meaning. It is a way to process qualitative data so that what has been learned can be communicated to others.”* (Hatch, 2002)

A word frequency or “word count” analysis was conducted on the datasets to define the most frequently used words in the publications and the tweets in order to glean correlative patterns through appropriately weighting emergent themes. As defined by Pennebaker `et al`. (2003), word counts are based on the belief that all people have distinctive vocabulary and word usage patterns, with these patterns becoming what are often referred to as “linguistic fingerprints”. However, as highlighted by Carley (1993), there is an underlying assumption that words which are more important and have greater significance for the user would be used more often.

Miles and Huberman (1994) note that there are at least three reasons for word counting analyses, namely:

•To identify patterns more easily

•To verify a hypothesis

•To maintain analytic integrity

While this corroborates the reasoning for using this technique for the analysis of the datasets, as themes can be more readily identified in this manner, it should be also noted that there are weaknesses to word count analyses. As discussed in Leech and Onwuebuzie (2007), one drawback is that word count can decontextualize the word to a point where it is not understandable, and that in a sentence, the words before and after a key word would define the context of the phrase and the manner in which it was used, which is important when identifying and understanding themes. Another weakness is that users do not necessarily need to use a word frequently to portray important concepts.

### 14.4.2.Keyword-in-context

Another analysis technique that has been considered is that of “keywords-in-context”. This technique is a data analysis method that reveals how respondents use words in context by comparing words that appear before or after “keywords” (Fielding and Lee, 1998).

This analysis method provides greater depth and context to data, which may otherwise be less rich in information, and therefore, not as appropriate for use for this research. Results from a word count or a word frequency analysis, as discussed earlier, would provide the basis of further contextual research and analysis to be conducted on the resultant data. This would allow the researcher to delve deeper into the themes and behavioural psychological traits displayed by the users from these datasets.
