
## 2.2.2.9.*Post-incident actions*

Having reached a state of normalcy after the ransomware infection it is imperative that the organisation carry out a post-incident review (PIR). The PIR is carried out to ensure that all lessons learned are captured and fed back into the process. The ultimate aim is to ensure that similar incidents do not occur again, and if they do, then any previous incident experience is captured and distilled down into knowledge which is captured in the organisation’s appropriate playbooks.

In the PIR meeting all the parties that were involved in the incident should have representation. It is important to have this meeting chaired by a person of sufficient authority that can guide the meeting along. PIR meetings are not a forum to deride other teams for their performance or lack thereof. This is not constructive but will put them on the defensive and will therefore prevent valuable lessons from being documented from the previous incident.

The PIR should consider what went well and what went badly. It should identify areas of improvement and minute any actions to be reported back to the review in a timely manner. Areas to consider may include any of the following:

•`Policies`

•`Patch Management`

•`Vulnerability Monitoring`

•`Alerting`

•`Firewalls and blocking software`

•`Employee training`

•`Preparatory tasks that may help in the future`

Finally, organisations need to ensure they have sent out any additional notifications that are required both internally and externally. Nothing enrages employees more than lack of communication. If you do not put out notifications to update the organisation’s employees, then they will fill this communication void with hearsay and conjecture. This can ultimately derail all the good work carried out by the incident responders. Organisations and individuals also have to bear in mind their reporting requirements in relation to any relevant legislation and regulation that their organisations may work under.

### 2.3.Business Risk and Cyber Insurance — Its Place in Your Cyber Response Toolkit

It seems like almost every day we hear about a new system that has been exploited or a news story on how some organisation has had a data breach (BBC, 2017; BBC, 2018). As more and more of these breaches and exploits become available, more boards of companies are beginning to wake up to the risks posed to their organisations.

The management of an organization can no longer ignore these threats. On their part, governments and regulators have set up new laws and regulation to try and create a consistent framework and consistent breach reporting mechanisms across regions. In fact it could be argued that the relatively new European Union General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is already shaking up certain parts of business risk. Albrecht (2016) posits that businesses were already pointing to GDPR as a major risk and that it was being looked at as a key management task for an organisation.

Not all organisations are taking the responsibility for GDPR regulation as seriously as others. Addis and Kutar (2018) point out that a large number of organisations may not have been compliant with the GDPR regulation by the time it was implemented in May 2018. In fact, if you look around you will see that larger organisations have implemented GDPR programs in their organisations and are actively pursuing them. Conversely, a lot of smaller companies are not aware of their responsibilities under GDPR and are woefully unprepared for any actions that may be taken against them (Addis and Kutar, 2018).

One recent article by Page, Kaur and Waters (2017) looks at directors’ liability and the growing concern as to what it means in the industry. The authors point out that cyber attacks and data loss are two of the biggest concerns facing companies. Because of regulation in certain sectors of industry directors are having more and more regulation and legislation heaped on them. Directors now must look at ways to mitigate this risk against their personal liability whilst discharging their duties efficiently.

### 2.3.1.*What is risk management?*

Marotta *et al*. (2017) state that “Risk is the possibility of suffering a loss or harm”. Looking at this statement we can divine that risk is the outcome of several factors.

•`Incident` — The occurrence of a risk

•`Threat` — A cause of the risk

•`Vulnerability` — A weakness that can be exploited to create an incident

•`Impact` — The direct and indirect consequences of the actions of a threat on a vulnerability

The management of the above four items is called risk management. It is all about identifying risks in an organisation and then putting plans in place to handle or mitigate risks.

Risk management is made up of two distinct parts.

•`Risk identification` — Involves listing the elements of risk: threats, vulnerabilities and impact.

•`Risk analysis` — Involves carrying out the analysis of risks, focusing on the probability of a risk happening and the impact that risk would have on the organisation.

The World Economic Forum defines the above as a simple calculation.

Risk = probability × impact

Having carried out your risk analysis you can then move on to define a risk treatment. This allows you to define the measures you wish to implement to handle the identified risks (Taylor *et al*., 2016).

There are only four ways to handle risk.

•`Accept` — A company may accept the risk of an outcome happening if there is deemed to be a low likelihood of the risk materialising.

•`Reduce` — A company may put controls in place to reduce the impact or likelihood of a risk materialising.

•`Transfer/Share` — This is where an organisation may look to transfer the risk to another party, e.g. buying insurance policies to cover the risk.

•`Avoid` — Some risks are just not worth taking on and a business may choose to avoid them, e.g. choosing not to entering a new market where the risks are not fully understood.

The next section of this chapter discusses how you could transfer/share the risk with a third party in the form of a cyber insurance policy.

### 2.3.2*Cyber insurance*

It could be said that cyber insurance first started to appear around the 1970s. A company could purchase insurance to cover them for computer crime (Marotta *et al*., 2017). From those early days specialist insurance has sprung up to assist companies in attempting to mitigate the risk of a cyber event. Property and liability insurance policies will not, in general, cover a cyber event (Biener, Eling and Wirfs, 2015).

When an organisation looks to procure a cyber insurance policy it is useful to have some terms of reference to put to the insurance organisations. This allows the organisation to easily judge each company on the same basis. A useful term of reference could be using the same taxonomy for the discussions with the insurers, so that the organisation is discussing similar products on a similar footing.
