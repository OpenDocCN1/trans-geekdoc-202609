
At the same time that countries are adjusting their regulations and creating/empowering their national authorities, citizens are coming together in many countries to file suits against corporations for data breaches. There are indications that consumer associations have filed lawsuits against Facebook for violations of personal data rights in Portugal, Spain, Belgium and Italy (“DECO processa Facebook e pede indemnização de até 2000 euros por utilizador,” 2018).

With increasing fines, different mitigation techniques are likely to be considered and taking out insurance policies to transfer the risk is a possibility. Currently only Finland and Norway have legislation that allows insurance policies to cover GDPR fines and it is unclear if that is a possibility in eight other countries. In 20 jurisdictions GDPR fines are likely uninsurable (Leemans and Molony, 2018).

### 15.9.Information Technology and the GDPR

Technology is simultaneously the motor behind the need for a GDPR and a critical factor in GDPR compliance.

The proliferation of data services and data-enhanced business results from the development of IT domains like cloud technologies, social media and business analytics, the proliferation of computational devices (smartphones, Internet-of-Things, etc.) and the evolution of computer networks, not only cabled but also wireless. All these created business opportunities and enhanced the average quality of life, but also created privacy challenges that originated the need for regulatory legislation.

For companies to comply with the GDPR, they, paradoxically, will need to rely on technology, given that it is not possible to depend on humans to do all that is needed to elevate data security to acceptable levels.

One of the rights that GDPR grants to EU residents is the right to receive a copy of all the data that an organisation has on him and/or to transmit that data to another organisation, as long as that data was obtained from the data subject (actively provided or observed) and does not compromise the rights of other data subjects. This forces organisations to have integrated information systems and to develop automated forms of providing that data in a manageable format, not only due to the potentially high number of requests, but also because there is a time limit of one month, extendable in certain cases to three months, to satisfy the request (REGULATION (EU) 2016/679 OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL — of 27 April 2016 — on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/ EC (General Data Protection Regulation), 2016).

Ensuring that data is not easily accessible by non-authorized parties forces organisations to both encrypt sensitive data and to implement adequate access control processes. Two of the existing (and discussed) cases of fines relate exactly to this compliance requisite. Given that there is no effective access control without effective identification and/or authentication mechanisms, organisations will need to evolve beyond the traditional password systems that suffer from the paradox that they must simultaneously be complex, so they are hard to guess, and simple, so that they are easy to remember. Biometric technologies can play a major role in this (de Magalhães, Revett, and Santos, 2008).

The right to be forgotten, when applicable, and the limitations to data storage also create a challenge. Organisations must be ready to delete any data subject’s data that is no longer required to be stored, without deleting any data that actually has to be stored and without affecting the stability of their systems. Again, organisations must rely on technology to be able to do this effectively and within the legal timeframe.

Accountability is also critical under the GDPR. Organisations carry the burden to demonstrate that they have done all that is reasonably expected to protect the personal data that they process. If an incident occurs, being able to trace the data path is critical to understanding what happened and where something went wrong. Insured accountability might even demonstrate deliberate actions taken by an employee in clear disrespect for the implemented policies. For this to be possible, solutions like Private Key Infrastructures (PKI) must be implemented. PKI can ensure accountability for instance in network storage (Yumerefendi and Chase, 2007) or in e-mail communications (de Magalhães, Santos, and Nunes, 2006).

Technology is, in many ways, the protection of a digital infrastructure and, therefore, it will need to be the base for a secure organisation. Nevertheless, it is important that the independence of the DPO remains intact. Therefore, technology suppliers cannot be the organisation’s DPO.

### 15.10.Conclusions

The revision of the main features of GDPR, of the adjacent guidelines and of the current application of this regulation show that this is a complex normative with profound implications on the way that companies do business in the EU market. However, at the same time, it is clear that many of the requirements of the GDPR were already in place before the EU passed this legislation.

In an increasingly connected world that relies on a fast growing and innovation-based technology industry, the number of incidents is likely to grow until the mindset changes and the hardware and software players realize that security by design and security as a process are part of the normal development of any solution. In the meantime, organisations will have to navigate their way between the GDPR, the particularities of each of the countries’ national regulations and all the applicable regulations. The latter include not only EU regulations but also other nations’ regulations, given that information services are usually offered worldwide, which means that, while dealing with the GDPR, organisations might find themselves having to deal with several different regulations. Examples of states with similar but distinct regulations are:

—The Australian Privacy Act of 1988 with all the updates made until November 2018 and its more than 300 pages (Privacy Act 1988, 2018);

—The California regulation on privacy protection (The California Consumer Privacy Act of 2018, 2018), which is likely to implicate changes in several states across the United States; and

—The Brazilian law on personal data protection, approved in August 2018 and set to be enforced in February 2020 (Lei n^o 13.709, 2018).

At the same time, organisations must also adhere to business specific regulations, like the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) regulations (34 CFR 99 — Family Educational Rights and Privacy, 2010) or the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996, 1996).

Companies definitely need to change their posture on personal data protection and understand that they must either hire a team to support them in the active protection of data or hire specialized services, as the challenges associated with this task are multidisciplinary. Companies will also need to avoid the temptation of hiring their technology suppliers as DPOs, under the penalty of not only failing to comply with the GDPR but also not being able to distinguish between what is a genuine data security concern and what is a commercially-driven concern.
