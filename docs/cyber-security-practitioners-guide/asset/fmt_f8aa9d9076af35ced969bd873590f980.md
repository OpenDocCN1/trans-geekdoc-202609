## 14.2.2.*Social media and current events*

Social media platforms are considered an essential communication tool. Their virulence can be attributed to their ease of use and availability to complementary technology, such as smart phones.

Twitter is a social media platform with a unique model on which users post and interact with short messages known as “tweets”. These messages were originally subject to a character limit of 140 characters; however, in 2017, this was doubled to 280 characters. It is a requirement to “register” to the service to post a tweet; however, anyone can read public tweets, irrespective of registration. The platform was created in 2006, and rapidly gained popularity worldwide, with more than 100 million users posting 340 million tweets per day and handling an average of 1.6 billion search queries a day in 2012 (Twitter, 2012). By 2016, Twitter had more than 330 million monthly active users, and the company had 35+ corporate offices around the world (Twitter, 2018).

Social media has also become much more important as a news source in recent years, seeing almost a 20% rise from 2013 to 2016, with the use of print sources dropping back the same amount in the same period of time. TV as a news source has also seen an erosion from being the single most widely used source of news. Online sources (inclusive of social media) have overtaken TV in terms of reach, with at least 92% of the UK population having access to the internet (Nielsen, 2017).

The research conducted by Nielsen (2017) investigates how people find their news online, whether they go directly to a news agency or to a newspaper, to social media sources or to search engines. The results of the study state that while 47% of those surveyed say they go directly to the websites of broadcasters or newspapers, 20% find their news via various search methods, and 25% via social media methods. There is a suggestion that the digital delivery of ondemand news could lead to the formation of what is termed in the study as “filter-bubbles”, in that people will only get information from a few sources that would confirm their pre-existing views. However, the study goes on to suggest that people who obtained their news from utilising search engines or social media in a search capacity were more likely to consult different and more varied sources of news than those who did not. The results of their survey however do show a proportion of users who obtain their news from social media, without necessarily “searching” or corroborating the sources, and who trust the information they receive on their newsfeed or stream on their social media platform of choice.

## 14.2.3.*Wider relative research*

Research into the analytics of the keyword metrics of radicalisation on social media is relatively scarce; however, several wider research studies into radicalisation and the prevention of radicalisation have been conducted.

Lara-Cabrera *et al*. (2017) used risk assessment factors and metrics to evaluate radicalisation on Twitter. The study was focused on the statistical results of the data collected by analysing predetermined linguistic markers such as profanity, negative emotions and discriminatory terminology for certain behaviours, and comparing a dataset of tweets of ISIS supporters obtained from the group “Anonymous” and another dataset they compiled themselves of a random sample of Twitter users. Their research concluded that the tweets that they deemed to be more radical in nature contained more profanity and negative views about western culture and statistically were longer in length.

Torok (2016) discusses how social media has become a mainstream recruitment platform for radical extremists. The research discusses the psychological models mentioned earlier in this chapter as well as narratives from religious literature. They conclude in presenting the concept that there are three broad types of narratives that fuel violent extremism. The first occurs when a person’s grievances stimulate a strong emotional response to perceived injustices. The second occurs when the person sees jihad as an avenue to actively address these grievances. The third occurs when the person commits to jihad as a goal.

It has been noted by researchers that although the rise of online criminal and extremist activities has led to social media analytics being incorporated into security practices, these analyses have not become established in academic research or intelligence practices. A study by Yang Hui (2016) discusses collection methods and analytical tools for the study of social media for intelligence and to counter violent extremism. Multiple variances of analyses are discussed in this paper, and the paper finds that the technological capabilities of social media analytics are improving. There is a need for perspectives from social science to complement social media analytics in order to provide a wider contextual basis from which to discuss the results of analyses.

De Leede *et al*. (2018) authored an interesting study into radicalisation and violent extremism focusing on women and the development of their roles in society. These ranged from passive traditional roles in a Jihadist ideological construct to more active and operations roles. The study comprehensively reviews the pathways to radicalisation, considering socio-economic issues as well as ideological narratives. The study concludes with advice about the importance of a diverse, multi-faceted approach to policy implementation, and of investing in grassroots initiatives to communicate and liaise with individuals at a local community level, whilst avoiding the marginalisation and labelling that can adversely hinder prevention. These themes are echoed in the UK Government’s “Prevent” strategy.

Kruglanski *et al*. (2017) discuss the motivation factors underlying a shift towards extremism that are identified in the literature, giving examples of commonly reoccurring themes of *honour, vengeance, religion, loyalty to the leader, perks in the afterlife* and *feminism*, as detailed in assessments of specific cases in Bloom’s (2005) research. Kruglanski *et al*. (2017) acknowledge the appropriacy of these thematic indicators; however, they go further to explore an underlying theme, which they label *the quest for significance*. This is described as a fundamental desire to matter and to merit respect, which ties in with empirical psychological studies into universal human motivations such as the need for esteem, achievement, meaning and control. The paper goes on to discuss the manner in which this quest for significance could be triggered through a *deprivation of need*. This term describes how a perceived loss of significance by an individual can incite a desire to reinstate said significance by whichever mechanism. This is a technique used by recruiters looking to radicalise individuals, utilising, for example, the affront to one’s social/ethnic identity through the experience of Islamophobia. The paper suggests that individuals in this situation who are in such an aggrieved position could then find themselves being directed by extremist and radical narratives. They might allow themselves to be unwittingly recruited to sate the need for significance after their perceived loss.
