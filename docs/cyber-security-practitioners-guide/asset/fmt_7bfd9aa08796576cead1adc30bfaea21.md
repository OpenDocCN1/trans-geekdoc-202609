
While discussing legislation, it is important to point out the nature of surveillance. The terrorism acts above give definition to what acts constitute a criminal offence under UK law, however, much of the evidence required to corroborate and increase the guilt threshold from “reason to suspect” to “beyond all reasonable doubt” would often require targeted information and intelligence about the suspected individual. Under current UK legislation, this “targeted” approach, which will interfere with a person’s legal right to privacy, can only be warranted by a public body, such as the Court, or in matters relating to National Security, the UK Home Secretary. Targeted surveillance of an individual would include interception of communications and monitoring of an individual to gather intelligence on their habits and affiliations, with the purpose of collating evidence to substantiate the appropriate offence, as documented under the Warrant (HM Government, 2016).

It is for this reason that the “Prevent” strategy adopted by the UK Government relies on information on an individual and/or their activities by another party, or information gleaned from open source, publicly available information that does not breach the Data Protection Act (now the General Data Protection Regulation) or an interception of data, which would contravene the Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act, thereby making evidence used inadmissible in court. Therefore, the researcher decided to use anonymised data in the data analysis in order to comply with appropriate legislation, as the purpose of the research is to determine whether a proof of concept can indeed be gleaned.

Jurisdiction is defined by international law as “the limits of the legal competence of a state…to make, apply, and enforce rules of conduct upon persons” (Lowe and Evans, 2006). It also concerns “the extent of each state’s right to regular conduct, or the consequences of events”. The difficulty underlying jurisdiction in the context of offences purportedly committed in or utilising cyberspace is attribution. The operational mechanics of the internet give users the ability to disguise their identity and location; leading to inherent difficulties in determining, under current legislative thresholds, the perpetrator and originating territory or location. While it is agreed internationally that allied states need to share information appropriately to legislate within the confines of current legislation, there is little agreement about what information can and should be shared. Academics and the legislative community have made several efforts to conduct research studies, one of which is the “Tallinn Manual”, a 2-part book series which references legislation and defines jurisdiction utilising legislation used in air, in space, on land and at sea, as well as sovereignty and human rights (NATO Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence, 2013; NATO Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence, 2017).

The legislative conundrum concerning the jurisdiction of cyberspace means that traditional legislative definitions of crime and criminology do not fully fit with offences that culprits commit, or conspire to commit, online. Jahankhani *et al*. (2014) discuss the limitations of applying conventional legislative thinking and methodology to cyber criminality and proposes that there are two general categories of cyber crime: active and passive crimes. An active crime can be defined as that in which a criminal offence is directly committed by an individual using a computer, whereas a passive crime refers to the support and advancement of and conspiracy to an illegal activity.

Extremism is defined in a psychological context by Kruglanski *et al*. (2017) as “a *motivated deviance from general behavioural norms, and is assumed to stem from a shift from a balanced satisfaction of basic human needs afforded by moderation, to a motivation imbalance wherein a given need dominates the others”.* In essence, normal behaviour is that in which the needs of an individual remain relatively balanced and in sync with each other. On the other hand, an extremist mindset is one in which one given need dominates and outweighs all others, which in turns affects other thought processes and behaviours. This makes it difficult to model with traditional methodologies, given that behavioural traits can vary greatly depending on the individual when in a state of imbalance.

### 14.2.1.*Psychological traits and linguistic theory*

The definitions of terrorism and extremism describe a pattern of behaviour that leads to an intention being held or an action being committed that is contrary to the legislation. However, to better identify such actions or intentions in the remit of the Prevent strategy, it is prudent to consider any psychological research and markers, to assess if they could also play a part in the identification mechanism.

Radicalisation is a multi-faceted, complex phenomenon. As such, consideration should be given to how to approach the analysis and further understanding of causal factors. De Leede *et al*. (2018) discuss the caution required when translating research findings regarding increased vulnerability to radicalisation into policies and programmes, as “*labelling certain groups as more vulnerable to radicalisation can be counter-effective as it can increase stigmatisation and feelings of estrangement”*.

To make progress in developing a profiling methodology utilising the analysis of data generated on social media, psychological and social models must also be utilised to correlate data with behaviours for the purposes of profiling in a wider intelligence context. To understand patterns of behaviour drawn from an analysis of data, an analysis of the “Big Five Personality Traits” and demographic variables should also be considered (Pang, 2017).

Both Borum’s Four-Stage Model into a Terrorist’s Mindset (Borum, 2011) and Moghaddam’s Staircase Model (Moghaddam, 2005) propose an escalation of behaviour and trends, with each step leading to the next.

Borum’s four-stage model was developed as a conceptual model to give law enforcement officials insights into the process of radicalisation, albeit to be used as a simpler starting tool (Borum, 2011).

Moghaddam’s model is more descriptive in nature. It focuses on psychological over sociological factors, and outlines the route to extremism and terrorism as a staircase of stages, with fewer and fewer individuals progressing onwards as the stages increase. However, the model’s first stage assumes a personalised adversity to individuals. Research into this area highlights that there are a variety of reasons as to why people engage with extremist and radical narratives. However, it is recognised in Young and Rooze’s (2013) review that the model is useful in its metaphor of the staircase, as it highlights that there are a number of obstacles to overcome on the pathway to radicalisation, which prevents a greater number of individuals from becoming terrorists. It also recognises the role of morality in the individual’s path to terrorism.
