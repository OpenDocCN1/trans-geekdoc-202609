
One aspect of the Regulation that is very relevant for the choice of the DPO is the level of protection granted to this position. The GDPR clearly states in Paragraph 3 of Article 38 that the DPO “shall not be dismissed or penalised by the controller or the processor for performing his tasks” (REGULATION (EU) 2016/ 679 OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL — of 27 April 2016 — on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/ 46/ EC (General Data Protection Regulation), 2016). This means that if the organisation choses to nominate a DPO from the existing staff it has to be careful to ensure that such a person has no conflict of interest and that if a situation arises where there is a need to dismiss that person no doubts will be raised regarding the reasons for the dismissal. If the DPO can correlate the dismissal with an action or a set of actions performed in the capacity of DPO, the organisation will not be able to dismiss him. This might be an incentive for organisations to hire external DPOs. Even if the protection is also applicable to them, at least there isn’t any risk of misunderstandings in cases which require dismissals.

Another argument for hiring external specialized DPOs is the fact that the DPO cannot have a conflict of interest. Even if it is not clear what a conflict of interest is, the existing guidelines indicate that senior managers (such as chief executives, chief operating officers, chief financial officers, chief medical officers, heads of marketing departments, heads of Human Resources or heads of IT departments) cannot occupy this position. This restriction also applies to those in the organisation that determine the purposes or means of processing (Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, 2017b). Therefore, it isn’t easy to find someone in the organisation with the technical capacity to perform the role of DPO and someone who can do so without any conflict of interest. It is also important to notice that the issue of conflict of interest also applies to external DPOs, under a contract. The common practice of hiring a company that is already a technology supplier is frequently a violation of the GDPR. Companies that provide consultancy on the means to process data (recommendations on software adoption, database manipulation maintenance, data analytics, data collection tools, etc.) are in clear conflict of interest if they also assume the position of DPO.

### 15.7.Data Protection Impact Assessment

Demonstrating compliance is a pillar of the GDPR. The document is based on the notion that organisations have the responsibility to protect personal data, but it is not a naive document. The legislator understands that absolute security can only happen in a utopia and that incidents can happen regardless of what measures are in place. Therefore, organisations need to be able to prove that they have done what is reasonable, considering the underlying risk, in order to protect the data they process. The Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is the pillar tool of that demonstration.

Information security professionals know that any security plan starts with the identification of the assets to protect and a corresponding risk analysis.

The importance of the DPIA cannot be overestimated. Not only is it fundamental to the correct development of a security strategy, but it is also closely related to the fines that are associated with noncompliance. The WP29 wrote it clearly:

“Under the GDPR, non-compliance with DPIA requirements can lead to fines imposed by the competent supervisory authority. Failure to carry out a DPIA when the processing is subject to a DPIA (Article 35(1) and (3)–(4)), carrying out a DPIA in an incorrect way (Article 35(2) and (7) to (9)), or failing to consult the competent supervisory authority where required (Article 36(3)(e)), can result in an administrative fine of up to 10M€, or in the case of an undertaking, up to 2 % of the total worldwide annual turnover of the preceding financial year, whichever is higher (Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, 2017c)

It is impossible to summarise the description of the guidelines and the procedures to follow when carrying out a DPIA and how to assess the level of the risk such that all this information fits in this section. But the guidelines (Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, 2017c) give a list of what to look for when deciding if a DPIA is needed. These items on that list deserve some consideration:

—“Evaluation or scoring of data subjects”: this type of data processing can have applications in many different situations, from work performance to health insurance. Whenever the data subjects get a comparative position towards other data subjects, organisations should consider this as an indicator of high-risk processing.

—“Automated-decision making with legal or similar significant effect”: automated decisions are a growing concern of legislators. The EU has a convention that covers this type of processing (Convention for the Protection of Individuals with regard to Automatic Processing of Personal Data as it will be amended by its Protocol CETS No. 223, 2018). It is important to notice that, under the GDPR, data subjects have the right to be informed that a decision was made using automated processes. They also have the right to have a human review that decision. It is also likely that in most cases humans will not change those decisions, given that companies design the automated processes to reflect the organisation’s policies and humans have to follow the same criteria. This might be a concern related to machine failure or to the imprecision of the existing algorithms. In fact, machine-learning techniques have been developed quite recently and have a level of error that is unneglectable. There is no way to know how the confidence level of the algorithms will evolve, but it makes a lot of sense to protect citizens from automatic mistakes.

—“Systematic monitoring”: the whole notion of systematic monitoring has already been discussed in this chapter.

—“Sensitive data or data of a highly personal nature”: the notion of sensitive data can vary according to the culture and ethics of a certain population. Health records, criminal records and data regarding sexual, political or religious orientation are usually considered sensitive data or highly personal data. But some less evident data can also be included in this category, either because of concrete temporary or regional circumstances or because they allow for indirect disclosure of highly personal or sensitive information. For instance, detailed lunch records can reveal food habits that provide clues on health problems or religious options. Other types of sensitive data are those related to personal communications, including metadata (dates, recipient, etc.) and many countries have specific laws regarding this.

—“Data processed at a large scale”: the concept of large scale has already been discussed in this chapter.

—“Matching or combining datasets”: this definition can be tricky. What is a dataset? This is a hard question to answer from the technological perspective. However, the spirit of the legislation seems to be that whenever there is no reasonable expectation of a common citizen to see two different sets of collected data as interrelated, then this is a red flag that elevates the risk. This type of situation, by definition, goes beyond consent, so it can only happen in special situations.
