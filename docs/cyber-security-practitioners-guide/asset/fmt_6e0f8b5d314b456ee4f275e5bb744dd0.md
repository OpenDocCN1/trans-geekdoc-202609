
An insider can be described as a person who possesses some combination of knowledge and access that distinguishes his or her relationship within the organisation from that of the people that are outside (see Gelles, 2016). One of the core objectives of this research paper is to recognise and address the risk that an insider poses to an organisation. The Centre for the Protection of National Infrastructure (CPNI) describes an insider as a person who exploits, or has the intention to exploit, their legitimate access to an organisation’s assets for unauthorised purposes (cpni.gov.uk, 2018), see Appendix D.

### 4.3.Behaviour Traits

Behaviour traits can be considered an important factor when determining the potential threat of an insider and can provide valuable insight when designing a security model. It is important to establish what constitutes normal behaviour so a baseline can be constructed and used to identify suspicious activities.

Baseline construction would involve active monitoring of normal operational activity. This needs to be carefully carried out to ensure the accuracy of the data. It may not be sufficient to only deploy the proximity solution and analyse the movement data before one builds a robust baseline; it is possible that this approach may not be thorough enough. The researcher therefore decided to actively monitor staff, covertly, to ensure there was no contamination of the results.

This was achieved through a passive time and motion process for each job role. Over a period of two months (December 2017–Jan 2018), a clear picture of the working practises was constructed to reinforce the data being collected by the proximity monitoring solution.

Understanding and identifying adverse behaviour traits using human movement patterns can be considered difficult as one is dealing with a quantitative process. Building in qualitative elements may be subjective as the emotive state of the target may not be quantifiable.

The research here closely monitored each of the targets, identifying common behaviour patterns and routines.

### 4.4.Threat Detection Methodology

Employees can have an in-depth operational knowledge of an organisation, how it functions and its inherent weaknesses. Whilst this can be considered an asset, if those employees have malicious intentions they can represent a significant risk to the business.

Since the collapse of Enron (royce.house.gov, 2018) and post the 2008 financial crisis, organisations have been under renewed scrutiny and are increasingly becoming risk-focussed. Businesses now face heavy penalties if they fail to address the threat of a data breach. A Gartner report (gartner.com, 2018) suggests organisations are making significant capital investments to mitigate the likelihood of a successful cyberattack, including new technologies designed to identify spurious or unusual network or system activity.

Whilst it could be argued that new security solutions have significantly reduced the risk of a technically-based attack, the threat of a data breach instigated by an insider with malicious intent represents an unacceptable level of risk to many businesses.

The threat of a data breach was Dakota’s primary business driver when implementing an enhanced proximity monitoring solution. Senior management believed the level of risk relating to the insider threat was unacceptable and agreed to implement an enhanced security solution to reduce the risk.

Due to data protection and privacy laws, specifically the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) that was enforced on the 25<sup>(th)</sup> May 2018, organisations wishing to monitor staff movement must carefully consider the legalities relating to staff monitoring (see regulation and privacy laws) (ico.org, 2018).

Dakota decided that the highest risk relating to the insider threat came from 3<sup>(rd)</sup> party contractors and therefore decided to address this specific area. This is contrary to the findings of the Centre for the Protection of National Infrastructure (CPNI) (2013) paper ‘Insider Data Collection Study’ that revealed the majority of malicious insider activity was undertaken by permanent staff (88%), with only 7% of cases involving contractors, and 5% involving temporary staff (cpni.gov.uk, 2018), see Appendix D.

### 4.4.1.*Roles and relationships*

The core objective of this research paper is the creation of a robust threat detection model that will incorporate an effective algorithm that could be applied directly to the movement of contract staff within a controlled business environment.

Prior to the construction of the security model, the researcher needed to identify and analyse the targets and their function.

To facilitate the maintenance of a modern building, numerous external specialist personnel are required. These individuals may require constant access to an organisation’s office space and dependent on the role, are potentially unsupervised.

Within the target organisation the roles have been identified as follows:

•8 × cleaning staff

•4 × electricians

•4 × air conditioning maintenance engineers

•6 × catering staff

•8 × building maintenance staff

•4 × structured cabling specialists

•6 × outsourced IT staff (peripherals)

•4 × waste engineers

•2 × datacentre maintenance staff

•4 × security

Each of the 50 contractors will have specific and potentially unique characteristics that need to be analysed and incorporated into the threat detection model.

These roles may be focused around a singular function or task; however, some roles may be multi-functional, which presents a complex challenge when designing an effective algorithm to detect a threat to the organisation.
