
The raw data of the datasets were compiled into a Comma Separated Value Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. The researcher then imported and transposed the datasets into Nvivo. The word frequency analyses of the two datasets were conducted separately, so that they could be appropriately compared.

For each of the datasets, two types of word frequency (or word count) analyses were run. The first was an “exact match” query, in that the frequency of each specific keyword would be counted and tabulated. The second type of query was with “specialisations”, which includes keywords of a similar meaning or definition into the count. The researcher conducted this second analysis with the intention of adopting some of the pros of a keyword-in-context approach. By counting other words with a similar meaning or definition and compiling them into the count, a level of context could be gleaned. It would also help dial back the potential for anomalous results.

### 14.5.Keyword Metric Results of the Data Analysis

The purpose for the use of the Religious Texts dataset was to act as a control and baseline for the keyword analysis. The two publications captured by researchers in this dataset are those that are named by so-called ISIS as their official publications, and are internationally recognised as being material that is authored by and for their cause. Therefore, the Religious Texts dataset was used to facilitate an accurate, pragmatic keyword analysis, and to see if the same thematic trends can be found in both theoretic and legislative terminology and these publications. This would provide a solid foundation for further research.

This dataset was obtained from a US Department of Defence Contractor. It contains 2685 religious texts scraped from multiple issues of 2 successive publications over a 3-year period. These quotes and texts are most likely representative of the arguments, themes and justification used within ISIS’s ideology.

The word cloud generated from the exact keyword frequency analysis of the dataset clearly shows the terminology and recurring themes present in the publications. Religious and violent themes are evident here, which were expected from an exact analysis of keywords found in a data source based on ISIS’s ideology.

Another word frequency query was run against the dataset, running exact matches with stemmed words, synonyms and specialisations. The inclusion of words with similar meanings and definitions would give a wider context to the keywords gleaned from the query, thus improving the results.

The results from the word frequency query with specialisations are similar to those from the exact match query. The “period” and “months” keywords are of interest, as this illustrates the use of and reference to key dates and timeframes; something which could be of wider interest in an intelligence context when comparing these details with posts on social media platforms.

The results of this query corroborate the assertions made previously in this paper that the word frequency query with specialisations would provide richer results with a degree of context for analysis. The word cloud also illustrates the increased diversity of the results, and together with the full set of results from which the sample was taken, would prove more useful in a subsequent and more detailed analysis of the results.

The purpose of conducting a keyword analysis on Pro-ISIS Twitter use was to provide a sample of tweets from known ISIS-supporting accounts, to assess if there were any trends or correlations that could be gleaned when the results of the keyword analysis of Pro-ISIS Twitter use were compared with the results of the keyword analysis of the dataset of texts originating from published propaganda.

The results from the exact match word frequency query illustrate the limitations that can occur with a narrow query scope. In this instance, as the data was captured from Twitter, and as the tweets themselves contain links to external sources, the keyword query has counted components of web addresses as keywords, such as “http”, “www”, and “com”.

The keyword frequency query shows more relevant data, with mention of key locations, as well as terminology relating to religion, war and terrorism. The word count for this query is ideal to visualise the results in their entirety for the analysis. While the web address anomalies still exist within this cloud, many other keywords can be noted from the cloud. Interestingly, the themes from this dataset vary from those from the dataset of religious texts. The exact match keywords identified from this dataset can be linked to current events, and themes linked to the ideological narrative. The religious texts gleaned from the publications refer to more religious themes, rather than the nature of or location of attacks.

However, in the vein of thought of extremism and exploitation, it could be inferred that the tweets discussing current events and providing an alternative narrative to that of the mainstream media could attract individuals looking for that very alternative narrative that they have been unable to find elsewhere, and thus that the social media platform could become the gateway for further exploitation and radicalisation by directing users to other propaganda sources such as the publications which have been collated in the first dataset used in this analysis.

A word frequency query with specialisations was again also conducted on this dataset. As seen with the previous dataset, this additional query does not result in a wholly new set of analysis results, but rather provides a depth of field or context to the results.

There are some anomalies that exist in the results, due to the inclusion of the “@”, a prefix for all Twitter handles, “2016”, the year from which most of the tweets were scraped by the originator of the dataset, and the web address components that appear in the previous queries. The results from this query begin to show a trend within this data set, compared with the exact match query, due to the wider context and grouping of words into a singular shared theme.

The word cloud for the word frequency query with specialisations shows the larger results of the query, and shows that the most frequent keywords that appear on the tweets relate to current events or trending topics on Twitter at the time of the post. It could be that these Twitter users are utilising the mechanics of the Twitter platform to maximise the visibility of their accounts and the other tweets they have written, to gain a larger audience and attract viewership from users that may not already be subscribed to a narrative which supports their ideological objects.

### 14.5.1.*Analytical comparison of the keyword metrics*

The analysis itself has provided clear fundamental support for the overarching theory hypothesised; that the use of keyword metrics to identify radicalisation and exploitation on social media for use in a wider intelligence picture is indeed possible. However, more indepth research into the keyword datasets is required. Whilst various compilations and datasets are available through some internet forums, online data repositories and indeed the “Dark Web”, verifying the authenticity and trust of an unknown source is difficult. The use of untrusted and untested sources could invalidate the results and findings of the analysis. However, verified sources for such data were available, although perhaps not as readily as the research first assumed. One of the biggest factors inhibiting such analysis of keyword metrics from these platforms was the ability to source viable datasets within an appropriate timescale. The sensitive nature of the subject matter within these datasets may also deter many researchers from further study into such an area.
