
An information security policy is an appropriate organisational measure to demonstrate what steps are required to comply with the “security principle”. Further to policy, organisations do need to consider security and other related matters such as the co-ordination between key people within the organisation. For instance, the security manager will have to know about the commissioning and disposing of any IT equipment, physical access to premises, equipment given to anyone outside the organization including computer maintenance, etc.

All this of course brings in additional security considerations and may influence the business continuity arrangements. However, information security policies provide pointers on how the organization can protect and recover any personal data being held. A regular check needs to be done to ensure that security measures remain appropriate and up to date.

Although technical measures play a key role in the protection of personal data, it is often the case that security incidents occur due to theft or loss of equipment or incorrect disposal of equipment. Technical measures here include cyber security, which is complex and ever-evolving.

From a security point of view, it is best to assume that an organisation’s systems are vulnerable and to constantly take steps to protect them through factors such as:

•The security of the network and information systems, including those which process personal data.

•The security of the data held within the organisation’s systems, including BYOD systems. These can be secured by ensuring appropriate access controls and data security policies.

•Online security, including the security of the organisation’s website and other online services, or of applications used by the organisation.

•Device security with a clear policy on BYOD.

All these need to link to the nature and practices of the business. For example, if the organisation is allowing staff to work from home, then measures must be introduced to ensure that this does not compromise the organization’s security, and these measures must be appropriate to the nature of the personal data held and the harm that might result from any compromise. The Cyber Essentials Scheme is a good starting point as it provides a basic set of controls, however, risk analysis should be also be looked at in parallel.

### 7.3.1.*Threats to personal data security*

Organisations adopting BYOD architectures need to be aware that they are collecting personal information about employees, customers, advisors and third-party consultants. That information might be names, contact details (e.g., phone, email, address), date of birth, national insurance number and critical information such as servicelevel agreements and non-disclosure agreements. Furthermore, organisations may occasionally require the collection of certain types of personal data for legal compliance purposes. Regardless of how it is collected, recorded and used — for instance, it may be collected using a computer or recorded on other media, which include hard copy pages and pictures from a CCTV — all this personal information must be handled properly to ensure compliance with the European GDPR.

In the United Kingdom, organisations should comply with eight principles, which are summarised as follows:

•Personal data shall be processed fairly and lawfully

•Personal data shall be obtained or processed for specific lawful purposes

•Personal data must be adequate, relevant and not excessive

•Personal data must be accurate and kept up to date

•Personal data shall not be kept for longer than necessary

•Personal data shall be processed in accordance with the rights of the data subjects

•Personal data must be kept secure

•Personal data shall not be transferred outside the European Economic Area (EEA) unless there is adequate protection

The three major threats to data security are data tampering (integrity), data loss (availability), and unauthorized access or leakage (confidentiality). Considering these threats risk affecting data security, they must be treated as main priorities for organisations. There are two main types of threats (Garba *et al*., 2015). Organisational threats occur when users abuse their privileges on computer systems and perform actions without authorization, or without having the legal rights to perform those actions. Systemic threats consist in the misuse of information by individuals who have legitimate access to it. These threats are also called insider threats when they occur in organisations.

### 7.4.Guidelines to Enhance Security Posture on Mobile Devices

The protection of mobile devices must be taken with the seriousness it deserves. This is because, nowadays, organisations along with their employees tend to use personal devices daily for professional purposes. Furthermore, new generations are becoming familiar with technology from an early age. For instance, children are using tablets and smartphones to play video games and download applications from marketplaces, exposing these devices to outsider threats and social engineering techniques. Malicious APIs uploaded on trusted and untrusted sources are likely to be the source of this threat. It is normal having meetings with stakeholders at places outside the organisation (e.g. cafeterias, restaurants) to create new partnerships between companies, and personal devices tend to get connected to public Wi-Fi networks. It is necessary for organisations to consider the protection of personal devices within their risk management framework, in light of the recently implemented European GDPR.

This section mentions security practices that protect mobile devices even when they are connected to mobile and public wireless networks. Regardless of the mobile operating system one uses (e.g. Android, Blackberry, Apple), these guidelines can be applied to any smart mobile device that has access to the internet and is used for both personal and professional purposes. It is important for the user to understand that using personal devices is commonly allowed in most organisations. Nevertheless, there are some exceptions (e.g., governments, federal agencies) where they work with UWYT policies because the sensitivity of the information they tend to manipulate requires higher levels of information assurance (Kale, 2013).

Now that the impact of the newly implemented GDPR mentioned above has been analysed and the benefits of implementing BYOD security architectures have been mentioned in [Section 7.2](#chapter7.xhtml#sec7.2), the implementation of the following security mechanisms and practices to protect mobile devices has been suggested.

### 7.4.1.*Strong passwords*

Nowadays it is very common that mobile devices which are used for professional purposes load default applications accessed by the user daily. For instance, users tend to immediately synchronize their smartphones to their e-mail and cloud-based accounts (e.g., Google Mail, OneDrive). Furthermore, most devices have a contract of access to 4G networks, with higher throughputs, allowing users to access internet-based services, download applications and documents, and upload files and folders to the cloud as well. The high rate of 4G devices acquired, and the high rate of cloud synchronizing that occurs, demands that the user have strong passwords. Despite having strong hashing algorithms and using salting techniques to strengthen passwords, if the mobile device is configured by the user to have passwords that are easy to guess/crack such as “Password” or “[Name][BirthYear]”, attackers can apply old cracking techniques to retrieve passwords (e.g., John the Ripper, Hashcat).
