
2. If “the core activities of the controller or the processor consist of processing operations which, by virtue of their nature, their scope and/or their purposes, require regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects on a large scale”. In this case it is necessary to clarify the meaning of “core activities”, of “regular and systematic monitoring” and of “large scale” and, for that, one should consult the guidelines emanated from WP29 and endorsed by the EDPB. Unfortunately, this document does not provide clear definitions for all of these concepts, but it does exclude some situations and clearly include others. According to WP29/EDPB, core activities are the key operations executed by the organisation to pursue its objectives. For an information security professional, this definition relates closely to the concept of critical processes. So, even if a company deals with a large amount of personal data that doesn’t necessarily mean that the organisation is obliged to nominate a DPO. For instance, salaries processing is not considered a core activity, therefore it does not fall into this category. Regular and systematic monitoring is any activity done, continuously or recurrently, either according to a system, a method or any organized process or as part of a plan or a strategy to track or profile data subjects, including but not limited to, over the Internet (Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, 2017b).

3. “The core activities of the controller or the processor consist of processing on a large scale of special categories of data pursuant to Article 9”. Those special categories are “Processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health or data concerning a natural person’s sex life or sexual orientation”. One must notice that, in general, the GDPR prohibits organisations from processing these types of data without consent from the data subject in the cases where the national law does not prohibit that consent. However, there are several exceptions, presented in paragraph 2 of article 9, mostly related to the execution of legal obligations or public interest (REGULATION (EU) 2016/ 679 OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL — of 27 April 2016 — on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/ 46/ EC (General Data Protection Regulation), 2016). In the cases where that processing is legitimate, the concept of large scale is critical. The guidelines for DPOs indicate that several factors must be considered to verify if a certain case can be considered large scale processing. Those factors are the number of data subjects (either in absolute numbers or as a percentage of the population), the volume of data, the range of different data, the duration of the processing activity and the geographical extent of the data activity. Examples of large scale processing include the processing of patient data in a hospital, the processing of individuals’ travel data in a city transportation system, the geo-location of clients in a chain of stores and the processing of consumer data by banks and insurance companies. Examples of situations where there isn’t large scale processing include patient data processed by an individual physician and the processing of criminal convictions and offenses by an individual lawyer (Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, 2017b).

The DPO “shall be designated on the basis of professional qualities and, in particular, expert knowledge of data protection law and practices and the ability to fulfil the tasks referred to in Article 39”. Those tasks include informing the organisation of and advising the organisation on the obligations resulting from the GDPR, monitoring compliance, assigning responsibilities related to data protection, raising awareness, providing training to staff involved in data processing, providing advice relative to data protection impact assessment and cooperating with the supervisory authority. The DPO also needs to take into consideration the risks associated with data processing operations (REGULATION (EU) 2016/ 679 OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL — of 27 April 2016 — on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/ 46/ EC (General Data Protection Regulation), 2016).

This means that the DPO needs to have legal and technical knowledge. Given that each country in the EU can increase the level of regulation associated with the GDPR and that some dispositions are complemented by national laws, the mandatory legal knowledge might include regulations from up to 28 countries depending on the activity of the organisation. Also, because some data processing is frequently outsourced, the DPO should have some legal knowledge regarding contracts and, if the third party is in a different country, of international law. At the same time, the DPO should possess knowledge of privacy protection techniques, which in turn demands that he possess technical knowledge in a specialized domain: information security (data security, network security, etc.). Therefore, there aren’t a lot of professionals that can fulfill this task on their own. This calls for the constitution of corporations specially dedicated to this task, but that is not what is currently happening in Europe. While there isn’t any data on this, the author’s experience is that either companies hire one expert in either law or Information Technology (IT) or they hire the services of an existing company, usually one that already provides them with services. Again, most of those companies focus either on the technological aspects or on the legal aspects, when the GDPR calls for an integrated approach. There are only a few rare cases where legal and technological companies have come together to provide this service or when a company constitutes an interdisciplinary team that includes legal and technological experts. The recommendations of WP29/EDPB are that the DPO must also have some personal qualities like integrity and high professional ethics (Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, 2017b). While this relates to personality, it also calls for some knowledge on Social Ethics, Deontology and Theories of Justice. Therefore, ideally the DPO should have training in Philosophy. The WP29/EDPB also recommends that DPOs have knowledge of the business sector of the organisation that hires them and, if they are serving a public authority or body, that they have a sound knowledge of the administrative rules and procedures applicable to that organisation. Because raising awareness and training is one of the DPO’s responsibilities, he should also have knowledge of Education and/or Training.

In conclusion, one DPO is a super professional, with an impeccable social and professional record of “good behavior” and with a sound knowledge of several types of laws, several domains including IT, Philosophy, Education and Public Administration and the specific activity of the organisation. Again, this calls for joint teams, specialized in this activity.
