
## Understanding probability distributions

In the field of machine learning and deep learning, probability distribution is a must concept to understand. It helps in better understanding and analysis of the huge amount of data, which is given as input to machine learning algorithms. Distributions help in better visualization of the data and understanding what is happening underneath. Let us begin to understand this concept with the help of a simple example. When we roll a die, there will be a chance for six possible outcomes, as it is a random event. We can represent the outcomes of a random event with the help of a random variable. If x is a random variable, then its probability distribution is described by p(x), which implies all the possible outcomes of a random event along with their corresponding probabilities.

For the random variables having discrete value as an outcome, like flipping a coin or rolling a die, discrete probability distributions are used for representation. And for those random variables whose outcomes are continuous, like the price of houses, it is better to describe them with continuous probability distributions.

There are more than 20 different types of probability distributions widely used in the field of data science in both discrete and continuous space to model various real-life phenomena, such as normal, binomial, exponential, Poisson distribution, and many more. Among all of the distributions, we would like to discuss the importance of one distribution here, called **normal distribution** (or the bell-shaped curve or Gaussian distribution). This is the distribution that represents the behavior of many real-life situations, and so it is used in a wide range of applications. It may be the reason behind calling it normal distribution. For a distribution to be normally distributed, it should be bell-shaped and symmetrical around its mean, as shown in figure 8.6:

![](img/7e519fee1b702b91799cdf1664598392_572_0.png)

Figure 8.6: Normal distribution curve

It is an example of a continuous probability distribution in which all the possible outcomes in the range (−infinity, +infinity) can be calculated. This is the simplest distribution, which is easy to understand, but there are various kinds of data that are very complex and cannot be represented with a normal distribution. That is why there are other distributions also available.

Every real-life problem that is solved by machine learning or deep learning requires data in any form, such as text, audio, video, images, greyscale, and so on. All of these data are internally stored in numerical form and follow some probability distributions. These distributions represent the inbuilt characteristics of the datasets. Consider an example of a picture of a dog. A dog has some special features, such as floppy ears, four legs, and so on. When a dataset is created having lots of dog pictures, with all of them sharing similar special feature’s structures, then this data set would definitely follow a specific probability distribution. Similarly, a floral dataset would have some different but specific probability distribution.

It is clear now that probability distributions represent the crucial characteristics of datasets, so it is an important concept to understand. Now, if we want to generate a new sample of a flower or a dog, then we need to generate a sample having similar probability distribution as of flower or dog. So now, if we look at the preceding statement carefully, this is exactly what we are trying to do in GAN. In GANs, the generator network tries to generate new samples having similar probability distribution as of the real data set.

Now, it is very important to mention some points regarding these probability distributions:

- The probability distributions specific to images, audio, and video datasets are very complex distributions over very large spaces.
- Any explicit representation of these complex distributions is not known.

However, we still want to generate new samples, so what could be the approach to follow? We will begin by generating a random variable from a simple, well-understood distribution, such as the Gaussian distribution or uniform distribution, and transforms it into the target probability distribution. A number of different techniques exist that can generate more complex random variables, such as the inverse transform method, Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, rejection sampling, and others.

Using the preceding methods, we can generate a random variable that follows a given distribution by making it go through a well-designed transform function. So, in simple terms, the main purpose of the transform function is to reshape or deform the initial probability distribution to some other target probability distribution.

In GANs, the idea is to model the transform function by an appropriate deep learning model, such as a neural network or convolutional neural network, that takes as input a simple N-dimensional uniform random variable and returns another N-dimensional random variable that should follow, after training, the required probability distribution. Once the architecture of the network has been designed, the training begins. After training, the network optimizes to express the correct transform function by updating the network parameters.

## Mathematical generalization of GAN

Let us try to understand the preceding discussion once more, although mathematically, this time around. The following terms are used in the mathematical representation of the GAN, as shown in figure 8.7:

![](img/7e519fee1b702b91799cdf1664598392_574_0.png)

Figure 8.7: Mathematical representation of GAN

Here is a list of symbols used in figure 8.7:

- G → Generator
- D → Discriminator
- D(x) → Discriminator’s output for real samples
- G(z) → Generator’s output
- D(G(z)) → Discriminator’s output for generated samples
- θ₁ → Parameters of Generator (weights and bias, and so on)
- θ₂ → Parameters of Discriminator (weights and bias, and so on)
- p_data(x) → Distribution of real data
- x → Samples from p_data(x) distribution
- p_z(z) → distribution of input to the generator
- z → Sample from p_z(z) that is, latent space
- p_g(x) → Generated sample’s distribution

The training process of the GAN begins with an input (z) (random noise vector), a sample taken from a latent space having a normal probability distribution p_z(z) given to the generator giving the output with probability distribution $p_g(x)$, which is equal to $G(z)$ (generator output) that is, $p_g(x) = G(z) = x^*$, as shown in figure 8.7. Latent space is a simple hidden representation of data points having no meaning, and that is why the samples (z) taken from it are nothing but random noises.

The discriminator is trained on the real data samples $(x)$ with probability distribution $p_{data}(x)$ (not known) to classify between real (1) and fake (0) samples and gives $D(x)$ as the output in the range {0, 1}. This trained discriminator with parameters θ₂ is used to identify the output of generator $G(z)$, that is, fake samples as real or fake, and giving output as $D(G(z))$ again within range {0, 1}. So, the goal of the training is to find parameters θ₁ for the generator network such that the distribution generated by it $p_g(x)$ (green), matches closely to the true data distribution $p_{data}(x)$ (blue) that is, $p_g(x) = p_{data}(x)$, as shown in figure 8.8:

## GAN training challenges

Training a GAN is not as simple as it appears. The discriminator and the generator networks are trained simultaneously in a game-like situation, where both networks want to be experts in their domains and overpower the other. Therefore, GANs suffer from some common problems. All of these problems are active areas of research and are discussed as follows:

- Failure to converge: The scope for the improvement in the generator depends upon the expertise of the discriminator. As training progresses, the generator reaches a level where the discriminator is unable to identify between the real or fake samples. As a result, the discriminator starts giving random feedback with 0.5 probability. Therefore, the generator starts to train on useless or junk feedback. Finally, the model parameters oscillate, destabilize, and never converge.
