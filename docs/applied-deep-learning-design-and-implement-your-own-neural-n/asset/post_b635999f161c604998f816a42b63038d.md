## Convolution

The other important term in image processing is convolution. The convolution process is almost the same as the correlation process but has a small difference. In the convolution process, the filter is first reversed by a 180-degree angle and then applied to the spatial domain of the image.

The advantage of convolution over correlation is that convolution has the associative property while correlation does not have this property. By the associative property, the convolution allows to convolve of multiple filters into a single filter and further convolving that filter to an image.

Suppose we have an image $I$ and a filter $F$. Then, the mathematical representation of the convolution for 1-D is given as follows:

$F * I(x) = \sum_{i=-N}^{N} F(i)I(x - i)$ (4.4)

In the case of 2-D convolution, the kernel is flipped both vertically and horizontally. The mathematical representation of 2-D is as follows:

$F * I(x, y) = \sum_{j=-N}^{N} \sum_{i=-N}^{N} F(i, j)I(x - i, y - j)$ (4.5)

## Spatial filters

The spatial filter is the filter used for filtering or passing certain frequency components. For example, a filter that passes high frequencies is known as a high-pass filter.

A spatial filter consists of two elements as follows:

- A neighborhood (typically a small rectangle).
- A predefined operation is performed on the pixels of the spatial image incorporated by the neighborhood.

The filtering process generates a new matrix of a pixel having coordinates the same as the coordinates of the neighborhood center. Spatial filters are broadly categorized as smoothing and sharpening filters, which are discussed as follows.

## Smoothing filters (low pass filters)

In image processing, smoothing filters are used for noise reduction and for blurring an image. Blurring is a pre-processing task used in the removal of a small amount of information from the input image. Blurring can also be used for noise reduction from an image.

Smoothing filters are of the following two types:

- Linear smoothing filter (mean filter)
- Non-linear smoothing filter (order statistics filter)

## Linear smoothing filter (mean filter)

Linear smoothing filter or mean filter is the average or mean of the pixels enclosed in the filter mask’s neighborhood. These filters are also known as low-pass averaging filters. These filters work by replacing every pixel’s value of the input image with the intensity levels in the filter mask’s defined neighborhood.

Mean filters are of two types as follows:

- Box filter or an averaging filter: In the box filter, all coefficients of the filter are the same. Figure 4.12 shows a box filter:

- Weighted average filter: In the weighted average filter, the coefficients of the filter are different. The value of the center pixel is higher than the remaining values of the coefficients:

## Non-linear smoothing filter (order statistics filter)

An order statistics filter works on the principle of ordering the pixels enclosed in the area of the input image masked by the filter. Then, the value of the center pixel is replaced by the pixel value calculated by the result of the ranking.

Non-linear smoothing filters are classified as follows:

- Min filter: This filter is also known as the minimum filter, as in this, the value of the center pixel is replaced by the minimum pixel value present in the filter window.

- Max filter: This filter is also known as the maximum filter, as the value of the center pixel is replaced by the maximum pixel value present in the filter window.

- Median filter: It is the best filter in the category of the non-linear smoothing filter. It replaces the neighborhood of the pixel with the median of the intensity values after sorting the pixel values.

## Sharpening filters (high pass filters)

The sharpening filter is used to highlight the transitions in intensity. These filters are also known as derivative filters. Sharpening filters are based on first- and second- order derivatives. They are just opposite to the smoothing filters; that is, these filters remove blurring from the input images and highlight the intensity transitions in the edges.

The derivative of any function is defined with respect to differences. These differences can be defined in many ways. Various types of discontinuities that exist in an image are ramps, Edges, Isolated points, the steps, and constant intensities are shown in figure 4.16:

![](img/7e519fee1b702b91799cdf1664598392_238_0.png)

Figure 4.16: Horizontal scan line section of an image

The first-order and second-order derivatives are used to figure out these discontinuities in an image as follows. For defining the first-order derivative, we have the following three conditions:

- The first-order derivative should be $0$ in flat segments or, say, areas having constant intensity.
- The first-order derivative should not be $0$ on the onset of change in intensity (step).
- The first-order derivative should not be $0$ along the ramps.

In one-dimension, the first-order derivative is defined as follows:

$f'(x) = f(x + 1) - f(x)$ (4.6)
