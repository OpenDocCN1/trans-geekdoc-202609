## AlexNet

AlexNet was proposed by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton in 2012. The goal of AlexNet was to make the process of image classification more accurate.

AlexNet architecture consists of five convolutional layers, three pooling layers, and two fully-connected layers. It is divided into two parts over two GPUs. As it is a heavy network, in order to enhance the processing of AlexNet, one part is handled by one GPU, and the second part is handled by the second GPU, as shown in figure 4.171:

![](img/7e519fee1b702b91799cdf1664598392_333_0.png)

Figure 4.171: AlexNet architecture. Source: Krizhevsky, Alex, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey E. Hinton, “Imagenet classification with deep convolutional neural networks”, Advances in neural information processing systems, 2012

Every convolutional network takes a very specific type of input. AlexNet takes the input image as 227 * 227 * 3. So, if your image is of the size, say, 1024 * 1024 * 3, then you have to resize your image using bilinear interpolation. But it may not be very useful as most of the resizing operations are lossy; that is, most of the image information can be lost. So, another way is to work over different parts of an image and then combine these features.

Figure 4.172 shows the architecture of AlexNet:

![](img/7e519fee1b702b91799cdf1664598392_334_0.png)

Figure 4.172: Simplified AlexNet architecture. “Source: Krizhevsky, Alex, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey E. Hinton, “Imagenet classification with deep convolutional neural networks”, Advances in neural information processing systems, 2012”

Table 4.3 shows the detailed architecture view of AlexNet. The output of the last fully-connected layer is fed to 1,000 SoftMax output neurons, which provide the class distribution over different 1,000 class labels. As we go down the network, we are applying more and more filters, i.e., we are asking more and more questions, but our spatial resolution keeps on decreasing due to convolution, stride, and pooling. At some point of time, we have to decide where we have to stop, and now we have to open it. In this way, in AlexNet architecture, we got a feature vector like 6*6*256, so these are the important features that we get. If we do a lot of aggregation, then useful information will be lost, and we will be left with only global information that may not be much useful for classification.

So we want to go up to that limit where we feel that after this, if we go, then we will have a huge amount of losses, and it may not be a very suitable feature vector size to solve the classification problem. We can see that most of the weights in AlexNet are actually coming from fully connected layers; that is, (4096*4096) are the number of weights that exist between Fully Connected Layer 1 and Fully Connected Layer 2 in AlexNet as shown in figure 4.172. Moreover, we do not use much fully connected layers as weights become exponential.

The output size filters in AlexNet are obtained as follows:

$$Output_{Size\ with\ padding} = \frac{N + (2 \times P) - F}{Stride} + 1$$

$$Output_{Size} \text{ with padding} = \frac{227 + (2 \times 0) - 11}{4} + 1 = \frac{227 - 11}{4} + 1 = 55$$

So, the size of the output feature map is (55 x 55 x 96) after applying 96 filters of (11 x 11 x 3) size with padding = 0 and stride = 4 over the input image of (227 x 227 x 3) as shown in table 4.3. Furthermore, if we apply Max pool of (3 x 3) with a stride of two and zero padding, then the size of the output image is (27 x 27 x 96) (refer to table 4.3):

$$Output_{Size} \text{ with padding} = \frac{55 + (2 \times 0) - 3}{2} + 1 = \frac{55 - 3}{2} + 1 = 27$$

## ZFNet

ZFNet evolved just after AlexNet in 2013. It was proposed by Zeiler and Fergus. It is similar to AlexNet instead of an 11*11 filter, and its filter size is 7*7 with stride 2 during convolution Layer 1. Furthermore, during convolutional Layers 3, 4, and 5, ZFNet uses 512, 1024, and 512 filters instead of 384, 384, and 256 in AlexNet. ## VGGNet (16-layer architecture)

VGGNet is another model of the convolutional neural network proposed by K. Simonyan and A. Zisserman at the University of Oxford in 2014. VGGNet is said to be one of the best version models of CNN till date. Figure 4.174 shows the architecture of VGGNet. It is one of the most symmetric networks.
