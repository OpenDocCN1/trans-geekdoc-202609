# Convolution in 2-D

As we know that images are shown in a two-dimensional space (x, y), and these spatial locations are highly correlated with their neighborhood so we cannot flatten the entire two-dimensional spaces into one-dimensional space as shown in the previous section (see figure 4.53). If we flatten the image into one-dimensional space, then the spatial relationships with the neighboring pixels get destroyed, and if we apply 1-D convolution over this flattened image, then a lot of information associated with features gets lost. So, the need for 2-D convolution comes into existence. In figure 4.54, 2-D convolution is applied using a 3-D filter. Though the filter is 3-D, the convolution is 2-D; why? This is because we are moving in x and y direction as the depth is fixed. The depth of our filter should match with the depth of our image. As shown in the right side of figure 4.54 (b), if the size of our image is H × W × C, then the size of filter should be as w × h × C, that is, the depth C’ of filter should match with the depth C of our image. As the depth is fixed, we are only moving in x and y direction. That is why this is a 2-D convolution obtained by applying the 3-D filter. For example, the size of the input image is 224 × 224 × 3, and the size of the filter is 3 × 3. Then, by default, this filter is treated as a 3-D filter such that its depth will get matched with the depth of the considered image during 2-D convolution. So, the size of the 3-D filter that should be applied over 224 × 224 × 3 image is 3 × 3 × 3. You can also apply different-sized filters depending on the fact that what kind of features you want to extract, like fine-level or coarse-level features. Other possible filters can be of the size like 5 × 5 × 3, 7 × 7 × 3, 11 × 11 × 3, and so on. You can check that depth of each and every filter matches with the depth of the input image. For more clarity, say our image is something like 512 × 512 × 32. Then, the size of our 3-D filter can be 3 × 3 × 32 or 5 × 5 × 32 or 7 × 7 × 32 or 11 × 11 × 32, and so on.
