There remain multiple opportunities to improve this performance. First, the system is designed to recognize gestures lasting 1 s, which is possible without delay due to reduced latency from this window (Supplementary Note 18). To address this problem, a potential solution would be to integrate an additional learning model for signal segmentation to detect gesture start and end points.

To improve performance in complex gestures, future work could explore the implementation of multi-task deep learning models with each model to recognize gestures along a single axis. This multi-model architecture would allow parallel processing of any type of signal, enabling simultaneous multi-gesture recognition.

The window could further reduce latency by minimizing the time required to acquire a complete input sequence, enabling faster response. In this case, more complex CNN architecture (for example, deeper networks or larger kernels) would be required to extract discriminative features from shorter signals. # Methods

## Preparation

Several glass slides were cleaned using acetone (IPA) and deionized water. Two glass slides were coated with polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) at 2000 rpm for 30 s to remove a layer. A layer of Ecoflex was coated at 2000 rpm for 30 s for curing on a substrate. The substrate was then coated with polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Sylgard 184, silicone 10:1) at 2000 rpm for 30 s, for curing in the oven. Separately, polymeric (PI, HD Micros) was coated onto Ecoflex (Mist) at 2000 rpm for 30 s, followed by baking on a hot plate at 150 °C for 1 h and baking in a nitrogen-protected layer at 300 °C for 1 h.

## Fabrication of the transfer circuit

The PI-coated and the CNT-coated glass slides were attached by ultrasonic vibration (250 kHz, series ultrasonic, QUASOL II, Novacosam) to reach 80 °C for 10 min. For 1 min, the PI-coated film was layered onto the coated glass slide and then patterned using pulsed laser (600 W, Laser Mark's) according to a Custom Auto Editor. After removing the material on the patterned film, it was lifted using a water-table and then cured at 80 °C for 10 min. The substrate was removed using deionized water. After drying, the glass film was cleaned by IPA. The circuit was then bonded with silver solder (MS33, LTIP). The solder was used for bonding the chips to the patterned film, by curing on a hot plate at 40 °C for 24 min. The second layer of Ecoflex was coated at 2000 rpm and cured on the first layer slide as the substrate for fabricating the second layer. A solvent was then applied to remove Ecoflex at designated locations, using a solvent without access to the next and second layer slides. Subsequently, the solvent was removed to reveal the first layer. The microcircuit was preprogrammed using a customized evaluation approach. The third layer was fabricated on Ecoflex/PMMA-coated glass slide, aligned and pressed with the first and second layers through alignment markers observed under a microscope. The second and third layers were cured by connecting 0.0 Ω resistors on a hot plate at 240 °C for 5 min. Finally, the transfer circuit was encapsulated by filling degassed Ecoflex over the 2 glass slides that sandwiched the circuit, for low curing at 50 °C for 20 min. The PMMA-coated glass slides were removed by IPA.

## Fabrication of stretchable substrate

Four inks were formulated into the stretchable substrate: Ag, ZnO, and TiO (600 mesh). The Ag ink was used for the circuit, which was formed using a mixture of Ag, polystyrene-ethylene- (SEBS) resin toner with a weight ratio of 9.0:1. The Zn ink was used for the substrate and was synthesized using Zn, ZnO (25 wt% in water) with a weight ratio similar to Ag. The TiO ink was used as the battery substrate, prepared by mixing TiO2 with calcium carbonate (33 wt% in water) with a weight ratio of 9.0:12.6.
