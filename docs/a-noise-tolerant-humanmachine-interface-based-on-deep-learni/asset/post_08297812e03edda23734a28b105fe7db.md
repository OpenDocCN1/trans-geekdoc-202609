The analysis confirms that the maximum strain does not exceed 6.3%, indicating that the Ga electrodes remain well within their elastic limit and do not yield or undergo plastic deformation. To mitigate computational load, mechanical simulations were performed on layers rather than the entire assembly. In the actual sensors, vertical interconnect access layers can lead to localized strain concentrations when stretched. **Extended Data Fig. 3: Biaxial cyclic stretchability test of the sensors.** a. Photos of the sensors under simultaneous 100-cycle tensile forces along both vertical and horizontal directions up to 20%, showing plastic deformation after 300 cycles of biaxial stretching, which demonstrates the robustness of the design. b. Bluetooth signal strength under biaxial stretching. The RSSI remained above -60 dB across all tested levels, indicating stable and continuous communication. Two competing effects influence signal strength during stretching. First, mechanical strain increases the resistance of interconnects, leading to transmission loss (reduced signal strength). Second, as the device stretches, the antenna progresses away from nearby components, reducing parasitic capacitance and decreasing signal loss (improved signal strength). Due to these opposing effects, the signal strength initially decreases from increased resistance and subsequently increases as parasitic loss diminishes. The graph presents the average values, with error bars indicating the standard deviation (n = 15).

**Extended Data Fig. 4:** Illustration of the process for generating the composite dataset (for example, gesture 2) with 20 signals of gesture 2 while sitting. **b.** Motion artifacts induced by running. These motion artifacts are divided into six stages with each stage lasting 1 s. **c.** Motion artifacts induced by high-frequency vibrations. These motion artifacts are divided into six stages with each stage lasting 1 s. **d.** Motion artifacts induced by the combination of high-frequency vibrations and running. These motion artifacts are divided into six stages with each stage lasting 1 s. **e.** Superimposed signals for training. The superimposed signals are generated by adding motion artifacts to the gesture signal (for example, a signal of gesture 2) superimposed with 18 stages of motion artifacts in **b** and **d**. The stages are fixed rather than by randomly selecting segments from continuous artifact recordings. Since random selection can unintentionally introduce overlapping segments, where identical portions of motion artifacts appear in multiple superimposed signals, using fixed stages is key to extracting generalizable features. Otherwise, the model may learn to recognize repeated artifact patterns patterns. To mitigate this, we deliberately divided the motion artifacts into distinct, non-overlapping stages.

**Extended Data Fig. 5: Efficacy of transfer learning across different numbers of gestures.** Confusion matrices of the prediction results are based on a, one-shot, b, three-shot, and c, four-shot transfer learning for gesture recognition. In one-shot transfer learning, the minimum observed accuracy is 69% for gesture 5, which is considered suboptimal. This limitation is attributed to the fact that a single gesture signal is obtained from an individual only one posture: either sitting or lying down. When two or more shots are used in transfer learning, gesture signals during both sitting and lying down are collected. As a result, the recognition accuracy can stabilize and converge. Therefore, two-shot transfer learning has been adopted because it balances the time for collecting signals and the accuracy (Fig. 3c).
