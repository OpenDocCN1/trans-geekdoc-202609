
# But why Ethereum and not Bitcoin?

The Ethereum blockchain is designed as a Turing Complete virtual machine (on which you could write programmes or contracts that can solve any reasonable computational problem). In particular, it supports programming languages that are either mainstream or easy to use. Today, Ethereum is no longer the only blockchain that supports smart contracts. The alternatives include EOS and Cardano. Even the Bitcoin blockchain has acquired the capability to support smart contracts through RSK, a platform layer built on top of it.

## What exactly is a smart contract?

Many definitions exist for the smart contract application. It is simply an agreement written in a programming language so that a computer can execute it automatically. Instead of writing a contract in plain English for counterparties and arbitrators such as judicial officers to interpret, the contract is written in the language of the computers so that machines can not only read and understand it but also execute it. The difference between a contract on a piece of paper and on software code is more than how they are written: the paper contract is enforced externally (away from the paper), while the contract written in code self-enforces.

## Smart contracts already exist in the real world

Even without the blockchain, many smart contracts have been around us for decades. Nick Szabo, in his paper, gives the example of a vending machine^([1](#ch06.xhtml#ch06fn1)). An inbuilt protocol guides the transaction when you want to buy a can of soda from it; the action of inserting a coin into the machine triggers a chain of automatic events to deliver the can of soda to you. This chain of events is executed by a programme installed in the computer.

Perhaps a more relatable example is signing a subscription to something like an online magazine or a software solution. To some extent, this also happens when you set up a standing order with your bank to settle recurring bills such as rent. In each of these cases, a computer is instructed to make a particular regular action (e.g., make a payment) when a specific condition is met (e.g., a date in the month) with little human intervention. The computer programme has to follow the classical logic of “If-Then”: if it is the third day of the month, (then) pay the subscription to the magazine, pay the software as a service fee, or pay rent to the landlord’s account, and so on.

The point here is that smart contracts can be deployed, launched, and run on centralised server systems, and it has happened many times. It is true that blockchain does not have to exist for smart contracts to be in use. However, there are critical qualities that blockchain technology offers a smart contract, which makes it more potent.

Overall, blockchain gives smart contracts the capacity to handle agreements with more significant implications such as selling real estate. In particular, the technology gives smart contracts immutability and enforces it with a finality. After all, it is undesirable that a counterparty, trusted third party, or an unauthorised outsider can change the details or the execution process of the contract.

On centralised server systems, an administrator or a hacker can access the contract easily while it is active and make drastic changes. Its power or control is given to one party, who can change the rules at their fancy. In contrast, once a smart contract is live on the blockchain, no one can change its details or the course of execution. The smart contract has to run its full course. Furthermore, the existence of the contract does not depend on the availability of a particular server or computer that someone could unplug and render the contract unperformable. On the blockchain, smart contracts are sovereign. They have a life of their own that no one has the power to control.

Blockchain also provides smart contracts that are visible and executable by all participants. Such smart contract are performed on a public ledger. All the counterparties can observe every detail of the execution, making verifiability possible as the shared ledger is a shared truth for all counterparties. The blockchain leaves a clear and provable path for every element recorded or executed on top of it.

Smart contracts have a high level of privacy as counterparties do not need to involve a trusted third party in their transaction. More importantly, thanks to high-level cryptography, they do not have to expose their real-world identities. Neither does the blockchain network need this information to verify that the smart contract is executing as intended.

## Challenges of using smart contracts

Like any other technology, the implementation of smart contracts, especially on the blockchain, faces several challenges. One challenge is the legal hurdle. Smart contracts have to be recognised before existing laws to be truly enforceable. Within the existing laws now, it is easy for one counterparty to ignore the performance of a smart contract and seek a desirable enforcement from a court of the law. Many points can make the smart contract pronounced null and void. For example, in some countries, the law expressly requires the involvement of a third party like a notary or a commissioner of oaths for the contract to be considered valid. In others, a signature on a physical piece of paper is a significant component of a contract.

Another challenge is having the most knowledgeable and skilled people writing the contracts. As it is now, the majority of the most proficient programmers are not lawyers, and on the other hand, most lawyers are not programmers. If smart contracts are to be trusted with the execution of very critical agreements, they have to be programmed by people who should not make mistakes with interpreting the law and also cannot produce a smart contract riddled with bugs.

It is also true that most people want or need to read a contract before it is activated. Many would not be comfortable having a smart contract run on the blockchain before they know every detail of it and, in particular, how it affects their interests. Unfortunately, however, the average person cannot read and understand instructions written in a computer language.

Another major challenge is overcoming errors and bugs in the code, which could break the execution or serve as a loophole that others can take advantage of. As we saw in [Chapter 4](#ch04.xhtml), one dramatic example was when the first major decentralised anonymous organisation (DAO), a virtual entity on the blockchain, failed to take off because its smart contract code had a bug. A hacker exploited it and attempted to steal close to US$60 million.

Last but not least, smart contracts might fail to sort out any ambiguity in contracts. Very long contracts often have ambiguous statements, which tend to occur, not as a result of intent, but by accidental constructions. People, rather than computers, interpret such ambiguities better. The counterparties can clarify the situation and perform the contract according to the agreed-upon intent. If there is a dispute, they put the contract through an arbitration process. But nothing can be done once a smart contract is live on the blockchain. If it adopts an unintended interpretation, the counterparties will have to live with the consequences.

## Creating your own smart contract

Despite its challenges, smart contracts might just be what the Internet is waiting for. Systems like the IoT will benefit a lot from smart sovereign contracts as smart devices across the vast Internet can enter into contractual terms and can perform automatically.

Smart contracts are smart because they can execute contractual clauses without human intervention. It could indeed improve the application of other technologies, and also stand to benefit from them. For example, it could be even smarter by leveraging AI.
