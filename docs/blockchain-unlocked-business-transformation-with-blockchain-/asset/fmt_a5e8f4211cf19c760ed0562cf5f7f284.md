
# Public vs. Private Blockchains: Key Distinctions

Earlier in [Chapter 1](#ch01.xhtml), we introduced the possible distinctions in blockchain, especially with regard to permissions. Between public and private blockchains, three primary areas of differences exist. These are (1) access to read and edit data on the shared ledger, (2) how the core software is developed and maintained, and (3) how much the system relies on game theory to function effectively.

## Access

Anyone can join a public blockchain. Not only that, anyone can read as well as help to maintain the shared ledger without needing permission from anyone. Usually, the only barrier to entry is the ability to acquire the necessary hardware and energy resources. Thus, public blockchains are also referred to as *permissionless* blockchains. One critical feature of public blockchains is that participants remain anonymous (or more precisely, *pseudo-anonymous*). No one is compelled to disclose their real-world identity. Indeed, pseudo-anonymity is considered a selling point of public blockchains. Arguably, it is an essential feature and the only way to maintain privacy, given that anyone can access data on a public blockchain.

Bitcoin is the first public blockchain. As long as one can afford to buy the mining `ASIC` (Application Specific Integrated Circuit) chips or a node and power it efficiently, he or she is free to join the network without being required to disclose his or her real-world identity, whether as a miner or as a user.

When a new node joins a public blockchain, it can get up to date with the rest of the network by downloading the entire ledger from other nodes on the network. When the owner of the node decides that they no longer want to be part of the network, they do not need to inform anyone of their decision to pull out.

With private blockchains, however, you cannot simply join the network. A private blockchain is organised in such a way that while it is still a peer-to-peer network like a public one, the owners of the nodes on the network know one another. Indeed, each node becomes part of the network through an elaborate process.

Often the nodes on a private blockchain network are there through a contract entered into in the real world (think paper and pen). If you want to join the network, you need to persuade the appointed administrator or all the stakeholders and go through a negotiation process. If the stakeholders feel that you do not bring any value to the network or meet their requirements, they can decline your application. Thus, private blockchains are referred to as *permissioned* blockchains.

There is an important point to make at this juncture: a private blockchain can be open or closed. It is open if the members of the public can access and read or use the data and applications on the blockchain. It is closed if only those with permission can access, read, or use the applications on the blockchain. Thus, a blockchain may be private, but the applications on top of it are accessible to everyone.

Another important point to keep in mind is the owners of the nodes use their real-world identities on private blockchains. Anonymous players are not accepted. Everyone is known to everyone. This makes private blockchains attractive to entities that have to observe stringent regulatory requirements, in particular, Know Your Customers (`KYC`), Anti-money laundering (`AML`), and anti-terrorist financing. In particular, commercial banks and insurance companies have opted to build private blockchains with knowledge of whom they are working with being the main reason.

## The Core Software

Now, let's look at how public and private blockchains differ in terms of the development and management of the core software.

A blockchain core software is the piece of code installed on your computer so that it can communicate and collaborate with others on the peer-to-peer network. The core software implements the rules of engagement (known as a protocol).

Public blockchain software is almost always open source, which means it can be copied without infringing any copyright. Satoshi Nakamoto released the Bitcoin code under the open-source `MIT` licence. This is how thousands of other blockchains have been developed; most of them are essentially copies of the Bitcoin blockchain. On the other hand, most private blockchains release their codes under more rigid, limited, or exclusive copyrights. This often means you cannot copy what is uniquely theirs without infringing on a copyright.

Even then, most private blockchains have their code developed behind the scenes. But more importantly, the development and maintenance of the private blockchain core software is undertaken by a specific and official team that is tasked with the responsibility. Stakeholders have a formal mechanism to pick and manage the team for the project. One way this is often done is that each stakeholder sends in a developer to be part of the team. Another is that the stakeholders form a consortium or a company that takes over the responsibility of building and managing the protocol. The latter is more common. The two most prominent private blockchains are managed through consortiums. In December 2015, the Linux Foundation and several major financial institutions and technology companies launched `Hyperledger`. Earlier that year in September, another group of financial institutions launched `R3`, another private blockchain consortium.

In public blockchains, it is the entire community of users that is responsible for the core software development and maintenance. In particular, the project relies on volunteer developers to provide patches on the core software and release new versions to the community. Some members of the community may hire core software developers to work on the project.

For example, several developers that have worked on the Bitcoin project over the years have been hired (or sponsored) by private companies that have built businesses on the blockchain. Examples of companies that have sponsored developers to work on the Bitcoin project include the Shanghai-based Bitcoin exchange `BTCC`, or `BTCChina`, and payment processor `BitPay`. It is also common for companies that build applications on top of a public blockchain to assign some of their developers to work on the core software. With Bitcoin, we have companies like `Blockstream`, `Chaincode Labs`, and `Ciphrex` doing precisely that.

Some not-for-profit organisations collect funds from the community to hire developers to work on the core software. Again, Bitcoin offers some examples, including `Bitcoin Foundation` (active in the early days) and `Digital Currency Initiative` by `MIT Media Labs`. Most of the other public blockchains have foundations that either retain part of the mined coins, receive part of the transaction fees, or raise money from the community using other means to support the development and maintenance of the core software^([1](#ch05.xhtml#ch05fn1)).

Getting the necessary resources is one thing. Changing critical parts of the public blockchain protocol is never easy, because often the entire community or at least a majority must form a consensus. Different players end up having conflicting interests. A case in point was the attempt to scale the Bitcoin protocol so that it could handle more transactions. The community had always struggled to form a consensus as to whether to increase the block size or have the community build second-layer scaling solutions such as `Lightning Networks` and `SegWit`. In 2017, the community was engaged in what is referred to the User Activated Soft Fork (`UASF`) Civil War, which led to the split of the network into Bitcoin and Bitcoin Cash^([2](#ch05.xhtml#ch05fn2)).
