
## Game theory

Finally, let’s look at the third factor that differentiates public blockchains from private blockchains, which is the system’s reliance on game theory. In any system that brings together two or more entities with their own selfish interests, game theory is useful. It is more or less about giving the right incentives to actors within a system: rewarding those who play by the rules and punishing those who attempt to break them.

Most private blockchain networks adopt a centralised governance structure that can reward good actors and punish bad actors. In particular, it can banish anyone who goes against the rules agreed upon by all stakeholders. This is made easier by the fact that all players are known and no one can rejoin the network without permission.

However, often on public blockchain networks there is no one to punish or reward. Anonymous actors join and leave the network at will. Because of this, public blockchains have to rely on more robust game theory to function correctly. You can call it the automated game theory. With Bitcoin, for example, if you choose to try to break the rules, no one will stop you or banish you from the network. Rather, game theory will work against you.

More specifically, you will be spending a lot of resources on paying electricity bills and buying hardware, like power supply units and ASIC miners. But the network will ignore you and you will end up making huge losses for nothing. However, if you decide to use those resources to help the network instead, it will reward you.

## Choosing between private and public blockchains

Is the public or private blockchain better?

Public and private blockchains have different strengths and weaknesses. As I have already pointed out, it is easier and faster to change a private blockchain protocol, making such blockchains more adaptive. Private blockchains are also great for entities that need to observe stringent regulatory requirements. For example, commercial banks have found it hard to use the Bitcoin blockchain because players on the platform remain anonymous. Thus, private blockchains allow them to meet necessary regulatory requirements.

However, public blockchains provide guaranteed permanence and immutability. You are almost always sure that rules are not going to change abruptly. Public blockchains are great for applications that are good for society. But, for whatever reasons, governments and entities that benefit from the status quo will not allow such applications to flourish if they have access to a switch to turn off. In short, both types of blockchain have excellent use cases.

**What’s So Smart About Smart Contracts?**

*The concept of smart contracts is the building block of the digital market.* Nick Szabo arrived at this conclusion in the mid-1990s, more than a decade before Satoshi Nakamoto designed the Bitcoin blockchain^[1](#ch06.xhtml#ch06fn1). Nick Szabo is a computer scientist, cryptographer, and a legal expert who is one of the people suspected to be Satoshi Nakamoto. This suspicion only grows when you explore his known works or read the academic literature that he has written, including those on smart contracts.

### The killer app of blockchain, or are they?

Nevertheless, many within the blockchain space believe that smart contracts could turn out to be the *killer* application for the technology. The one that could finally make the blockchain technology truly mainstream. That said, you probably first heard about smart contracts because of its association with blockchain. However, you must know that this is an idea discussed long before blockchain or cryptocurrencies came into being. Nevertheless, smart contracts and blockchain are turning out to be very intertwined. Indeed, there is reason to believe the former might end up providing the perfect use cases of the latter, and vice versa.

The concept of smart contracts has been implemented in one form or another for decades. This has mostly happened in stand-alone or centralised server systems. However, the first place where the idea is explicitly discussed is in the academic article titled *Smart Contracts: Building Blocks for Digital Markets* that Nick Szabo published in 1996^[1](#ch06.xhtml#ch06fn1). This is a document that anyone who has an interest in this technology or who wants to understand how it works, especially from a technical standpoint, should find the time to read.

The central theme of the paper is a proposal on how to apply the legal principles that have guided the offline contractual interactions in the digital space. Nick Szabo asks, “What parts of our hard-won legal tradition will still be valuable in the cyberspace era? What is the best way to apply these common law principles to the design of our online relationships?”

His solution? Smart contracts, which he defines as “a set of promises, specified in digital form, including protocols within which the parties perform on these promises.” He further explains that contractual clauses would be embedded in the computer hardware and software in a way that makes it difficult or expensive to breach the contract. Interestingly, Nick Szabo describes how public-private key cryptography, the primary component of the blockchain, would be central to making really efficient smart contract applications possible.

Fast forward to 2013, as Bitcoin was getting noticed by the public, the corporate world, and even international governments, a young Canadian by the name of Vitalik Buterin published a white paper that proposed Ethereum, an alternative to the Bitcoin blockchain. In the paper, Buterin explained the weaknesses of the Bitcoin blockchain and how Ethereum was a better blockchain for not having them. One of the primary differences between Bitcoin and Ethereum was that the latter would support the developing, launching, and running of smart contracts. Before Ethereum came into the picture, hardly anyone linked smart contracts to blockchain technology.
