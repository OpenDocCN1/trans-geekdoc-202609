
The biggest challenge small businesses face in using blockchain is a shortage of employable talent. Experienced blockchain developers are few and far between, and those who are experienced are expensive, often beyond the means of small businesses. However, hiring is not the only way to give your business the capabilities it needs to embrace blockchain. Team members can be trained to acquire the skill, as finding relevant courses, especially online, is becoming easier. Learning platforms like [Coursera.org](http://Coursera.org), Udemy, and Skillshare offer a growing selection of courses for you to choose from based on your specific needs.

The skill set required to build a blockchain system comes from designers, developers, game theorists, and token economists. Designers help you structure what you have in mind. Game theorists help designers determine what relationships should exist between different participants and, more importantly, how to use their interactions to benefit the system as a whole. Finally, developers bring the entire vision to life. However, for less complex blockchain projects, you may only need a developer with some design and game theory knowledge.

### The Blockchain Level or Type Your Business Needs

The third question is the extent to which you need to adopt the technology and what type of blockchain best suits your business. As a business, you must decide whether you need a public or private blockchain. Do you need to use an existing Blockchain-as-a-Service, or do you need to build a new application on an existing blockchain? Indeed, you must determine if you need a brand-new blockchain and build it from scratch.

In other words, blockchain technology has several levels, primarily including as-a-service, applications built on top of a blockchain, and the core protocol itself. The level at which you adopt the technology depends on your specific needs. If your needs are unique and there are no available as-a-service applications, you may need to build your application on an existing blockchain. If you realize that no existing blockchain can serve as a suitable backend for your application, you may need to build the blockchain first and then develop the application.

Upon completing this workbook, you should be ready to make key decisions on how to proceed with technology adoption. As technology changes, I will update this workbook over time, so it will continue to evolve. This means that in the long run, the workbook can be used by others at any time. You are welcome to use this workbook as a starting point to begin your company's blockchain journey, analyzing the relevance of your business to blockchain technology.

## Glossary

• **51% Attack**: A situation where a malicious actor attacks and gains more than 51% of the entire network's computing power (hash rate)
• **Artificial Intelligence (AI)**: Creating computer applications that behave with human-like intelligence
• **Bitcoin**: The first cryptocurrency and store of value launched on a blockchain, launched in 2009
• **Blockchain**: A shared digital ledger of information distributed across different computers, recording transactions in chronological order, making transactions expensive and difficult to tamper with, allowing potentially opposing parties to cooperate
• **Blockchain-as-a-Service (BaaS)**: A blockchain service offering that allows customers to use cloud-based services to develop and host their blockchain applications, functions, and smart contracts
• **Cryptocurrency**: A digital currency that uses encryption techniques to regulate the generation of currency units and verify the transfer of funds, operating independently of a central authority
• **Decentralized Autonomous Organization (DAO)**: An organizational system controlled by smart contracts
• **Decentralized Applications (dApps)**: Open-source software that leverages blockchain technology
• **Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT)**: A database that exists across multiple locations or among multiple participants; it is decentralized, eliminating the need for a central authority or intermediary to process, validate, or authenticate transactions
• **Hard Fork**: A split of a blockchain network into two separate networks
• **Hash Rate**: Computing power
• **Initial Coin Offering (ICO)**: A fundraising mechanism in which new projects sell their crypto tokens to raise capital for new crypto assets, companies, or venture capital. Similar to an Initial Public Offering (IPO), where investors purchase shares in a company
• **Internet of Things (IoT)**: Adding smart devices to a system to assist with data collection and analysis, as well as process automation
• **InterPlanetary File System**: An open-source peer-to-peer hypermedia protocol designed to make the web faster, safer, and more open
• **Miner**: A type of node that secures the blockchain by computing transactions
• **Node**: Any electronic device capable of connecting to the network, including phones, printers, and computers
• **Proof of Stake (PoS)**: An algorithm designed to achieve distributed consensus on a blockchain
• **Proof of Work (PoW)**: A model for blockchain to achieve consensus
• **Smart Contract**: A computer protocol that facilitates, verifies, or enforces a contract, making contractual clauses unnecessary
• **Token**: A unit issued on a blockchain for a limited or specific function or purpose, such as Ethereum Gas and Storj tokens
• **Tor**: An anonymous communication protocol released in 2002
• **Turing-Complete Virtual Machine**: A computer capable of writing programs or contracts to solve any reasonable computational problem
