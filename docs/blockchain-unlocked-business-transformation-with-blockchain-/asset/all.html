It is as simple as A, B, C

Many emerging forms of technology and trends are of concern to innovators, entrepreneurs, and end-users around the world. Now let’s go through each of the elements in my ABC formation and summarise how each might impact the world.

A for Artificial Intelligence

Machines possessing cognitive capabilities akin to humans, or AI, has existed as an idea since the 1950s at least. However, we have always failed to actualise it. The beginning held great hope that the mass application of AI was within reach. Those who worked on it and kept close tabs believed that within a decade or two, machines would take over industrial processes, including aspects of planning, designing, and execution. The primary expectation was that, with very little human input, it would solve many of the problems faced by society.

However, the dream died in the sixties and seventies as the attention of governments, technology companies, and universities went elsewhere. As a result, funding for AI studies and innovations dried up. This period has been dubbed the AI winter period. Then in the eighties and nineties, there was an awakening. A new generation of scientists returned to this technology that once upon a time inspired a lot of hope in academia, business, government, and society. A few governments and investment firms were ready to listen again and offer the necessary financial support. In the years since, many steps were taken. But it was not until the turn of the millennium that we began to see meaningful development in AI and machine learning.

This time, what stood in the way was the lack of other necessary technical capabilities. While AI is possible in theory, it needed a high volume memory and fast speed data processing capacity, which were not available in the 1950s but have come into being gradually over the years, as dictated by Moore’s Law. Now we have the most appropriate technological environment to actualise AI in the most useful way. As a result, almost every major technology company has a robust AI project and numerous startups globally are working on something AI-related.

AI stands to make life a little easier as more processes are automated and therefore increase the speed of delivery. The technology makes the production of goods and services a little cheaper and has the potential to provide an efficiency that often lacks in human labour. For example, a machine could perform an operation on a patient with far fewer chances of error as it has the advantage of processing more data with more accuracy within a shorter time than a human surgeon.

Most of us already have some form of experience with an AI application. For example, Facebook has deployed it to perform facial recognition, while other social media apps like Telegram have developed the necessary capacity for third parties to programme and deploy chatbots to respond to queries and provide critical information to users. Notably, these chatbots learn on the job and become more effective with time. Even more disruptive AI applications are coming into the market. Practically every major car manufacturer is testing self-driving-car, and some countries are even putting the necessary legislation in place to accommodate this new reality.

B for Blockchain

The letter B stands for blockchain. This technology has made achieveable several things that just a decade ago seemed impossible, including the collaboration of independent entities on peer-to-peer networks on the status of transactions. In other words, it has made it possible that multiple independent computers on a decentralised network agree on a single truth through a consensus process, taking just a few seconds without the need for a central authority. Another value it brings is the immutability of digital data. Before blockchain, there was no way to protect online data from being arbitrarily changed aside from limiting access. Even then, you had to trust that the administrator of the system is honourable enough not to interfere with the data. With blockchain, data is safeguarded by a protocol instead, and with which no one can interfere. This characteristic of immutability also solves the age old problem of double-spending, which had stood in the way of having truly digital cash.

Importantly, through the smart contract application and a native digital currency that supports micropayments, the blockchain can automate financial transactions between AI devices in the Internet of things (IoT). For example, a self-drive car could pay for the charge received at a charging station. Simply put, the self-drive car and the electric charging station can enter into and complete a business transaction without input from a person.

Thus, blockchain is best suited to secure the data that is processed and stored by the AI system through its unique characteristics of having immutability and a decentralised backend. The technology also provides mechanisms through public-private key cryptography to provide privacy to those who interact with the intelligence devices.

C for Cybersecurity

The Internet is still largely the wild west. Various actors are practically free to do anything they want. The anonymity gives criminals the cover they need to steal, blackmail, or even destroy critical data as well as systems. It is not easy to fix this problem as the Internet comes with several huge dilemmas. While people and companies need their data online to access or provide services more efficiently, doing so exposes them to the risks of theft, blackmail, and breach of privacy. Another dilemma is that attempts to make the Internet safer often results in trampling on the rights of some users. For example, making it mandatory to identify yourself to use the Internet in order to track possible bad actors necessarily breaches everyone’s right to privacy.

Nevertheless, cybersecurity is becoming a major concern, especially with so much sensitive data and processes going onto the Internet. Individuals and businesses lose billions of dollars annually to hackers. The risk could be higher with new technologies like AI and blockchain, forcing businesses, governments, and individuals to bring more data, money, and processes online. It is also important to point out that as the Internet becomes faster, and more expansive and inclusive, hackers are growing smarter and more powerful over time. The question then: how can the Internet be made safer for all without trampling on the rights of any?

Interestingly, the solution to cybersecurity also comes from new technologies, in particular, blockchain and AI, which provide us with tools that we have never had before. In the case of blockchain, it allows specific pieces of data to be secured through immutability and decentralisation. As mentioned earlier, immutability makes it difficult to change the form of data once it is created, while decentralisation makes it difficult to steal or destroy data because doing so requires one to take over thousands or even millions of independent computers, which is, of course, impossible. Thus, blockchain makes it possible to secure the Internet without affecting the privacy of Internet users.

Blockchain also offers another way to protect data. Through cryptography, data can be encrypted and only accessed with a private key, which can be stored offline. The AI technology, too, gives the capacity to monitor systems in a way that human labour cannot replicate. Relying solely on human oversight is always inadequate. The technology can detect and prevent threats that humans cannot easily foresee.

Blockchain and the Byzantine Generals’ Problem

The Byzantine Generals’ Problem stemmed from the computer science description of a situation where parties involved must agree on a single strategy in order to avoid failure. However, some of the parties involved are corrupt and disseminating false information and are otherwise unreliable.

Let’s go into the story.

An entire army, split into two divisions, has gotten a city surrounded, but have a complicated logistics issue. The divisions are positioned on each side of the enemy city (East and West). The city is strong enough to defend itself against one division, but will fall if attacked by both. As such, both divisions need to attack at the same exact time. If the divisions do not attack at the same time, they will lose.

Thus, the generals of each division need to agree on the exact moment of when to attack. They can only communicate by sending a messenger back and forth the divisions without any other way to communicate. It is ancient times after all. For example, General A will send the message, “Hey General B, we are going to attack on Wednesday. Will you attack with us?”. The messenger then delivers the message to General B, who in turn responds, “We can’t do Wednesday—ladies night. How about Thursday? If we attack on Thursday, will you attack with us?”. The messenger delivers the message back to General A, and so forth.

However, there is a twist: the messenger could be hijacked and caught while delivering the message. Instead, a #fakenews messenger could intentionally send a wrong message to deceive the other general to attack the city on the wrong day. There is no way to check if the message is real. How do they create a “trustless” system that ensures victory in when they attack?

This is the Byzantine Generals’ Problem.

What is blockchain?

A blockchain is a virtual machine that runs on a peer-to-peer network of computers. It can host and run almost all kinds of applications. Payment systems, cloud storage, smart contracts, land registries, supply chain management systems, websites—you name it. Any application that can be launched on a centralised server can be launched on a blockchain.

Blockchain can also host and run applications that cannot work on centralised server systems. We already have all these applications running on the legacy Internet (i.e., the Internet pre-blockchain). What better than to have them on a virtual machine running on top of a peer-to-peer network?

That is a fundamental question. It is at the heart of why blockchain has a future. But before we answer this, let’s get a big picture of how blockchain functions and why it is a virtual machine.

Computer networks have been around since the 1960s. (By networks, I mean several computers having channels for communicating with one another.) The earliest ones were centralised2, which meant computers formed had to communicate with one another through a central server. The server was a computer with the capability to facilitate communication between computers. Over the years, peer-to-peer networks developed. This is where one computer could communicate with another without having to go through a central server. Indeed, the Internet itself is a form of peer-to-peer networks. But peer-to-peer networks in the strict use of the word came into being in the late 1990s. It was first experienced in Napster, a network one joined by installing a particular software to share music with others3.

But even with that development, some things could work without a central server. In particular, a payment system. For a payment system to work, a central server had to exist to host and maintain a transaction ledger. People on the network had to trust that the record was accurate. In particular, people in charge of the server had to be trusted that they could not falsify the records. But still, servers were hacked and data stolen or records arbitrarily changed.

Permissioned vs. permissionless

The implementation of the blockchain can be categorised as two types: permissioned (only a select few can read/write) or permissionless (anyone can read/write). These blockchains may be expanded into three subcategories:

1. Public blockchains

2. Consortium blockchains

3. Private blockchains

While public and consortium blockchains may be centralised or decentralised (decisions may be made either by a central body or a selected multiple group of people or by anyone), private blockchains are always centralised (decisions are made by a central body).

Whether the blockchain decision-making is centralised or decentralised is distinct from how its data is distributed. In the latter, or a distributed ledger, data or information may be stored at multiple points with possibly multiple groups. However, in the former, the decision-making and writing of a new block or voting for a change in the base protocol of the blockchain may centralised or decentralised.

The founding fathers, who popularised the blockchain through Bitcoin, intended for the initial use cases to be permissionless and decentralised, i.e., having a decentralised model, in which everyone can participate, contribute, and collaborate together—very much reflected in the characteristics of Bitcoin.

For the purposes of simplification in this book, references to the blockchain and the distributed ledger may be used interchangeability (although technically they are not exactly the same). In some instances, the blockchain may be generalised to be referred, too, as either a centralised or decentralised collaborative model.

These different approaches will be illustrated further in the use cases from various industries in Part 2 of the book.

How the Bitcoin system works

Enter the Bitcoin, the first cryptocurrency and Store of Value (SoV) on the blockchain.

Making independent computers agree on and contribute to the single ledger can be described as one of the most exciting IT innovations of the 21st century. In late 2008, an individual or a group going by the pseudonym Satoshi Nakamoto published a white paper4. In it, they describe a peer-to-peer network that maintains a shared ledger of transactions through an elaborate consensus mechanism.

Independent computers in a peer-to-peer network do not need a central server to facilitate interaction amongst them. To communicate with one another, each computer has to download and install core software. It is this core software that contains the rules of engagement (protocol). Indeed, the essential thing that Satoshi Nakamoto did was to figure out the protocol and actualise it in a core software that every computer that joins the network has to install. Over the years, a group of volunteer programmers has continued to maintain the core software through consensus.

Through these consensus rules in the core software, computers in the Bitcoin peer-to-peer network maintain a ledger of transactions. You see, in a centralised system like a bank, a central authority stores and maintains the ledger of transactions through its agents (both human and machine tellers). Bitcoin has a ledger too. However, unlike in a centralised payment system, it is stored as a copy in each of the computers on the network.

Now, the biggest challenge is to have everyone have a completely identical copy of the ledger. Having one copy with transactions that are not in another would make the payment system fail. Satoshi solved this problem by introducing the concept of mining. Using the protocol rules, the computers in the network pick one of them to update the ledger every ten minutes.

How exactly do they undertake the process?

The computers on the network participate in a competition with rules provided by the protocol. The competition involves each of the computers taking transaction requests made within ten minutes and combining them into a block. Each computer will then hash the data in their block to fit a value according to the protocol requirements. The first computer to find the value will have its version of the block accepted as the next valid one on the ledger. It will join those that were accepted earlier.

All the other computers will synchronise their copies to the one by the winning machine. The computer that contributed the next valid block keeps the mining reward, newly released coins. This process repeats itself and is in some ways similar to a legislative election. Several people compete in an election and the winner gets the power to write laws, or in this case, transactions on a shared ledger instead. It is also not after every four or five years but every ten minutes.

The Bitcoin ledger was given the name blockchain, because the data is stored in terms of blocks that form a chain.

It turns out this way of forming a consensus on the status of a shared ledger or data storage, can even be used on transactions that are not related to payment. A peer-to-peer network using a similar protocol can agree on the execution of application code, for example. And that is what makes the network a virtual machine that can take the place of the old centralised server. By having the ability to form a consensus on transactions and data, computers in a peer-to-peer network can function as a unit. Or a single virtual machine that can execute all kinds of applications.

Back to our earlier question: why should we prefer this virtual machine to the old centralised server?

One of the most significant advantages of using blockchain is that the technology removes the single point of failure that we have with servers. There is no one point of the blockchain you can attack to disable it. As long as any part of the Internet is up and running, the blockchain will continue to work.

The blockchain also makes digital data or records immutable. Once a record is made, no one can delete or change its content arbitrarily. This, for example, makes the possibility to hack a land registry and change ownership of an asset almost impossible.

It not only makes peer-to-peer transactions possible but also cheaper, as it removes third parties that make transactions slow and expensive.

Let’s stay with the example of land titles to illustrate this point. If you want to purchase a piece of real estate, in most countries today you will have to depend on many third parties: real estate agents, the land registry office, where you confirm ownership, notaries, and of course, lawyers to draft the sales agreement. If countries put land titles on the blockchain, most of these trusted third parties become irrelevant. And you spend less on the transaction. You can search a piece of land in the registry, and using a private key, the owner of the asset can easily prove to you that they are really the owner. Using a smart contract application, both of you can draft a sales agreement. Also, through an escrow service on the platform, you can swap the land title and the agreed price simultaneously. All of this can happen within minutes.

In every single industry you look at, in similar ways, the blockchain can make things easier and cheaper.

How is blockchain likely to change businesses?

The technology is going to change businesses in three primary ways:

1. Efficiency, reliability, and security to business systems.

As we will not need any trusted third parties that are critical to current systems, blockchain will make transactions faster and cheaper. It also offers some integrity in how businesses handle data. It secures data; importantly, it gives people control over their data. This is good for end users but not exactly to businesses. There are so many businesses around that are built on using data collected from people. Those businesses might have to rethink their strategy as many people will find a way to be stingy with their information. The technology allows them to give only data that is most necessary for transacting with a business.

The blockchain also improves how intellectual property rights are handled. Any business that comes up with unique ideas will find it easy to not only register their innovation but also prove to others that they came up with the idea first.

2. New businesses will be created.

Blockchain is creating entirely new businesses. We’ve already seen the rise of mining companies as well as digital asset exchanges.

In 2016, we witnessed an attempt to build Decentralised Autonomous Organisations (DAO). This is a company that does not have a centralised management structure, but has investors and carries out its operations through contracting. All its decisions are made through ongoing voting by investors. If this model becomes popular, existing businesses may have to discover ways to do business with DAOs.

3. Other businesses will be killed.

Blockchain is going to make many business models and businesses themselves irrelevant. To survive, most businesses might have to rethink their models. Otherwise, they risk facing the same demise as Kodak or Blockbuster, which did not innovate or upgrade their businesses to keep up with world trends; Kodak, because of digital cameras, stuck to printing photos, while Blockbuster stuck to renting out DVDs in the era of YouTube and Netflix. We are already seeing banks, insurance companies, and other industries taking active steps to understand the technology and how it might affect their business models.

Even disruptive businesses such as Uber and Airbnb can easily be disrupted themselves, because they are centralised entities that aggregate resources and sell them in the marketplace. Blockchain makes it possible that riders and drivers, for example, can meet and transact without having to go through a third party like Uber. With an open-source blockchain application that is not owned by any corporate entity, homeowners can access travellers and efficiently serve them aided with smart contracts like they would on Airbnb. And they won’t have to give a cut of their earnings to anyone.

Even though a lot of hype surrounds what blockchain can do, it is reasonable to expect the technology to change how we do a lot of things significantly. And that means we need to be proactive in learning about it.

What Blockchain Cannot Do

We’ve established that blockchain can do a lot. It has the potential to disrupt business in ways many of us have not yet imagined. But blockchain is not a panacea. It is not a one-size-fits-all solution to every problem that society faces, even if it is the impression you get when you visit any online blockchain forum or attend a blockchain meetup, summit, or conference. The belief that this technology can solve any problem thrown at it is very pervasive within the space.

Think of any industry. Most likely, there are startups claiming to build blockchain solutions for particular issues. The mushrooming of such blockchain startups was at its height in 2016 and 2017, mostly driven by the Initial Coin Offering (ICO) craze. In those two years, blockchain startups raised over US$30 billion in investment. One startup, Bancor, a decentralised cryptocurrency exchange, raised US$153 million in three hours. Another, Basic Attention Token (BAT), a digital advertising token that provides advertising services on their browser network, raised US$35 million in 30 seconds. Dozens of similar blockchain investment stories were recorded in 2017 and 2018.

However, most of the startups had nothing more than nicely polished white papers. It is no wonder that much of the money was lost. Most of the startups globally that raised money during this period either never delivered any working product or struggled to come up with something that could resonate with a viable market.

The siren call of the blockchain

According to Ameer Rosic, a blockchain entrepreneur, investor, and founder of the online blockchain innovation hub, Blockgeeks, up to 90 percent of all ICOs failed1. “The fact is, that around 99 percent of all ICOs out there will fail,” Ameer stated in a social media post, “And that’s not exaggerated doom and gloom, over the last few years, thousands of cryptocurrencies have been created, and over 90 percent of them have failed.”

The reason so many of them have struggled is that they either scrambled to put together an idea with blockchain in it or rushed to include the technology in product designs just so that they could get money from investors eager to be part of the blockchain revolution. It is also fair to state that it is cool to have the word blockchain in your product description. It somehow makes a business look innovative, futuristic, and conscious of technological trends. Even major global brands such as Microsoft, Samsung, Barclays, and others could not resist this allure.

Many of the business ideas that were sold in 2016 and 2017 for ICO investment could have been better implemented without involving the blockchain technology at all. It is hard to tell what exactly drives the narrative that blockchain should be part of everything. It is a movement by the general public, perhaps through ground-up organisation, as they have lost trust and confidence in the bodies governing the various aspects of the world. My guess is this reflects a desire for power back to the people.

But the enthusiasm that grows in someone once they discover and understand the genius of the design and architecture behind the technology, as well as the financial interests that often follow, must play a role. Even where the technology provides the most value, it has limitations; this is hardly mentioned in forums that discuss blockchain.

In general, blockchain is sold as a technology that provides three primary advantages: (1) better security to data and systems, (2) improved privacy for users online, and (3) more efficiency in terms of speed, capacity as well as cost. Let’s look at each one.

Better security to data and systems

Blockchain is the first innovation to make digital data truly immutable. Once a set of information is recorded on the shared digital ledger, it cannot be arbitrarily changed. This is very powerful, as it means it is now possible to make systems resistant to many forms of attacks, including man-in-the-middle, SQL Injection, and malware attacks. Also, as the system uses public-private key cryptography, there is no worry about the security of the online channels used to transfer system-access credentials such as passwords and PINs. A user keeps the private key for signing transactions locally (and even offline) and only shares the corresponding public key. The blockchain also uses an elaborate consensus mechanism that makes it impossible for a lone player to make critical decisions or changes to records without the involvement of other critical stakeholders.

Given these characteristics, the blockchain seems like an excellent replacement for the server backend’s storage and processing of critical data. For example, it can make a better backend for a land registry. If land titles are on a blockchain backend, they cannot be easily changed by either a hacker or a dishonest person with the right credentials.

However, even with all the security measures that the blockchain provides, it is still up to us to make it work or fail. Human intervention is still an important security factor. Blockchain cannot protect information systems from human error, poor judgment, or sabotage. Let’s use the land title registry for example again to illustrate this. Even on the blockchain, it still requires people to do the survey, the subdivisions, and register the titles. Unfortunately, there is no shortage of things that can go wrong here. The wrong title could be assigned to the wrong person. The wrong size of real estate could be entered into the system for a particular title. Or some real estate might be left entirely out of the system.

Besides, even an incorruptible land registry on the blockchain requires the courts to enforce ownership rights with, possibly, the threat of force. How those are used is determined by individuals in power. It is also possible that should a dictator come into power, they can choose to ignore the blockchain system and go ahead to distribute land the way they want using another system.

It is important to point out that even though blockchain makes hacking a lot more difficult, it does not prevent other methods of gaining illegitimate access to systems. The Bitcoin blockchain has never been successfully hacked since its launch in 2009. In his book Digital Gold, New York Times journalist Nathaniel Popper writes about Argentinian entrepreneur Wences Casares’s attempts to hack the Bitcoin blockchain. When he learnt about Bitcoin in 2010, Casares thought it was too good to be true. He offered $500,000 to two online security entities to attempt to break the system. They failed2.

But while the Bitcoin peer-to-peer ledger has never been compromised, billions worth of Bitcoins have been stolen from wallets of exchanges as well as individuals. The hackers have failed to hack the Bitcoin shared ledger and change the records there. However, they have figured out how to achieve the same goals by going for the private keys of wallets holding value. With blockchain, the focus shift from gaining access through other forms of attacks to accessing the private keys that can then be used to authorise irreversible transactions. However, this form of hacking is not any less harmful. So we can say that blockchain cannot provide total security from hacking or unauthorised access.

Improved privacy for users online

Now let’s look at the other advantage that blockchain provides, and that is privacy it affords users online. Blockchain has never been anonymous; it is only pseudo-anonymous, which means that like with centralised payment systems, you need a username to use a system on the blockchain. The only difference is that on the blockchain this username need not be your real-world identity. Still, if someone is able to connect your real-world identity and your username on the blockchain, then your privacy is blown.

Bitcoin proved that people could send and receive money online without having to disclose their real-life identity. The goal that Satoshi Nakamoto, the creator of Bitcoin, seemed to have was to make Bitcoin behave like cash online. Many believed Bitcoin was electronic cash, but not for long. It quickly dawned on users that the Bitcoin ledger (a blockchain) is public and anyone can access it.

It turns out that there are now companies that build solutions to help law enforcement and other entities figure out the movement of value and data on the blockchain. And with a little analysis, they can connect the pseudonyms on the blockchain with the real-life identities of the users using data collected from various sources. One such company is Chainalysis, founded by Jan Moller, Jonathan Levin, and Michael Gronager in late 2014. The company claims to help banks, businesses, and governments understand how people use cryptocurrencies3.

Their website states: “We provide blockchain data and analysis to government agencies, exchanges, and financial institutions across 40 countries. Our compliance and investigation tools, education, and support help our customers understand what’s happening on blockchains.”

Of course, this vulnerability of the Bitcoin and other blockchains has inspired developers to design and build blockchains that obfuscate transactions further. Monero and Zcash are two examples of such blockchains. But even with these, privacy loopholes exist. In particular, users can be identified through monitoring the onramp and offramp points. For anyone to get their money into a cryptocurrency, they need to use an exchange. As exchanges are centralised entities for the most part, we are seeing them come under strict “Know Your Customer (KYC)” requirements. Only a few years ago, one could easily use an exchange without having to disclose who they are. But this has gradually changed. Now, even peer-to-peer exchanges like LocalBitcoins require users to register themselves using government-issued identification documents such as passports and driving licenses.

More efficient speed, capacity, and cost

What about efficiency? How much can blockchain provide? Blockchain has proven that it is possible to speed up financial transactions; we can send any amount from one corner of the globe to another in a matter of minutes. However, blockchain scalability is still a work in progress, especially when applications that demand a significantly high number of code executions are launched on a blockchain platform. In late 2017, a team of developers launched a game called Cryptokitties on the Ethereum blockchain. Due to its high capacity demands, the blockchain was overwhelmed, slowing down every other application on the platform. It is important to point out that Ethereum was the first blockchain to be designed as a backend for multiple applications.

On the Bitcoin blockchain, developers have struggled to provide speed and efficiency. It appears that users have to sacrifice speed for security and to have a relatively high level of privacy when they choose to use cryptocurrencies.

For a long time, scaling of the Bitcoin blockchain has been a major point of interest and contention. In particular, the Bitcoin community has debated how to make the system confirm more transactions per second to effectively compete with centralised payment systems such as Visa and MasterCard. Solutions like SegWit and Lightning Network have added a lot of capacity. However, this is still insignificant compared to centralised server systems. Visa, for example, has a capacity of up to 50,000 transactions in a second, while the Bitcoin network can only confirm less than 100 transactions.

There are a few projects now trying to create hybrids of blockchain and conventional servers using innovations such as the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS). Such systems can have the best of both worlds by combining the speed of traditional servers and security of the blockchain. Thus, we can say that blockchain cannot provide infinite capacity.

The blockchain can deliver online experiences that were not previously possible. It has proved that it can provide better value in applications that have previously existed. It can bring forth applications that are only possible on the blockchain. A good example is the smart contract application, or agreements written in code that self-executes when predefined conditions are met.

However, expecting the technology to be applicable in every situation is not realistic. Neither is the expectation that it is perfect. Sometimes designing systems without blockchain is the right thing to do after considering technical needs, cost, and the expected user experience (UX). It is also important to point out that blockchain cannot function in a vacuum. Aside from the necessary skills needed (which you can assess in the free bonus at the end of this book), the technology requires other resources to be of meaningful use. For example, while we might believe strongly that the blockchain is the solution to the global unbanked, it cannot work without other infrastructures such as access to the Internet, available electricity, and affordable technical devices like smartphones.

Blockchain can only be useful if it is applied where it is truly needed. Many problems require more than blockchain as a solution. Often, politics, human resources, and socioeconomics need to be fixed first before the technology becomes useful.

The Art and Science Behind Tokenomics

Tokenomics is a relatively new term. Canadian blockchain angel investor and venture advisor, William Mougayar, claimed to have coined it sometime in 2017. Mougayar himself is a fairly well-known individual within the blockchain space. Aside from investing in various blockchain startups and sitting on several boards, he is an author, blogger, speaker, and the founder of Token Summit, which is an annual conference on the Token-based Economy1.

As you’ve probably already guessed, Tokenomics is a compound of the words token and economics. It substitutes the term economics of tokens. Defining the root term “token” will help us have a better grasp of Tokenomics.

Tokens are not new.

It is essential to point out that tokens did not arrive with the blockchain. Tokens have always existed before blockchain, such as loyalty points and casino chips. In Switzerland, local authorities sell rubbish bags as tokens2, which are sold at a price that is several times higher than the cost of production. The added price is deemed what you pay for the rubbish that is put into the bag.

What the blockchain has done is to make digital tokens easier, secure, and more convenient to create, manage, and use.

So, what is a token?

According to the Google dictionary, a token is a thing that represents or stands for something of value. A token does not have intrinsic value, but is assumed to carry the value of the asset it represents. For example, a casino chip is not useful, except for its representation of value that a player can redeem from the casino in the form of cash. On the blockchain, there is a little confusion as to which assets are tokens. Depending on who you ask, all digital assets that are created, managed, and used on top of a blockchain are tokens. This includes cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin, Monero, Ether, and Zcash. There are even literature, presentations, and published books that use the words token and cryptocurrency interchangeably.

However, a token and a cryptocurrency are not the same thing in the strictest sense of the words. Unlike crytocurrency, a token is a unit on the blockchain that is issued for a limited or specific function or use. This can be where it is used or the extent to which it is used. For example, a casino chip is a token because you can use it only within the casino and only for gambling.

Loyalty points that a retail store, or any other business entity, creates and awards are tokens. You can use them only within the issuer’s establishment and for the purpose they define. You cannot—unless there are exceptions—use loyalty points issued by one entity to get benefits from its competitor or third-party business. An example of a token on the blockchain is Steem, which is used as a native utility coin on Steemit, a blockchain-based blogging and social media platform. Users pay and get paid in Steem. And it is an incentive for those who create content on the platform. There is hardly any third-party store that you can shop at using the Steem token.

On the other hand, cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin are units of value that are used as a medium of exchange, store of value, and unit of account across many platforms. In other words, they do most of the functions of a legal tender, even though they are not. They are private digital currencies. This is why we call them cryptocurrencies (currencies that use cryptography).

The fact that an asset is a token today does not mean it cannot be a currency tomorrow. It is important to point this out. In the early days, Bitcoin was more of a token than a currency. Its primary function was to serve as an incentive. People kept their machines running to support the Bitcoin network and get the coins in return. Over time, Bitcoins became acceptable as a means of payment outside the network. We can argue that this turned it into a currency.

What types of tokens exist in blockchain?

In broad terms, there are four types of tokens you can create on the blockchain. It is important to point out that these categories can overlap, so a token can fit easily into two or even three of them.

Initial Coin Offering tokens

Initial Coin Offering (ICO) tokens are created for the sole purpose of raising capital for a project. It is a means of collecting funds to go into building a platform. Such a token is particularly useful for funding the development of open-source projects. The buyers of this token do not expect dividends. Instead, they hope that when the project is finally built, the value of the token will go up, and can be sold to others at higher prices for profit.

Due to pressure from regulators, however, we are not seeing any ICO tokens being issued. Anyone who wants to sell tokens has to define them as either utility tokens or security tokens. Some tokens are now being issued through exchanges, which is referred to as the initial exchange offering (IEO). Instead of the startup in question selling the token, it engages an exchange to do this on its behalf.

Utility tokens

The second category comprises utility tokens. These are tokens created to provide access to specific services and privileges on a platform. One example is the Storj coin, which you use to pay for space on the Storj network, a decentralised cloud storage service. It is easier to sell utility tokens compared to other forms of tokens, especially the security tokens. However, you need to prove to regulators, such as the Securities Exchange Commission (SEC) in the US, that it does serve a particular purpose on your ecosystem and that you are not selling it simply to raise capital.

Assets-backed tokens

The third category of tokens that startups and projects create on the blockchain are the assets-backed tokens. These are tokens created to represent real-life assets such as real estate, gold, artwork, and even fiat currency on the blockchain. They provide a mode of digitising these real-world assets in a way that makes it easy to trade them and thus increase their liquidity. An example is Tether, which is backed by (and pegged to) the US dollar. Tether makes it easy to move US dollar in and out of the Bitfinex exchange. It is one of several stablecoins, which include TrueUSD, Paxos Standard, and Binance USD.

Security tokens

A security token is the share capital that a startup or a company sells at the marketplace. People who buy this type of token expect dividends and also have a say in how the company is managed. They expect the company to have a mechanism in place through which they can influence the management and policy. This token has not been issued often, due to the strict regulatory requirements in the majority of countries about issuing stock using the blockchain. It is a type of token that works better with companies and not open source projects.

Whether you issue an ICO, utility, asset-backed, or security token depends on a couple of factors. The regulatory environment is one of them. You may consider a utility token instead of a security token because the former does not expose you to a lot of legal challenges. Another factor is the kind of project you are working on. For example, is it an open-source project or a product by a private company? If you are building an open-source project, an ICO token could be the best way to go. But if you are working on a product as a private company, a security or utility token could be what you need.

What makes a great token?

Now let’s turn our attention to what tokenomics is exactly. Anybody can create tokens on the blockchain. You can do it right now in under five minutes3. But those tokens will be worthless until there is a sizable number of people who find them useful and worth holding in their wallet. The harder part of token issuance is to provide a reason for other people to accept them as being useful or having any value.

And that is where tokenomics come in.

Tokenomics is all about designing a token that solves a pressing problem and creating a plan so that it reaches the intended users. If you fail on these two fronts, hardly anyone will ever find out about your token. Let’s find out the ingredients that make for great tokenomics.

Real need

The first ingredient is a genuine need for the token. You shouldn’t create a token simply because you can. Or because you need to sell it and make some quick cash. If you envision growing your project into something big in the future, you should only create a token because it plays a vital role in the big idea you have beyond bringing in cash. In 2017 and 2018, many startups raised hundreds of millions of dollars by selling blockchain tokens to FOMO investors (simply, investors who fear missing out). After the sale, many lost value and became nothing more than figures in wallets. The investors realised that no innovative products were forthcoming after the token.

Things have changed a lot since then. Investors are a little wiser now. A well-written white paper is not enough to make them buy your tokens. To convince them, you have to design a system that serves a purpose and provides value to users. Not to mention the strict regulatory requirements for approval that have been put in place in many jurisdictions, including the US.

The token should be a much-needed cog in an elaborate system. With a genuine need, a token stands to grow in value over time, and holders get other benefits from it. For example, it is difficult to imagine how the Storj network would work without a native means of payment. Payment methods such as PayPal or credit cards cannot work on such a system, as these methods cannot be included into a smart contract on the blockchain, which is something that the Storj protocol requires.

If you must raise capital using a token that does not play a role in your final product or project, you should consider issuing a security token. This is share equity in your company. You will issue it following the rules that govern the conventional share issue and sale of securities. However, the rules are extremely stringent in most countries, and you may never get approval to carry out one.

Circulation model

Another ingredient of good tokenomics is a carefully considered circulation model: the life cycle and path of a particular token. There are three major token circulation models.

The first one is the circular model, where the token moves around the system from one hand to another indefinitely without a predetermined end. It more or less functions as cash. The Storj token is used in this model. A person that needs space on the network buys the token from an exchange and then pays the farm owners with it. Those who earn the Storj tokens, by providing storage space, sell them at an exchange for cryptocurrency or fiat currency. And the cycle starts again.

A second model is the source-to-burn model, where a token is issued to be used only once. Once spent, the token is destroyed. This is ideal for tokens that are used as tickets or access tokens. Once spent, these tokens are no longer needed and are replaced by newly generated ones.

The last major model is the straight-and-back model. This is where a token is issued, goes out to perform a function, then it comes back to the one who issued it. A casino chip is a perfect example of a token that uses this model. You buy it for use within the establishment, and once done, you return it (assuming you kept it or won more) to the issuer to redeem fiat money.

Game theory

Another ingredient in tokenomics is game theory, which designs the platform, project, or product so that all stakeholders are incentivised in a way that benefits the project. For example, it is through a robust game theory that Bitcoin has survived and continued to grow, even though it does not have a central authority to manage and guide it. On the Bitcoin network, people are incentivised through a mining reward to provide the hardware resources necessary to maintain the shared ledger. Satoshi Nakamoto also seemed to have given a lot of thought on how to protect the network from bad actors. The founder made any attempt to attack the network extremely expensive. It is more beneficial to play by the rules on the network than to break them.

Any token that desires to be successful has to have its game theory well thought out. The right incentives should be provided for the right people so that they act in such a way that furthers the project’s success. It is important that you figure how to create value in a digital token, incentivise people to hold the coin, and more importantly, incentivise early adopters to spread the word for the project.

A robust game theory is very important for a decentralised ecosystem, as the opportunity to change things is unlikely once the project is out of the core developers’ hands. If you have the budget for it, it is important to engage the services of a qualified game theorist to help you figure out how to create a self-preserving ecosystem.

Supply

Another thing to think about when designing your token is its supply. What is the exact number of tokens that you need to have in circulation? Do you need it capped? If not, what should be the emission rate?

If your tokens are to be used only once, such as a ticket, you may have a cap with a constant emission rate so that the right volume is maintained. If your tokens have an indefinite lifespan and do not get burnt, you may consider a hard cap like that of Bitcoin. This helps to avoid inflation, which translates to growing value for each token over time as the system gains more users.

The token distribution model

Equally important to consider is the token distribution model. What percentage of the tokens will be sold? And what percentage will go to the developer team?

In token distribution, you need to inspire confidence in your project by holding onto some of the tokens. But you also should be careful not to hold on to a huge chunk, which can make your project appear as if it wants control over the market price movement. Holders should feel that the token’s price is not being artificially manipulated.

To design a successful token requires thorough consideration of every small part of the system. Many projects do not interrogate every bit of their design. For example, they pick the number of tokens, emission rate, and the issue price through guesswork.

Any decision you make on these issues needs to be data-driven. Research the right number of tokens needed, depending on the amount of activity you anticipate on your project. Begin with the number of users you expect to have. Also, do all you can to get the best estimates on transaction volumes. Coming up with viable models for these is where you need experts if you do not have the knowledge, experience, or skill.

Is Blockchain Secure

The blockchain technology is designed to hold value in digital assets such as currencies, tokens, and even data. Why is blockchain security critical? At the time of writing this book, the market capitalisation of Bitcoin, the native coin of the Bitcoin blockchain, is close to US$150 billion, while Ethereum’s Ether (ETH) stands at around US$15 billion. These are just two blockchain assets on a list of almost 5,000 on CoinMarketCap, a website that collects and publishes real-time blockchain asset statistics.

The real value on every blockchain in existence is much more than that listed on CoinMarketCap, if one considers the assets that are created on top of it. For example, the Ethereum blockchain, in addition to ETH, supports several coins, tokens, and decentralised applications (dApps). The value is even greater if we count the businesses that provide blockchain-related services such as wallets, exchanges, and mining. Not least are the entities that use the blockchain as the backend for storing and processing data for their applications and systems.

What makes blockchain secure?

We can consider the security of a blockchain from three angles. The first (1) angle concerns your own individual interaction with the system, in particular protecting your ownership of a blockchain asset and the privacy of your identity and activities. The second (2) angle concerns the possibility of a bad actor compromising, taking over, or destroying the entire blockchain network. The third (3) angle questions the robustness of the core software and, by extension, the protocol. In particular, whether bugs exist in the code that could make the system insecure.

We are going to look at these three angles in turn.

Security depends on you.

How secure is an individual’s assets, data, and identity on the blockchain?

The short answer is that your activities, assets, and identity on the blockchain are about as secure as the steps you take towards that end. In other words, your security on the blockchain depends a lot on you. Sometimes more so than the design of the blockchain.

How is that so?

On the blockchain, every user has a public key (or a public address), which is the identity used to interact and transact on the platform. Each public key has a corresponding private key. Think of the public key as your email address and the private key as the password you need to access your emails. When someone sends Bitcoins to your public key (address), the only way you can exercise control over those Bitcoins is through the corresponding private key. Thus, you need the private key to spend Bitcoins. It turns out that a private key is a kind of bearer tool; whoever has access can use it. Anyone who gets a hold of your private keys could spend your Bitcoins.

This applies to other applications on the blockchain too. If a land title is transferred to you on a blockchain-based land registry, for example, the only way you can prove your control over it is by using the private key that corresponds to the public key assigned to the title in the system. If anyone else has access to that, they could transfer the ownership of the real estate out of your hands.

This leads to the conclusion that your private key is supposed to be really private just like a password to an email account or a PIN for your debit card—no one else should know about it. Unfortunately, private keys have turned out to be the weakest link in blockchain security. Keeping them away from hackers is one of the most onerous responsibilities for users of blockchain applications.

Since the launch of Bitcoin in 2009, billions of dollars have been stolen by hackers who can remotely steal private keys to wallets holding significant amounts of blockchain assets, especially cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin. For example, in early 2014, Mt. Gox, an exchange headquartered in Japan and at the time one of the world’s largest Bitcoin exchange, lost Bitcoins worth over US$450 million to hackers. In January 2018, Coincheck, another Japanese exchange, lost US$530 million to hackers1. According to a report by Chainalysis, a blockchain research company, money lost from exchange wallets was over US$1.2 billion in 20182.

Fortunately, users have several measures to protect their private keys, and hence their assets, from theft. One way is to generate and store the private keys offline, creating a “cold storage”. This uses a cold storage hardware wallet such as Trezor, KeepKey, or Ledger. Another way is to use an offline script to generate the private keys and print them on a hard copy. Users can store private keys in a safe place, but share the corresponding public keys to those who need to send Bitcoin (or any other asset).

Is the blockchain network secure?

Now let’s turn our attention to the security of the entire blockchain.

Many reasons could make someone want to compromise the entire blockchain. It includes wanting control to manipulate entries on the transaction ledger. There might also be the goal to destroy the system, especially to stop it from being a threat to other existing systems. We also cannot rule out the possibility of someone or an entity doing it just for the fun of it.

A blockchain network can be compromised through the “51 percent attack”, in which a bad actor acquires a computing power (hash rate) more than 51 percent of the entire network. The bad actor can use this hash rate to overrule other stakeholders or edit the shared ledger arbitrarily. While such an attack seems plausible, in reality it is almost impossible to pull off. Not least because it is a costly undertaking.

In the case of the Bitcoin blockchain, you would need to accumulate (at the time of writing) over 110 million terahashes (TH/s)3. That is a lot of computer power. Indeed, the Bitcoin network as a unit is the world’s most powerful computer. You have to beat that. Besides buying many mining chips, each one a very expensive device, you need a source to provide you with more electricity than, say, a country the size of Switzerland consumes4.

In short, you need to commit a lot of resources. Once you acquire 51 percent of the hash rate, the rest of the network has the option to fork the blockchain and leave you with a shell. It can create a copy of the ledger up to the point of 51 percent (i.e., acquiring a hash rate above 51 percent of the network), then create a new branch and continue updating a valid ledger. Then you might have to launch another expensive counter-attack. There is hardly any justification for wasting resources in this way; it makes more economic sense to use the resources to mine for the network and receive the mining reward. Indeed, the game theory behind the Bitcoin blockchain is designed to incentivise people to build the system instead of ruining it. In short, it pays more to play by the rules than to break them.

That said, the blockchain space is concerned that developments in quantum computing might make it easier and cheaper to attack and destroy blockchain networks. Such a compromise might come in the form of an ability to break blockchain cryptography. It will become easier to figure out private keys without having to steal them5.

Thus, blockchain developers and innovators are thinking ahead to produce protocols that would make quantum computing inconsequential to blockchain technology. Two of the quantum-computing resistant protocols are Ethereum’s Serenity and zkSTARKs6.

Is the code and protocol robust?

Now let’s look at the third and last angle through which you can ascertain whether a blockchain is secure. Any device on the network has to install and run its core software to communicate and form a consensus with another on the shared ledger, as the blockchain relies on it to execute the protocol. The first version usually comes from the founder of the network; for example, founder Satoshi Nakamoto wrote the first version of the Bitcoin core software. Subsequent versions are products of collaborative work by different players or core developers. Anybody can propose a change to the core software and protocol through the Bitcoin Improvement Proposal (BIP) process. The proposer talks to other members of the community, creates the next version of the core software with the changes, and persuades others to download and run it. The process is more complicated than my description here. But I’m guessing you get the idea.

Like any piece of code, a blockchain core software can end up with bugs and weak points of execution. These can expose users to many risks, such as double-spending and attack points. Major blockchains have had bugs discovered. Some of the bugs have been serious, risking the value held on the blockchain. In late 2018, a flaw was discovered that could have been exploited to shut down part of the Bitcoin network and allow bad actors to create Bitcoins out of thin air. It was kept a secret (for a number of days) by top Bitcoin core developers until they were able to fix it7.

In early 2016, Slick.it, a German blockchain startup launched the Decentralised Anonymous Organisation (the DAO) on top of the Ethereum blockchain. The entity was supposed to act as a company with only investors and no management or staff. The investors would, through voting, contract service providers to perform on behalf of the DAO. It turned out that there was a bug in the code that actualised the idea. But an attacker exploited it and began the process of stealing close to US$150 million raised by investors. To stop the attack, the Ethereum network upgraded its core software and rolled back transactions. This led to the split of the network into two: Ethereum (ETH) and Ethereum Classic (ETC)8.

In summary, the blockchain is not foolproof. Any consideration of a blockchain security in the three levels described in this chapter should be in comparison to other existing systems. In many ways, the blockchain is more secure than the systems it seeks to replace. Blockchain cryptography offers users more control and security over their activities and assets. The game theory that governs blockchains make attacks less likely. As for bugs in codes, blockchains are open source projects and tend to have a caring community; any bug is usually discovered and fixed before it can harm the ecosystem.

Private vs. Public Blockchains

The Bitcoin was an application on the first blockchain. Since its launch in January 2009, over 3,000 public blockchains have sprung up, according to the digital assets listed on CoinMarketCap. With interest in the technology growing each day, we should expect the number to keep growing.

All blockchains have a similar basic structure. They are platforms on top of peer-to-peer networks or independent computers that collaborate to maintain a shared ledger in real time. However, each blockchain implements a protocol that is at least slightly different from the next. Each is designed to solve a particular problem. For example, the Bitcoin blockchain’s primary focus is payments, while the Ethereum blockchain is, more or less, a virtual machine that can serve as a backend for all kinds of applications.

With that stated, blockchains can be grouped using several criteria. This chapter looks at one of these: how blockchains function due to design. In this regard, there are two primary types of blockchains: every blockchain can be categorised as public or private

Private vs. public: What’s the difference?

Earlier in Chapter 1, we introduced the possible distinctions in blockchain, especially with regard to permissions. Between public and private blockchains, three primary areas of differences exist. These are (1) access to read and edit data on the shared ledger, (2) how the core software is developed and maintained, and (3) how much the system relies on game theory to function effectively.

Access

Anyone can join a public blockchain. Not only that, anyone can read as well as help to maintain the shared ledger without needing permission from anyone. Usually, the only barrier to entry is the ability to acquire the necessary hardware and energy resources. Thus, public blockchains are also referred to as permissionless blockchains. One critical feature of public blockchains is that participants remain anonymous (or more precisely, pseudo-anonymous). No one is not compelled to disclose their real-world identity. Indeed, pseudo-anonymity is considered a selling point of public blockchains. Arguably, it is an essential feature and the only way to maintain privacy, given that anyone can access data on a public blockchain.

Bitcoin is the first public blockchain. As long as one can afford to buy the mining ASIC (Application Specific Integrated Circuit) chips or a node and power it efficiently, he or she is free to join the network without being required to disclose his or her real-world identity, whether as a miner or as a user.

When a new node joins a public blockchain, it can get up to date with the rest of the network by downloading the entire ledger from other nodes on the network. When the owner of the node decides that they no longer want to be part of the network, they do not need to inform anyone of their decision to pull out.

With private blockchains, however, you cannot simply join the network. A private blockchain is organised in such a way that while it is still a peer-to-peer network like a public one, the owners of the nodes on the network know one another. Indeed, each node becomes part of the network through an elaborate process.

Often the nodes on a private blockchain network are there through a contract entered into in the real world (think paper and pen). If you want to join the network, you need to persuade the appointed administrator or all the stakeholders and go through a negotiation process. If the stakeholders feel that you do not bring any value to the network or meet their requirements, they can decline your application. Thus, private blockchains are referred to as permissioned blockchains.

There is an important point to make at this juncture: a private blockchain can be open or closed. It is open if the members of the public can access and read or use the data and applications on the blockchain. It is closed if only those with permission can access, read, or use the applications on the blockchain. Thus, a blockchain may be private, but the applications on top of it are accessible to everyone.

Another important point to keep in mind is the owners of the nodes use their real-world identities on private blockchains. Anonymous players are not accepted. Everyone is known to everyone. This makes private blockchains attractive to entities that have to observe stringent regulatory requirements, in particular, Know Your Customers (KYC), Anti-money laundering (AML), and anti-terrorist financing. In particular, commercial banks and insurance companies have opted to build private blockchains with knowledge of whom they are working with being the main reason.

The core software

Now, let’s look at how public and private blockchains differ in terms of the development and management of the core software.

A blockchain core software is the piece of code installed on your computer so that it can communicate and collaborate with others on the peer-to-peer network. The core software implements the rules of engagement (known as a protocol).

Public blockchain software is almost always open source, which means it can be copied without infringing any copyright. Satoshi Nakamoto released the Bitcoin code under the open-source MIT licence. This is how thousands of other blockchains have been developed; most of them are essentially copies of the Bitcoin blockchain. On the other hand, most private blockchains release their codes under more rigid, limited, or exclusive copyrights. This often means you cannot copy what is uniquely theirs without infringing on a copyright.

Even then, most private blockchains have their code developed behind the scenes. But more importantly, the development and maintenance of the private blockchain core software is undertaken by a specific and official team that is tasked with the responsibility. Stakeholders have a formal mechanism to pick and manage the team for the project. One way this is often done is that each stakeholder sends in a developer to be part of the team. Another is that the stakeholders form a consortium or a company that takes over the responsibility of building and managing the protocol. The latter is more common. The two most prominent private blockchains are managed through consortiums. In December 2015, the Linux Foundation and several major financial institutions and technology companies launched Hyperledger. Earlier that year in September, another group of financial institutions launched R3, another private blockchain consortium.

In public blockchains, it is the entire community of users that is responsible for the core software development and maintenance. In particular, the project relies on volunteer developers to provide patches on the core software and release new versions to the community. Some members of the community may hire core software developers to work on the project.

For example, several developers that have worked on the Bitcoin project over the years have been hired (or sponsored) by private companies that have built businesses on the blockchain. Examples of companies that have sponsored developers to work on the Bitcoin project include the Shanghai-based Bitcoin exchange BTCC, or BTCChina, and payment processor BitPay. It is also common for companies that build applications on top of a public blockchain to assign some of their developers to work on the core software. With Bitcoin, we have companies like Blockstream, Chaincode Labs, and Ciphrex doing precisely that.

Some not-for-profit organisations collect funds from the community to hire developers to work on the core software. Again, Bitcoin offers some examples, including Bitcoin Foundation (active in the early days) and Digital Currency Initiative by MIT Media Labs. Most of the other public blockchains have foundations that either retain part of the mined coins, receive part of the transaction fees, or raise money from the community using other means to support the development and maintenance of the core software1.

Getting the necessary resources is one thing. Changing critical parts of the public blockchain protocol is never easy, because often the entire community or at least a majority must form a consensus. Different players end up having conflicting interests. A case in point was the attempt to scale the Bitcoin protocol so that it could handle more transactions. The community had always struggled to form a consensus as to whether to increase the block size or have the community build second-layer scaling solutions such as Lightning Networks and SegWit. In 2017, the community was engaged in what is referred to the User Activated Soft Fork (UASF) Civil War, which led to the split of the network into Bitcoin and Bitcoin Cash2.

As evident from the Bitcoin’s struggle to scale, public blockchains are very slow at changing to meet new expectations. On the other hand, private blockchains have clear governance structures that make decisions about the improvement of the core software and protocol easier and faster. However, I should point out that owing to the challenges of managing the core software, several public blockchains have implemented elaborate governance structures where users vote to approve changes to the core software. The public blockchains with such an arrangement include Cardano, Dash, and EOS.

Game theory

Finally, let’s look at the third factor that differentiates public blockchains from private blockchains, which is the system’s reliance on game theory. In any system that brings together two or more entities with their own selfish interests, game theory is useful. It is more or less about giving the right incentives to actors within a system: rewarding those who play by the rules and punishing those who attempt to break them.

Most private blockchain networks adopt a centralised governance structure that can reward good actors and punish bad actors. In particular, it can banish anyone who goes against the rules agreed upon by all stakeholders. This is made easier by the fact that all players are known and no one can rejoin the network without permission.

However, often on public blockchain networks there is no one to punish or reward. Anonymous actors join and leave the network at will. Because of this, public blockchains have to rely on more robust game theory to function correctly. You can call it the automated game theory. With Bitcoin, for example, if you choose to try to break the rules, no one will stop you or banish you from the network. Rather, game theory will work against you.

More specifically, you will be spending a lot of resources on paying electricity bills and buying hardware, like power supply units and ASIC miners. But the network will ignore you and you will end up making huge losses for nothing. However, if you decide to use those resources to help the network instead, it will reward you.

Choosing between private and public blockchains

Is the public or private blockchain better?

Public and private blockchains have different strengths and weaknesses. As I have already pointed out, it is easier and faster to change a private blockchain protocol, making such blockchains more adaptive. Private blockchains are also great for entities that need to observe stringent regulatory requirements. For example, commercial banks have found it hard to use the Bitcoin blockchain because players on the platform remain anonymous. Thus, private blockchains allow them to meet necessary regulatory requirements.

However, public blockchains provide guaranteed permanence and immutability. You are almost always sure that rules are not going to change abruptly. Public blockchains are great for applications that are good for society. But, for whatever reasons, governments and entities that benefit from the status quo will not allow such applications to flourish if they have access to a switch to turn off. In short, both types of blockchain have excellent use cases.

What’s So Smart About Smart Contracts?

The concept of smart contracts is the building block of the digital market. Nick Szabo arrived at this conclusion in the mid-1990s, more than a decade before Satoshi Nakamoto designed the Bitcoin blockchain1. Nick Szabo is a computer scientist, cryptographer, and a legal expert who is one of the people suspected to be Satoshi Nakamoto. This suspicion only grows when you explore his known works or read the academic literature that he has written, including those on smart contracts.

The killer app of blockchain, or are they?

Nevertheless, many within the blockchain space believe that smart contracts could turn out to be the killer application for the technology. The one that could finally make the blockchain technology truly mainstream. That said, you probably first heard about smart contracts because of its association with blockchain. However, you must know that this is an idea discussed long before blockchain or cryptocurrencies came into being. Nevertheless, smart contracts and blockchain are turning out to be very intertwined. Indeed, there is reason to believe the former might end up providing the perfect use cases of the latter, and vice versa.

The concept of smart contracts has been implemented in one form or another for decades. This has mostly happened in stand-alone or centralised server systems. However, the first place where the idea is explicitly discussed is in the academic article titled Smart Contracts: Building Blocks for Digital Markets that Nick Szabo published in 19961. This is a document that anyone who has an interest in this technology or who wants to understand how it works, especially from a technical standpoint, should find the time to read.

The central theme of the paper is a proposal on how to apply the legal principles that have guided the offline contractual interactions in the digital space. Nick Szabo asks, “What parts of our hard-won legal tradition will still be valuable in the cyberspace era? What is the best way to apply these common law principles to the design of our online relationships?”

His solution? Smart contracts, which he defines as “a set of promises, specified in digital form, including protocols within which the parties perform on these promises.” He further explains that contractual clauses would be embedded in the computer hardware and software in a way that makes it difficult or expensive to breach the contract. Interestingly, Nick Szabo describes how public-private key cryptography, the primary component of the blockchain, would be central to making really efficient smart contract applications possible.

Fast forward to 2013, as Bitcoin was getting noticed by the public, the corporate world, and even international governments, a young Canadian by the name of Vitalik Buterin published a white paper that proposed Ethereum, an alternative to the Bitcoin blockchain. In the paper, Buterin explained the weaknesses of the Bitcoin blockchain and how Ethereum was a better blockchain for not having them. One of the primary differences between Bitcoin and Ethereum was that the latter would support the developing, launching, and running of smart contracts. Before Ethereum came into the picture, hardly anyone linked smart contracts to blockchain technology.

But why Ethereum and not Bitcoin? The Ethereum blockchain is designed as a Turing Complete virtual machine (on which you could write programmes or contracts that can solve any reasonable computational problem). In particular, it supports programming languages that are either mainstream or easy to use. Today, Ethereum is no longer the only blockchain that supports smart contracts. The alternatives include EOS and Cardano. Even the Bitcoin blockchain has acquired the capability to support smart contracts through RSK, a platform layer built on top of it.

What exactly is a smart contract?

Many definitions exist for the smart contract application. It is simply an agreement written in a programming language so that a computer can execute it automatically. Instead of writing a contract in plain English for counterparties and arbitrators such as judicial officers to interpret, the contract is written in the language of the computers so that machines can not only read and understand it but also execute it. The difference between a contract on a piece of paper and on software code is more than how they are written: the paper contract is enforced externally (away from the paper), while the contract written in code self-enforces.

Smart contracts already exist in the real world

Even without the blockchain, many smart contracts have been around us for decades. Nick Szabo, in his paper, gives the example of a vending machine1. An inbuilt protocol guides the transaction when you want to buy a can of soda from it; the action of inserting a coin into the machine triggers a chain of automatic events to deliver the can of soda to you. This chain of events is executed by a programme installed in the computer.

Perhaps a more relatable example is signing a subscription to something like an online magazine or a software solution. To some extent, this also happens when you set up a standing order with your bank to settle recurring bills such as rent. In each of these cases, a computer is instructed to make a particular regular action (e.g., make a payment) when a specific condition is met (e.g., a date in the month) with little human intervention. The computer programme has to follow the classical logic of “If-Then”: if it is the third day of the month, (then) pay the subscription to the magazine, pay the software as a service fee, or pay rent to the landlord’s account, and so on.

The point here is that smart contracts can be deployed, launched, and run on centralised server systems, and it has happened many times. It is true that blockchain does not have to exist for smart contracts to be in use. However, there are critical qualities that blockchain technology offers a smart contract, which makes it more potent.

Overall, blockchain gives smart contracts the capacity to handle agreements with more significant implications such as selling real estate. In particular, the technology gives smart contracts immutability and enforces it with a finality. After all, it is undesirable that a counterparty, trusted third party, or an unauthorised outsider can change the details or the execution process of the contract.

On centralised server systems, an administrator or a hacker can access the contract easily while it is active and make drastic changes. Its power or control is given to one party, who can change the rules at their fancy. In contrast, once a smart contract is live on the blockchain, no one can change its details or the course of execution. The smart contract has to run its full course. Furthermore, the existence of the contract does not depend on the availability of a particular server or computer that someone could unplug and render the contract unperformable. On the blockchain, smart contracts are sovereign. They have a life of their own that no one has the power to control.

Blockchain also provides smart contracts that are visible and executable by all participants. Such smart contract are performed on a public ledger. All the counterparties can observe every detail of the execution, making verifiability possible as the shared ledger is a shared truth for all counterparties. The blockchain leaves a clear and provable path for every element recorded or executed on top of it.

Smart contracts have a high level of privacy as counterparties do not need to involve a trusted third party in their transaction. More importantly, thanks to high-level cryptography, they do not have to expose their real-world identities. Neither does the blockchain network need this information to verify that the smart contract is executing as intended.

Challenges of using smart contracts

Like any other technology, the implementation of smart contracts, especially on the blockchain, faces several challenges. One challenge is the legal hurdle. Smart contracts have to be recognised before existing laws to be truly enforceable. Within the existing laws now, it is easy for one counterparty to ignore the performance of a smart contract and seek a desirable enforcement from a court of the law. Many points can make the smart contract pronounced null and void. For example, in some countries, the law expressly requires the involvement of a third party like a notary or a commissioner of oaths for the contract to be considered valid. In others, a signature on a physical piece of paper is a significant component of a contract.

Another challenge is having the most knowledgeable and skilled people writing the contracts. As it is now, the majority of the most proficient programmers are not lawyers, and on the other hand, most lawyers are not programmers. If smart contracts are to be trusted with the execution of very critical agreements, they have to be programmed by people who should not make mistakes with interpreting the law and also cannot produce a smart contract riddled with bugs.

It is also true that most people want or need to read a contract before it is activated. Many would not be comfortable having a smart contract run on the blockchain before they know every detail of it and, in particular, how it affects their interests. Unfortunately, however, the average person cannot read and understand instructions written in a computer language.

Another major challenge is overcoming errors and bugs in the code, which could break the execution or serve as a loophole that others can take advantage of. As we saw in Chapter 4, one dramatic example was when the first major decentralised anonymous organisation (DAO), a virtual entity on the blockchain, failed to take off because its smart contract code had a bug. A hacker exploited it and attempted to steal close to US$60 million.

Last but not least, smart contracts might fail to sort out any ambiguity in contracts. Very long contracts often have ambiguous statements, which tend to occur, not as a result of intent, but by accidental constructions. People, rather than computers, interpret such ambiguities better. The counterparties can clarify the situation and perform the contract according to the agreed-upon intent. If there is a dispute, they put the contract through an arbitration process. But nothing can be done once a smart contract is live on the blockchain. If it adopts an unintended interpretation, the counterparties will have to live with the consequences.

Creating your own smart contract

Despite its challenges, smart contracts might just be what the Internet is waiting for. Systems like the IoT will benefit a lot from smart sovereign contracts as smart devices across the vast Internet can enter into contractual terms and can perform automatically.

Smart contracts are smart because they can execute contractual clauses without human intervention. It could indeed improve the application of other technologies, and also stand to benefit from them. For example, it could be even smarter by leveraging AI.

Some very complex smart contracts can be designed to receive real-time data from designated online oracles. It needs to be able to not only process the data but also decide if it is appropriate.

If you need to write a smart contract, the first step is to learn a programming language used for the purpose. Most often, it is Solidity, which is used on Ethereum, RSK, and other smart contract blockchain platforms. The second step is to install the appropriate client software on your local device and learn to navigate it.

Being informed, knowledgeable, and skilled in smart contracts is probably an excellent decision. It might turn out to be one of the applications on the blockchain that will have an immense impact on the function of society.

Should My Business Use Blockchain

Almost everyone you meet claims that blockchain is the future. There is a lot of talk about how the technology is going to change business like how the Internet has from capital markets to the music business. And because of all this, you are probably wondering whether you are doing enough so that your business does not fall behind.

Arguably, you should always be proactive and quick to leverage any new technology that comes along. Nevertheless, the first step should always consider if it gives your business an actual advantage. This often is in the form of cutting both original and operational costs, improving efficiency in processes and systems, securing value, and giving your customers an even better user experience. Consider if it also offers you an opportunity to create new products that your target market would find valuable or useful.

The biggest mistake you could make is to adopt a new technology just for the sake of it. Or because everyone else is embracing it and it seems cool to do so too. Instead, adopt a technology because your detailed research, audit, and analysis have convinced you that it would add real value to your business. This is the best way to proceed in business. And it applies to blockchain too.

Does your business actually need blockchain?

It would be hard for me to give a yes or a no given that I know nothing about your business. What I will do is to help you arrive at the appropriate conclusion. And the best place to begin is to figure out precisely what the blockchain technology offers. By knowing what I call the “values” of blockchain, you can determine whether it will help your business move forward.

So what are the things that blockchain offers, and how can they be helpful to a business?

What blockchain provides to your business

The list of values that blockchain provides is not very long. This is a good thing because it becomes easier to decide whether you need the technology to form part of your systems. The technology might be useful to your business, but just not yet. Perhaps you might have to wait for it to evolve a little more before you find an appropriate use in your business. It is also important to point out the applications that other businesses and groups run on top of the blockchain can be useful to your business in more nuanced ways than the raw blockchain itself. Thus, it is good to be on the lookout.

There are five primary values that the blockchain can offer your business: (1) decentralisation, (2) immutability, (3) tokens, (4) privacy, and (5) smart contracts.

1. Decentralisation

The first value that blockchain offers businesses is decentralisation. This removes single points of weaknesses or failures in data management and in the hosting and running of applications. As a result, it gives more security and stability to systems and processes. In 2009 when blockchain was first revealed through Bitcoin, almost everyone thought it would be great for payment solutions only. However, it slowly became apparent, especially after the launch of Ethereum in July 2015, that the technology could power a wide range of applications. Indeed, as it is a virtual machine on top of a peer-to-peer network, it could replace the centralised server backend.

The fact that the blockchain is a virtual machine on a peer-to-peer network makes it almost impossible to disable. There is neither a switch to turn off nor a central node to attack and bring down the entire network. Any business relying on conventional backend servers is always exposed to the possibility that the backend may be compromised or even taken offline. This could affect its operations severely even if the backend is offline for just a few minutes. While a business could have backups to minimise any effects, the experience to the end-user is often not the same until the primary servers are back online. Also, in a centralised system, an attacker only has to concentrate on a single or a few points.

The blockchain significantly changes how things work. As long as the Internet exists, the blockchain will always be online. Moreover, the blockchain backend provides more security and stability to applications and business data. There is no worry about possible downtimes caused by a technical hitch in a server or a compromise by hackers, which removes backup costs. You also do not have to worry about a third party compromising your systems such that you are unable to serve your customers effectively. The technology at least makes it a little harder to sustain attacks such as the denial of service, system takeover, and data corruption.

2. Immutability

The second value that the blockchain offers businesses is the immutability of digital records. Once content is created, no one, not even a system administrator, can change it. Before Satoshi Nakamoto released Bitcoin, there was hardly a sure way of making digital data frozen once it was created. The only way that a business could preserve the integrity of digital data was to ensure that access to it, especially to its editing mode, was jealously protected. Keeping out those who want to change records for personal gain, sabotage, or even for fun has always been a very expensive affair. According to a report by insurance company Hiscox, businesses around the world lose on average US$200,000 to Internet insecurity1.

It is very costly to protect the integrity of data by keeping out anyone who may have reason to change or delete it. What blockchain does is to burn data onto a shared ledger. This makes it near impossible to arbitrarily undo, modify, or delete a record as a consensus has to be achieved first.

Furthermore, the network ignores any action that defies its rules. In other sections of this book, I have explained how the concepts of mining and game theory help to preserve the integrity of the blockchain system. Of course, it could be argued that mining is as costly as protecting data in centralised server systems. Indeed, mining can be very expensive. For example, the Bitcoin network consumes as much electricity as a sizable country like Switzerland uses. However, the cost of maintaining the blockchain does not increase with the number of users or applications. Rather, the cost could become negligible with more users and applications on the blockchain. Also, there are new blockchain designs with consensus protocols that do not require enormous amounts of electricity. For example, blockchains on the Proof of Stake (PoS) protocol such as Tezos, NEO, and DASH use a negligible amount of electricity.

3. Tokens

The third value that blockchain offers businesses is the ability to create sovereign digital assets. These are digital assets that exist on the Internet independently of your business and are valuable assets with a life of their own. These assets include currencies, securities, and tokens, which we discussed in Chapter 3. While your small business might have no reason to create a digital currency, it might have a use for tokens and securities. Tokens created on the blockchain could be useful to a business in many ways. They can be used in a loyalty points system, for example, or to tokenise realworld assets. For instance, if you are a real estate agent, you could tokenise the property you control and make it easy to track, transact, and manage. You can also use tokens as an incentive in the game theory of your online system to reward specific actions.

Perhaps we are most familiar with the use of tokens as a tool for raising capital through ICO and Initial Exchange Offerings (IEO). In this case, you create a token on the blockchain and sell it to the public as a means of getting money to fund specific projects. When these tokens are sold to raise capital, they are easily reclassified as securities, which then calls for the issuer to meet existing regulatory requirements for issuing equity shares. Nevertheless, the blockchain makes it easier to tokenise capital technologically.

4. Privacy

The fourth value that the blockchain technology offers is for parties to securely and effectively transact online without having to disclose much of their personal information. Earlier I explained the concepts of anonymity and pseudo-anonymity that exist on the blockchain. If you need to assure customers of their privacy while transacting online with your business, then the blockchain is a technology worth exploring. It has the necessary tools that make it possible to offer your customers the highest level of privacy. It can be argued that privacy is the primary selling point of the blockchain technology. Indeed, most people choosing to use blockchain applications, in particular, payment solutions, do so primarily because of the privacy that they believe the technology gives them. Often, they sacrifice smooth user experience and fast transactions for confidentiality.

5. Smart contracts

The fifth value that the blockchain offers is smart contracts. The application allows two or more parties to write an agreement in code and have it self execute when predefined conditions are met. Since the blockchain is immutable, once the contract is launched, no party can change it. It has to run to its conclusion. Besides the immutability of records, what makes blockchain the most suitable technology for hosting smart contracts, is the native currency aspect. It is true that most contracts involve money. Using conventional payment methods to settle a self-executing smart contract is a challenge mainly because the payments can be reversed, cancelled, or frozen. Entering into a smart contract on the blockchain that uses a blockchain native currency assures all counterparties that every part of the agreement can be performed. Some have described the smart contract as the most revolutionary application of the blockchain.

Customising your blockchain

We have gone through some possible values of the blockchain for your business. Each value is customisable in many ways. Using the blockchain in your business will involve delicate balancing acts. You may have to sacrifice some things for others. For example, speed for privacy, security, and immutability.

Moreover, certain things assumed to be offered by the blockchain turn out not to be so. One of these is efficiency. Compared to centralised systems, blockchains are often less efficient. In many cases, users have had to trade efficiency, convenience, and great user experience for things like security and privacy. Before jumping onto the blockchain bandwagon, you need to ask which value you seek, and whether you can get it elsewhere without such sacrifices. With that said, future faster blockchains with far better user experience are being developed.

It is important to emphasise the need to customise the technology for your business. As with the Internet, how one business uses it is often quite different from another, customising it to fit its specific needs and requirements. You should think of blockchain in the same way.

Taking the Leap to Blockchain

You can design and build your own blockchain for your business. However, before embarking on this journey, consider the following three questions carefully: (1) do you really need a new blockchain?, (2) do you have the capacity to design and build a new blockchain?, (3) can you bring together and inspire a community to support and maintain it so that in the long term, it is a genuine blockchain?

If any answer to these questions is a “no”, then it is simple: do not proceed with designing and building a blockchain for your business, because you are highly likely to fail in the endeavour. Besides, you are putting yourself in a situation where you will accumulate costs that could probably never be recouped.

To help you decide whether at the end of it all you should even consider designing and building a blockchain, let’s go through these three critical questions in detail.

Do you need your own blockchain?

The fact that your business needs a blockchain solution in its systems and processes does not directly translate to having to design and build it yourself. In fact, the majority of businesses do not, and will never, need to build any blockchain to benefit from the technology. We can think of a business building a new blockchain for its needs in the same vein as attempting to build the Internet from scratch to, for example, support an e-commerce site or run a sales management application. It does not make sense.

It takes a lot of resources to build a new blockchain. I don’t just mean money, but time and expertise too. Some might even say blockchain is its own specialisation within the programming community. It is unlikely that a business owner will learn how to write a blockchain to save costs. He or she would have the big picture in mind when it comes to his business and may read up on it to know how to communicate with the developers.

That stated, it is still very early days for blockchain. Therefore, many businesses will find that they have to build some kind of infrastructure from scratch as nobody else has built the kind of foundation they need. With time, however, there will be less need to build the basic blockchain infrastructures. After all, they are mostly technological “common areas”, where an infinite number of businesses can use a single blockchain.

How to access blockchain for business?

It is essential to recognise that blockchain solutions are accessible to businesses on three basic levels.

Using existing applications

On the topmost level, a business gets services from existing applications on existing blockchains. On this level, a business does not build anything. Instead, in many cases all it has to do is plug in and start using a blockchain solution. Many companies are now designing and building enterprise blockchain solutions in the form of dApps, that other businesses can tap into and use often at a fee.

For example, if you want to accept Bitcoins or other cryptocurrencies as payment for your goods and services, all you need to do is to pick a crypto payment processor. Most would have all the tools that your business needs for that purpose. Or if you need to store files on the blockchain cloud, you may consider signing up on a service like Storj, Sia, or Filecoin—or any of the Software as a Services (SaaS) available on the blockchain.

Creating a dApp

The second level on which a business can access blockchain services is through designing and building its own dApp on existing blockchains. This is when no Blockchain application can provide the kind of service it needs. For example, if you require an app to perform a process that is unique to your business, you may have to build one yourself. Are there other reasons to design and develop your own dApp, even though such a service exists? Yes, for instance if you are not so sure about exposing your internal processes or data to third parties.

You can design, build, and run an application on many of the existing blockchains like Ethereum, EOS, and Cardano. However, you need to have a high level of technical know-how to do so. It is not just plugging in and using a service: a skilled developer will need to not only design and build it, but also ensure that the application interfaces well with the blockchain on which it runs. It is vital that the application has a friendly user interface (UI) as well as user experience (UX). In other words, building this application will go through the processes and steps any typical application goes through. However, going by the trends we are witnessing within the space, it will not be long before almost anybody can build a blockchain application. A few of the newer blockchains are coming up with application templates or building blocks, which will make it easy for even those with little coding skills to build very functional as well as appealing dApps. One such blockchain is EOS, on which you can put parts together to create an application with little coding needed. The goal by the EOS core development team is to give a similar experience as building a website using WordPress1.

Building your own

Building a blockchain is the third and the lowest level. But it is also the most demanding level to engage a business. Deriving a blockchain benefit at this level should only happen if neither the application exists to give a business the needed capacity on the blockchain nor the blockchain to build and run the required app. With the growing list of blockchains and applications running on top of them, businesses are finding that whatever they need has already been built. As the saying goes, there is no need to reinvent the wheel. The primary motivation for developing new blockchains is almost never an internal need for a particular business, but primarily a desire to deliver new technology and ways of doing things to broader society.

Looking at most blockchain designers like Vitalik Buterin, Daniel Larimer, and Charles Hoskinson, they did not build Ethereum, EOS, and Cardano, respectively, because they needed a blockchain for a system or for business. For them, it was more or less a philosophical motivation. More importantly, all three of them were building something for a community of users, which includes an infinite number of large, medium, and small businesses.

Moving along.

Can you design and build a blockchain?

Let’s assume you have a compelling reason to build an entirely new blockchain. Or maybe you’ve invented a new way to implement a blockchain protocol and fix weaknesses seen in other blockchain protocols. The next question to ponder is whether you have the necessary resources to deliver it. The most critical resource in this regard is the technical know-how. If you already have the skill in-house, your mission is half achieved. If not, however, be aware that acquiring these skills is a significant challenge to surmount.

You may choose to train for the skill. Fortunately, a few establishments now offer training on blockchain development, the most popular of these entities being Blockgeeks, Hyperledger, and ConsenSys. Another option is to hire someone who has already trained or acquired the necessary experience. This seems like an easier and faster route; however, given that blockchain is a new technology, the number of developers who are good at it is not that high, and companies and startups are struggling to attract the right talent. Because of this shortage, a relatively huge budget may be needed to pay the best talents.

The third option is to engage the services of a specialised blockchain solution development company. There are several such companies, and more coming up come up every year. The best are set up by individuals who have worked on the early blockchains. It turns out that many of the first Bitcoin and Ethereum core developers have gone on to set up their own blockchain solutions companies, offering designing and building applications among other services. ConsenSys is one of these that specialise in designing and building enterprise applications that run on the Ethereum blockchain. Another company that contains a lot of blockchain development talent is Blockstream. The Canada-based company is home to some of the most respected blockchain experts, including Adam Back, Pieter Wuille, and Eric Svenson2. Another company that offers blockchain development services is Ciphrex co-founded by former Bitcoin core developer Eric Lombrozo and his father, Enrique Lombrozo.

Can you create and inspire a true blockchain community?

What makes a blockchain is the fact that independent computers on a peer-to-peer network work together, collaborate, and form a consensus on transactions on a shared ledger. The blockchain needs to inspire a community that can maintain itself in the long run. It is also about stand-alone computers on a peer-to-peer network forming a virtual machine or some form of an operating system that supports and runs different kinds of applications.

If your blockchain runs on a single machine or a private network that you own and manage, it does not qualify as a blockchain. It would be regarded as more or less similar to the centralised server system.

Therefore, after designing the protocol and writing the code, it is really important to invite others to join you in building a robust peer-to-peer network. It is not an easy process; you need to give them value that they cannot find on other blockchains so that they will commit their resources to make yours work. This could be either because your protocol is specialised for a particular use or niche or simply made of superior technology. After Satoshi Nakamoto designed and built the Bitcoin core software, he embarked on building a community to keep the project viable through online forums like the P2P Foundation and the Cypherpunk mailing list. This was an essential step.

Making a viable blockchain

A blockchain needs a community around it to be viable. Satoshi Nakamoto must have faced many difficulties convincing people to try his invention. Not least because it was something wholly new and unprecedented. You will face similar, though not quite the same, challenges. People are now spoilt for choice; at the time of writing this book, there are over 3,000 blockchains in existence. You would have to convince them why yours is better for them than any of the thousands out there.

To summarise, before setting out to build a new blockchain, you need to determine whether your business actually needs it, in particular that the existing blockchains do not have the capacity and features it needs. Also, you need to ascertain if you are able to build the blockchain and at what costs, and if it all indeed makes business sense. Then lastly, bring together and inspire a community to maintain it in such a way that it is secure and worthy of being regarded as a blockchain.

The Applications

Use Case 1:
Banking and Finance

There is little question that the most successful use case of the blockchain so far is the delivery of financial services outside traditional banking and financial systems. This is through two primary types of applications: cryptocurrency and decentralised finance, or DeFi, as they are commonly called.

The former is more pronounced at this point, but is unlikely to remain so in the coming decade, at least going by the upcoming DeFi projects. We will look at both types of blockchain applications in detail later.

Blockchain: threat or evolution?

First, this has to be stated: blockchain is the first real threat to the central banking system. However, if the industry embraces it with the right approach, the technology has the potential to change it significantly and for the better. It is apparent that the banking and finance sector can derive far more changes from the blockchain than it did from the Internet in past decades.

The Internet has changed banking and financial services in a significant way too. However, it did not even attempt to tamper with the balance of power within the ecosystem. More or less, the traditional financial institutions have retained their control on the banking system. One could even argue that the Internet has helped the big financial institutions entrench their hold further.

The Internet as a new technology has only increased efficiency and extra channels through which banks could reach their customers. It also helped them cut operational costs. Most banks have either gone slow on opening new branches or closed some of their existing branches, because they can deliver most of their services online. This has definitely had the effect of increased profit margins. According to one report by the BBC in June 2018, over 60 bank branches had been closing down every month in the United Kingdom. A spokesperson for the Royal Bank of Scotland (RBS) was quoted as saying that changing consumer habits inform this trend:

“Since 2014, the number of customers using our branches across the UK has fallen by 40 percent, and mobile transactions have increased by 73 percent over the same period. As customers continue to change the way they bank with us, we must change the way we serve them, so we are investing in our branches and re-shaping our network, replacing traditional brick and mortar branches with alternative ways to bank.”1

On its part, the blockchain does more than offer banks and other financial service providers better, cheaper, and more efficient ways to deliver their services. More than anything else, it tampers with and even shifts the balance of power within the ecosystem. In particular, it gives end users more say about how they receive financial services. These are the consumers of the bank’s product and services, for example, the everyday man on the street and his deposits or a business using the bank to complete its transactions. It can even stretch as far as wealth management for retail investors. The blockchain also reduces the barriers to entry in the financial services industry, at least in terms of technical infrastructure. Of course, anybody thinking of starting a financial services business has to meet often stringent regulatory requirements.

More importantly, the blockchain technology provides the tools for individuals and businesses to transact directly without having to go through trusted third parties. We call this peer-to-peer (P2P) transactions. For the first time, it is possible for two or more entities (or counterparties) to engage in a complex but well-structured financial transaction, including remittance, without having to involve any bank or financial institution. This is a significant shift as to how society functions in as far as money is concerned.

How crytocurrency changed the landscape

At this point, let us begin by exploring how blockchain is transforming banking and finance through cryptocurrencies. The world first learnt about blockchain through the success of its first-ever application, Bitcoin. The technology has continued to count its most successful apps among cryptocurrencies. Now we have hundreds of other cryptocurrencies, in addition to Bitcoin, including the likes of ZCash, Dash, Monero, and Litecoin. Every year, more and more new coins join the list, and will likely continue far into the future.

The traditional banking and financial system is built on fiat money, which is a currency backed by the power of the state. Through cryptocurrencies, the blockchain challenges the long-held belief that money has to be issued by the government. Indeed, human society has witnessed before periods when private currencies were in great use. For example, between 1837 and 1866 in the United States’ history is called the wildcat banking period. The term “wildcat” refers to the rampant privately issued money at the time. Banks, local governments, churches, hotels, and even small businesses issued their own currencies. This practice was put to a stop by the National Bank Act of 1863.

Is Bitcoin even money?

By 2009, when Satoshi Nakamoto launched Bitcoin, very few could imagine a financial system not based on government-issued currency. Perhaps this is why many have asked if Bitcoin should even be considered money. Expectedly, the state did not take kindly to the challenge of its power to issue money. For example, during a congressional hearing on cryptocurrency in the United States in 2018, Congressman Brad Sherman expressed the frustration the government had over the rise of cryptocurrencies. He said:

“It [Bitcoin] hurts the US government in (…) our ability to have the dollar as the chief means of international finance, which is what has underpinned our ability to impose sanctions. An awful lot of our international power comes from the fact that the dollar is the standard unit of international finance and transactions clearing through the New York Fed, and it is critical for major oil and other transactions. [Bitcoin] puts us in a position where the most significant sanctions we have on Iran, for example, would become irrelevant.”

His proposal was for the country’s legislature to pass a law that would stop cryptocurrencies and thus protect the government’s ability and power to control the financial system—maybe a 21st-century version of the National Bank Act. However, the fact that Bitcoin, as well as other cryptocurrencies, do not have central points of control makes it very hard for them to be stopped even with the threat of use of force.

At the centre of the traditional banking and financial system is the fractional reserve banking. This system allows financial institutions to hold only a small percentage (10 percent in the US) of the deposits and issue the rest as credit. One effect is that banks somehow end up creating money. For example, if you deposit $100, the bank is supposed to keep $10 and give the rest out as a loan. But often, banks do not wait for deposits. They simply approve loans that are not backed by any money. Basically, they give out digits on their ledgers, creating new currency out of thin air.

With currencies created on the blockchain, this is impossible. The units of value in circulation will always be capped by a protocol. For example, in the case of Bitcoin, that number is 21 million; if future currencies are going to be designed and created on the blockchain, the model for issuing loans has to change. This is already happening, as we will see later with DeFi applications. If one cryptocurrency becomes dominant, controlling the economy through money supply will become ineffective.

Eliminating double-spending

Another way the blockchain is transforming finance and banking through cryptocurrency is by introducing a new solution to double-spending, a problem that can occur when we spend money online. Physical cash is simple: if I give you a dollar bill or coin, I no longer have that dollar. I cannot spend it a second or third time. But on the Internet, things or files can be replicated to infinity. For example, if I send you a photo on the Internet, I remain with a copy that can be sent to another person. Likewise, you too can forward your copy that came from me and still remain with a copy to pass on if you choose.

If money behaved online like photos, documents, or music files do, we would have a major problem at hand. Double spending could lead to excessive inflation. Money would practically cease to be money because it would lose value. Thus, for digital money to be viable, double-spending has to be taken care of somehow.

Before Bitcoin was invented, the only way to take care of this problem was to have a trusted third-party entity or authority maintain and secure a ledger of transactions. Whenever someone spends a digital unit of value, an authority has to state by updating the ledger that the person has spent it and no longer has a copy that could possibly be sent to another person. Such a trusted third party authority has typically been a bank, financial institution, or payment service company.

Having a trusted third party like a bank or a company like PayPal, Venmo, or Skrill maintain a ledger and prevent the double-spending problem has been sufficient to make digital currency functional. According to one report by the payment company MasterCard, many countries have already attained over 50 percent preference for digital payment methods, and the trend towards cashless societies is strong. Some countries, including Hong Kong and Sweden, are approaching a 90-percent cashless society. The bulk of digital payments in these societies are done using centralised payment methods. So it appears as though the problem of double-spending is no longer an issue, and nothing further needs to be done.

However, using centralised trusted third parties to fix the problem of double-spending has its shortcomings. For one, it makes the cost of transactions relatively high as the companies have to take care of their overheads and also generate profits for their investors. The trusted third party also ends up becoming a single point of failure; an attacker has only one place to concentrate on if they want to steal data or compromise the system. Also, they have access to all private user data, and can choose to do whatever they want with it. In other words, the privacy of users is in the hands of the trusted third party. Last, but not least, the trusted third party can choose, or be forced, to censor transactions or deny some people services for reasons they deem appropriate.

Reducing the need for third parties

The blockchain makes it possible to get rid of such trusted third party entities as users can agree on the status of a shared ledger through consensus. Thus, if I send you $50, other network users can witness and record that I have spent and no longer own that $50, which now belongs to you. Because a community does the verification and confirmation, the cost is relatively lower. And because the system is decentralised, there are fewer single points of failure. Also, users have control over their own data. Because they are pseudo-anonymous on the shared ledger, no one can know their real-world identities except for the participants in the transaction. Users download the core software (or client), implementing strict rules of engagement that leave little room for mistakes. More importantly, it does not allow for discrimination and therefore, no censorship can happen.

One more critical capacity that blockchain through cryptocurrencies offers the banking and finance industry is the ability to give the cash experience online. When you give someone physical cash, there is nothing on the bill or coin to indicate where it came from or what purpose it has. This makes money even more fungible. One coin will always be treated the same with a similar coin of the same value. On the other hand, if I pay you through PayPal, for example, there will always be a connection between me, you, and that transaction. This leads to money having a traceable history, which is not always a good thing as some money can be treated as tainted. Also, the private lives and the transaction histories of those who make the payment and receive the payment are accessible to others.

Even though cryptocurrency transactions are also recorded on a ledger, everything is obfuscated so that it is junk data, unless the parties in the transaction choose to reveal themselves. In essence, this gives digital money the qualities of the physical cash.

Making micropayments possible internationally

Making micropayments possible across even international borders is another way blockchain, and in particular, cryptocurrency, is transforming banking and finance. Micropayments may seem unnecessary, especially considering the long distance, until you begin to look at the number of online services and products that could benefit from the consumer’s ability to pay small amounts of money such as pennies at a time. With most traditional financial services, you cannot send values below specific limits because the cost would exceed the value. PayPal, for example, sets the minimum transaction at $5. Xoom has it at $10 and Transfast, at $50. Instead, cryptocurrency makes it possible that even cents can be sent across the world with relatively low or even no cost. That opens up the possibility of businesses accepting micropayments for particular services. For example, instead of bulky monthly subscriptions, online magazines can allow the reader to pay for a single article using a micropayment solution.

Transactions can be completed at even greater speeds. While the Internet has made the flow of information to happen at the speed of light, it still takes days and even weeks to move money from one corner of the globe to another even when the money is in the digital form. Through cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin, the blockchain has proved that money can move around the world at the same speed as information.

It is essential to point out though that the movement of cryptocurrencies has been slowed down due to other reasons. Over time the experience of sending cryptocurrency is becoming almost the same as sending fiat. This is less to do with technological capacity and more to do with regulatory requirements. It should not be confused with the ability to process a high number of transactions, which has been Bitcoin’s scalability journey.

That said, cryptocurrency transactions have slowed for other reasons too. For the longest time, the primary use of cryptocurrencies, in addition to speculation, was to move money from one corner of the globe to another. There remain very few places where you can pay for goods and services in cryptocurrency. You almost always have to convert your cryptocurrency to fiat, where most delays and bottlenecks occur. As more exchanges are forced to meet stringent regulations, using cryptocurrency is becoming slower and more expensive.

Nevertheless, cryptocurrencies are providing an option outside of the banking system—even if people cannot spend cryptos to pay for goods and services at local businesses. That will happen slowly and, over time, be something normal. Meanwhile, banks will have a worthy competitor that they not only worry about but also learn from. Indeed, many banks, central banks, and other financial institutions are already seeking to use cryptocurrencies to improve their service delivery. They realise that they must plan for the future where people do not have to go through them when sending one another money, the distance notwithstanding. But more on that later. We need to look at the second way blockchain is transforming banking and financial services.

Moving money from point A to B is not the only service that banks and most other financial institutions offer. It is often neither the primary product nor the main source of revenue for them. It is also not their source of influence on the economy. If you look at the annual financial reports of most major banks and financial institutions, interest from loans and other forms of credit are at the top of the list of sources of their revenue, as well as power, in the financial sector. Also, high on the list are ATM fees, overdraft fees, and other types of fees. Loans and related services are their main products. Thus, even if blockchain were to take over the entire remittance business, traditional financial institutions would feel little impact.

Decentralised finance applications

However, the blockchain is finding usefulness in other aspects of banking and finance. This is where decentralised finance (DeFi) applications come in.2 These are special dApps that provide other banking services, including credit, currency exchange, insurance, savings, and even portfolio management. Unlike conventional banks and financial institutions, DeFi services are offered in the peer-to-peer arrangement; one person can give another credit without going through a trusted third party. After all, banks do not actually give credit from their own finances but from what is collected from customers as deposits into savings account. Indeed, much of what banks do is to facilitate the movement of value between the borrowers and savers. Of course, they also provide security and assurance with regard to repayment. But then they take an interest and share part of it with the depositors.

Blockchain removes the need for facilitation by a third party, whether a bank, an insurance company, or a portfolio managing entity. It provides the tools that make it possible for two individuals to extend a variety of banking services to one another. It is even possible for corporations to get credit from the public.

The smart contract application

The primary tool that makes peer-to-peer DeFi transactions possible is the smart contract application. If you recall from Chapter 6, the smart contract allows for drafting and enforcement of agreements through computer programs. It auto executes when the terms and any pre-agreed conditions are met. This single application makes it possible to trust giving a stranger a loan that will be repaid as stipulated in the agreement. For example, as security for the loan, the smart contract could include an asset such as a land title or digital tokens. In the event the borrower fails to pay by the agreed time, the smart contract automatically transfers the asset title ownership offered as security to the lender.

In addition to supporting the smart contracts for peer-to-peer financial services, the blockchain also makes it possible that people can prove their identity as well as reveal the reputation they have accumulated without them being able to manipulate the records.

Oasis, an application that is part of MakerDAO (a dApp on the Ethereum blockchain), is an example of a DeFi application that is already built and working. On Oasis, one can borrow money by using digital assets as collateral, trade with other peers, and also save. Savings will earn you interest as the money is loaned to others who pay back with interest. When taking a loan, one has to lock in the collateral using a smart contract application. MakerDAO is basically a platform that is maintained by a community of users through an elaborate governance structure. Indeed, DAO stands for a decentralised anonymous organisation.

The currency exchange

Another financial service offered through DeFi is the currency exchange, which is another primary service provided by banks and financial institutions. In the last few years, decentralised exchanges (DEXs) have become a reality. These are platforms powered by the blockchain and smart contracts, in particular, where you can sell one currency for another in a peer-to-peer arrangement. One DeFi application that offers currency exchange service is the Unisawap, which runs on top of the Ethereum blockchain too.

Other DeFi applications that are attracting attention include the market prediction platform Augur, decentralised insurance protocol Etherisc, and P2P lending platform CoinLoan. It is still too early to tell if these DeFi applications will grow to become really successful. However, they are a representation of the future.

For both cryptocurrencies and DeFi applications, perhaps significantly reducing the cost of financial services is one of biggest impacts of blockchain technology on banking and finance. This is very important, especially in regard to reaching the global unbanked population. According to World Bank estimates, over two billion people around the world have no bank account, excluding them from economic activities, which has dire consequences for their wellbeing.

It is important to point out that while such a big number of the global population does not have access to financial services, almost 90 percent of the global adult population has access to mobile phones. These devices can serve as a financial services point of sale. This coupled with the blockchain reducing the cost of offering financial services makes it possible to reach the majority of the global unbanked.

What are banks doing about the rise of blockchain technology?

The blockchain technology is a threat to traditional banks and financial institutions. But it is also an opportunity become better. Indeed, banks seem to see this latter reality and are responding in their own best interests. One way they are doing this is by investing in being a part of the blockchain evolution. Mainstream banks and financial institutions have even given the technology their own name: distributed ledger technology (DLT). Others have opted to undertake individual blockchain projects. For example, Barclays, JP Morgan Chase, and HSBC are just some of the major banks investing in blockchain startups.

Some have opted to be part of consortiums that are designing and building the technology for the industry. As mentioned in Chapter 5, one of the two most prominent consortiums is the Linux Foundation-led Hyperledger. The consortium released its first blockchain protocol, dubbed Fabric, in June 2016.3 The other consortium is R3, which released their first version of the protocol known as Corda in November 2016.

By embracing the technology, banks and other financial institutions hope to use it in different ways. At the top of the list is to increase collaborations among themselves and streamline interbank transactions. The technology has the potential to reduce costs, shorten waiting time for customers, and minimise fraud through more robust Know Your Customer (KYC) mechanisms.

It is almost naive to think that the blockchain technology will lead to the demise of banks. It is not possible, if we consider the role of regulation in how technology and businesses evolve. Instead, we should expect a new reality in which banks and other financial institutions use technology to strengthen their systems. And we should also expect a future where end users have more choice, and perhaps stand to get a better user experience.

Use Case 2:
Supply Chains

The blockchain technology offers four primary advantages to supply chains: (1) improved efficiency and reduced operations costs, (2) simplified compliance and quality certifications, (3) reliable provenance for end consumers, (4) and tools for making trade finance faster, secure, and cheaper.

However, first, let’s understand what a supply chain is and how it affects enterprise, consumerism, and even regulatory compliance. It is ironic that the majority of people are unaware of the complexity and critical role of supply chains, and how, as the foundation of enterprise, they affect almost every aspect of our lives. They literally facilitate the world’s functions; without them, things grind to a halt, which can have very adverse effects on society. People could go without basic supplies such as food, water, transport, electricity, medication, and almost everything else that makes life comfortable, or even possible.

At the time of writing (in 2020), we are witnessing this most vividly with the Covid-19 pandemic. Arguably, the core of issues from the pandemic is the freeze of logistics. If planes are not flying, goods are not moving. Blockchain cannot help with that, but it can impart transparency to the “queues” for logistics, which will serve as a form of accountability to the consumer.

Supply chains influence how much we spend on goods and services. According to the Council of Supply Chain Management Professionals (CSCMP), a global body of domain experts, up to 60 percent of the products’ cost comes from the nature of the supply chain they use. Efficient supply chains result in less costly goods and services, while less efficient ones often translate to consumers having to spend more on their supplies. This is a primary reason to improve the efficiency of supply chains. In addition to a better user experience for everyone, we also get to make things cheaper in the long term. Using blockchain technology is one way to do that1.

What exactly is a supply chain?

A supply chain is the interlinked activities, processes, and transactions that must occur for a product to get to the end consumer. Or everything between the point of raw material extraction and the delivery of the final product to the end consumer.

Supply chains exist in three primary sections or episodes: there is the supply chain (1) before manufacturing, (2) during manufacturing, and (3) after manufacturing up until the final product reaches the end consumer.

The supply chain before manufacturing would typically be activities and processes like farming, animal husbandry, and mining. It is important to point out that often where one supply chain starts is where another ends. Using the example of bread, it may seem that the supply chain starts at the farm where the wheat is grown. However, the wheat farm is also a consumer in itself, and thus have other supply chains ending at it. For example, the farm has to acquire machinery, seeds, fertiliser, herbicides, pesticides and other inputs necessary for crop (wheat) farming.

The starting point of a supply chain is often not a single point but multiple. For example, the bread supply chain does not begin at the wheat farm alone. It also begins at the dairy and chicken farms, at the sugarcane plantation, and perhaps also at the laboratory for food colouring. These “other” starting points of a supply chain provide critical ingredients for baking bread. We should not forget the supply chain of the machines and tools used for transporting and preparing the ingredients either, as well as the supply chain for the bread packaging.

At the manufacturing stage, the raw materials are turned into the final product. Using the example of wheat again, wheat and other ingredients are turned into bread. The entire process might happen at the same place or be carried out in smaller tasks at different locations or even by different businesses. For example, weighing, measurement, and mixing of ingredients for bread making can happen in one location while the actual baking is in another.

Finally, the after manufacturing supply chain involves all the activities and processes to bring the finished product to the consumers. Bread is transported from the bakery to the warehouse and then to the retail shop near the consumer. This includes marketing to create awareness for the product.

Activities between these three sections such as transport and warehousing are also part of the larger supply chain. So are other things like labour, insurance, electricity, security, and customer support. Furthermore, customers may be unhappy with the goods delivered. It may be damaged at the factory or while in transit, or not what they ordered and no one notices until it reaches the end customer. A good supply chain has to create a reliable channel through which the consumer can return the product to the retailer, and even back to the manufacturer.

From a business perspective, a supply chain is the channels and means of bringing in raw materials or stock, the process of value addition, and the tasks involved to get the final product to the customer. For a business to be successful, it has to manage its own supply chain effectively. This includes sourcing the best raw material or stock at the most competitive prices, hiring the most appropriate talent, and getting the right equipment and services. It also includes setting up the best standards and quality assurance processes. The supply chain also creates a working sales funnel, which includes the methods of delivering the products to the end consumer. Last but not least, a great supply chain system should help a business plan appropriately. For example, by determining when to buy new supplies which will not result in idle stock that accumulate storage costs.

How blockchain is changing the supply chain

As you can see, supply chains are very complex, which creates a real challenge to make them work for a business or a consumer. There are so many decisions to consider. In fact, a product can go through an infinite number of possible supply, which is not a good thing.

It also turns out that businesses usually do not have a direct control over the entire supply chain of its products. For example, a bakery can directly control from whom it buys ingredients and how it makes the bread. However, it cannot control how the wheat is grown or sugar processed.

A business’s only leverage is to use available information to choose the right business partners. With this, a business can tell easily how the supply chains of its partners affect its own supply chain and by extension its profitability. Indeed, freely available information offers a business a lot of powers to choose its partners.

Unfortunately, right information is not always forthcoming, because everyone wants to present their best side to the world. Sometimes this means being dishonest or leaving out critical details, especially where profits are involved. The good news is that blockchain can solve this problem to a large extent. The technology promises to let everyone to get the most reliable information from any section of the supply chain so that they can use it to make sound business decisions.

Businesses and end consumers have similar needs for making good decisions when it comes to information about supply chains. Like businesses, consumers often want to know how the supply chains of those they transact with affect them. For example, a consumer might want to know if the bread they buy from the retail store is made from organically grown ingredients or how much carbon print it has accumulated.

Regardless of whether you are a producer, a supplier, a manufacturer, a retailer or even a consumer, the supply chain must work well for you. It should deliver the right goods and services at the right time and at the best price, but also provide credible data to help with decision making.

Blockchain for a “third generation” supply chain

A supply chain that leads to a better experience for all stakeholders takes excellent management. Supply chain management is simply the process of making sure that the supply chain is efficient and serves the interests of an organisation, and even those of an end consumer, appropriately. Most organisations have someone who specifically takes care of this part of its business. This person is referred to as a supply chain manager or logistics manager. Their work involves ensuring that the organisation uses a supply chain with the least friction, which has the least cost and provides the best results or quality. As you can imagine, doing this needs access to quality information.

The blockchain is about to usher in what I call the “third generation” supply chain management systems. Before we look at what that is exactly, let’s first look at what we have had up to this point. The first generation supply chain systems were paper-based. Every record was written down or printed on paper and filed in cabinets. Meanwhile, the post office played a critical role by being the primary carrier of communication between businesses.

Even though the supply chain data was very detailed and could be very useful, its full potential was hardly realised, because it was difficult to retrieve and analyse. It existed mostly in silos—file cabinets kept away in offices. A way to integrate the information held by different stakeholders did not exist and, therefore, a broader and clearer picture of a supply chain could not be drawn. A business could only know as much about the supply chain it had no direct control over as what others were willing to share. Meanwhile, consumers were almost completely locked out from the system. They had to rely on what the retail store or the marketers told them for all their shopping decisions.

The Internet brought about the second generation of supply chain management systems. Information was digitised and, more importantly, could be remotely accessed. However, digital data on the Internet came with its own challenges. At the top of the list was the difficulty to preserve information integrity. It became even easier to falsify files. While files can be shared remotely over the Internet, the security of the servers on which they are hosted is always under threat from hackers and anyone who want to change, delete, or manipulate the information. And even though digital information can move practically at the speed of light, payment methods have remained slow, sometimes taking weeks to complete a transaction.

The blockchain can fix these weaknesses of the digital supply chain management systems, and it can do even more. Let’s look in detail at the four things the blockchain is doing to supply chain management. These are adding efficiency and cutting costs, making compliance and certification reliable, providing secure provenance, and making trade finance better than what has been for decades.

Adding efficiency and cutting costs

The blockchain can significantly cut the costs incurred in the current supply chain management systems in several ways. Buying expensive supply chain management software solutions could become unnecessary. As a peer-to-peer open source protocol managed through community consensus, the blockchain is a product does not require payment to access. The technology also removes the need to maintain or lease servers. As a common ledger shared by all stakeholders, there can be little duplication of information or tasks.

Blockchain also secures digital files shared by stakeholders, because information on the blockchain becomes immutable. Once a file is created and stored on the ledger, it cannot be changed arbitrarily by anyone, including its creator. Also, the threat of hacking to systems is little without a centralised server to attack. Instead, in its place thousands of independent computers act as a single unit to store data and execute application code. However, users have to protect their private keys for specific files. Meanwhile, the technology offers tools, in particular smart contract applications, which allow business counterparts to give limited access to each other’s files. For example, if A is doing business with B, each can open up files for the other for the duration of the transaction. Once the transaction is done, the access is stopped.

Making compliance and certification reliable

The blockchain offers superior ways to protect inspection and audit reports for compliance and quality certification purposes. As things stand today, an organisation can claim to have a certification even if it does not. Or it can falsify inspection records and fool its partners on the supply chain. A certifying or auditing authority can publish a certificate or a report on the blockchain that is accessible to everyone. Even though the certification is issued directly to the business, its details cannot be changed due to the immutability of the blockchain. Meanwhile, the audited entity has the power to determine who can view the report. On the blockchain you do not have to trust the holder of a piece of information but rather the system that generated it.

Providing secure provenance

In regard to provenance, the blockchain offers what the modern end consumers really seek. In recent years consumers have become more interested to know the details of the journey behind products. For example, they may want to know the source of energy used to manufacture the products, whether the labour was fairly remunerated, or that no child labour was involved. They may have questions for health reasons, about raw materials from the manufacturer used or whether it was grown organically. Today’s consumers want to be sure that the supply chain is not only ethical but also safe for them, others, and the environment.

However, producing goods in an ethical, healthy, and environmentally friendly can be costly. Therefore, companies may falsify information so that they continue to use less costly production processes but at the same time allay the concerns of consumers. The blockchain would not only make it easier to access information about the history of a product but also make it difficult, thanks to its immutability, to change records. Consumers can thus confirm that a product, for example, is indeed organic by going online and checking its path. The same goes for any manufacturer with similar claims of being environmentally friendly, green, having the least carbon print, etc.

Making trade finance better

Last, but not least, the blockchain offers the means to make trade finance even better than what we have today. Trade finance is the process through which two transacting businesses in different countries settle payment. The current system is slow, bulky, and costly, not least because a transaction could involve several banks, financial institutions, and other entities. This takes weeks before each of the banks can manually scrutinise the documentation and give approval. The blockchain offers smart contracts, which makes it unnecessary to have many trusted third parties in a single transaction.

Also important is the fact that native currencies on the blockchain can complete a smart contract written to execute a trade finance transaction. Thanks to this ability, blockchain significantly speeds up the transaction to a matter of hours.

Integrating blockchain with other technologies

Among other things, blockchain can help with the deployment of other technologies that are meant to provide efficiency, more capacity, and better user experience. In particular, it can support the integration of supply chains with the Internet of Things (IoT), adding smart devices to the system to help with the collection and analysis of data, as well as automation of processes. For example, a container carrying medicines could have embedded temperature readers to send data directly onto the blockchain. This would help identify the possibility of damage due to temperature conditions that are either too high or too low. Other devices could provide information about how the container is secured and the number of times it is opened. Cameras could even record openings while storing the files on the blockchain as part of the product history.

Artificial intelligence (AI) could form part of the supply chain with blockchain as a reliable backend. The blockchain offers a more secure backend to store and process AI protocol, as well as data that the system generates. Different kinds of dApps could implement AI on the blockchain. Some dApps could help predict events as accurate future market demands to alert manufacturers, suppliers, and retailers to make the necessary plans.

Now let’s look at examples of specific use cases of blockchain for supply chain management.

In early 2019, Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC), an oil company owned by the United Arab Emirates, announced a collaboration with IBM to design, build, test, and launch a supply chain on the blockchain, which would track the movement of oil from wells to refineries and finally to the consumer. It would also automate transactions, shorten shipping time, and reduce the costs of monitoring the oil supply chain. Not only that, it would provide investors access to information about the performance of the company as well as other entities along the chain. This is an important project to watch given that ADNOC produces over three million barrels of oil daily, and its success would be a major milestone that encourages other major oil producers to embrace the technology2.

Another specific supply chain blockchain use case is the Tracr by De Beers, a blockchain supply chain that tracks diamonds from the mine to the consumer. Tracking diamonds is critical given that its mining and sale is often a source of conflict, suffering, and death, especially in West Africa. Indeed, this situation inspired the Hollywood movie Blood Diamond. By setting up the Tracr system, De Beers, one of the biggest handlers of precious minerals, is seeking to lock diamonds acquired through unethical means out of its system. In May 2018, De Beers announced the successful tracking of its first 100 pieces of diamond from the mine to cutter, polisher, and to a jeweller on the Tracr blockchain supply chain management system.

The Tracr system uses uploaded photos, detailed description, unique physical properties, and location to track a piece of diamond. The end goal is to assure the customer that their money is not ending up in the hands of those causing war and suffering3.

One sector that has seen real use cases of blockchain is the art industry, which has applied the technology to protect and secure the provenance of pieces of art. Fake art is a multi-billion dollar industry and many people are often tricked into buying counterfeit or fake pieces. The blockchain technology has provided the tools to make it easy to not only track a piece of art but also for a buyer to prove that whatever they are being sold is original and not a fake. Several startups building art supply chain systems and notable names are Artory, Verisart, and Provenance.org. Dadiani Fine Art and Christie’s are some of the major art auction houses that have embraced blockchain to track and safeguard art4.

Challenges in using blockchain

As with any other technology, not everything is perfect with the application of the blockchain technology on supply chains. It has a few challenges to overcome to achieve the best results.

One of these challenges is the difficulty of safeguarding the credibility of data before or at the onramping point. We know that the blockchain protects and makes data immutable. However, the data has little protection from the technology beforehand. Erroneous, misleading, or poorly collected data could still find its way into the system. One way to correct this is to enable real-time collection of data using IoT or to set up independent data certification processes.

Businesses have to protect their data from competition. Knowing what a business competitor is doing is always very powerful. And it can facilitate one organisation driving another out of the market. For this reason, many businesses and even countries might feel apprehensive about posting their data on a public ledger. They may feel the need to protect it by not having it recorded on the blockchain. One way to fix this is to use advanced technologies such as cryptography to increase intellectual property rights. Enhancing enforcement in the legal system will also be helpful.

Getting the right talent and skill to implement the blockchain-based supply chain systems is a challenge, as it is still early days for the technology. Technical blockchain experts are relatively few, which makes it a little harder for any entity to want to build a supply chain management system on the blockchain. However, this may change with time as blockchain technology becomes mainstream and more capacity is built.

Unfriendly regulations are an issue as many countries are wary of free unchecked movement of value, especially in the form of money. While systems can be designed to complete trade finance transactions in a matter of minutes, demands of regulations in countries involved can still slow it down to hours, days, or even weeks.

Regardless, there is a lot of potential in applying the blockchain technology to improve supply chains. Going by the many startups that are building applications to support this use case, a major movement is likely in the coming years, especially with the involvement of big names such as IBM, De Beers, and Christie’s.

Use Case 3:
Government

Like most technologies, blockchain is as useful in the public sector as it is in the private sector. Governments around the world are already embracing it for different use cases. Some have set up task forces to advise them on how to proceed. Others are a little more ahead and in partnerships with the private sector in various processes to design, develop, test. Some are already implementing applications meant to change how they deliver services to citizens.

We can categorise the major impact of blockchain on government in two ways: (1) improving exist processes and systems and (2) changing the idea of government altogether. In this chapter, we look in detail at both and the possible impacts on the government.

Blockchain for existing systems and processes

We begin with how blockchain can improve existing systems and processes. This happens to be the most feasible blockchain application in the public sector, not least because it would cause less disruption and not tamper with the existing balance of power, which is very important for governments. With this stated, the technology can improve government systems and processes in the following four primary ways: (1) protect data that the government accumulates for public service delivery, (2) improve transparency and build trust in public institutions, (3) automate critical processes, and (4) cut costs. Let’s dig deeper into these four ways. For each, we look at an example of a government project somewhere in the world that is in progress or has already implemented.

Data protection

Just like in the private sector, data is critical for public service delivery and is the mainstay of governments anywhere. Through data, the government can identify citizens and their various needs. It also formulates and delivers solutions and services using data. Not only the central government needs its data protected. The same need also exists in independent public institutions or its branches such as the judiciary, the revenue (tax) service, and electoral agency. To this list, we can also add the local governments and authorities.

But why protect the data that the government collects and stores?

Both state (think foreign surveillance) and non-state actors (private citizens) can threaten government data. Everyday countries attempt to steal data from one another to gain military, economic, and diplomatic advantages. Many times it is also to sabotage one another. An excellent example of a reason one country may need to steal data from another is to influence election outcomes. The goal, in this case, is that whoever comes to power would then serve their economic, military, or global diplomatic interests.

Non-state threats to government data come from individuals and organisations that might want to use it for financial gain. Often the data that government holds needs to be protected from elements within itself who might want it for selfish interests or other unethical reasons.

The need for higher data protection has grown as governments globally digitise their systems as a way to deliver services more easily. While conveniences are gained through this digitisation process, systems and data become more exposed to threats that transcend international borders.

The threat is both to individual citizens and the government as a whole. If data about you, which the government holds, falls into the wrong hands, it can be used to steal your identity, which in turn might be used to steal your property or even commit crimes elsewhere in your name. Likewise, a takeover of government systems or theft of its data, as we have already touched on, can compromise, sabotage, or weaken it in one way or another, especially before its peers internationally.

In its annual performance report, released in January 2020, the Kenyan Judiciary department identified tampering with records as one of its most significant challenges1. According to the report, many in the East African nation beat justice by orchestrating the theft and destruction of case files and evidentiary material in the court registries and custody. Sadly, the theft and removal are done with the help of members of staff who readily accept bribes. Consequently, this situation ends up making it easier for Kenyans with financial means to have their way while the poor hardly get justice in the courts.

The Kenyan judiciary sees digitisation of court files as a solution as it would make it easy to track, and more specifically, know who accesses them at what time. However, digitisation alone cannot fix the problem effectively. Those who tamper with the hardcopy files in cabinets (the members of staff at the registry) would most likely end up having authorised access to the digital databases as they have critical responsibilities in terms of filing and maintaining the records. They could still be bribed to delete or change details in the digital files.

The blockchain offers institutions like the Kenyan Judiciary a means to make their digital files even more secure and tamperproof. It gives digital files immutability and also time-stamps them, which makes it near impossible for anyone to delete or arbitrarily make changes. The decentralised nature of the blockchain systems makes it possible to protect the data even better from external attacks. It is less susceptible to threats that target centralised points of failure. The technology also makes it easy to deploy public-private cryptography, which is ideal for public information systems. While the system makes arbitrarily changing or deleting files impossible, it provides the mechanism for a procedural addition of necessary changes in a transparent environment.

One mega public project using the blockchain to safeguard the integrity of data is Smart Dubai. The Ruler of Dubai, Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum, who is also the Vice-President and Prime Minister of the United Arab Emirates (UAE), unveiled the project in October 2013. Smart Dubai is supposed to make Dubai one of the best cities in the world to live in, making most services accessible easily and at competitive costs online. It would also make things such as healthcare, education, and city management more efficient.

To achieve the Smart Dubai dream, blockchain, though a later addition to the project, is intended to serve as the backend for other emerging technologies used in the project, such as AI, Big Data, and IoT.

Transparency and trust

How can blockchain help governments become transparent?

Transparency in governments all over the world is very much lacking. While it is the public that elects the government, the processes and systems in public institutions are never accessible to the majority of the citizenry. The broader public often does not know what goes on within government; only those high up in the hierarchy of government have access to the most critical systems and processes. Often, they are the only ones who understand how they work. Everyone else has to trust—that the policy and decision-makers as well as the implementers are doing what is expected of them. This opaqueness creates the right environment for corruption, unethical practices, and, consequently, poor service delivery.

In most countries around the world, rigged elections, theft of public funds, and cronyism are the order of the day. Speaking of elections, there is no better place to begin creating transparency than with the process through which governments are put in place. Indeed, elections are the bedrock of modern societies. However, in most countries, it is often questionable whether the will of the people is accurately captured through elections, or if it is respected. With closed voting systems, it is always possible for those in power or influence to tamper with results and therefore impose the executive, the legislature, and by extension, the judiciary on the people.

The public needs to witness what goes in the voting systems as well as other systems in government. The blockchain makes it possible for the entire voting system to be open to every voter, who would be able to verify and audit the process in real time. With the blockchain technology, voters can know that their votes have been counted and added to the final tally of the chosen candidate. At the same time, the system would not be exposed to security threats.

In 2014, the Danish libertarian party Liberal Alliance began using blockchain for its internal elections, becoming the first political outfit to use the technology for this specific purpose2. In late 2018, the Democratic Party of India built a blockchain voting system through which over three million members could vote on particular policy questions. It was made possible with the help of the Australian blockchain startup Horizon State3.

However, the most established example is in Estonia. The Northern European country is best known for adopting technology in its public institutions, with 99 percent of public services accessible online. The country first used an e-voting system in 2007, when much of the rest of the world did not even have plans for such kind of system. The Estonian government has embraced blockchain as a technology that could help make their e-voting system even more secure and robust4. It is important to point out other public projects around the world, besides electoral systems, are using blockchain to enhance transparency.

Automation

Governments around the world are on the move to increase how fast citizens can access services. That calls for automating most of the processes involved in service delivery. By looking at what is to be replaced, one can appreciate the difference a system upgrade can do to the lives of the citizens. We have used the example of land titles back in Chapter 1, which drives the point home here too. In most third and even first world countries, the process of buying and selling real estate takes not only a lot of effort but also time and money.

Usually, the first step is searching for the title in the land registry, which may involve making a formal application to the registry by filling out a form and handing it in or sending it through the postal address. This is critical to make sure that the seller owns the land according to the official records. One may need to pay a fee for this service. In many cases, if you need to cut the waiting time, you may have to pay a bribe to someone. Otherwise, you have to wait for a few days or even weeks for the feedback, because a clerk at the registry has to go through the database, often manually, or through a stack of files in cabinets to locate one with the information you need.

With an automated system, the process takes not more than a few minutes, which you can do from anywhere. You do not need to travel to the land registry office to perform your search. What’s more, it costs you nothing. Many governments around the world are in the process of automating land transaction processes so that anyone can go through the process from the comfort of home or the office. More importantly, it will take mere minutes to get proof of title ownership. In addition to this, the blockchain offers the mechanism for transacting real estate without having to visit any office. Through a private key, a seller can not only prove that they own the piece of property but also transfer it to the buyer easily and quickly, but securely. The smart contract applications on the blockchain make it possible to complete the transaction without involving third parties.

In 2016, the Republic of Georgia, through its National Agency of the Public Registry (NAPR), launched a pilot project to move the country’s land registry system onto the blockchain5. The project was made possible with the help of the Bitfury Group, one of the major and earliest Bitcoin mining companies. Within two years of the project, close to two million titles were backed up and time-stamped on the Bitcoin blockchain. The goal of the project is to restore public trust in an institution that has been riddled by corruption over many years6. Another country that is attempting to build a land registry system on top of the Blockchain is Sweden. The Swedish Lantmäteriet, the country’s land registration authority, has been working with Chromaway, a blockchain startup, to implement the project7. Besides land and property management, the blockchain is being applied in many other areas for the primary purpose of automating processes securely and reliably.

Cutting costs

Governments are basically like business organisations when it comes to managing costs, even though many of us do not usually think of them that way. Like with any prudent business, the expenditures need to be within the revenue limits. What’s more, citizens appreciate that they receive quality services without having to pay high taxes for it. In this regard, blockchain can be beneficial. The technology can help governments not only cut costs but also eliminate some items in the annual budget. In particular, the technology offers a cheaper but also better way to keep and maintain records. It is very expensive to maintain centralised server databases in terms of leasing or acquiring the necessary hardware and software. It also costs a lot more to obtain the right security solutions to ensure that only the right people have access. With blockchain, governments can use open-source protocols and systems for which they do not have to pay. In most instances, the highest cost of using blockchain is the initial cost of setting up the systems, which is primarily labor or skill related.

According to the team behind Smart Dubai, a project we looked at earlier, when it is complete, it will save the city of Dubai close to US$1.5 billion every year in document processing alone8. That is vast savings however one looks at it.

Blockchain for a new way of government

After looking at how the blockchain can improve existing government systems and processes, let’s shift gears and look at the second impact that the technology can have on public administration or governance: the possibility of changing the basic idea of government.

Blockchain can do more than just improve government processes and systems. It has the potential to usher in entirely new forms of government. It could spell the end of the representative form of government as we know it and replace it with one where citizens have more direct involvement, and in particular have a say on how things function daily. After all, the basic idea behind the legislature is that all citizens cannot necessarily meet in one place to deliberate on the issues that affect their lives. It has been impossible, of course, because of the lack of logistics to support such meetings. Therefore, we elect some from among us to represent us in the decision making within the government.

However, we have come a long way, and technology continues to make things that just a few years ago seemed impossible possible. If today you have an opportunity to travel in time to the early 20th century and tell people living then that in just under 100 years, it would be possible to do things like video conferencing, they would probably think you are talking about magic and not reality.

The blockchain provides an option for electing the legislature and the executive, and hoping that they do the right thing. It offers the mechanism through which citizens can take up most of the responsibilities of daily government functions as a collective and directly perform at least part of these. This was incomprehensible just a decade ago. This might seem unrealistic or absurd at first glance. However, after exploring what the blockchain is capable of doing, and what it has already been tested to do concerning governance structures, the idea of direct government does not appear far fetched.

The DAO model

In April 2016, Slock.it, a German blockchain startup, announced a new project known as the Decentralised Anonymous Organisation (DAO). This would be an investment organisation without a leadership structure or a team of employees to undertake its activities. Instead, the DAO would be managed through a smart contract system or protocol on the Ethereum blockchain. All decisions would be made by members through some form of a consensus mechanism achieved through direct voting9. In simple terms, the DAO was a company with shareholders but no board of directors, chief executive officer, or any other kind of employee.

The idea was so attractive, exciting, and popular that within a funding period of 28 days, it managed to raise over US$150 million from investors who would become the shareholders. Through the smart contract protocol or system, the shareholders would vote on projects to be undertaken by the organisation and also engage service providers to act on its behalf. A shareholder could propose a plan, along with the required budget, and then persuade others to approve it through the vote. Also, a service provider could suggest business ideas to the DAO and try to persuade the shareholders that they could deliver results that would benefit the organisation. Once the members approve a project, a smart contract would lock the required funds in an escrow account. With that, the service provider would proceed to work on the project. Once complete, the smart contract guiding its execution would release the payment from the escrow account.

The DAO, however, did not get to be fully implemented. In June 2016, just two months after its launch, a hacker found a bug in its primary smart contract code and attempted to exploit it and steal about US$50 million from the DAO wallet. This resulted in one of the most significant hard forks within the crypto space. Since the payout had to be completed on the Ethereum blockchain, the community faced the option or rolling back transactions and denying the hacker the funds from the DAO wallet. The community had considerable disagreement on how to proceed. Some favoured rolling back the transaction to deny the attacker the DAO funds, while others thought doing so would jeopardise the immutability promise of the blockchain, and US$50 million was not enough reason to do that.

With no consensus, the Ethereum network had to break up into two independent blockchain networks. Those who supported the transaction rollback kept the name Ethereum (ETH) for their network, while those who opposed it adopted the new name Ethereum Classic (ETC). The hacker ended up cashing out some funds in Ethereum Classic tokens. Shortly after, the DAO was shut down and the investors refunded.

Nevertheless, the idea of the DAO did not die, and a few other projects have been set up using this particular model of governance. One of the most successful projects is the DASH blockchain network, where members pick something they can do for the network and persuade the community to fund them. If the community is satisfied, it approves a budget that goes into an escrow account, and the proposer gets to work. The funds are then released to them as milestones are met. Another project on the DAO model is MakerDAO.

Likewise, a form of DAO could replace the government with all citizens becoming shareholders. The privilege to vote on projects could be acquired, for example, through the payment of taxes to a treasury wallet. Contractors, as well as citizens, could propose necessary public projects, which could then be approved and voted on by citizens who also elect the undertaking contractor. The winning contractor would have the budget put in an escrow account, and after completing the job, they get paid, all according to a smart contract that guides the execution of the project. It is important to point out that the examination, discussion, and voting could all happen through a portal or a mobile application. This would make it convenient for citizens to participate regardless of where they are located.

Of course, we cannot say that we are ready for entire governments to be turned into a DAO. Still, it is possible that parts of it can, which would perhaps improve service delivery, increase transparency, and reduce incidences of corruption. It would also enhance public participation.

Challenges in using blockchain

Now let’s shift our focus to the challenges that the blockchain technology has to overcome before the government adopts it, whether for improving existing processes and systems or for creating utterly new governance structures.

Just because the blockchain has something of value to offer does not mean that it will be automatically embraced and used. Even with all of its advantages for the delivery of public services, we might never witness it happen on a large scale due to challenges that might prove hard to overcome.

The first challenge that blockchain has to overcome is becoming more compatible with existing laws. For most processes on the blockchain to be accepted, they have to be recognisable before the law. For example, agreements executed through smart contracts can only be legally binding if the law recognises them. Otherwise, the courts can always overrule whatever the smart contract achieves. Thus, the law has to be changed for the full potential of the application to be realised.

The second obstacle is resistance from beneficiaries of the current governance systems. The government is a massive enterprise with many beneficiaries. It turns out that those that benefit the most from how the government functions are also the holders of the keys to change. For example, politicians make a living out of running for office and being representative of the people in the legislature, the executive, and the judiciary. For a change to happen from representative to direct government, they (the politicians) must change the relevant laws. However, it is expected that they will stand in the way of any change that threatens their position.

Another challenge is the lack of a large enough pool of experts to help design, build, and implement the blockchain applications for government. This has been a significant challenge since the early days, especially with large private corporations also building blockchain development teams.

Blockchain has a lot of potential to change how government works. How far we embrace it for this purpose depends on how ready we are to disrupt what we have always known about the idea of government. It also depends on whether we have the capacity and the resolution to overcome the challenges that might stand in our way.

Use Case 4:
Payments

The use of blockchain for payments is extensive and can be classified into three primary types: (1) customer to customer (C2C) payment solutions, (2) business to business (B2B) payment solutions, and (3) a combination of these, which can be B2C or even C2B. Since the last can easily be understood after going through the first two, we are going to spend time on those in this chapter.

The customer to customer (C2C) blockchain payment solutions falls into three further subtypes: cryptocurrencies, tokens, and stablecoins. However, before we turn our attention to them, we have to first go through what makes blockchain payment solutions unique.

Blockchain benefits for payments

Before the blockchain, even in the Internet era, it was not practical for a sender to get money to a receiver without a middle person, unless they were close enough for money to exchange hands physically. A middle person usually had to facilitate the transaction and understandably take a cut for their trouble (more about the cost in a moment). On the blockchain, it is technically possible to send value in the peer-to-peer path or channel, even if the sender and the receiver are on the opposite ends of the globe. In addition, the technology offers several advantages. Let’s discuss two main benefits.

Improved security for payment transactions online

First, blockchain provides improved security to payment transactions between the sender and receiver. It achieves this through the use of public-private key cryptography as well as the immutability of the ledger on which the transaction is executed. This is not to say the value on the blockchain cannot be stolen. It has happened many times. Tens of millions of dollars’ worth of cryptocurrencies are stolen annually from exchanges and individuals. However, the technology provides better tools for safeguarding payment transactions.

More privacy for payment transactions online

Secondly, as we have already learnt from previous chapters, the technology makes payment transactions online more private because it applies cryptography and pseudo-anonymity. Thus, while the transaction ledger is public, users can mask their identity on the blockchain by generating random public addresses to use instead of personally-identifying information like a real name or ID number. And since the random public address does not need to be attached to one’s real-world identity, one reading through the shared public ledger cannot tell who exactly the participants in each recorded payment transaction are.

Moreover, payment transactions executed on the blockchain are relatively faster than the other options we have, especially for sending large amounts of money. A bank wire transfer might take up to a week for money to move from one part of the world to another. On the blockchain, however, it takes seconds to minutes, the amount and distance notwithstanding.

Even though payment transactions on the blockchain are secure, private, and faster, they are relatively cheaper compared to payments made through trusted centralised third parties. With most blockchains, the business model does not put pressure on the end-users in terms of high fees, though they do have to pay fees for blockchain payments.

For example, on the Bitcoin blockchain, you often need to attach a fee to hasten the process of confirming the transaction. After all Bitcoins have been mined, the fees paid by users will become the primary incentive for miners to continue keeping their machines running and maintain the shared ledger. Thus, over time the fees will become mandatory for transactions to be verified and recorded on the shared ledger.

However, any fees paid by users on the Bitcoin blockchain are determined by a protocol, not a private business that bases such decisions on making more profits for its shareholders. Also, new protocols are being developed that will not require users to pay any fees. On the IOTA blockchain, for example, users have to do some work, which involves confirming two previous transactions, for their own transaction to be approved1.

Furthermore, with blockchain payment solutions, it is practical to make a payment an integral part of the automated settlement of an agreement through smart contracts. This means payments stipulated on a business contract or any form of agreement can be automated and made to self-execute when the predefined conditions are met—without involving a third-party enforcer.

On conventional systems, someone or some entity has to release funds attached to a contract manually. This could be a counterparty to the contract or a third party such as a bank acting on behalf of the counterparty. On the blockchain, it is easy to code the money into a contract and have a computer protocol take care of everything, including releasing payment at a time and point when it is needed. Thus, payments on the blockchain can easily be written into smart contracts. Relatedly, payments on the blockchain can support the interaction of smart devices in the growing IoT environment. After all, we are heading into a future where intelligent devices would need to pay one another using methods on the blockchain.

It is also important that blockchain can support micropayments. You can use the blockchain to make payments amounting to mere cents, regardless of the location of either the one making the payment or the one being paid. Such micropayments make services that could never have been offered using traditional payment methods possible. A good example is a browser automatically paying on behalf of a user for every single article read on a website or blog. The Brave browser is already implementing this. As a user, you can fund an inbuilt wallet with Basic Attention Tokens (BAT), and the browser will send small amounts to the sites you visit depending on the amount of time you spend on it or the number of articles and videos you view2.

Meanwhile, in the IoT environment, a smartphone can pay for Internet access to hotspots as you travel around the city throughout the day, for example. Using traditional payment methods to implement this kind of use case is near impossible because they do not support micropayments. Even if they did, the cost per transaction could be higher than the amount being paid.

Blockchain disadvantages for payments

Now, after going through some of the things that make payments on the blockchain better than other methods of payment, it is only logical to look at its disadvantages.

Perhaps one of the most significant disadvantages of payments on the blockchain is that they have not been adopted at a massive scale. The places where you can go to today, online or offline, and settle your bills using blockchain-based payment methods are still minimal. Typically, if you need to make a purchase using the payment method on the blockchain, you still have to convert to fiat currency first. The majority of payments settled on the blockchain are related to speculative trading on exchanges and trading platforms.

Another disadvantage is that payment methods on the blockchain are still not clearly defined before the law. In fact, it is illegal for a business to accept payment in the form of digital currency on the blockchain, such as in Columbia, Ecuador, and Bolivia3. In these three South American countries, the government has taken a clear legal step of banning the use of cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin as an option to fiat currency issued by the state4.

The majority of the digital currencies on the blockchain experience high volatility. While this is good for speculation, it works against a currency’s ability to be used as payments for goods and services. In particular, businesses are wary of accepting Bitcoin because they do not want to lose value to currency price movements in the market. Where the profit margin is thin, a business could sell an item or service for a good profit only to wind up making a loss due to price changes that happened just a few minutes after closing a sale. This is especially critical where a business has to convert its revenue to fiat to pay its suppliers.

The majority of businesses that accept cryptocurrencies for payments have partnered with payment processors like BitPay, which immediately convert their earnings into fiat and thus help them avoid losing value due to cryptocurrency volatility. This defeats the purpose of accepting cryptos in most cases, especially considering that a payment processor is a business that charges a fee, therefore making the transaction a little more costly.

Scalability to meet existing demand is one of the primary challenges that cryptocurrencies have had to face. For example, demand was high as the price of Bitcoin grew in late 2017 and early 2019, which increased the number of transactions that needed to be confirmed on the shared ledger. At some point, the waiting time was up to 16 hours. This inconvenienced users and completely went against the idea that Bitcoin payments are fast5.

Customer to customer (C2C) payment solutions

Now let’s look at particular payment methods for C2C that are actualised on the blockchain. As mentioned at the beginning of this chapter, the C2C blockchain payment applications come in three primary categories. Let’s go through how they work as well as their particular examples.

Cryptocurrency

Cryptocurrencies form the most developed category of blockchain payment use cases, and of all kinds of applications. Indeed, the world first discovered the cryptocurrency (Bitcoin) before learning about the blockchain technology behind it.

The term cryptocurrency is often used to describe any digital asset created and managed on the blockchain. However, its stricter meaning can guide us to determine what qualifies as a crypto. Perhaps it helps to recognise that the word “cryptocurrency” is a compound from two words: cryptography and currency. We can easily deduce that whatever carries the compound term has to be a currency that uses some form of cryptography. In other words, even though cryptocurrencies are decentralised and use cryptography as part of their underlying architecture, they are supposed to serve just like fiat money. They are supposed to be a medium of exchange, a store of value, and a unit of account.

It is not easy to tell precisely how many cryptocurrencies are out there, because they often grouped together with other blockchain assets. More importantly, the line between cryptocurrencies and other types of blockchain assets is often blurred. There are, however, a few that are clearly cryptocurrencies owing to what they were designed to do. Of course, Bitcoin is one of them as it was primarily intended to be electronic cash. ZCash, Litecoin, and Monero are others that you could easily say, strictly speaking, are cryptocurrencies.

While the use of cryptocurrencies as payment has not gone mainstream, where it really matters—being used to pay for goods and services, the number of transactions completed on the blockchain has been on a steady increase. For example, in 2010, the Bitcoin blockchain processed about 30,000 transactions per day. The number at the time of writing this book is around 500k.

As mentioned earlier, the Bitcoin blockchain, in particular, has struggled to scale. And its clogging, as well as that of Ethereum, the second largest network in terms of market capitalisation, rallied the communities to seek scalability solutions. In the case of Bitcoin, the scalability journey has been anything but smooth. Disagreements over what option to adopt has led Bitcoin to have to split into several independent networks, including Bitcoin, Bitcoin Cash, and Bitcoin Gold.

At the time of writing this book, there is a lot of optimism regarding the scaling of the capacity of significant blockchains to support even more payment solutions. In the case of Bitcoin, we are witnessing the embracing of the Lightning Network protocol, which many views as the ultimate scaling solution for the network.

The lightning network, which is also adaptable to other blockchains in particular Litecoin, makes it possible that not all payment transactions can be registered on the blockchain. Still, all can be secured by the technology. The lightning network simply allows for two or more people to create a payment channel. This is basically a smart contract application running on the blockchain, which enables them to send money back and forth for as long as they want or need. However, out of all the transactions they ever complete, only two get to be recorded on the blockchain: that which created the payment channel and which closes it and settles the net balances for all participants.

But the lightning network is not the only scaling solution being deployed to increase the capacity of the Bitcoin blockchain to support more payment transactions. The others include Sidechains, which is a protocol that allows different blockchain to interoperate, and SegWit, a protocol that makes it possible to record payment transactions on the blockchain with less data from each. For Ethereum, there is the Raiden Network and the Plasma second-layer scaling solutions, similar to Lightning Network and Sidechains, respectively.

The infrastructure for using blockchain for payment purposes has improved a great deal over time. In the early days, sending and receiving Bitcoins and other cryptocurrencies required you to install the core application on your desktop. It was very cumbersome considering that you had also to download the entire blockchain ledger, which for Bitcoin has grown to over 260GB6. It also had a terrible user interface and user experience.

Over time, developers designed the light wallet protocols, which allowed for an application on a smartphone to interface with a node on the network and serve as an end-user interface to the blockchain. The lightweight wallet protocols made it possible to interface with the blockchain without having to download it to your local device. Today, mobile and browser wallets have become the most used in making payments on blockchain, and only a minority of users still install the core desktop wallet. The mobile wallet generally has an excellent user interface, and is also convenient and fast.

The development of wallets has gone beyond software applications. You have the option of buying hardware wallets, devices specifically designed for holding cryptos, and making payments. The major brands of hardware wallets include Trezor, Keepkey, and Ledger. There are also companies building smartphones with specific capabilities for making payments using blockchain solutions. One of the startups that raised capital during the ICO craze was Sirin Labs, which has proceeded to manufacture the Finney Blockchain smartphones. Sirin Labs raised about US$157 million in December 20177.

Indeed, even the mainstream mobile brands are designing hardware compatible with blockchain. The Taiwanese consumer electronics company HTC is one of those that have released smartphones with inbuilt blockchain capabilities1. Samsung, one of the leading brands in the smartphone industry, has also taken steps to provide users and developers with more blockchain capacity in their devices, including inbuilt wallets for making payments using cryptocurrencies.

Tokens

Basically, tokens and cryptocurrencies are created the same way. The primary difference between the two is their functions, or how they are used. Cryptocurrencies are used as ordinary money to buy goods and services. Tokens, on the other hand, are used for a specific purpose. For example, the Storj token can only be used to pay for storage space on the Storj network, a decentralised cloud storage platform, and not to make payments elsewhere. The Ethereum Gas token lets you access the Ethereum virtual machine (EVM), where you can run your applications. Storj coin and Ethereum Gas represent a category of tokens known as utility tokens. Utility coins are used to pay for a specific product or service, but they are also used to provide access to particular platforms.

Another category of tokens are the security tokens (which we talked about in Chapter 3), which are explicitly created to be sold to investors as a way to raise capital. Most security tokens also end up being utility tokens. For instance, a startup can sell tokens to raise funds, but those tokens even end up serving a specific function in their internal system or network. In particular, the tokens can be used to pay for a service, access, or other forms of products.

Many startups that want to release tokens to the market are careful to package them as utility tokens, because regulators globally are coming down very hard on tokens that are sold to raise capital. The general view is that any startup selling them should follow the stringent requirements for issuing capital share. Indeed, it was the crackdown on the sale of tokens to raise capital that slowed down the ICO craze of 2017 and 2018. In that two years, startups had raised a total of more than US$30 billion. Some like Telegram managed to raise as much as US$1.5 billion.

With that stated, tokens are a potential payment method on the blockchain, especially within specific networks and platforms. Tokens on the blockchain can easily take the place of traditional loyalty points. Not to mention that they are very easy to create using smart contract applications on the Ethereum blockchain. You could take about ten minutes from reading this book and create your own tokens on a platform like the Waves blockchain. First, though, you would need to visit the Waves platform to download and install the core client on your computer. After creating an account, you create your tokens easily and quickly by choosing your specifications8.

Stablecoins

Finally, let’s look at stablecoins, which were created as a response to the wild volatility of the blockchain assets. By now we realise that the price of most cryptocurrencies is definitely wild, especially when paired against fiat currencies such as the US dollar. If you hold most blockchain assets, you should be prepared to gain or lose a lot of value within a matter of minutes. Stablecoins are digital assets on the blockchain that are pegged on more stable assets in a ratio of 1:1.

As the name suggests, it is supposed to make them less volatile or more stable, especially when paired with fiat currencies.

Most stablecoins in the market at the moment are pegged against the US dollar. A stablecoin offers users the agility and flexibility of the cryptocurrency as well as the relative stability of the US dollar. It makes it easy to move value around the globe while at the same time protecting against volatility. Stablecoins have become a payment method of choice, especially on exchanges where traders need to make payments in US dollars to acquire cryptos or sell cryptos for US dollars in a speedy way.

A stablecoin has to be issued with a company or centralised entity that would commit to holding the amount of dollars (or other assets) equal to the number of units of stablecoin issued. The essence of the structure is that users can redeem their stablecoin for the asset that backs it. Of course, the use of a centralised system to back a coin on the blockchain creates some challenges, one of which being transparency and accountability. For example, in April 2019, Tether, one of the first stable coins to be issued, faced accusations when its audits reflected malpractices and allegations that each coin was not actually backed by a dollar9. It was also concerning that the company was part of one of the largest crypto exchanges, Bitfinex, creating a possible conflict of interest.

Today the list of stablecoins has grown to over ten, of which the most popular are Tether, USD Coin, TrueUSD, Paxos Standard, and Gemini Dollar. All serve primarily as a payment method for traders who want to quickly move their dollar value between exchanges as well as between cryptos and the US dollar without losing critical time.

At the time of writing this book, it is not clear whether stablecoins will be adopted as a means of payment for goods and services. However, a form of stablecoin is very likely to get mass adoption in the future.

We must touch on one more type of payment service that has developed on the blockchain—the remittance service. Around the world, a significant amount of money flows as remittances by immigrant workers in developed countries back to their families and dependents in third world countries. According to the World Bank, the amount sent annually as remittance around the world is close to US$600 billion10.

It turns out to be very costly for an immigrant worker to send money back home. In some instances, it costs as high as ten percent to twenty percent of the value involved. This is money that could change the fortunes of an impoverished family in the Philipines or many African countries. Some startups are using the blockchain to cut the cost involved in handling remittances so that the sender and the recipient have more money to spend on more pressing needs.

Several startups and banks are using blockchain to make it cheaper for people to send money back home. Union Bank and Rebit are examples from the Philippines, a country with a large population of its citizenry working in countries like the UAE, the US, and Europe.

Indeed, Filipinos who work abroad bring into the country as much as US$35 billion annually.

Business to business (B2B) payment solutions

Now let’s turn our attention to B2B payment solutions that are being designed on the blockchain. For decades, companies and other institutions have used SWIFT, a messaging system that banks use to negotiate to settle interbank payments to move money from one corner of the globe to another.

The SWIFT system depends heavily on trust among the participating entities. It is also very slow, especially as critical parts of transactions have to be processed and approved manually. Thus, it can take days and even weeks for money to move from one country to another. This system could be replaced by a more efficient one that uses decentralised ledger technology (DLT). Ripple is a platform designed to perform exactly that. It offers speed and efficiency. And it is less costly. Meanwhile, through consortiums such as Hyperledger and R3, banks are looking at building similar solutions that can facilitate payment transactions amongst themselves.

While the focus of blockchain applications has shifted to other use cases, there is still a lot of potential in payments, and we are likely to see more innovation in the near future.

Use Case 5:
Healthcare

The blockchain is poised to achieve one of the most disruptive transformations In the healthcare sector. This is by (1) making possible an efficient medical information management system that is interoperable both nationally and internationally, (2) offering speed in processes, security for data as well as the privacy for patients, and most importantly, (3) introducing a novel mode of medical data ownership and usage, which places the patient at the centre of the information management system.

The earliest form of medical information management system was paper-based. The sharing of data amongst healthcare providers relied primarily on the postal services. Often, patients would physically lug hardcopy files from one doctor to another.

The second phase came with the arrival of digital information technology. Hospitals, healthcare centres, and other players in the healthcare sector could digitise files. With the Internet, these files could be sent instantly from one corner of the globe to the other. However, while it improved interoperability significantly and cut the time it took for a file to move from one point to another, many gaps and need for improvement remained.

Why interoperability matters in the medical field

Before we look at those gaps and needs for improvement, and how blockchain helps with fixing them, let us first recap the importance of interoperability and data sharing in the medical field. The need to make medical records shareable among different healthcare providers has been established over the years. A patient’s genetic makeup and health history, including past tests, medications, and procedures, often play a critical role during care.

How medical data is shared is a matter of life and death. According to research by scientists from the Johns Hopkins Hospital in Maryland, US, medical errors, including those occasioned by poor coordination among healthcare providers, form the third leading cause of death in the United States, after heart diseases and cancer. The situation is likely even more dire in countries with less developed medical systems.

If a patient uses the same doctor or medical facility every time care is needed, his or her medical records, including longitudinal history, would always easily accessible to the physician, and that could afford them the most appropriate care. However, many times we are forced to seek health care from other doctors or health facilities. This is often occasioned by the need to receive more specialised attention, use better equipment, or simply get a different medical opinion. It is also often the case that a patient moves to a new home in a different part of the country and has to find new doctors or hospitals to take care of their health needs.

It also often happens that emergencies happen while one is away from his or her regular medical service provider. For example, you may, unfortunately, have a heart attack while on a business trip, or become a victim of an accident while on vacation in a different country. In such situations, doctors or physicians, who have never had prior contact with you, step in and give you the care you need. In the least, they need to provide you with the necessary immediate attention before you can get to your regular healthcare provider.

Whatever the reason, it makes a huge difference if doctors seeing you for the first time have a clear, detailed, and comprehensive understanding of not only your current state or condition but also your prior medical history. For example, they need to know whether you are allergic to certain drugs, have pre-existing conditions such as unstable blood pressure, or if you have had a similar health situation before.

Through accessing and analysing these records, and knowing past records by previous health practitioners who attended to you, your new doctor can gain an understanding to give you the most appropriate care.

Before the Internet, it was challenging to access such longitudinal medical records of a patient quickly. Often patients or their next of kin have to request the medical files from their primary healthcare providers, which would be delivered as hard copies to the patient’s new doctors or hospital. Alternatively, the doctor would make the request. Expectedly, the entire process of sharing medical files was slow, costly, and insecure. It could take days of back and forth communications before, finally, the file lands on the new doctor’s desk.

The process would also cost money not least due to the search, preparation, and sending involved. While on transit, the file would be exposed to possible theft, damage, or loss. It goes without mentioning that in an emergency, the file may never arrive fast enough. The new doctors handling the situation would be wasting a lot of valuable time trying to gather a preliminary understanding of the patient.

Another major challenge was getting a truly complete picture of the patient’s health history, because it was almost impossible to gather data from all the healthcare providers who has ever treated the patient. Often it may be possible to get data from the most immediate physician or hospital, but the further back on the chain one attempts to follow, the harder it becomes. In a nutshell, every doctor the patient sees gets only a snapshot of the medical history, and each does not have a way to verify what the others has gathered.

An efficient, interoperable medical information management system

Then digitisation and the Internet came along. With medical records stored in the digital form, it was easier to share them with others. However, the digitisation and availability of the Internet were not enough to allow patients to easily move with their health history to the different doctors and physicians whom they needed to see.

First, there was a need for a platform through which healthcare practitioners could share the data securely and easily. Such a platform was necessary because the medical record management systems used by different healthcare providers could not communicate with one another. Patients and doctors still had to make a request before the file would be emailed. While it took less time than sending hardcopy files, it was still not efficient enough. The patient still had a difficult time having all the pieces of their medical history together in one file.

Meanwhile, most medical facilities have remained hesitant to connect their internal systems to the outside world due to security fears. These fears are not unfounded. In the last few years, ransomware attacks on hospitals have become very common. In such attacks, anonymous hackers capture or encrypt data in hospital systems and demand money before decrypting the files. In late September 2019, for instance, several hospitals in the USA and other countries were hit by this form of attack, and many were forced to turn away patients, creating a situation that risked lives1.

This is in addition to the fact that, for the longest time, there has not been an easy plug-in mechanism of sharing data. Hospitals and medical practitioners out there use very different medical information systems. Attempts have been made, however. As a way to make different medical record management systems to talk to one another, governments around the world have helped design and set up medical record exchanges (a form of a data clearinghouse) through which interoperability can be achieved. In 2004, the US federal government set up the Health Records and Health Information Exchange (HIE) to achieve the interoperability of electronic health records (EHR) across the nation.

While this helps to make medical records accessible to wherever they are needed, there are still challenges in the way. According to a study published in 2017, about 82 percent of non-federal hospitals in the US exchange information. However, only 38 percent of physician practices and 17-23 percent of long-term care facilities are part of the network. Across the world, the outlook is not any better2. In developing countries, the adoption of technology for the management of medical records is almost non-existent, either due to political instability or the lack of the resources for setting up the necessary infrastructure, and that continues to hamper service delivery3.

It is also important to point out that even if where patients’ records are stored and accessed through highly developed systems, the interoperability hardly goes beyond international borders. If you leave your country for a business trip or vacation, your medical information will often not be available should you need to see a doctor while in a foreign country.

Both intranational and international medical interoperability could be improved significantly through a technology that is easy to plug in and use, which is precisely what blockchain is attempting to offer. With the system on the cloud, a hospital in rural Africa may be able to plug in with simple desktops, without a need to build elaborate system, and still efficiently serve patients. And if you are travelling there, with your file on a blockchain system, the hospital does not have to have a direct working relationship with either your healthcare provider or a medical data clearinghouse back at home for a doctor to access your file and understand how to treat you better. All they need is the private key you have, or your next of kin can provide.

Aside from the lack of the necessary logistics to support real time and unhampered sharing of medical records, the medical sector has always struggled with another critical issue: the question of who exactly owns medical records. Is it the patient from whom it is collected or the medical facility that does the collecting?

In the US, the patient has a right to the data collected by a doctor or medical facility. But, because of logistical challenges, the patient has always failed to exercise full or at least a significant control over the data, including how it might be used for research purposes. The patient’s hands are tied and he or she has to trust that the data is not only well protected, but also not being given or sold to third parties without their knowledge. The only action they can take is to request a copy of the file from the healthcare provider. The blockchain provides the tools to make it possible that the patient has a significant say about the storage, usage, and sharing of their data.

The question of who owns medical data is a critical one, especially now that personal medical information is turning out to be a multi-billion enterprise4. Major pharmaceutical, technology, and insurance companies, as well as colleges and government institutions, are all interested in medical data to help with their research and innovation. Much of what these entities are doing could end up benefiting humanity. It is, however, important that they access the data through proper and ethical means, and that includes getting express permission from the individual owners of the data--the patients.

In many countries, it is still not clear who exactly owns medical data legally. This may be the case because there has not been an infrastructure through which a patient can fully exercise a right over their data. The blockchain provides that capability, and maybe that could provide the necessary leverage for demanding a change of the laws around the globe.

Bringing speed, security, privacy

Before we look in detail how exactly the blockchain gives the patient power over their data, let’s first touch on how it improves the way data is handled in terms of security and privacy.

The way blockchain is going to secure data and offer users privacy in the healthcare sector is not too different from what we have already covered in the other use case chapters. The technology provides a cryptographic layer of security to the data. It also makes the data less susceptible to attacks on single points of failures as the storage of the data is spread on a peer-to-peer network. Besides, given that the data is immutable and any change to the records has to be confirmed by many independent computers on the peer-to-peer network, attacks like the ransomware attacks could be significantly reduced. If an attacker wants to change files, they have to take control of all the computers in the network.

Some suggest that medical files do not need to be stored on the blockchain. Rather, the technology should only be used to provide references and indexes to the files to make them easier to find. The main reason cited for pushing for this kind of hybrid system is that blockchain is slow and has issues with scaling. While a valid point, it is, however, rooted in the perspective that the technology is not growing.

We are already witnessing how blockchain scaling solutions could make it as fast and accessible as the traditional server systems. Even if hospitals may prefer having a local database, there are better and improved ways to store files for the blockchain such as through the use of the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS) protocol, which shards (breaks files into pieces) and stores them in numerous devices, increasing the security of the files.

In terms of privacy, like with the other use cases, blockchain makes medical data more private through the use of cryptography. While the ledger is accessible to anyone, the data itself is not. You would need a private key before you can discern not only which file belongs to who but also the details in the file. Contrast this to the server systems where anyone with access to the system can see every detail of every profile, including hackers who can somehow manage to break into the system.

A novel mode of medical data ownership and usage

Now let’s have a look at how blockchain can change the ownership of medical data and how that impacts the shareability. The blockchain can turn over the ownership of the medical data to the patient. That is in particular possible due to three features of the blockchain: (1) data becomes sovereign on the blockchain, (2) access to the data can be limited through cryptography, and (3) the access to the data can be regulated through smart contract applications.

Sovereign data through cryptography and smart contracts

A simple data model is where a patient’s file has a public key and a corresponding private key. When a physician creates notes to add to the file, he or she will use the public key to encrypt the file. Whenever there is a need to read the files or add more data, the patient will use their private keys to decrypt the file and give access.

A more sophisticated system would include a multi-signature (several private keys for a single public key). And that means besides the patient, another person can have a copy of the private keys and can use it to decrypt the file on behalf or together with the patient. The file system can also include a smart contract that can guide details like how long access granted to others can last and what particular parts of the file they can read.

The technology is flexible enough that even in a situation where the patient is not supposed to see some part of the content or file, it could be made possible. For example, the patient might have a key that would allow their primary healthcare provider to use their own key to give other healthcare providers access to critical files inaccessible even to the patient.

Turning the ownership of medical records to the patient has several results. For example, the patient gets the power to decide who can use their data for research. If they do not want their data to be used, they do not just say no, but they do not provide access. Even more, they can earn when signing access to institutions that want to use the data for research. The fact that the data can be only accessible through the permission by the patient minimises its unethical sale to third parties. If the law provides that the healthcare provider has some right over the data, any sale can be managed through a smart contract so that all parties in the transaction can be protected.

The use of blockchain in the healthcare sector will also help make it easy for insurance companies to onboard and interact with those who take medical cover as well as efficiently settle their claims. In particular, this is because the insurance company does not need to go to third parties to verify the validity of the information a customer provides. Even though it is the patient in control of the data, including access to it, the data is protected through the immutability of the blockchain. Once a doctor, a lab technician, or a pharmacist makes a note, it is permanent. No one can delete or change it arbitrarily.

Blockchain could also help cut the cost of healthcare in several ways. First, it offers a more efficient and less costly way of storing and sharing data as compared to centralised systems. Second, it reduces the duplication of tests and procedures. If a patient has had a test done in another hospital, a new doctor can easily access that data and use it to make critical decisions instead of asking the patient to pay for the same test in their facility for probably the same results.

The technology could also help reduce the cost of insurance policies. That is because the incidences of fraud are minimised. Often, the cost that comes from fraud is passed on to honest clients.

The technology also reduces the chances of downtime as the shared ledger does not rely on a single server or a couple of servers. Even if half the Internet were destroyed due to a major catastrophe, patient data would still be accessible on the existing segment of the Internet. That is how secure the blockchain makes patient data.

The Medicalchain model

Now, are there projects that are already building medical data management systems on the blockchain as at the time of writing this book?

Yes, a few exist. One of those is Medicalchain, which has so far raised over US$24 million in two rounds, with one being an ICO in 2018. Co-founder Dr. Abdullah Albeyatti is a medical doctor with broad and extensive training. He has substantial experience in how patient data is handled and the challenges that exist. Before he got involved with Medicalchain, Albeyatti had helped built Discharge Summary, a platform through which hospitals could share accurate notes of discharged patients with the family doctor or general practitioner. Its success in Leeds Hospital Trust, UK, provided the motivation to work on Medicalchain5.

The startup has managed to form partnerships with some major brands within the medical field, with one of those being Mayo Clinic, the medical research centre headquartered in Rochester, Minnesota, but with a global reach. In the UK, Medicalchain has partnered with Groves Medical Group, a healthcare service provider with over 30,000 patients. The latter was the first healthcare provider to adopt and test Medicalchain’s first application, known as Mychain. Through Mychain, patients can chat with doctors using a video call, offer them access to their data, and receive basic healthcare services.

To help with managing the identity of the users of their platform, Medicalchain has partnered with Civic, a platform that leverages the blockchain to establish and authenticate digital identities. This is a layer of protection for all users of the platform. It means that even though it is open for everyone to use, building fake accounts for whatever purpose is made to be a little harder.

The foundational structure of Medicalchain uses two blockchains: Hyperledger and Ethereum. The former handles data processes, while the latter takes care of financial transactions that involve settling of bills or sale of data for research purposes.

On the Medicalchain system, only the patient can provide access to their medical files to healthcare providers through the use of a private key. Where the patient is not in a position to provide such access, doctors can retrieve the key from a hardware bracelet worn by the patient. The bracelet is similar to sort often used to list critical medical conditions and allergies for vulnerable individuals.

Medicalchain has a native token known as Medtokens, of which one function is a form of payment to settle bills, especially for telemedicine-related services. It is also supposed to facilitate the ability of patients to provide access to entities that want to do research using their data. The patient can negotiate and receive payment in Medtokens, which they can convert to the currency of their choice on exchanges.

Medicachain is not the only medical information management system on the blockchain project. Other healthcare blockchain projects similar to Medicalchain include Dentacoin, Project Shivom, and Factom. There are also SimplyVital Health, Coral Health, Robomed, Patientory, Chronicled, Blockpharma, and Tierion6.

Challenges in using blockchain

Finally, let’s look at the challenges that have to be overcome to make blockchain a truly useful technology in healthcare information management systems. Just like with the application in other sectors, a few hurdles exist and it is not going to be very easy to use blockchain in the healthcare sector.

One challenge is the scalability of the blockchain and, by extension, the speed that can be achieved. We have already seen that blockchain has struggled to scale. Nevertheless, it has to gain a substantial capacity to handle millions of transactions that take place in hospitals around the world. The technology is not there yet, but good progress is being made.

Another challenge is a standardisation of how files are created, stored, and retrieved. Every hospital, medical practice, or country having their own standards, guidelines, and frameworks could hamper interoperability. The different systems could find it difficult to interface. More critically, it could make it hard for doctors and healthcare providers to arrive at the same interpretations of the same sets of data. And this could easily put patients at risk.

Laws and regulations will need to change in different countries to accommodate not only a new structure of medical record-keeping but also the ownership and usage of medical data. In particular, the law might need to reflect the existing technical ability of patients to exercise rights over the data that is collected by healthcare providers.

Last but not least, human resource capacity needs to be built to design, develop, and run these new systems. At the moment, as in the other use cases, there is a shortage of talent with the right skills to build blockchain-related systems.

In conclusion, the blockchain offers the most disruptive changes in the healthcare sector. It is one of the sectors where the technology could do more than just improve the systems and processes; it could tamper with the balance of power and turn the patient from not having any say to having complete control. However, while this promise of disrupting the balance of power might sound acceptable, it could turn out to be the obstacle that stands in the way of change, especially as those who benefit from the current structure might fight the change. It could take more than the potential for blockchain for the healthcare sector to change.

Use Case 6:
Insurance

In May 2016, five major insurance companies from around the world came together to form Bi3 (Blockchain Insurance Industry Initiative), a consortium and a vehicle for adopting and leveraging the blockchain technology. The creation of Bi3 was on the back of an emerging trend, right after major banks and technology companies had founded Hyperledger and R3, the two biggest blockchain consortiums for the banking industry. The membership of Bi3 has only grown since. At the time of writing this book, the number stands at close to 50 and includes the likes of Allianz, Swiss Re, and Generali Group.

Initially, Bi3 took a different path from Hyperledger and R3 by experimenting with the Ethereum blockchain to see if the second-largest blockchain would support specific solutions for the industry. The latter two consortiums went straight into building their own blockchains, which came to be known as private, permissioned distributed ledger technologies (DLT) or enterprise blockchains. Bi3, at some point, decided to move on and embrace this new type of blockchain. But they did not build their own; in 2017, they adopted the newly released Hyperledger Fabric.

However, Hyperledger Fabric apparently failed to meet its specific needs as Bi3 soon moved on to Corda, the blockchain platform designed and built by R3. In July 2019, Bi3 released its first application on Corda: B3i Cat XoL, which was specifically designed and constructed to fix issues related to setting up and managing reinsurance processes, contracts, and transactions.

Blockchain benefits for insurance

Reinsurance is only one of the four primary areas of insurance that stand to benefit from blockchain technology. The other three are (1) health and life insurance, (2) fraud detection and prevention, and (3) property and casualty insurance. Let’s look at how blockchain could disrupt each area of insurance.

Reinsurance

Reinsurance is a critical component of insurance. Without it, businesses within the industry would fail more often. Even though insurance companies cover risks and fund claims, they need to make a profit, which is the primary motivation of any business or investment in the sector. An insurer needs to make enough to settle claims, meet operational costs, and have some balance as a return on investment.

However, it is tough to predict the extent of probable damages and how much claims the insurance company might be required to pay out. In all possibility, an incredibly colossal claim might come along and threaten not only the profit margin of the business but also its ability to continue operating. For example, if an insurer covers earthquake-related risks, it might find itself settling a lot of claims if there is a major earthquake in several cities and towns, where most of their clients live. Or in a cover against fire, a large section of the city could up in flames, affecting many of its clients. In these situations, the insurance company could find itself writing more checks than it can afford to fund and easily go out of business.

Reinsurance was invented for this reason, as its arrangement helps insulate insurers from huge claims that could destabilise them financially. Simply put, an insurance company enters into an arrangement with another or several other insurers so that they share risks. Thus, in case of really huge claims, each takes care of a smaller portion. Expectedly, such a contract includes splitting the premiums paid by the insured.

This form of spreading risk is borrowed from the practice of ancient Chinese traders. When shipping goods from their source to the market, each trader would give out portions of their goods to be carried in other trader’s vessels. In return, they also received portions of the goods of others on their vessel. Even if one vessel got lost and every trader in the group may lose a portion of their goods, none would lose an entire stock.

Similarly, with reinsurance, each insurer chooses to share their risk with others. It also receives some of the risks of others. Thus, should a major claim arise, no single insurance company would have to carry the entire burden alone1.

This arrangement has always been at a cost, though it serves the insurers well. The participants have to go through a long process of negotiations, including back and forth paperwork. As one can imagine, it requires multiple contracts for the process of sharing risks among insurers, the splitting of premiums, and the processing of claims. If, for example, a policy is shared among ten insurance companies, then at least ten contracts need to be negotiated, signed, and managed. A lot of effort is spent trying to find consensus and build an environment of transparency for all stakeholders.

B3i identified this part of insurance as one needing the blockchain technology the most, which is most likely why their first of its kind application, B3i Cat XoL, happens to cater to it. It speeds up the process of negotiations, cuts the paperwork involved, and facilitates the implementation and execution of the contracts.

It is important to point out that the blockchain architecture fits well with the reinsurance arrangement in the following ways: (1) each insurer is an independent entity like a node on the blockchain network, (2) each insurer has to trust the other on the network, (3) all insurers in the reinsurance arrangement require a common truth, and (4) contractual terms guide the engagement amongst the insurers.

In the reinsurance arrangement, no entity loses its independence; each continues as a separate business with a working relationship with others. Nevertheless, they need to have a close cooperation, including sharing information and value from the policy they facilitate together. The blockchain replaces the manual steps with a protocol, which speeds up the processes.

The blockchain architecture facilitates smooth transactions among various independent entities. All stakeholders could form a consensus in real-time, so that processes which could have taken days or even months can be completed in a matter of minutes. On insurance applications like the one B3i has designed and built, each broker, insurer, or reinsurer is not really a node on a network. However, the technology still provides them with a mechanism they form a consensus as independent players.

Before blockchain, the only way different insurers could work together and share risks is through cultivating and sustaining trust amongst themselves—not an easy thing to achieve. Working only with others known to it significantly limits the options of reinsurers. Using blockchain, trust through familiarity is a non-essential. An insurer or broker can work with any other broker, insurer, or reinsurer—from any part of the globe—as long as the details of the offer are acceptable.

Because brokers, insurers, and reinsurers have to work with the same set of facts and goals, especially where they share risks, it is important that the blockchain makes available a shared ledger on which they all are on the same page at every point of their transactions. For example, a broker could see what the insurer and the reinsurers are doing, and likewise so could every insurer see what other stakeholders in a transaction are doing. Moreover, one stakeholder cannot arbitrarily change the content of a contract without the input of the others.

Last but not least, the blockchain makes it easy for the stakeholders to form contracts and execute them, as they can apply smart contracts to automate the process of sharing risks and meeting obligations. Without human intervention, according to agreed terms, premium payments for a policy can be spread out. Even settlements can be finalised in the same way.

Health and life insurance

Now let’s look at how blockchain can change healthcare insurance. We have already looked at some aspects of this application in the previous chapter on healthcare (Chapter 13). Some of the ways blockchain improves healthcare insurance include improved security for data, enhanced privacy for stakeholders, and automation through smart contracts.

Digitisation of the insurance and healthcare systems has been necessary because it improves the efficiency and the reach of services. However, it also gives rise to security challenges. Medical records form one of the most sensitive categories of data, which is in high demand by both ethical and unethical entities. Any system that carries such data, like those related to healthcare insurance, are at a high risk of attack or compromise. The blockchain protects data through anonymity, cryptography, and immutability. Also important is the fact that potential clients can carry data that a broker, insurer, or reinsurer can trust at face value, and not need to verify. This speeds up processes and reduces the cost involved, especially as it makes the entire onboarding and policy evaluation process cheaper and faster.

Closely related to security is privacy. Insurance companies often need to know the health condition of the policyholder, or at least the risks during the application and justifications during the claim settlement. This data must be handled with utmost privacy, especially when the insurer involves others in the transaction, such as reinsurers. The blockchain enables the policyholder to have full control of their data, including being able to offer limited access in terms of scope and time. They can show only what they need to and for the necessary period. The policyholder can even prove the authenticity of the data without exposing sensitive details through advanced blockchain protocols such as the zero-knowledge proofs (zk-snarks). For example, the policyholder can prove that the doctor authenticated medical records without the insurer ever having to see the actual medical notes. This allows the insurer to act on actual data without infringing on the patient’s privacy.

Healthcare insurance can also benefit a great deal from smart contracts and interoperability that the blockchain provides. Automating processes through code that executes actions when predetermined conditions are met could be very useful in healthcare insurance. Life insurance is an excellent demonstration of this, even though it is a little different from a medical cover. Due to the slow verification process, it takes up to months, and even years, before a life insurance claim is settled. This could be reduced to as short as days after the death. Or even shorter if there is interoperability between the systems of hospitals, public social services, and insurance companies. This means when a death occurs, a death certificate is issued and the information is fed into the hospital system, while the insurance company gets a notification that triggers the processing of the claim.

Fraud detection and prevention

Now let us look at how blockchain can help deal with the problem of fraud in the insurance industry. The industry in America alone loses close to US$80 billion annually to fraudulent activities, according to a report by Coalition Against Insurance Fraud, a leading American anti-fraud alliance. It could even more severe in other countries. The common types of insurance fraud are giving misleading information during the application, making exaggerated or fake claims, and also claims made through stolen identity. The list of those who do insurance fraud includes policyholders, brokers, service providers like hospitals, and even employees of insurance companies.

Insurance fraud becomes a cost on consumers and eats into the possible profits for brokers, insurers, and reinsurers. For example, in the US, the cost to consumers annually is between US$400 and US$700 per family2.

The primary enabler of insurance fraud is poor data collection, processing, and management. Either the wrong data gets into the system or it is tampered during processing.

The blockchain offers the capacity to fix some of the loopholes used to initiate fraud at key stages, such as at the point of onboarding as well as during the life of the policy and the processing of the claim. It provides the mechanism for creating and sustaining credible identities. For example, blockchain solution Civic helps individuals and organisations manage their digital identities. After creating a profile on the platform, you build a reputation over time. Every institution to whom you submit your identity for a service or access, whether a government institution, a private business, or an online platform, can independently verify its authenticity. This verification by a third party becomes part of your profile’s reputation3.

Such blockchain identity solutions could help minimise insurance fraud emanating from fake or misrepresented identities. Moreover, accumulating the data and reputation through interacting with other entities can play a significant role in proving whether the details they provide during the application or at the point of making a claim are accurate or truthful.

Once the data has been recorded in the insurance system, the blockchain makes it immutable. Thus, no one, not even the highest-ranking admin of the system, can arbitrarily change the details on the record. If editing is needed, a transaction affecting the particular change has to be submitted on a shared ledger where all stakeholders would at least be notified about it or get an opportunity to approve it.

The blockchain can manage how claims are processed to minimise incidences of fraud. First, the identity of the claimant can be verified with high precision using solutions like Civic, coupled with biometrics—to ensure the Civic identity has not been hijacked. This prevents the wrong person from placing and benefiting from a claim. In many incidences, the process could be automated so that data is received from AI and IoT driven oracles, and that the data triggers processing of claims without human intervention. Later in this chapter, I provide an example of a product by Etherisc, which fits well this particular scenario.

When it comes to tackling fraud, blockchain provides better ways for verifying identities, protecting and making data immutable as well as automating steps that are usually susceptible to interference.

Property and casualty insurance

Finally, let’s look at how the blockchain helps with Property & Casualty (P&C) insurance, which deals with the compensation for damage and loss of assets such as housing and cars as well as for personal injury.

The benefits derived from blockchain technology are similar to other use cases—security, privacy, and automation—so we won’t repeat the details here. Instead, we look at how blockchain technology could be used as a component of a broader technological ecosystem that would provide a better experience for customers as well as brokers, insurers, and reinsurers. In particular, the blockchain is turning out to be a critical component to building the next integrated insurance systems, which includes other emerging technologies, primarily AI, IoT, and Big Data. Insurance companies could find a use for AI by using data to predict outcomes and automate processes, while making most of the decisions that have always been made by humans. Meanwhile, IoT provides an opportunity for collecting and sharing data more efficiently. For example, instead of relying on data by a shipping company on the conditions around the transportations of insured items, an insurance company could receive data directly from sensors and other devices embedded in the shipping vessel.

Motor insurance is another scenario where these four technologies can come together to provide a better insurance experience for all stakeholders. Your car could be fitted with different kinds of sensors to collect data on its performance. In the case of an accident, the devices could send the insurer an accurate report on what exactly happened. Hence, the insurer would already be aware of your situation and understand whether you need compensation and what amount—all before you even reach out to them.

The blockchain would make the data collected immutable so that the insurance company would not be able to cheat the consumer with changes that suit their interest in minimising the amount of settlement they need to make. Meanwhile, it protects the insurer from possible fraudulent claims.

How blockchain is changing the insurance industry

We are already seeing the industry toying with these technologies. While we might not see mainstream adoption shortly, a real potential exists for the long term.

And when that time comes, these technologies stand to be of the most use to the industry with the blockchain as their backend technology. The data collected, analysed, and used by the industry is better stored and processed on the blockchain for privacy and security reasons. In addition, AI bots and devices in IoT would have their processes guided by smart contracts on the blockchain.

It is important to point out that tests have already shown that the blockchain could provide a massive boost to the insurance industry. The benefits come in the form of cost cutting, operations sped up, security improved, and enhanced privacy. At the same time, it gives the industry a lot more transparency.

According to a Bi3 report, their reinsurance prototype testing indicated that counterparties in a policy contract gain 30 percent efficiency when they adopt the technology, because the application eliminates duplication, speeds up reconciliation, and reduces the waiting time. It also lowers liquidity risks4.

Others aside from B3i are building blockchain capacity to provide solutions to the insurance industry. IBM is among the most significant mainstream technology companies that have taken a keen interest in blockchain technology. One project that the company has been working on is building specific applications for the insurance industry that leverage on the blockchain technology. In this regard, the company has partnered with several insurance businesses and organisations. One of those partnerships is with the American Association of Insurance Services (AAIS), the largest rating bureaus and insurance advisory in the United States. Through the collaboration, they have designed and built the open Insurance Data Link (openIDL), a platform that facilitates and automates insurance regulatory reporting as well as streamlines compliance requirements. The primary function of this platform is to improve efficiency and accuracy in the interaction between insurers and regulators5.

In addition to established insurance and technology companies embracing blockchain and using it to innovate, some developers and entrepreneurs have founded startups to operate within the insurance space. One of these, Etherisc has developed a protocol on top of the Ethereum blockchain to support the building and running of dApps. The DIP is the native token to Etherisc and facilitates transactions on the decentralised insurance applications, in particular those that are guided with smart contracts. Indeed, digital tokens native to applications and protocol is another critical component put on the table for the insurance industry by blockchain. Native digital value tokens are important concerning the automation of processes and transactions through smart contracts. It is almost impossible as the technology is today to have a sovereign smart contract that is funded from the traditional payment methods; a centralised payment can be compromised by a third party such as through cancelling of the service thus affecting the execution of the smart contract.

In July 2019, Etherisc and the nonprofit organisation Oxfam announced the start of the enrollment of rice farmers in Sri Lanka to the application jointly designed and built. The application would help alleviate poverty through micro-insurance for the small scale paddy farmers6. The product, accessible through mobile devices, would facilitate farmers insuring their crops against extreme weather. Since most of the processes are automated on the blockchain, the cost is reduced, making the premium payable relatively low. Also, the farmers do not need to submit claims. Instead, the system put in place can detect and determine when the weather has been extreme enough to destroy the crop in the farm, triggering a smart contract-driven claim settlement.

One of Etherisc’s first licensed products is a flight delay insurance, which travellers buy to be compensated in case their flight is delayed or cancelled. To qualify, the insurance application has to be 24 hours before the scheduled flight and can confirm the trip from an oracle online. The traveller provides a receipt to prove that he or she is booked for the flight. Once the policy is paid for, either in crypto or traditional payment methods, a smart contract on the Ethereum blockchain is activated and relies on data about the flight fed by the oracle to make critical decisions about the policy. In the event the flight is delayed or cancelled, no paperwork or form needs to be filled to make a claim. The smart contract automatically receives the notification, triggering a payout.

Other Etherisc products include hurricane protection, crypto wallet insurance (which compensates you in the event your crypto wallet is broken into, and your digital assets are stolen), and social insurance. Etherisc is just one among many insurance blockchain startups. Other startups include Guardtime, Fidentiax, Dynamis, and Lemonade7.

Challenges in using blockchain

Similar to other use cases, challenges exist in the adoption of blockchain for the insurance industry. The most prominent challenge is the need for change in the law and regulatory policy so that some of the processes made possible by the blockchain are not only enforceable but also recognisable. In particular, the law and regulatory environment have to recognise that blockchain technology makes it easy for trans-international transactions. Therefore, it should allow policy buyers, brokers, insurers, and reinsurers to operate internationally with little hindrance.

Of course, there is the issue of technical capacity. At the moment, not every broker, insurer, or reinsurer can build applications on the blockchain. The good thing, however, is that consortiums like B3i could help a lot in making the industry players access this new technology. We are also likely to see more startups come up to build ready to use solution.

In summary, blockchain has already shown that it can transform the insurance industry in various ways. This will benefit both insurance businesses as well as its customers by improved speed, security, privacy as well as reduced cost.

Use Case 7:
Energy

One of the faults many find in the blockchain technology is its extreme consumption of energy. The computers on a peer-to-peer network consume energy in the process of forming consensus on the validity of transactions recorded on the shared ledger. “Bitcoin is slowing the effort to achieve a rapid transition away from fossil,” a Wired article declared in 2017. “Bitcoin trading uses so much power that it could push global temperatures up past a threshold that yields deadly consequences,” an article published in the Business Insider in 2018 stated1.

The blockchain “threat” as well as cure?

While these statements are a little exaggerated and sensational, their premise is not too far from being accurate. The Bitcoin blockchain, in particular, is very energy-intensive. According to several studies, the Bitcoin network consumes as much power as an entire country equivalent to Ireland or Austria (which is about number 39th on the list of countries by electricity consumption). Because of that, according to another study, it contributes as much carbon emissions as the city of Las Vegas2.

Facts like these insinuate that the blockchain technology is not a solution, but a threat or at least a great source of problems, when it comes to the issue of energy and greenhouse gas emissions. However, once you scratch the surface of the issue, you find out that, for the most part, the technology is a solution. Indeed, one that could significantly change the energy sector for the betterment of society.

To begin with, over time, the technology has proven that it can check its consumption of energy as well as how much carbon it releases to the atmosphere. It is now an accepted fact that blockchain does not need to consume a colossal amount of energy for it to be functional. Indeed, we have seen the rise of consensus protocols that make blockchain consume negligible amounts of energy. Indeed, even some of the blockchains that were initially designed with energy-intensive consensus mechanism, in particular, Proof of Work are transitioning to less energy-intensive ones like Proof of Stake.

For example, Ethereum, the second-largest blockchain in terms of market capitalisation, is (as at the time of writing this book) in the process of moving from Proof of Work to some form of Proof of Stake through its Casper core software implementation, and that will enable it to cut up to 99 percent of its current energy consumption.

Some in the Bitcoin community have suggested that the Bitcoin consensus protocol, which, as we have seen, consumes the most amount of energy, should be replaced a less energy-intensive one. It is turning out to be a serious movement and a likelihood that sometime in the future, Bitcoin will abandon Proof of Work.

Blockchain benefits for energy

However, blockchain is doing even more than just embracing consensus protocols that reduce the amount of energy it consumes. Indeed, this technology can provide solutions to the many problems that the energy sector faces. Even more, it could be the foundation that spurs incredible innovation in the energy sector.

In particular, the blockchain technology offers the following four primary values to the energy sector: (1) help with data management, (2) support new systems of energy distribution, (3) serve as a mechanism for tokenising energy for more efficient management, supply, and trading, and (4) be the backend technology for the transition to green energy.

Data management

Let’s begin with data management.

Information is at the core of how energy is produced, distributed, and consumed. Players within the energy sector, in particular utility companies, need to collect, process, store, and leverage data on such aspects as how much energy is produced, the cost of producing and distributing it, the amount each end customer consumes, and the billing process. The utility companies also need to track their assets, capital, debts, and liabilities to prevent unnecessary loss. Indeed, the areas of data management in the energy sector are very extensive when you consider the entire supply chain.

The Kenya Power example

To better explain the existing need for a better data management system in the energy sector, let us focus on an apt illustration from the retail part of the supply chain.

The East African country of Kenya is turning out to be a home of innovations. For example, the country was the first to embrace mobile money services and has maintained the highest penetration of the service. Today, close to 96 percent of households in the country have at least one mobile money service account.

This financial service accessed through USSD text messages has managed to increase financial inclusion from below 30 percent to over 90 percent. Indeed, it is a technological shift that has changed many things, such as the way Kenyans consume and pay for their energy. It made it easier to access electricity and, in particular, more comfortable and more convenient to pay the power bills.

Kenya Power, the country’s power utility company, was, however, not satisfied with having a mobile money account number to which consumers would send their payments. It decided to be a little more creative with this new payment service. The mobile money service facilitated a complete overhaul on how the utility company provided services. Before, electricity consumers had to go through a lot of trouble to receive services. In particular, the time to pay for the electricity bill every month turned into an unpleasant chore. Usually, one had to queue for hours just to make the payment at the local office of the power utility company, which meant taking time off from work and other engagements to take care of the electricity bill.

Despite the trouble it took to settle the bill, one could not afford to miss the deadline. If for whatever reason, you could not make it to the local utility office to settle your bill, you had to find someone to do it on your behalf, and often that meant setting aside some money to pay them, which was a small cost to pay considering what would happen if you did not pay the bill in time. If you missed the deadline, the company would send technicians over to your place to disconnect it from the grid. And that was a terrible situation to find yourself in.

To get reconnected, you had to apply by filling paperwork, settling the outstanding bill, and paying the fees to cater for the logistics of the technicians coming back to your home or premises. Even after doing all this, you often had to wait for days, weeks, or even months before they showed up to get you back online. Sometimes, you had to bribe someone before a team would beassigned to attend to your issue.

This system was not good on the utility company’s side either. In addition to poor customer experience, which made customers not happy with the service provider, the process of service delivery was costly and very inefficient. Every month the company had to send out an army of staff to the field to manually read the meters and deliver the figures to the company offices to be fed into the system for invoices to be generated. Since the data was captured manually, a lot could go wrong. That includes inaccurate capture of the values on the meter as well as errors when transferring the data from hard copy forms to digital databases.

There was also the issue of deliberate misrepresentation of facts. For example, the member of staff reading a meter could capture on the official documentation fewer units than actually consumed for customers willing to bribe. This ended up being a cost to the company because while energy was consumed, it was never paid for, even though the company incurred a loss in production. Of course, ensuring enough members of staff to visit every customer every month translated to a huge wage bill.

Then in 2009, the company adopted a smart meter system that relied on the tokenisation of the energy delivered to customers. The energy units would be bought as tokens from the utility company using a mobile money service. Now instead of waiting for a specific time in the month to go queue with everyone else for hours, you could do everything using your phone from the comfort of your home or office. All you need to do is to send payment to the utility company’s mobile money account, and within minutes they send you a token code in a text message. You input this code on your smart meter, and you are connected to the grid3.

When the tokens deplete, the power supply automatically disconnects. But all you have to do is buy more tokens and feed the meter again. In addition to the convenience, you no longer had to worry about physical disconnection and the hustle of getting the technicians to come to reconnect or paying some fee for that.

For the utility company, it no longer needed an army of staff on the ground to read meters and report back to the office physically. The meters directly communicate with the internal company systems that generated and assigned tokens to customers after they paid from their mobile phones. And that cut the cost of operation significantly.

Everything seemed to have turned out well for everyone. However, that changed in late 2019 when it emerged that the tokenising energy system had been compromised. For close to two years, some members of staff colluded with rogue individuals from outside to steal from both customers and the utility company. It turned out that they charged customers up to five times the official rate and pocketed the difference. At the same time, they illicitly generated tokens and sold them in the black market and pocketed the revenue. The scheme was so massive that it threatened the financial soundness of the utility company4.

The tokenised energy system that the Kenya Power adopted is better than before. It is efficient, convenient, and less costly. However, its full potential can only be realised with a more robust data management system. The loopholes that rogue members of staff and others exploited have to be sealed.

Indeed, the Kenya Power utility token management system needs a technology that makes the generation and assignment of tokens as well as receipt of payment tamper-proof. These are the values that blockchain technology offers to any kind of data management system.

The blockchain makes possible the deployment of a token generation protocol that not even the person with the highest level of admin access can compromise. Indeed, the technology has proven itself in this regard beyond any doubt. For example, the Bitcoin blockchain is accessible to all. Nevertheless, not a single hacker has managed to hack the system and assigned themselves or others any tokens (bitcoins).

It also can automate the distribution of tokens through immutable smart contracts. Thus, if a customer sends payment to a wallet address, the smart contract will generate appropriate tokens and send them back to the client without room for third party human intervention. This leaves no room for inflated pricing or someone taking an unauthorised cut from what the customer pays.

Also, a system built on the blockchain is hard for an outsider to hack, without a central point to attack. In short, the blockchain removes the central points of failures that legacy systems like what the Kenya Power one deals with. Since the company can secure data and protect transactions, it is in a position to give excellent customer experience and avoid a loss to theft and corruption.

Kenya Power has not yet (as at the time of writing this book) embraced blockchain to fix the issues it has faced regarding the management of its energy token generation and sale. However, others in the energy sector, in other countries, have seen the potential of the technology to secure data and distribution channels. One of those is the Chilean National Energy Commission (CNE). In 2018, the public energy regulator in the South American country of Chile announced the launch of a blockchain energy project.

The project is a partnership with the blockchain software technology company, ConsenSys. It would explore the Ethereum blockchain for use in recording, storing, and tracking data related to the production, distribution, and consumption of energy in the country.

Of course, a blockchain data management system would also benefit from improved privacy for users. We’ve already seen how this works with the other use cases. It is not any different in the energy sector.

New systems of energy distribution

Now, let’s look at the second way blockchain could improve the energy sector.

For a long time, the flow of electric energy was a one-way traffic. It started from a power generating entity, which produced the energy from a hydroelectric dam, a windmill farm, a geothermal plant, or a nuclear energy plant. This energy was then passed on to a utility company, which in turn sold it to the end consumer. In some cases, the energy-generating company was also the utility company handling the retail part of the supply chain.

However, in the last decade, things have begun to change, and the supply chain has ceased being one-way traffic. The old model is being replaced with one where there is more energy being generated locally by many to supplement what comes from the primary supplier. It is now common around the world for consumers to generate electricity in particular from solar and wind. Many reasons exist for why it is becoming popular and easier to generate one’s electricity, including the fact that the technology needed has improved over the years. The solar panels and batteries necessary for local energy production have way higher capacity than those available a few years ago. Also, the global population has become a lot more aware of the need to protect the environment and understand that choosing greener sources of energy is one way to contribute to its protection.

It turns out that with time consumers are generating more energy than they can consume. A few utility companies have put systems in place so that such consumers can release their surplus power to the grid so that others can use it. The most basic technology that supports this is a two-way meter and transformer, which can transmit energy both into and out of premises and homes. The resulting transaction is referred to as net metering.

In a nutshell, when a solar panel or a windmill farm on the customer’s home or premises produces less than what the customer needs, the grid provides the extra amount of energy. On the other hand, if the solar panel or windmill farm produces more than the customer needs, the excess energy is released to the grid. The utility company sells and buys electricity from the customer depending on the customer production and consumption. At the end of the billing period, the net is calculated and settled accordingly. If the customer consumed more than they produced, they pay for the top-up that came from the grid. In the event they consumed less than they produced, the utility company owes them a revenue calculated on the pre-agreed rate per unit. The utility company can pay them in cash or carry the balance over to cancel out future transactions.

Ultimately, net metering helps the consumers cut the amount of money they spend on power as well as gives them a source of revenue. Meanwhile, the power utility company has more ability to supply to other consumers connected to the grid and does not have to rely on a single energy supplier. Indeed, over time the utility company might get a significant part of the energy it distributes from its customers. Meanwhile, this model opens up the sector to healthy competition, which is beneficial to all stakeholders, especially as generating electricity for the grid might be a viable business for many. Moreover, consumer-generated power is cheaper. But more importantly, it is often more friendly to the environment.

So what role does the blockchain play in this architecture?

The blockchain technology can make this system of selling power to the grid even better by providing the mechanism of forming and executing contracts between the power company and consumers who sell back to the grid. Blockchain provides a means to facilitate transactions between the individual generators of energy and the utility company. The apt tools for this purpose include smart contracts and native means of payment (token or cryptocurrency).

But more importantly, technology can support peer-to-peer transactions among consumers. We might see new infrastructures where people create networks through which they buy and sell energy among themselves without involving a third party. This stands to significantly cut the cost that consumers pay as the middle person with operational expenses, and the need for a profit margin is taken out of the equation. It might also help break the monopolies that have controlled the energy sector for far too long.

Monopolies tend to lead to focus on the bottom line and profit margins. This would no doubt drive electricity costs higher over time as there is no competition. High electricity costs would hit the underprivileged the hardest. Business would also see higher operating costs, which would undoubtedly be passed onto the consumer.

Some are already trying this new model of energy production, distribution, and consumption. One of those is Power Ledger, an Australian-based startup, which designed and implemented a system through which individuals create a network of energy producers and consumers known as “microgrids.” For now (at the time of writing this book), Power Ledger has designed the microgrids as part of the existing national power grid. However, the long-term plan is to have microgrids that are independent and self-sustaining but interconnected in a peer-to-peer structure, just like the blockchain networks. This could completely change the industry and transfer the decision-making power from major corporations to individual consumers.

Tokenising energy

The third way that blockchain can revolutionise the energy sector is by providing the capacity to tokenise energy. We have already seen a tokenisation in Kenya that made the sale or metering of energy convenient for consumers. Besides, tokenising energy for sale to the end consumer, the blockchain technology can also facilitate the tokenisation of energy for trading at the international markets. Oil is one of the commodities traded the most globally. The blockchain makes it possible that a sovereign digital token that represents every barrel of oil mined can be generated. This digital token can be traded in place of the actual oil it represents. This improves speed, convenience, and ease of transacting.

In addition to making the process of tokenisation easy, the blockchain allows different stakeholders to have a shared ledger through which all of them can monitor and track the energy asset in real-time. This provides transparency and minimises the chances of market manipulation.

In early 2017, financial solutions entity, Natixis, IBM, and commodity trading logistics company, Trafigura, announced a partnership to build the first blockchain solution for managing the US crude oil market. Using the Hyperledger Fabric platform, the three entities would establish a system through which crude oil transactions would be digitised. They chose blockchain because it offers security, transparency, and efficiency5.

However, perhaps the earliest most known attempt to tokenise oil and gas on the blockchain is by the government of Venezuela in early 2018. However, Venezuela, one of the largest oil producers with vast reserves, has been dealing with sanctions by the US and other countries, which have limited its ability to benefit from its oil. As a way to circumvent the economic sanctions, the South American country created the Petro, a cryptocurrency backed by its energy reserves. The Petro was launched in an ICO that ran between February and March 2018. The government claimed that it managed to raise US$3.3 billion from the sale of over 30 million Petro coins. Internationally, the Petro seems to have gotten acceptance in Russia. But questions remain as to whether the Petro is indeed on a blockchain; it is most likely a centralised tokenising system. However, Venezuela could be the pioneer and others could follow. Indeed, Russia could be considering a similar project, according to some reports.

Another way the blockchain technology can help the energy sector is by providing better tools for managing carbon credits, which is a concept that is helping combat the problem of greenhouse emission. The carbon credit system allows companies and countries to emit greenhouse gases up to a specific limit. In the event they emit more than their limit, they are fined. However, if they emit less than the amount they are allowed to, they can sell the remaining credit to another company or country. The goal is to incentivise companies to cut on their emissions through the threat of fines as well as reward in the form of carbon credit they can sell to others6. Curently, tokens are a form of currency that can be traded on an exchange. For example, if emissions can be recorded accurately in the form of a token, (though some sort of methodology) it can be traded for cash or its equivalent in carbon credits.

Since carbon credits are a form of assets, they can be tokenised on the blockchain for easy tracking and trading. That can solve the problem of transparency in terms of valuation and pricing. A few startups are building blockchain solutions for managing the carbon credits market. The list includes Aircarbon, a Singapore based startup that announced partnership with the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) and Perlin, blockchain firm in the 2019 United Nations Conference on Climate Change (COP 25) in Madrid. Aircarbon is experimenting with a market solution for carbon credit trading and funding.

The backend technology for the transition to green energy

Finally, blockchain could be the component that has been missing to enable society to wean itself off the dirty, environmentally fossil fuel for cleaner sources of energy such as solar and wind. Managing the carbon credit system and supporting the emergence of decentralised grids are not the only solution the technology offers in this regard. Indeed, the majority of blockchain startups that target to disrupt the energy sector have leaned towards supporting green energy sources. The technology is providing a ready backend for these startups and reducing the cost of entry. Also, the technology provides means through which different players in the industry can seamlessly transact and interoperate.

Challenges in using blockchain

Now, what are the challenges the energy sector has to overcome to leverage blockchain?

The challenges of adopting blockchain in the energy are not very different from the ones that the other use cases face. The most immediate include the need for a change in the regulatory environment to support the new technical capacities that the technology makes possible. For example, in most countries, the utility company is a public monopoly, which has always been the case due to the current model of energy distribution that is possible so far. This model of energy production and distribution has made it hard for the private sector to offer alternatives. Therefore, there has never been a need to change laws to embrace other models.

Aside from changing the law to embrace the new realities, there is also the need to build the necessary technical capacity to develop blockchain infrastructure and protocols that meet the specific needs of the industry. As a new technology, there is not enough skill and talent to spearhead the change around the globe. It will change as demand grows. As the world is so connected today, I expect domain knowledge to grow together with demand as programmers find it more lucrative to help businesses willing to adopt blockchain to make that transition.

Of course, there is a need to change the mindset of the regulators, consumers, suppliers, and producers of energy. Opposition should be expected from those who benefit from the current systems.

Regardless, as we can see, numerous ways exist for the energy sector to benefit from blockchain technology.

Use Case 8:
Real Estate

Real estate is the largest asset class in terms of value. According to Savills, one of the oldest global real estate agents, the market is worth about US$217 trillion, more than all stocks and bonds combined. In comparison, the gold market is valued at slightly over US$7 trillion. Real estate is also one of the most stable markets one can invest in, and because of this, almost everyone wants a piece of it. Most people prefer owning the homes they live in. Unfortunately, real estate is also the most challenging asset class to get into for several reasons1.

The property opportunity—and hurdles

First, you have to go through a vast bureaucracy to make an acquisition. You need to look through many long lists of assets in the market, do research on real estate agents and escrow services, get appraisals, source your credit scores, and so on. Once both sides (buyer and seller) have the intent to transact, the necessary documents have to be drawn, which requires the services of attorneys, valuers, and others. The paperwork, large transaction costs, and potential for delays is immense.

Such extensive bureaucracy leads to the second obstacle: the cost of processing a purchase. In some jurisdictions, the cost of acquiring a real estate asset can go up to as much as 30 percent to 50 percent of the entire price. This is a significant obstacle, especially considering the substantial amount of money required for a down payment. This cost alone stands in the way of many would-be real estate investors.

Thirdly, as a real estate investor, you have to deal with the rampant fraud that exists in the industry. Agents and other players often misrepresent or fail to disclose material facts about the assets, which they are selling or facilitating a lease. After all, their motivation is to increase the size of their takings. Indeed, the details about a single piece of an asset can change as many times as the number of potential buyers or lessee. Also, many cases of scammers compromising land title registries and misleading investors to send money to wrong accounts have been reported. In one case, in Washington DC, US, a couple was tricked into wiring US$1.57 million to con artists. It turned out that the criminals had accessed the title registry servers and launched what appeared like a genuine transaction1. According to one report by the CIA, Americans lose at least US$150 million annually to fraud in the real estate sector2.

Fourth is the issue of asset liquidity. Being able to turn an asset quickly into cash is an essential consideration for many investors. At the moment, real estate is a low liquidity market. According to Zillow, an American online real estate database company, it takes an average of about three months before a listed house is sold. Many investors keep away from the market because of this reality3.

Fifth, as an investor, it is really challenging to manage a real estate asset after you have acquired it. This is especially so if you do not have the necessary experience or are busy with other engagements. Finding the right agent to manage the asset on your behalf is a complicated process, especially to avoid being a victim of fraud. Of course, the management not only includes the physical care of the asset, but also has to cater for the needs of those who utilise it and pay the appropriate fees and rates to the authorities.

The blockchain technology can fix most of these issues, making the real estate market-friendly, cheaper, safer, and convenient to everyone. It could significantly transform the industry in a way that benefits all. And blockchain startups and projects are already building various kinds of solutions for the market.

Blockchain benefits for real estate

What exactly can the blockchain technology do to the real estate market? The blockchain offers the following six benefits to the sector: (1) a better title system, (2) a better way of listing assets in the market, (3) tools to facilitate peer-to-peer trading, (4) tools for managing asset portfolios, (5) better mortgage financing options, and (6) more efficient but less costly escrow services.

Let’s go through each of these six points in detail.

Better title system

The title (deed) is a critical document as it proves ownership of a piece of real estate. For centuries, a title was a simple piece of paper that carried the real estate number, the name of the owner and the signatures of witnesses and notaries who observed and supervised the transfer. It also contained the seal of the official who oversaw the transfer, whether through sale or inheritance.

As sophisticated printing capabilities became more accessible, the paper alone ceased being authoritative enough to prove ownership. A registry with the records on ownership became a critical part of the title system. The files are usually in the custody of a third party authority (usually a public office), which would confirm the authenticity of the certificate and prove that its bearer is indeed who owns the piece of real estate in question.

The land registries have undergone major transformation over the years. And the most significant one recently is digitisation. Information technology has made it faster and easier to access and query real estate registries from anywhere in the globe. However, like other information and communications technology (ICT) systems, digital land registries remain susceptible to certain vulnerabilities. In particular, they come with numerous single points of failure that can easily be compromised. Hackers have been able to break into systems easily, making changes that end up scamming unsuspecting investors. The records are not safe even from those with the responsibility to protect them. With some level of inducement, the staff at the registries, especially in developing countries, often tamper with the records in a way that benefits those who pay them and that disenfranchises rightful owners of real estate assets. These are ills explored in the earlier chapters of this book.

Considering all the necessary and critical processes to buy or sell a piece of real estate, digital land title systems have not made investing in the sector efficient enough. The systems still need improvement, especially through a technology that offers efficiency, more security, and even interoperability.

We have already seen what the blockchain can do for ICT systems in other areas. What it offers land title systems is similar. First, blockchain makes the systems secure. In particular, it makes it hard for a hacker to attack the system, given that they have to be successful at taking control of many independent computers in a peer-to-peer network, which is near impossible. It also makes it a lot easier to prove ownership without having to go through a third party. On the blockchain, the title acquires some level of sovereignty (existing free of any particular authority) along with high provability. The title also becomes immutable. No one can arbitrarily alter its details.

Besides, titles on blockchain become even more potent when combined with other applications possible through this technology, such as smart contracts—a blockchain application explored extensively in this book. This combination makes trading and managing real estate a lot more convenient.

Startups and projects are already building title systems on the blockchain. One of those is Bitland, a startup founded in 2013. In Ghana, where Bitland is headquartered, close to 80 percent of the land is without title and is community-owned. This exposes those who live on it to possible dispossession. Using blockchain, the company plans to help communities in west Africa register and get titles for their lands for the first time.

In the United States, several projects have been set up to put real estate titles on the blockchain. In late 2018, Overstock.com, Inc., through its land management subsidiary known as Medici Land Governance, signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) with Teton County, Wyoming, US, to design and build a blockchain-based real estate title system. Earlier in the same year, Propy, Inc., a California-based blockchain startup, announced a pilot phase of a similar project carried out in collaboration with the City of South Burlington, in Vermont.

Better way of listing assets in the market

When it comes to real estate listing, the technology fixes many of the challenges experienced on centralised listing platforms. Listing is a critical component in the real estate markets, without which purchasers would not know of the existence of properties on sale and sellers would not have a channel to reach possible buyers. In the centralised systems, however, a listing is often disjointed from the other systems that facilitate the sale and lease transactions, such as financiers, escrow, and the title system. This often leads to poor presentation or a misrepresentation of the listed real estate assets.

It is easy to list assets that do not exist or that one does not actually control. This is usually for frauding potential buyers and investors. A listing platform on the blockchain can easily be global. Using API or similar protocols, it can interoperate with other real estate blockchain-based as well as centralised platforms, providing investors with an extensive list of options. Of particular importance is that other systems such as the title registry can vouch for the details of specific listed assets. Those who list can easily prove the existence of the piece of real estate. And they can do this by, for example, signing a listing using a private key they control that is connected to an asset in the title registry. The information provided is made immutable so that investors can get an accurate picture of what they intend to invest in.

Also, the blockchain offers the tools to build superior listing platforms that are free to use or cost a negligible price as compared to what centralised systems charge. That is because those platforms can easily be community-owned open source projects. However, even where they are privately owned, the cost can be low given that either the foundational infrastructure is free or the maintenance of the entire platform is funded through models only possible on blockchain such as native coins allocated to the developer team.

Imbrex is an example of a blockchain platform that seeks to connect all the steps involved in real estate sales and leases. On their platform, the asset gets a unique identification that it carries from listing right through to sale settlement. Imbrex also provides a native payment method as well as smart contract capability.

Facilitating peer-to-peer trading

Because a single transaction in the real estate market can take months before completion, most of those who choose to invest in the market have to be willing to be in it for the long haul. The blockchain is about to change that. The technology provides the tools that will make it possible to buy and sell real estate in a matter of minutes or hours and not weeks and months. What’s more, it supports peer-to-peer trading, where one individual sells directly to another without going through intermediaries. Indeed, the reason it takes a long time to complete a transaction involving real estate is because of the bureaucracy involved. This bureaucracy came into existance primarily as a way to protect investors, but now technology can more effectively provide such protection.

Blockchain technology can improve the buying and selling of real estate by first facilitating tokenisation. The house or land ends up having a digital value on the blockchain that represents it. This digital value, or a token, carries the unique details of the assets, including the location, size, and even ownership history. The token can be linked to the title of the asset in the blockchain registry. It can then be traded on an exchange, and whoever possesses it is recognised as the asset owner in the real world. This means a piece of real estate for sale could be sold within minutes of being listed. The peer-to-peer trading is possible thanks to the smart contract and sovereign escrow applications (more on this later). By removing most intermediaries, the blockchain cuts the cost of trading real estate significantly.

Tokenising assets on the blockchain greatly improves its liquidity. You will not, as an investor, have to wait for months before you are able to sell your assets and collect the cash. This makes the real estate market easy to join and leave. In fact, with blockchain applications, it could become possible to own a piece of land or an apartment for a day or two, and then sell it to make a profit.

Also, blockchain technology allows investors to buy small pieces of a large and high-value real estate as an investment. An investor can buy tokens (or fraction of a token) that represent a share of the asset. This is known as fractional ownership or co-ownership. What’s more, if multiple investors have ownership of a single piece of real-estate, their relationship can be regulated through multi-signature smart contracts. That means the revenue from the lease is automatically deposited to the wallet of each co-owner according to the shareholding.

It is important to point out that technology opens up the international real estate market to everyone. By using blockchain listing and asset trading platforms, you can buy a house, flat, or a piece of land from anywhere on the globe; you do not even have to set foot there. You also do not need to engage the services of local realtors and lawyers. All you need to do is just go to an online exchange and buy the token that represents whatever asset you think stands give you a profit either through leasing or selling later. Moreover, you could choose to own just a piece of it and not the entire asset. This latter option is ideal for investors who want to get a portion of the most valuable assets around the globe but do not have the cash to buy the entire assets4.

Meridio, a platform project by the blockchain company Consensys, is an example of a blockchain platform that tokenises real estate assets for easy and convenient trading. Meridio also allows property owners to sell their real estate in digital shares. Each property can be subdivided into many shares, and each represented as a token in the blockchain. Using the platform, several investors can co-own a single piece of property, which allows small investors to get a part of high-end real estate.

Managing asset portfolios

Now let’s turn our focus to how the blockchain helps investors manage their real estate after they have acquired it.

It turns out the blockchain also offers many potential solutions for managing assets after acquiring them. In particular, the technology provides the tools to help the owner easily lease out the place and receive revenue through smart contract guided transactions.

For long-term leases, this could include an arrangement where, after releasing the house, the renter sends the payment to a wallet every week or month. The smart contract could consist of a house managing agent to take care of the physical needs on the ground. However, the genius of the technology can be appreciated when it comes to the management of co-owned assets. A smart contract on the blockchain can efficiently regulate the relationship between different owners of a property. And this includes splitting revenue and responsibilities equitably. The owners of a real estate could leverage a DAO-like (decentralised anonymous organisation) structure to make critical decisions about the property, including hiring agents to oversee it.

The technology also offers apt solutions to short-term leasing or home-sharing. Recently, the real estate market has seen an increase in short-term leasing, and it appeared to be exponential growth. Airbnb was the first company to facilitate the transactions between homeowners and travellers who needed a place to stay for a day or two. The blockchain could make this experience even better for both hosts and guests. To begin with, the technology has the necessary tools to allow hosts and guests to connect and transact without going through a third party like Airbnb. This could bring down costs for both the host and the guest as everything the guest pays goes to the host.

The marketplace for lodgings could be an open-source dApp that is run by a community of users, a model like that of Bitcoin. Developers could maintain the platform by getting a small contribution from the community. It could be launched through an ICO initiative, where the development team sells the native currency of the platform to the public and raises funds to build and maintain it. The developer team could also retain an amount from the emitted native coin to go into maintaining the project.

The Airbnb example

The blockchain can fix some of the challenges that service providers like Airbnb are facing. For example, the host and the guest exchanging keys is always a challenging part of an Airbnb transaction. Where the host is not around to receive the guest, in many instances they are forced to leave the key under the doormat or with the neighbour. That is a huge gap in this otherwise very convenient service.

Slock.it, a German blockchain startup, has designed a blockchain solution that could fix this problem. It comes in the form of a smart lock that you open using a private key on the Ethereum blockchain. With the lock installed on the door, the host does not need to exchange a physical key. A smart contract guides the transaction on the blockchain; once the guest pays, the smart contract provides them with a key in the form of a passkey (a digital key), which works only for the duration of the stay. Once the agreed time of the visit elapses, the key ceases to function. The host no longer needs to worry about guests who might overstay or having to be present at check out. This is just one way the technology could help with managing real estate.

Some developers are already designing and building platforms on the blockchain that could give hosts and guests a better experience than what they get on Airbnb. One such blockchain-based home-sharing platform is Bee Token, which raised about US$15 million in an ICO in January 2018. The decentralised platform facilitates transactions in cryptocurrency.

Better mortgage financing options

The majority of homebuyers cannot pay cash; homes are often a costly investment. For this reason, mortgages are a huge business. Financial institutions pay the entire cost of the real estate asset to the seller on behalf of the buyer. The buyer then commits in a contract to repay the financial institution in instalments that are often spread out in an extended period, sometimes decades. In return, the mortgage company makes a massive return in the form of interests and transaction fees.

The blockchain technology is offering new ways of mortgage financing that is not only cheaper for the person taking the loan, but also opens up the market for the small investors to put in their money and generate revenue. Today, only banks and financial institutions can fund a mortgage. Through decentralised financial (DeFi) applications, the technology makes it possible to get mortgage funding from ordinary people who want to invest in such an instrument.

On the blockchain, the mortgage can be split into tokens, which are then sold to those who want to finance a mortgage with the hope of getting returns over a long period. The entire transaction is set on a smart contract so that every time a repayment goes out, the holders of the mortgage tokens receive what is equal to their part of the mortgage.

More efficient escrow services

Finally, how does the blockchain improve real estate transactions, in particular escrow services? As seen in the finance and banking sector (in Chapter 9), the escrow service is very critical in real estate transactions. It protects buyers, sellers, and the financiers from possibly being defrauded. This service, however, does not come cheap. On average, the escrow service can cost up to five percent of the value of the transacted asset. Through smart contract applications, the blockchain has made possible escrow that is sovereign and free to use. Two individuals can trade in a peer-to-peer transaction without involving a third party for the escrow service. The smart contract can hold both the title and the agreed payment and swap them when the counterparties meet the pre-agreed conditions.

Projects are already working towards providing blockchain escrow services to the real estate industry. One such project is Reasi by a startup based in California. The platform would facilitate end-toend real estate transactions and eliminate the need to rely on third-party service providers, in particular escrow companies.

Challenges in using blockchain

Blockchain has a few obstacles to overcome so that it can achieve its potential in the real estate sector. The first challenge is for regulations around the world to be aligned to the possible blockchain technical capacities. Without the recognition of law, transactions carried out on the blockchain would be null and void, such as decentralised mortgage arrangements and tokenised assets on the blockchain. Lack of recognition would slow real estate from embracing blockchain. Another challenge is the lack of interoperability. The title system has to talk to the listing platform, and both have to be linked to the trading exchange as well as the escrow service. Moreover, different financing applications and centralised real estate systems need to communicate with the other platforms. Without extensive interoperability, real estate transactions will remain slow and inefficient.

Of course, the real estate application of the blockchain has to deal with the scalability issues that we have talked about in the other chapters. Given the size of the global real estate market, the number of transactions on the blockchain could become massive.

Last but not least, all stakeholders, including investors, developers, regulators, and others, have to have a change of mindset so that they can embrace the systems in the new technology. In particular, those who already benefit from the current system and are in the position to influence policy have to be won over.

In summary, the real estate sector stands to gain a lot from blockchain technology, not least in efficiency, lowered costs, more security, and increased transparency. The technology can provide a superior platform at every stage of real estate-related transactions. Indeed, for almost every aspect of real estate, startups are building blockchain solutions to change it5.

Use Case 9:
Know Your Customer and Anti-Money Laundering

Two paradoxical realities have to be acknowledged in how blockchain relates to Know Your Customer (KYC) and Anti Money Laundering (AML) regulations and systems. First, the blockchain was initially designed to bypass existing KYC and AML systems. Second, the technology gives customers control over their own data, which may seem like empowering them to possibly tamper with material facts.

In both cases, the blockchain technology appears to be one that complicates regulatory efforts, in particular to track and manage the movement of money around the globe. One of the primary selling points of Bitcoin since its inception is the privacy and anonymity it offers users by blocking even the government from accessing their personal data, especially concerning how they spend money. This partly explains, for example, the rise and short-lived success of Silk Road and other dark web marketplaces for contraband goods using Bitcoin as the primary medium of exchange. While the technology to match sellers and buyers of contraband goods online has existed since 2002 (when the anonymous communication protocol known as Tor was first released), a digital payment method that was not easy to trace or block did not exist until Bitcoin came along in 2009. The cryptocurrency made it possible for people to transact pseudo-anonymously online after connecting using Tor, with little fear of being tracked. Though after Silk Road was seized and shut down in October 2013, it dawned on many that perhaps Bitcoin was not very private or anonymous after all. The launch of companies like Chainalysis confirmed this, building tools for tracking and linking transactions on the blockchain to real-world identities. Such blockchain investigative companies share this information with law enforcement agencies, regulators, and businesses that are willing to pay for it.

KYC and AML in a blockchain world

Erroneously, in the early days, many had described and perceived Bitcoin to be anonymous. It turned out that it is pseudo-anonymous instead. The difference? In the former, a user does not need any form of identity or profile to use a service. The best example of an anonymous payment method is physical cash. A payment made with physical cash in and of itself does not tie you to the transaction. In the latter, a user acquires a profile online that need not be linked to their real-world identity. And Bitcoin is one example. Even though you need a wallet address on the blockchain to receive and send payment, you are not required to attach your name or physical address to the wallet address.

Nevertheless, it has become a challenge over time to prevent linking a user identity on the blockchain to the real-world identity, because users need to convert their money between digital fiat and cryptocurrencies. Of course, to use fiat money online, users have to register themselves and prove their identity through submitting a government-issued document.

Making the situation worse is the fact that users are forced to buy or sell on centralised marketplaces (exchanges), which can be forced to share data with authorities. After all, exchanges are the points where the identity on blockchain and the real-world one can be linked. With Bitcoin proving not to be as private as many thought initially, other cryptocurrencies have been designed with the capability to mask user transactions, including the likes of Monero and Zcash. Additional services and protocols such as transaction mixers, TumbleBit, and Coinjoin have been designed for the same purpose. Decentralised exchanges (DEXs) are also coming into the picture. However, most of them support only conversions between cryptocurrencies (hardly between cryptos and fiats)1.

It remains accurate to state that the most anonymous digital payments are still only possible on the blockchain. However, regulators around the world are moving in to tighten surveillance and control on how cryptocurrencies are used. In particular, they have identified on-ramping and off-ramping points, such as exchanges and trading platforms, as very useful for this purpose; these service providers are centralised businesses and can therefore easily be regulated. Just a few years back, no proof of real-world identity was required to trade on the peer-to-peer trading platform Localbitcoins, for example. All one needed was to sign up with an email address and create any username he or she fancied. However, a wave of regulators around the world (at the time of writing this book) are demanding that exchanges and other crypto businesses put in place stringent KYC and AML systems. In March 2019, the Finnish parliament, where Localbitcoin is headquartered, passed a law that requires virtual currency service providers to verify the identities of their customers through government-issued documents and authenticate their physical addresses. Thus, if you use Localbitcoin to sell or buy cryptos, the platform can certainly tell who you are and the authorities can view your transactions. This scenario is being replicated around the world, and more service providers are demanding that their customers identify themselves2.

The pressure on virtual currency service providers to increase KYC and AML does not stop at requiring them to identify and verify who uses their services. At the time of writing this book, it seems there is an attempt to criminalise actions that result in the obfuscation of Bitcoin, and by extension, crypto transactions. In mid February 2020, Larry Harmon, a blockchain developer, was arrested for helping build a Bitcoin mixer known as Helix, which allegedly was used by traders on Alphabay, a darknet marketplace similar to Silk Road, to launder their proceeds. Many believe that if Larry Harmon is found guilty, it would be a clear message from the authorities that even helping to build a mixer that others can use to launder money is a crime. That stands to set a precedent that could see all services of crypto transaction obfuscation either shutting down or going underground. Whatever the case, regulators seem to be winning the war over the control of cryptocurrencies, a task that seemed daunting a few years ago3.

The blockchain evolution of KYC and AML laws

Now let’s turn our focus on the core point of this chapter: using blockchain technology to gain the capacity to identify and authenticate customers of financial services as well as help track and stop money laundering and other criminal activities. In a nutshell, it is about using the technology that powers cryptocurrencies to build better systems to support KYC and AML. It is also about how those systems can help businesses deliver better services to their clientele.

This is where the second paradox comes in. With old centralised KYC and AML systems, the customers provide data to the institution, which is then controls and manages it. The security of the data is wholly the responsibility of the entity that collects it. With a system on the blockchain, however, the data is not stored in a silo-ed database, where it is the responsibility of the company or regulator. Instead, it is stored on a shared ledger, which is not owned by anyone in particular. The data is in the control of the customer. The irony, however, is that while the customer gets more control over their data, they cannot change the details. The data can only change through a process guided by a tamper-proof algorithm.

Before we explore what blockchain offers in managing KYC and AML, let’s start from the beginning. What exactly are KYC and AML, and how important are they?

Know Your Customer (KYC)

Know Your Customer (KYC) is a process of identifying and collecting data about a customer or client, of which a critical part is understanding why funds are moved between users. The purpose of this is manifold. First, it makes it easy for a company or a business to serve the customers effectively. Any serious business would strive to have a clear understanding of their customer and how they can serve them better. By identifying and knowing who the customers are, a business understands their needs better, and can therefore provide the most apt solutions.

Secondly, a business sets up a KYC system in place to meet requirements in the law and regulations. This is often cited as the primary reason, and it is also the only reason that does not benefit a business directly. Nevertheless, for some types of businesses, mostly those in the financial sector, to operate in most countries, they have to put in place a robust KYC system. It is part of the mechanism that regulators and law enforcement rely on to prevent criminal activities such as money laundering and terrorist financing. For a while, crypto-related businesses did not need to abide by these laws and regulations as theirs was a new business concept. However, now regulators are taking steps to include them in the list of those that need to abide by the existing KYC rules.

Thirdly, KYC contributes to protecting consumers from the harmful activities of others. Both KYC and AML are often an impediment against corruption, drug trafficking, and scamming. That is because those who engage in these activities understand that they need to explain the sources of their proceeds to the financial institutions, and by extension, the law enforcement. It is also the same case for terrorist financing activities. Anyone who may want to fund violent activities understands that someone will be looking at and even recording their dealings. These criminal activities will on nevertheless, but regardless, the KYC and AML are in place and minimises the severity. While this reason is, to some extent, similar to the previous one, it does benefit the business. For example, by stalling scamming activities through identification and moderation, a business creates a safe environment for its customers. This puts it in a position to attract even more customers.

The fourth importance of KYC is that it makes it easy to interoperate with others in service delivery. When one service provider can easily communicate with another in regard to a particular customer, it facilitates the second service provider giving a better service to the customer. For example, in the financial sector, one’s interaction with a bank might be very critical to how another might choose to serve that same customer. If, for example, they pay their mortgage according to an agreed schedule, the second bank might be in a position to decide they are creditworthy.

Lastly, businesses need a KYC system to help service providers establish risk levels of particular customers. KYC is a backbone for credit issuance by financial institutions and forms the basis of product pricing as well as decisions to onboard. The credit score system, where customers are ranked according to their past repayment efforts and capability, helps the financial institutions avoid losses through defaulted loans. With new technologies such as blockchain, Big Data, AI, and IoT, new lending systems are emerging that are entirely based on data collected from users. For example, in the developing world (and in some of the developed countries), mobile phones have become the primary channel through which credit is issued to both individuals and small scale enterprises. The lending platforms on the mobile phone use personal data that they collect from the borrower’s use of the device and elsewhere as collateral. In fact, because of how efficient they are at processing and analysing the data, they do not require borrowers to fill any paperwork nor provide any physical collateral for the loan. And even a first-time application takes just a few minutes to be approved.

Anti Money Laundering (AML)

Anti Money Laundering (AML) is everything put in place to prevent criminals from stealthily bringing funds illegally acquired into the mainstream as clean money, including laws, regulations, and processes. In the 20th century, governments around the world extended the fight on crime to include making it difficult to spend its proceeds. Financial institutions are legally required to ascertain sources of funds going through their systems and report suspicious transactions to authorities. These measures have resulted in the rise of money laundering, where individuals and entities engaged in illegal activities such as drug trafficking or larceny mask what they generate as proceeds of genuine and legal businesses. The vice is very complicated in many cases, which makes it essential that robust systems are put in place to counter it. It is important to point out that AML is the component that follows KYC.

The KYC and AML systems have come a long way, aided by technology over time. In particular, ICT has helped with allowing information to flow. However, the systems have challenges that have remained unsolved.

One of those challenges is the high cost. According to a report that Thomson Reuters published in 2016, entities within the financial sector spend between US$60 million and US$500 million annually on KYC and AML compliance-related activities. That is a considerable cost, especially when you consider other industries as well. Of course, this cost, in the end, is passed on to the consumers making services really expensive for a section of the population. It also eats into the profits that the service providers could possibly make.

Despite the enormous budgets, the success rate is very low. In fact, one report has indicated that regulators and financial institutions are only able to catch about one percent of the entire amount of money that is laundered every year around the world—a pretty low success rate by any standards. The weak performance is mostly because of the slow sharing of data and over-reliance on people, to be honest, and provide the right information about their activities.

Errors can also occur in paper-based or ICT systems, primarily as they have to rely a lot on human intervention. When information is manually passed from a financial institution to a regulator or from a financial institution to another or regulator to another, bits of data can be lost or misrepresented.

Also, sharing information is often not in real time. Owing to the sensitivity of the centralised systems, whose security depends on controlling access, information is often shared among different stakeholders through case by case basis, which means that many illegal transactions can go through the system without detection. Closely related to the lack of sharing of data is poor interoperability between different financial institutions and regulators around the world. Without sharing of information, it is easier for money laundering activities, especially those that go across international borders to go undetected.

Another challenge is the duplication of activities and tasks, which happens both within the organisation and across the supply chain. A customer may have to go through the same verification process at different stages in the same institution or every time they show up for a service, which not only slows service delivery but also adds a cost that the institution has to absorb. The same applies when it is across the supply chain. If entities can collaborate and share data, then they all can avoid duplicating tasks, and that can cut the cost of all that is on the supply chain.

Centralised systems also result in slow service delivery. For example, in the financial sector, vast amounts of transactions have to go through an “AML holding period”, which is often more than five business days. During this period, the money is supposed to remain in an account as background checks are done to determine whether the funds are proceeds of money laundering. This AML holding period can set back a business transaction significantly.

Last but not least, a lack of transparency leaves customers in the dark about the information held by companies and regulators. They also do not have access to the process of making decisions that affect them.

The blockchain offers to fix most of these challenges and also provides an even better experience for customers.

What blockchain offers to KYC and AML systems

The first change that blockchain offers is giving control over data to consumers, while assuring service providers that the data is accurate and cannot be tampered with. The system provides better security to the data through decentralisation, immutability, and cryptography. When a service provider collects data from a consumer, it is added to a file that is managed through cryptographic keys. The service provider gets access from the consumer through a public key whose corresponding private key the customer owns. For that data to be filed, it has to be taken through the mining process where a decentralised peer-to-peer network of computers records it on the blockchain. This makes the data immutable, and not even the consumer who has the corresponding private key, which they use to provide access to the information, can change any detail of it.

The control over data that a customer has on the blockchain system includes determining how long a service provider can access their data. And in some cases, the user can choose to sell their data to those who need it for research or other purposes, which is different from what happens currently where the user has little say as to how their data is used.

Blockchain technology offers the capacity to establish provable and secure identities. Once a consumer creates a profile on the blockchain, third parties, including service providers, regulators, and those they do business with, can independently contribute to the building of that profile and identity. That means, over time, the profile will be very detailed and nuanced, which would make it easy for regulators and service to determine the person they are dealing with a lot of accuracies without having to waste time consulting third parties.

The other value that blockchain offers to the KYC and AML systems is the enhanced privacy of the customers. And this is possible because they control the data, in particular, who can see it and to what extent. The data is not saved in a database whose security relies on and manages access that, when breached, all the data of all the customers is accessible. The technology offers privacy to customers even when they need to prove themselves to the service provider and regulators.

The blockchain also provides the means and ways to standardise KYC and AML processes and operations through the use of decentralised protocols. Currently, every company or regulator uses a silo-ed system, and often it is very hard to make the different systems talk to one another. The blockchain, by its nature, makes it possible that various entities that are independent of each other can share a universal immutable truth.

The blockchain also provides an opportunity to reduce the high cost involved in managing KYC systems and collecting, processing, and sharing of data. Through smart contract applications, technology makes it easy to automate most processes. Also, it transfers the responsibility of safeguarding and protecting data to individual customers, which allows companies and regulators to focus on other critical functions. And that reduces the cost of operations.

An advantage close to standardisation that blockchain offers is improving interoperability between different systems. And this can happen so that different entities can securely access the same database on the blockchain to verify identity and transactions. Blockchain, together with other technologies, can also speed up the rate at which transactions are examined. For example, it can cut the AML holding time from days to minutes. And that partly is because much of the data is available on the shared ledger. External sources can also be queried from smart contract-driven oracles online. Smart contract applications on the blockchain can automate most of the procedures and processes.

Another value that blockchain offers to KYC and AML is transparency4. Given that data is on a shared ledger, every stakeholder can have access to it. In particular, consumers can know the exact information service providers are using to make decisions regarding them. They can easily tell whether the information is accurate and even get an opportunity to respond to particular concerns accurately.

The blockchain will allow stakeholders to avoid duplicating tasks both within their organisations and across the supply chains. If one unit or company has done background work in a particular area, others will not need to do that. They will be able to concentrate on other areas instead.

Also, blockchain makes the implementations of some regulations and laws easier. For example, the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) has made it mandatory that customers should give consent for the use of their information. The blockchain provides the consumers to do exactly that through the private keys (digital signatures) they can use to give access and even the conditions for data use.

Several startups are building blockchain solutions for KYC and AML. One of those is the American startup Cambridge Blockchain, which launched in 2015. The company has raised about US$15 million investment from significant entities, including PayPal Ventures and Digital Currency Group (the investment group that owns CoinDesk). It has also formed partnerships with major players in the financial industry. In January 2018, Cambridge Blockchain announced a partnership with IHS Markit’s kyc.com, a KYC service platform that counts close to twenty major banks in the US among its users.

Challenges in using blockchain

A few challenges to the adoption of blockchain in KYC and AML systems exist today5. First, all stakeholders have to agree on policies, procedures, and processes. Every business has a unique way of doing things that work for them, which extends to how it onboards and manages clientele. Thus, having these stakeholders agree on the specific set of protocols might not be secure.

The second challenge is about securing the validity of data, especially during the initial capture. Remember, once this data is recorded on the blockchain, it becomes immutable. This is critical given that a single erroneous entry about a client might affect them and their businesses immensely.

Stringent laws and regulations govern how data can be shared. Even if one entity has information that can help another simplify its operations, it might not share it because of existing privacy laws and regulations that do not allow a cross-platform flow of certain kinds of data. And this is true even where the technology has safeguards for private data.

In conclusion, while blockchain brought a capacity for individuals to transact online without exposing themselves to surveillance of the state as well as that of the global corporate. The same technology, however, offers tools that can be used to improve the systems that are used to execute KYC and AML procedures and processes.

Use Case 10:
Blockchain-asa-Service

The Software as a Service (SaaS) model has made it easier and more convenient for businesses to use different kinds of applications without having to incur substantial initial costs. In particular, small businesses do not have to worry about putting up the necessary infrastructure for hosting software or hiring the right technical skill to support and maintain it. All a business has to do is to create an account, customise the application capacity on a dashboard, and start using it, all through a web browser. In most cases, they pay for the service from the software vendor or a technology company, which could be billed monthly or annually. In some cases, the business pays for access per transaction carried out.

As we have already seen in the previous chapters of this book, the blockchain stands to provide all kinds of solutions to businesses and other entities. The general belief is that it could replace all the server backends within the next decade or so, which means almost every software application used by a business today will become available on the blockchain. Of course, by now we understand why the blockchain could be the better technology, offering security, privacy, efficiency, and new technical capabilities.

Blockchain-as-a-Service (BaaS)

Many processes, procedures, and activities are involved before and during a business using the blockchain. Just as with other types of technology, these can be overwhelming even for a large entity, which is precisely why Blockchain-as-a-Service (BaaS) is a welcome concept for many. Like other SaaS solutions, BaaS allows businesses to access all the advantages of the technology without having to worry about the technical backend operations or having to incur huge initial costs.

However, fundamental differences exist between BaaS and other SaaS solutions, in particular about who (1) hosts the software or application, (2) supports, and (3) maintains it. In this chapter, we are going to explore these fundamental differences.

The two levels of BaaS

First, however, it is essential to point out that the BaaS exists on at least two primary levels. On one level is the blockchain core software, or the installation to be part of the peer-to-peer network that supports the blockchain. And the level of the applications on top of the blockchain, also known as the dApps (though some dApps are actually centralised). Using Ethereum to illustrate this, the first level involves the hosting and maintenance of the shared ledger itself, the so-called Ethereum blockchain. The second layer is about the hosting and maintenance of, for example, a smart contract application on the shared ledger.

A business might need the core blockchain software or a particular application on the blockchain taken care of on their behalf. The first scenario happens when the company or entity intends to build an entirely new blockchain. They would need the tools and processes to do it in a way that attracts the least friction. In the second scenario, a business needs to use a particular application to automate, secure, or increase capacity. Most businesses, especially small scale ones, need both the blockchain and the dApp on top of it to come as SaaS. In other cases, they need BaaS, but they build and maintain their own application on top of it.

Benefits of using BaaS

Let’s explore why BaaS is critical. The following are some reasons businesses and other entities need such a service.

First, it is the most appropriate and practical way to sidestep the problem of lack of technical capacity, talent, or skill required to build a blockchain or an application on top of it. As things stand now (at least during the writing of this book), the job market is short on blockchain skill and talent. If you are looking to hire a blockchain designer, developer, or game theorist, for example, you may struggle, because not many are adequately prepared. During a Fintech symposium, the financial services company DTCC organised for top executives in the financial sector in May 2017, in New York City, the lack of appropriate blockchain talent to hire was cited as one of the biggest challenges most companies intending to leverage the blockchain technology in one way or another faced1. Due to this shortage, startups and small businesses in particular struggle to put together competitive teams. It makes sense that a business would instead tap into already built blockchain protocols and applications by third parties.

The second reason is that it helps businesses avoid worrying about the continuous maintenance of the system. More importantly, the security and robustness of the system they are using are handled by someone more prepared and competent enough. Having an internal engineer or a security officer can turn out to be very costly, especially for a small business. Using the blockchain as service by a third party gives a business the benefits of the technology, as the service provider takes care of much of the critical and emerging issues.

Thirdly, using BaaS minimises your regulatory responsibility as far as the technology is concerned. The designers, game theorists, and developers of the technology have to figure out the essential elements of its legality, sometimes designing the technology in such a way that it does not offend existing laws and regulations. Of course, every business has to understand that the buck stops with them as far as adherence to the law is concerned. However, having someone else take care of at least that responsibility can release a business to focus on other issues.

Another reason why a business would consider using BaaS instead of setting up their own systems from scratch is time. It takes months or even years to build a new blockchain or even some applications on the blockchain. Accessing BaaS, however, does not usually take that much time. Often it is a matter of opening an account and customising the dashboard to meet the specific needs of the business. This can take minutes instead.

The fifth reason is that it helps a business avoid unnecessary substantial initial costs. For example, building a blockchain from scratch means paying designers, developers, game theorists, and others. These skills do not come cheap. According to one report, a blockchain developer costs anywhere between US$80,000 to US$150,000, with an hourly rate of between US$70 and US$200. Besides, you need some hardware to get your peer-to-peer network started. Not to mention a marketing budget to convince others to join your network so that it is genuinely a blockchain. This huge initial cost can be difficult to recover, especially if the use of the blockchain or particular application required is not optimum or takes a long time to be so. Instead, a company can simply tap into a capacity it needs through BaaS, and then acquire more as it grows. This is much cheaper, a negligible cost compared to what is required to build, host, and maintain your own system2.

The sixth reason is that BaaS will allow a business to focus on its core operations while at the same time benefiting from the technology to the maximum. For example, a company involved in real estate management would be distracted if it decides to assemble a developer team to build a blockchain or even an application from scratch. Such a business would have better use of its human resources, time as well as finances to do other things if they found an already existing blockchain or application platform that could give them much of what they need.

Last but not least, it is almost impossible to have a blockchain with only one entity behind. Indeed, the idea of blockchain is that many independent entities collaborate through consensus to maintain a shared ledger. If a business sets up and controls the entire network of a blockchain, it does not qualify to be considered as a blockchain. It is just another database.

Blockchain models for services vs. SaaS

Now let’s look at how BaaS is delivered and how different it might turn out to be compared to other SaaS offerings. It is crucial to start by explaining that the initial BaaS offerings are not much different from the SaaS products we are familiar with. The question is rather whether it is the most inappropriate model for this technology. We will see how in a moment.

At the time of writing, several major technology companies are giving BaaS to businesses and government institutions around the world, including Microsoft (through its Azure blockchain platform), IBM, Amazon (through the blockchain on the Amazon Web Services project), Oracle, and Deloitte. Some companies have been set up expressly to offer BaaS and other related solutions, including the likes of Hyperledger, R3, and Factom. Over time, the launch of even more BaaS is likely.

Private vs public blockchains as a service

The most recognisable BaaS solutions have been enterprise or private blockchains, which are built by major financial and technology companies or consortiums. Access to join their networks as well as the permission to write and read the shared ledger are limited to known and approved members.

Centralised entities undertake private blockchains and quickly become the host of the software (and protocol), that also maintain and provide support to users. For example, Hyperledger hosts the Hyperledger Fabric blockchain, maintaining and offering support to any company or business that uses it as a service.

Private blockchains have marketed themselves as best suited for businesses, including co-opting the word “enterprise” to describe themselves. Many learning about blockchain for the first time are easily convinced that it is an option for those needing to leverage the technology for business. This is a mischaracterisation. But the argument can be made that so-called enterprise (private) blockchains are not the best option, especially for small businesses. Firstly, because small businesses cannot get a place at the table where critical decisions are made, for example, to join the Hyperledger network as an equal peer can cost as much as US$250,000 in registration fees. Secondly, the private blockchain is designed to primarily focus on the needs of the major financial institutions and technology companies behind them.

In the other hand, public blockchains such Ethereum, EOS, and even Bitcoin have a lot to offer businesses. In fact, it can be argued that especially small businesses could benefit better by using public blockchains. They are easily accessible as anyone can join the network without making any application nor paying any fees. Also, the small businesses can make proposals on the improvements of the core software (and protocol). Indeed, public blockchains are perfect for small businesses, which cannot build their own blockchain nor join existing consortiums as equal peers3.

Hosting, maintenance, and support for a private blockchain

Now, let’s see how a private BaaS works in regard to the three critical components of a SaaS: hosting, maintenance, and support. In private blockchains, the underlying software is hosted on a peer-to-peer network made up of nodes of known peers. Anyone joining the network has to make an application and be approved by the administrator or pre-existing peers. A business can then use this platform to run any app they want. This application could be a creation of the business, an application as a service by a third party on the blockchain, or part of the product offered by the company behind the blockchain.

As for support, the consortium or company that manages the private blockchain provides the necessary help to the users, who are mostly businesses and companies. The support team hired by the company would help businesses, for example, on-board and also respond to queries, and fix problems that users may face. The support also includes providing the necessary tools for those who need or want to design and build their own applications. The Corda blockchain, for example, has a support team hired by the R3 company.

Maintaining the core software, and even some specific applications, also falls in the hands of the developer team in charge, who also releases updates and also patches bugs that might come up. It is important to point out that specific applications on a private blockchain could be the product of an independent company. Thus, for the most part, a private BaaS is similar in many ways with software and application as a service by centralised providers or vendors.

Public BaaS

When it comes to public BaaS, the traditional model of a company with staff does not work. And that is in all the three critical components: (1) hosting, (2) support, and (3) maintenance, because all public blockchains are truly decentralised, including their governance structures.

Public blockchains can thus be a service as well as have the potential to change private enterprise. Businesses neither need to build public blockchains from scratch nor build applications on top of blockchain. They can use what is already available to them. Unlike the private blockchain, a lot of capacity on a public blockchain can be free or only have fees per transaction.

Let’s go through each of the three components of a SaaS regarding a public blockchain.

Hosting and maintenance by open-source community-supported projects

The public blockchain is already hosted on a peer-to-peer network of computers. If we take the case of the Bitcoin blockchain for example, the nodes on the network are incentivised through a reward mechanism to host and update transactions on the shared ledger through mining. The reward is the newly created native coins as well as the transaction fees that the users of the blockchain pay. In the early days, one had to download the entire ledger onto their local device before they could read or write on the blockchain. This is no longer the case; through lightweight nodes, one can do pretty much everything a full node is capable of.

Maintaining the core software can be achieved in several ways. Volunteers are the most basic. Using Bitcoin as an example again, anyone with software development skills can make proposals for and effect changes to upgrade the protocol or fix weaknesses identified, which, in Bitcoin, is done through the Bitcoin Improvement Proposal (BIP). A core software developer would write a proposal on the upgrade they seek to do on the core software, published on a forum member of the community can read it (the Bitcoin Mailing List). They could then go ahead and write the code for the core software, including the changes they want to see. With the new version ready, they could go ahead to persuade the community to download and install it. Indeed, by downloading and running it, users are voting to approve its adoption. If the change is a soft fork (compatible with the previous version), then part of the network embracing it and another ignoring it does not hamper the operations of the network. However, if the change is a hard fork (those running the new version fail to coordinate with those running the older version), there is a need for all to embrace it. Otherwise, in the absence of consensus, the network could split into two independent blockchains, which was the case for Bitcoin Cash when the Bitcoin network failed to form a broad consensus on the best scaling solution (in particular in regard to increasing the block size or the batch of transactions approved every ten minutes).

Other models for maintaining public blockchains

In addition to volunteers, public blockchains are maintained by developers who are paid by companies that use them. For example, wallet providers, exchanges (like Coinbase), and payment processors (like BitPay) have been known to hire developers to work on the Bitcoin core software. Core developers are also hired by non-profit organisations such as the MIT Digital Currency Initiative.

Other public blockchains have engaged other models for maintaining their core software and protocol. For example, DASH has a treasury that pays those who work on the project4. The treasury is in the control of the DASH Trust, a body whose members are elected by those who have nodes on the network. The Trust Protectors, as they are called, in turn, hire, manage, and pay a team of developers that maintain the core software and even provide support for the users of the blockchain.

Support for public blockchains

So, as far as the hosting and the maintenance of the public blockchain are concerned, the end-user businesses need not worry much. The support element instead is a bit complicated for some of the projects. There is no one really to call in the event you need technical help. Thus, if you are using a public blockchain for your business’s processes, you may have to hire your own technical staff to fix issues when they arise.

With that stated, very few businesses need such support. Help is more critical for using specific applications on the blockchain, which is such a low-level technology that they do not even need to know how it works. It is similar to centralised backend servers, which most businesses have little interest to understand as the front applications are often all that matter to them.

Now, most businesses using a public BaaS will interact with it through a specific application that does a particular function. This can be decentralised (not owned or controlled by a single entity) or centralised (owned by an entity that leases it out as a service). In both, the hosting and maintenance are already taken care of as they exist on top of a blockchain. For a dApp, several options exist as to how it can be maintained and how those who use it can get support. The maintenance can happen through volunteers just as it is the case with the core blockchain software. It is also possible for a centralised company to offer support as well as maintenance and get remunerated through the issuance of the native coin or through payments that users make. Indeed, utility tokens open up many opportunities in regard to how users of blockchain applications can receive support.

Ethereum blockchain is a public blockchain that has several private companies offering support. One such company is ConsenSys, which builds and maintains different kinds of applications on the Ethereum blockchain.

As for a centralised application, a company is most likely behind it, maintaining the application and also providing support to users. In this case, the public blockchain is more or less a server backend, and everything else is offered just like a conventional SaaS. Factom is an example of an application on the blockchain that provided as service by a centralised company. It facilitates businesses and other entities to put their record-keeping systems on the Bitcoin blockchain.

Importantly, applications on a public blockchain could use a DAO-like arrangement to provide maintenance and support. An excellent example is DASH, which I have mentioned before, where individual developers and experts offer services to businesses that use the blockchain. In return, the community pays them from the developer kitty.

Paying for blockchain as a service

Let’s look at one common feature of SaaS and how it is actualised in the case of BaaS: how businesses pay for it. This is one area that BaaS differs significantly with the prior SaaS model. In the traditional SaaS model, users pay for the service mostly through monthly or annual subscriptions. In contrast, blockchains come with native payment methods in the form of cryptocurrencies and tokens, which are used to pay for access to an application or a service.

Instead of basing payments on expiry of time (such as a month or a year), blockchain makes it easy for payments to be done per transaction, especially given that micropayments are not only possible on blockchain but also come at negligible costs. Moreover, smart contracts can easily be deployed to guide the execution and payment for the transactions.

With the growing understanding of the benefits that the blockchain offers, more and more businesses are going to want to adopt the technology. Finding BaaS is going to make it easier for them. In particular, it removes the obstacle of first having to understand how everything works. All a business needs to know is how the blockchain or an application on top of it can fix the problems that it faces.

Challenges in using BaaS

What are the challenges that stand in the way of adopting and using BaaS? The challenges are many, especially to enterprise users. Most are similar to other use cases of the technology: a lack of technical capacity, regulatory constraints, and users not understanding the full potential of this technology.

A unique challenge, however, is the lack of proper packaging of BaaS in particular with public blockchains. This makes it hard for users who have little technical know-how to leverage the technology even when they do not need to host and maintain it.

With that stated, however, BaaS stands to make the technology more accessible to more businesses and even individuals. Great strides are being made, especially as major technology companies with a lot of experience in leasing software as services, such as Microsoft, IBM, and Oracle, take a keen interest in blockchain.

Beyond 2020

In this chapter, we are going to look at some of the trends we should expect going forward. It is important to point out, however, that no one can accurately predict what exactly the technology is going to do or how it is going to turn out in the next five or ten years. Trying to do that is like predicting in the early 1990s that the Internet would bring us social media like Facebook, video platforms like YouTube, and video calling apps like Skype. Likely not even the experts who worked on the foundational infrastructure of the Internet could foresee what we have today. The technology just snowballed, and we discovered and invented things as we went along. In the same way, blockchain most likely will deliver services and solutions in the future that no one today can comprehend.

It is not impossible, however, to foresee particular trends, especially those that are likely to happen in the near future. We can also figure the general direction the technology might take in the long term though without clarity on the specifics.

Bitcoin will be used everywhere

First on my list is the widespread adoption of the blockchain technology. The hype around cryptos seems to have cooled down, but serious research by governments, mainstream technology companies, and even small to medium enterprises is underway. Those who ignored or dismissed cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin in the early days are now giving the underlying technology a serious look. They are launching projects, companies, and consortiums to help them find out the value in it for their businesses, systems, and in processes. Of particular interest to them is the security, privacy, and automation that the technology can provide. It is also attractive to them that blockchain can create new possibilities and also facilitate the adoption of other emerging technologies such as AI and IoT. These multifaceted efforts are going to translate to a massive movement with regard to the adoption of the technology in the decade that starts in 20201.

Blockchain will be at the heart of global finance

The technology is moving into the centre of the global financial system. Blockchain offers the tools necessary to build currencies fit for the 21st century. Interestingly, the year 2020 began with the global coronavirus (Covid-19) pandemic. One of the situations cited as contributing to the spread of the diseases was physical cash. The World Health Organisation made a point of encouraging governments around the world to find a way to minimise the use of coins and paper bills, as one person with a deadly virus could easily infect many others by touching and passing along physical cash that could theoretically be contaminated. This is just another reason for the push towards digital currencies. And because of its qualities, Blockchain is hard to ignore in the building of a new financial system that relies less on physical currencies. Some countries, including China, which is one of the largest economies in the world, have put actions in place to see the implementation of a government-backed digital currency2.

Nevertheless, blockchain is also going to continue challenging the idea that only the state can issue currency. Beyond 2020, more and more people are going to appreciate that private currency, or currency not issued through the power of the state, can be as efficient and even better in some circumstances. In other words, we stand to see the use of both the state and non-state issued currency in almost the same measure going forward, and that is thanks to the tools made available by the blockchain to society.

Increasing capacity of blockchain

In the short-term period coming after 2020, we are going to see a lot of effort go into scaling the capacity of blockchains. It is true that while it is revolutionary, the blockchain has exhibited weaknesses, one of which is low capacity and can be classified as critical. For example, Bitcoin has struggled to process more than ten transactions per second, which is very low, especially compared to payment services it seeks to replace. In late 2017, the Ethereum blockchain clogged because of increased activities from Cryptokitties, a gaming application launched on top of it. Blockchain developers have already begun focusing on this problem and are working on various ways of scaling the blockchain networks. Some of the solutions being designed to help with the scaling problem include Lightning Network, Raiden network, and Sidechains. The implementations of these scaling solutions are in the early stages as at the time of writing this book. Their full potential is most likely going to be realised in the decade after 2020. It turns out that it could well coincide with the increased adoption of this technology around the globe.

More energy efficiency

Fourthly, we should watch out for the development and deployment of consensus protocols that are less energy-intensive. We have already seen in the previous chapters that the initial blockchain consensus protocols, in particular, the Proof of Work that Bitcoin and Ethereum use consumes a lot of energy. This makes them harmful to the environment due to their greenhouse gas emissions. The newer blockchains are designed to use less energy-intensive protocols, and this trend is going to grow over time. Indeed, Ethereum plans to shift to Proof of Stake by 2022. The Bitcoin community might likely resort to doing a hard fork and adopting a more environmentally friendly consensus protocol sometime in the future.

True decentralisation

One trend most likely going to preoccupy blockchain developers, users, and enthusiasts is a movement towards the true decentralisation of blockchain networks. Some elements in the blockchain architecture have proven to be a threat to what was intended to be a decentralised peer-to-peer formation. Part of the security that the blockchain offers, specifically the immutability of records, comes from the fact that no individual or group of people can take over the network and change the rules of engagement. This is known as the 51 percent attack. While it has not happened yet, it is evident that a single entity can amass resources and end up controlling the most considerable hash rate in a network. We have already seen situations where some Bitcoin mining pools have reached a point close to having more than 50 percent of the network hash rate.

We already see blockchain protocols designed to overcome this threat. These are protocols giving nodes, including the smallest devices such as smartphones, the capacity to participate in the mining effectively. For example, the Tangle protocol used in the IOTA ecosystem makes mining something every user of the network engages in. Another step that developers are taking to mitigate possible centralisation of blockchain networks is writing protocols that block ASIC mining, which makes it expensive for the ordinary person on the street to participate in mining. The more people can afford to mine on a network, the more decentralised that network can turn out to be.

The sixth trend that we should expect to see grow in the period beginning in 2020 is the interoperability of networks. The true potential of blockchain is going to be achieved when different blockchains can communicate and share not only data but also value. Many foresee a future of a global network of blockchain networks. The technology to make this possible is already available and being tested. They include Liquid Network and Plasma Network, both implementing sidechain formations (several blockchains being interconnected). One major impact of this development is that some of the weaknesses that exist today in relation to the global movement of value will be solved. In particular, the reliance on centralised exchanges to convert the value from one blockchain to another might be a thing of the past, and that could increase security, privacy, and speed for the end-users. On a larger scale, that could end up making the entire Internet truly decentralised, in opposition to what it is today where a few corporations hold considerable sway.

More intentional technological investment

The seventh trend likely to become more visible post-2020 is the movement towards building blockchains that are resistant to emerging technologies, in particular quantum computing. Almost every major technology company—Google, Microsoft, IBM… you name it—is working towards making quantum computers a reality, and it seems we are already there. Quantum computers could provide the capacity to manipulate data in a way that has never been available to us before, and that includes doing it in unimaginable speeds. Of course, this technology stands to do a lot of good, including opening up more possibilities in medical science through new forms of research. It, however, also turns out that it poses a threat to blockchain protocols. In particular, it might make blockchain in its current form, insecure as a single quantum machine can have a hash rate that is faster than an entire network can achieve. And that could give it the ability to overrule a network and thus arbitrarily make changes to the shared ledger. It could also make it possible to break the cryptography that blockchains rely on so much to secure ownership of value. With that stated, the community behind blockchain is very much aware of this threat. Blockchain developers are already working on protocols that could be resistant to this powerful machine. These efforts are only going to increase in the period after 2020, especially as the adoption of quantum computing grows3.

The eighth trend that we are likely to see in the period post the year 2020 is fewer projects designed as quick money-making schemes. The focus is most likely going to be on projects designed to create value in the long term, in particular those that provide service, improve automation, and provide security and also privacy. We might never see a repeat of the 2017 and 2018 ICO craze. Going forward, there is going to be a more calculated investment into different kinds of projects. We are likely to witness the collapse of many of the projects that were initiated in 2017 and 2018 through massive ICOs. And that is going to be either because of regulatory interventions (such as what is happening to Telegram, where its founders are fighting the Security Exchange Commission over the legality of their ICO) or because the business models were never sound to begin with.

Other trends we should expect going forward include increased blurring of the line between the real world and the blockchain. In particular, a lot more real-world assets could be tokenised, such as real estate, cars, precious metals, and businesses.

Also, the Internet identity is most likely to move into the blockchain, especially with the launch of the Decentralised Identity Foundation (DIF) in May 2017, a consortium that is exploring ways of using blockchain for identity management on the Internet. The membership has close to a hundred technology companies and startups, including Microsoft, IBM, Accenture, Hyperledger, and MasterCard. The project is set to develop new protocols for identifying everything from websites, emails, and computers to real estate, and cars online.

We might also see increased adoption of smart contracts in the period, which might end up being the most important application of the blockchain, with its full potential being realised in this decade.

The period beginning in the year 2020 is going to be a very interesting one in regard to blockchain. The focus is most likely to shift from cryptocurrencies towards the other applications of the technology.

The Blockchain Onboarding Workbook for Businesses

You now have much of the foundational knowledge and information on the blockchain. You know what it can do, and how it could change business and society at large. Perhaps you want the blockchain technology to give your business some value and maybe it is time to take the next step. It could be why you picked up this book in the first place. I should point out, however, that this process should be executed with some level of caution as well as skill. I wrote this chapter precisely for this purpose and will guide you through this process.

Three steps to applying blockchain in your business

The process of applying the blockchain to your business should take three primary steps:

1. The blockchain suitability test

Identifying exactly how your business could make the blockchain technology part of its processes and systems.

2. The blockchain readiness test

Finding out what your business already has as well as what you would need to acquire to make the process easier. At this stage, in other words, you take stock of your capacity and needs.

3. The blockchain level or type test

Identifying the exact part of the blockchain technology to use as every business has a particular element that is most appropriate for it. At this stage, you want to be laser accurate on how you can acquire what you need with the least cost and effort.

These three steps are in a three-part workbook that I have created to help you figure out the answers in each critical stage. By using the questionnaire to assess each stage, you will understand how to handle each of these steps for your business in a customised manner. Take a look through it before reading the rest of this chapter. After going through each part in detail, go back to fill out the forms, which is downloadable to your device. The Blockchain Onboarding Workbook for Businesses is available online: BlockchainUnlocked.co.

Business blockchain suitability

The first part of the workbook is designed to help you figure out whether your business is suitable to use the blockchain technology. Nearly every business on the planet can, in one way or another, find some use for the technology. However, the benefit is not the same for each, and some businesses find critical uses for the technology. In contrast, others use it for what could be described as “cosmetic functions”―the only practical application is not at the core of the business operations. For example, here is a case of applying the technology to change the core processes and systems of a business: a gold trading company creates a digital token on the blockchain to represent the actual gold held in their vaults and make it easy for it to be traded. However, a hotel chain brand choosing to host its website on top of a blockchain like Ethereum or NEO is not applying the technology at the core of its operations.

Broadly speaking, the blockchain can help your business achieve at least one of three goals: fix a problem faced by your business, improve efficiency (such as cutting costs), and support innovation (such as creating new products and experience for your customers).

Fixing problems

Businesses face persistent problems all the time, some of which you are forced to accommodate as part and parcel of doing business. For example, as a small business, you might find it a little costly and demanding to set up a rewards points system. You would have to worry about protecting the user data you collect for the purpose, which distracts you from your core operations. The blockchain makes it possible for you to create and issue tokens easily to your customers without needing to collect their data, and more importantly, without having to use the services of a third party.

Improving efficiency

The technology achieves efficiency through cutting costs, hastening processes, and improving security for data and systems. For example, if it takes several days to move value from one corner of the globe to another, the technology has proven that it can significantly reduce that time to minutes and even seconds. Besides, it cuts the costs involved, in some cases (through particular protocols), to zero.

Supporting innovation

In regard to innovation, the technology offers you the opportunity and capacity to create new products and experiences for your customers in several ways. For example, using smart contracts and the micropayment capacity, you could automate certain transactions, opening up opportunities that did not exist before. For example, online magazines could use the technology to allow readers to pay to read per article. This would bring in more readers who have no need or desire for a monthly subscription when all they want is to read a single article.

Business blockchain readiness

This is the second step in the process of onboarding and making the blockchain technology part of your systems as a business or an institution. This stage analyses your business readiness or capacity to make the technology a part of your systems.

In particular, you need to determine whether you have the necessary talent and skill in your team. Also determine how much it would cost you to put in place the blockchain system, and whether you have such funds. Most importantly, justify the financial cost and whether it can be recouped.

The biggest challenge for most small businesses in using blockchain is the shortage of the such hireable talent. There are not many developers experienced in blockchain, and those who are come at a cost that might be out of reach, especially for small businesses. Hiring is, however, not the only option to give your business the capacity it needs to embrace blockchain. Members of your team could train for the skill, as it is becoming easier to find relevant courses, especially online. Learning platforms such as Coursera.org, Udemy, and Skillshare have growing courses to choose from depending on your specific needs.

The skillsets to build a blockchain system come from designers, developers, game theorists, and token economists. A designer helps you put together the structure you have in mind, while a game theorist helps the designer determine the relationships that should exist between different players, and, more importantly, how their interactions can be leveraged for the benefit of the entire system. Finally, the developer brings the whole thing to life. For less complex blockchain projects, however, all you may need is a developer with a little design and game theory knowledge.

The level or type of blockchain you need for your business

The third question is the extent to which you need to embrace the technology, as well as the type of blockchain that is most appropriate for your business. As a business, you must decide whether you need a public or a private blockchain. Do you need to use an already existing blockchain (as a service) or do you need to build a new application on an existing blockchain? Indeed, you have to decide whether what you need is an entirely new blockchain, which you have to build.

In other words, the blockchain technology comes in several layers, and the primary ones being as a service, an application on top of a blockchain, and the core protocol itself. The level at which you embrace the technology depends on your specific needs. If your needs are unique and there is no application as a service available for it, then you may have to build your app on an existing blockchain. If you realise that none of the existing blockchains can serve as an appropriate backend for your application, then you may have to build the blockchain first before making the application.

Glossary

• the 51 percent attack: a situation in which a bad actor attacks and acquires a computing power (hash rate) of more than 51 percent of the entire network

• Artificial Intelligence (AI): the creation of computer applications that act as smart as humans

• Bitcoin: the first cryptocurrency and Store of Value (SoV) on the blockchain, launched in 2009

• Blockchain: a shared ledger of digital information that is distributed across different computers and records transactions chronologically, making it costly and difficult to alter transactions and allowing potentially adversarial parties to cooperate

• Blockchain as a Service (BaaS): a blockchain service offering that allows customers to use cloud-based services to develop and host their blockchain apps, functions, and smart contracts.

• Cryptocurrency: a digital currency in which encryption techniques are used to regulate the generation of units of currency and verify the transfer of funds, operating independently of a central authority

• Decentralised Autonomous Organisation (DAO): a Smart Contract-controlled organisational system

• Decentralised applications (dApps): open-source software that leverages on the blockchain technology

• Distributed ledger technology (DLT): a database that exists across several locations or among multiple participants; it is decentralised and eliminates the need for a central authority or intermediary to process, validate or authenticate transactions

• Hard fork: split of a blockchain network into two independent networks

• Hash rate: computing power

• Initial Coin Offering (ICO): a fundraising mechanism in which new projects sell their crypto tokens to raise funds for a new cryptocurrency asset, company, or venture. Similar to an Initial Public Offering (IPO) in which investors purchase shares of a company

• Internet of Things (IoT): adding smart devices to the system to help with the collection and analysis of data, as well as automation of processes

• InterPlanetary File System: an open-source, peer-to-peer hypermedia protocol designed to make the web faster, safer, and more open

• Miner: a type of a node who computes transactions to secure the blockchain

• Node: any electronic device able to connect to the network, including phones, printers, and computers

• Proof of Stake (PoS): algorithm that aims to achieve distributed consensus in a blockchain

• Proof of Work (PoW): model for blockchains to come to a consensus

• Smart contracts: computer protocols that facilitate, verify, or enforce the performance of a contract making a contractual clause unnecessary

• Tokens: a unit on the blockchain that is issued for a limited or specific function or use, examples of which are the Ethereum Gas and Storj coin.

• Tor: an anonymous communication protocol released in 2002

• Turing Complete virtual machine: a computer with the ability to write programmes or contracts that can solve any reasonable computational problem
