### Blockchain and the Byzantine Generals’ Problem

The Byzantine Generals’ Problem stemmed from the computer science description of a situation where parties involved must agree on a single strategy in order to avoid failure. However, some of the parties involved are corrupt and disseminating false information and are otherwise unreliable.

Let’s go into the story.

An entire army, split into two divisions, has gotten a city surrounded, but have a complicated logistics issue. The divisions are positioned on each side of the enemy city (East and West). The city is strong enough to defend itself against one division, but will fall if attacked by both. As such, both divisions need to attack at the same exact time. If the divisions do not attack at the same time, they will lose.

Thus, the generals of each division need to agree on the exact moment of when to attack. They can only communicate by sending a messenger back and forth the divisions without any other way to communicate. It is ancient times after all. For example, General A will send the message, “Hey General B, we are going to attack on Wednesday. Will you attack with us?”. The messenger then delivers the message to General B, who in turn responds, “We can’t do Wednesday—ladies night. How about Thursday? If we attack on Thursday, will you attack with us?”. The messenger delivers the message back to General A, and so forth.

However, there is a twist: the messenger could be hijacked and caught while delivering the message. Instead, a `#fakenews` messenger could intentionally send a wrong message to deceive the other general to attack the city on the wrong day. There is no way to check if the message is real. How do they create a “trustless” system that ensures victory in when they attack?

This is the Byzantine Generals’ Problem.

### What is blockchain?

A blockchain is a virtual machine that runs on a peer-to-peer network of computers. It can host and run almost all kinds of applications. Payment systems, cloud storage, smart contracts, land registries, supply chain management systems, websites—you name it. Any application that can be launched on a centralised server can be launched on a blockchain.

Blockchain can also host and run applications that cannot work on centralised server systems. We already have all these applications running on the legacy Internet (i.e., the Internet pre-blockchain). What better than to have them on a virtual machine running on top of a peer-to-peer network?

That is a fundamental question. It is at the heart of why blockchain has a future. But before we answer this, let’s get a big picture of how blockchain functions and why it is a virtual machine.

Computer networks have been around since the 1960s. (By networks, I mean several computers having channels for communicating with one another.) The earliest ones were centralised^([2](#ch01.xhtml#ch01fn2)), which meant computers formed had to communicate with one another through a central server. The server was a computer with the capability to facilitate communication between computers. Over the years, peer-to-peer networks developed. This is where one computer could communicate with another without having to go through a central server. Indeed, the Internet itself is a form of peer-to-peer networks. But peer-to-peer networks in the strict use of the word came into being in the late 1990s. It was first experienced in `Napster`, a network one joined by installing a particular software to share music with others^([3](#ch01.xhtml#ch01fn3)).

But even with that development, some things could work without a central server. In particular, a payment system. For a payment system to work, a central server had to exist to host and maintain a transaction ledger. People on the network had to trust that the record was accurate. In particular, people in charge of the server had to be trusted that they could not falsify the records. But still, servers were hacked and data stolen or records arbitrarily changed.

#### Permissioned vs. permissionless

The implementation of the blockchain can be categorised as two types: permissioned (only a select few can read/write) or permissionless (anyone can read/write). These blockchains may be expanded into three subcategories:

1. Public blockchains
2. Consortium blockchains
3. Private blockchains

While public and consortium blockchains may be centralised or decentralised (decisions may be made either by a central body or a selected multiple group of people or by anyone), private blockchains are always centralised (decisions are made by a central body).

Whether the blockchain decision-making is centralised or decentralised is distinct from how its data is distributed. In the latter, or a distributed ledger, data or information may be stored at multiple points with possibly multiple groups. However, in the former, the decision-making and writing of a new block or voting for a change in the base protocol of the blockchain may centralised or decentralised.

The founding fathers, who popularised the blockchain through `Bitcoin`, intended for the initial use cases to be permissionless and decentralised, i.e., having a decentralised model, in which everyone can participate, contribute, and collaborate together—very much reflected in the characteristics of `Bitcoin`.
