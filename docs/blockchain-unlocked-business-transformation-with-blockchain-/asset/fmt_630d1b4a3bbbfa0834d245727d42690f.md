
# In both cases, the blockchain technology appears to be one that complicates regulatory efforts, in particular to track and manage the movement of money around the globe.

One of the primary selling points of `Bitcoin` since its inception is the privacy and anonymity it offers users by blocking even the government from accessing their personal data, especially concerning how they spend money. This partly explains, for example, the rise and short-lived success of `Silk Road` and other dark web marketplaces for contraband goods using `Bitcoin` as the primary medium of exchange. While the technology to match sellers and buyers of contraband goods online has existed since 2002 (when the anonymous communication protocol known as `Tor` was first released), a digital payment method that was not easy to trace or block did not exist until `Bitcoin` came along in 2009. The cryptocurrency made it possible for people to transact pseudo-anonymously online after connecting using `Tor`, with little fear of being tracked. Though after `Silk Road` was seized and shut down in October 2013, it dawned on many that perhaps `Bitcoin` was not very private or anonymous after all. The launch of companies like `Chainalysis` confirmed this, building tools for tracking and linking transactions on the blockchain to real-world identities. Such blockchain investigative companies share this information with law enforcement agencies, regulators, and businesses that are willing to pay for it.

## KYC and AML in a blockchain world

Erroneously, in the early days, many had described and perceived `Bitcoin` to be anonymous. It turned out that it is *pseudo*-anonymous instead. The difference? In the former, a user does not need any form of identity or profile to use a service. The best example of an anonymous payment method is physical cash. A payment made with physical cash in and of itself does not tie you to the transaction. In the latter, a user acquires a profile online that need not be linked to their real-world identity. And `Bitcoin` is one example. Even though you need a wallet address on the blockchain to receive and send payment, you are not required to attach your name or physical address to the wallet address.

Nevertheless, it has become a challenge over time to prevent linking a user identity on the blockchain to the real-world identity, because users need to convert their money between digital fiat and cryptocurrencies. Of course, to use fiat money online, users have to register themselves and prove their identity through submitting a government-issued document.

Making the situation worse is the fact that users are forced to buy or sell on centralised marketplaces (exchanges), which can be forced to share data with authorities. After all, exchanges are the points where the identity on blockchain and the real-world one can be linked. With `Bitcoin` proving not to be as private as many thought initially, other cryptocurrencies have been designed with the capability to mask user transactions, including the likes of `Monero` and `Zcash`. Additional services and protocols such as transaction mixers, `TumbleBit`, and `Coinjoin` have been designed for the same purpose. Decentralised exchanges (`DEXs`) are also coming into the picture. However, most of them support only conversions between cryptocurrencies (hardly between cryptos and fiats)^([1](#ch17.xhtml#ch17fn1)).

It remains accurate to state that the most anonymous digital payments are still only possible on the blockchain. However, regulators around the world are moving in to tighten surveillance and control on how cryptocurrencies are used. In particular, they have identified on-ramping and off-ramping points, such as exchanges and trading platforms, as very useful for this purpose; these service providers are centralised businesses and can therefore easily be regulated. Just a few years back, no proof of real-world identity was required to trade on the peer-to-peer trading platform `Localbitcoins`, for example. All one needed was to sign up with an email address and create any username he or she fancied. However, a wave of regulators around the world (at the time of writing this book) are demanding that exchanges and other crypto businesses put in place stringent `KYC` and `AML` systems. In March 2019, the Finnish parliament, where `Localbitcoin` is headquartered, passed a law that requires virtual currency service providers to verify the identities of their customers through government-issued documents and authenticate their physical addresses. Thus, if you use `Localbitcoin` to sell or buy cryptos, the platform can certainly tell who you are and the authorities can view your transactions. This scenario is being replicated around the world, and more service providers are demanding that their customers identify themselves^([2](#ch17.xhtml#ch17fn2)).

The pressure on virtual currency service providers to increase `KYC` and `AML` does not stop at requiring them to identify and verify who uses their services. At the time of writing this book, it seems there is an attempt to criminalise actions that result in the obfuscation of `Bitcoin`, and by extension, crypto transactions. In mid February 2020, `Larry Harmon`, a blockchain developer, was arrested for helping build a `Bitcoin` mixer known as `Helix`, which allegedly was used by traders on `Alphabay`, a darknet marketplace similar to `Silk Road`, to launder their proceeds. Many believe that if `Larry Harmon` is found guilty, it would be a clear message from the authorities that even helping to build a mixer that others can use to launder money is a crime. That stands to set a precedent that could see all services of crypto transaction obfuscation either shutting down or going underground. Whatever the case, regulators seem to be winning the war over the control of cryptocurrencies, a task that seemed daunting a few years ago^([3](#ch17.xhtml#ch17fn3)).

## The blockchain evolution of KYC and AML laws

Now let’s turn our focus on the core point of this chapter: using blockchain technology to gain the capacity to identify and authenticate customers of financial services as well as help track and stop money laundering and other criminal activities. In a nutshell, it is about using the technology that powers cryptocurrencies to build better systems to support `KYC` and `AML`. It is also about how those systems can help businesses deliver better services to their clientele.

This is where the second paradox comes in. With old centralised `KYC` and `AML` systems, the customers provide data to the institution, which is then controls and manages it. The security of the data is wholly the responsibility of the entity that collects it. With a system on the blockchain, however, the data is not stored in a silo-ed database, where it is the responsibility of the company or regulator. Instead, it is stored on a shared ledger, which is not owned by anyone in particular. The data is in the control of the customer. The irony, however, is that while the customer gets more control over their data, they cannot change the details. The data can only change through a process guided by a tamper-proof algorithm.

Before we explore what blockchain offers in managing `KYC` and `AML`, let’s start from the beginning. What exactly are `KYC` and `AML`, and how important are they?

### Know Your Customer (KYC)

`Know Your Customer (KYC)` is a process of identifying and collecting data about a customer or client, of which a critical part is understanding why funds are moved between users. The purpose of this is manifold. First, it makes it easy for a company or a business to serve the customers effectively. Any serious business would strive to have a clear understanding of their customer and how they can serve them better. By identifying and knowing who the customers are, a business understands their needs better, and can therefore provide the most apt solutions.
