# 2.4.2 REGISTRATION

Image registration is a fundamental process in neuroimaging that involves alignment and spatially mapping images obtained from different modalities or at different times. This technique is essential for integrating information from multiple sources and facilitating meaningful comparisons across datasets. Registration is particularly crucial in studies involving longitudinal imaging, where it enables the tracking of structural or functional changes in the brain over time [22].

One common application of image registration is the alignment of structural and functional images from MRI. For instance, the work of Jenkins and Smith [22] introduced the FMRIB's Linear Image Registration Tool (FLIRT), a widely used tool for linear registration in neuroimaging. FLIRT allows for the precise alignment of different MRI modalities, facilitating the combination of anatomical and functional information for comprehensive analyses.

In the realm of PET and CT, registration techniques are essential for co-registering functional and anatomical images. The work by Woods et al. [23] outlined the mathematical principles of image registration and the Automated Image Registration (AIR) software, contributing significantly to the field. The AIR has been instrumental in aligning PET and CT images, enabling more accurate location of functional activities within the anatomical context.

The significance of image registration extends beyond anatomical alignment, encompassing applications such as inter-subject registration for population studies and spatial normalization for group analyses. As neuroimaging research advances, the development of sophisticated registration algorithms, such as nonlinear and deformable registration techniques, continues to refine the accuracy and reliability of image alignment, enhancing the interpretability of complex datasets [23].

# 2.4.3 DIMENSIONAL REDUCTION

Dimensionality reduction is a critical technique in neuroimaging that addresses the challenges associated with high-dimensional datasets, where the number of variables or features is significantly larger than the number of observations. The vast amount of information generated by neuroimaging modalities, such as MRI and fMRI, necessitates efficient methods for selection and extraction. Dimensionality reduction methods aim to preserve the essential information while reducing the dataset's complexity, facilitating straightforward analyses and interpretation.

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a widely employed dimensionality reduction technique in neuroimaging. By transforming the original variables into a new set of uncorrelated variables (principal components), PCA captures the most significant sources of variance in the data. This technique has been applied to tasks such as fMRI data analysis, where it aids identifying patterns of brain activity [24].

The work by Varquaux et al. [26] introduces the use of dictionary learning for dimensionality reduction in neuroimaging. By representing the data as a sparse combination of learned basis functions, dictionary learning captures essential features while discarding redundant information. While commonly in single-cell RNA sequencing, its application has been extended to neuroimaging studies for visualizing complex brain connectivity patterns [25].

In conclusion, dimensionality reduction methods play a crucial role in enhancing the interpretability and ability of neuroimaging. While PCA, t-SNE, and dictionary learning represent just a few examples, ongoing research continues to explore and develop advanced techniques tailored to the complexities of high-dimensional neuroimaging data [26].
