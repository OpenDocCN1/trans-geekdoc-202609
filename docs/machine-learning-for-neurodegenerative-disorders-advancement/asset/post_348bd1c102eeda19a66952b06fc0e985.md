As demonstrated in Figure 4.3, Huntington's disease is caused by a genetic mutation in the huntingtin gene (HTT), which leads to the production of an abnormal version of the huntingtin protein. This mutation involves an expanded guanine-adenine-guanine (GAG) nucleotide repeat in the gene. Normally, this is repeated 10 to 35 times within the gene, but with Huntington's disease, it is repeated 36 to more than 120 times [62, 63]. As a result, an abnormal version of the huntingtin protein is produced, which accumulates in the brain, causing the degeneration of neurons. This degeneration primarily affects the basal ganglia and cerebral cortex, leading to impairments in movement, cognition, and behavior associated with the disease [64]. Huntington's syndrome is unique among neurodegenerative disorders due to its genetic predictability; children of a parent with Huntington's disease have a 50% chance of inheriting the faulty gene [65]. There is currently no cure for Huntington's disease, and there is no way to slow or stop the brain damage it causes. However, there are treatments available to help manage symptoms of the disease and improving quality of life [66].

Treatment for Huntington's disease focuses on managing symptoms and improving quality of life [67]. Medications are often employed to manage mood disorders associated with Huntington's disease. Table 4.3 presents an overview of current pharmacological strategies for treating Huntington's disease. Several therapeutic approaches targeting various aspects of the disease's symptoms and underlying causes are highlighted. Dopamine receptor blockers are common in Huntington's although they can lead to side effects such as dystonia and psychosis [68]. Glutamate modulators, including memantine, aim to modulate glutamate, reducing excitotoxicity and offering neuroprotective benefits [69]. Specifically selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), such as fluoxetine; and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), such as duloxetine, also manage the mood disorders and depression frequently associated with Huntington's disease [70, 71]. Tetrabenazine, which targets vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2), is employed to control chorea and can be used for depression [72]. Innovative gene silencing therapies, such as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), with tomersen as a prime example, represent a novel approach that targets the genetic root of the disease. These therapies work by reducing the production of mutant huntingtin protein and are currently under clinical investigation, potentially offering a way to modify the progression of the disease.

### 4.2.1.4 Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, also known as Lou Gehrig's disease, is a progressive neurodegenerative disease that affects nerve cells in the brain and the spinal cord. First identified in 1824, ALS is responsible for nearly 30,000 deaths annually worldwide [1]. Projections indicate a significant increase in the incidence of ALS, with an expected increase of 69% by the year 2040 [73]. The hallmark of ALS is the gradual degradation and death of motor neurons, which are essential for controlling muscle movement [74]. As both the upper motor neurons (located in the brain) and the lower motor neurons (located in the spinal cord) die, this loss of neurons leads to a reduction in the brain's ability to initiate and control movement, resulting in progressive weakness and atrophy of muscles. The onset of ALS can be subtle, where patients progressively experience muscle weakness, twitching, and cramping, affecting speaking, swallowing, and breathing [75]. The progression of ALS varies among individuals, with the average life expectancy ranging from two to five years from the time of diagnosis. However, some individuals with ALS live ten or more years [76, 77].

The exact cause of ALS is not well understood, and it is believed to involve a combination of genetic and environmental factors. In approximately 10% of cases ALS is inherited, and in these familial cases, mutations in several genes have been identified [77]. The most common genetic cause is a mutation in the C9orf72 gene, followed by mutations in the SOD1, TARDBP, and FUS genes [78]. The cause of the majority of ALS cases, which are sporadic, remains unknown. Researchers are investigating a multitude of potential causes, including chemical imbalances, disrupted immune responses, and mishandling of proteins within nerve cells [79]. One of the features observed in both familial and sporadic ALS is the abnormal accumulation of proteins in motor neurons, which leads to cell death [80].

There is no cure for ALS, and treatment focuses on managing symptoms and maintaining quality of life. Table 4.4 provides a comprehensive overview of the enhanced pharmacological approaches for treating ALS. Riluzole, which targets glutamate release, is a significant therapeutic approach; the first drug approved for ALS, has shown efficacy in slightly prolonging patient survival by reducing damage [73, 81]. Antioxidant therapy, with edaravone as a key example, targets oxidative stress, a factor believed to contribute to ALS progression. This therapy has been shown to potentially slow functional decline in the early stages of ALS [82]. Table 4.4 also highlights ongoing research into anti-inflammatory agents (e.g., quercetin) aimed at mitigating inflammation with ALS, which could slow progression [83]. Gene therapy represents a cutting-edge approach, focusing on specific genetic mutations found in familial forms of ALS. ASOs targeting such SOD1 or C9orf72 are in experimental stages, reflecting the move toward personalized medicine in ALS treatment. Stem cell therapy, which is still in clinical trials, offers a promising avenue through the potential use of stem cells to regenerate or repair neurons, providing a novel strategy for neurogeneration and neuroprotection in ALS [84]. Each of these reflects the ongoing efforts and innovations in tackling the challenges of ALS, offering hope for more effective treatments in the future.

## 4.2.2 TRADITIONAL APPROACHES TO DRUG DISCOVERY AND REPURPOSING

The traditional drug discovery pipeline is a complex and multifaceted process that often spans more than a decade and involves a significant investment of resources. Typically, target identification and validation involve identifying and confirming the biological target, usually a protein or gene, that is implicated in a disease [86]. The rationale is that modulating this target through a drug will have a therapeutic effect. This stage is crucial and requires a deep understanding of disease biology, often derived from basic research in areas such as genetics, molecular biology, and cell biology [87]. Following target validation, researchers have engaged in high-throughput screening, where libraries of chemicals are tested for their ability to interact with the target. This opens up the use of advanced robotics, data processing, and sensitive detectors to assess thousands of compounds rapidly [9].

After identifying potential compounds, the next phase is lead optimization. In this stage, researchers focus on improving the properties of compounds to improve their effectiveness as drugs. This involves refining their binding to the target, increasing their stability in the body, reducing potential side effects, and improving their ability to reach the target within the body. This process of optimization requires a series of iterative cycles in which compounds are chemically modified, tested, and refined. Once a candidate has shown promise in these preclinical trials, it enters clinical trials, which are rigorous tests in humans for assessing the drug's safety, efficacy, dosage, and side effects. Clinical trials are typically conducted in three phases: phase I assess a small group of people; phase II trials evaluate efficacy and side effects; and phase III trials compare the new drug to standard treatments in a larger population. Population is not only time-consuming but also expensive, with costs running to billions of dollars [9].
