# 2.5 Random Forests

Disease identification and classification in neuroimaging have been significantly advanced through the application of ML techniques, and random forests (RFs) stand out as a robust algorithm in this context. Random forest, an ensemble learning method, consists of an ensemble of decision trees trained on random subsets of the data. In neuroimaging, RF has been widely employed for accurate and reliable classification tasks due to its ability to handle high-dimensional and complex datasets effectively.

An illustrative example of the successful application of RFs in disease identification is found in the work by Khedher et al. [36]. The study employed RF to discriminate between individuals with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), mild cognitive impairment (CI), and healthy controls using multimodal neuroimaging data, including structural MRI and PET scans. The results demonstrated the efficacy of RFs in accurately classifying different cognitive states, showcasing its potential as a valuable tool for early diagnosis and intervention in neurodegenerative diseases.

The versatility of RFs extends to various neurological and psychiatric conditions, making it a popular choice in disease classification tasks. Their ability to handle feature importance, non-linearity, and interactions among features makes RFs well suited for capturing patterns in neuroimaging data. As ML applications in neuroimaging continue to expand, the role of RFs in disease identification and classification is expected to further contribute to our understanding of various brain disorders [36].

# 2.5.3 Deep Learning Approaches

Disease identification and classification in neuroimaging have been revolutionized by the advent of deep learning approaches. Deep learning, particularly CNNs and RNNs, has demonstrated remarkable capabilities in automatically learning patterns and representations from complex neuroimaging data. CNNs, with their ability to capture hierarchical features, have been extensively applied to tasks such as brain tumor classification in MRI [37]. RNNs, on the other hand, are well suited for sequential data and have found applications in tasks like predicting neurological disease progression over longitudinal data [38].
