# 4.2 BACKGROUND

# 4.2.1 NEURODEGENERATIVE DISORDERS

Neurodegenerative disorders are a group of diseases characterized by gradual degeneration of the structure and function of the nervous system. They are known for their progressive nature and typically result in various cognitive and physical impairments [12]. This degenerative process can vary in its onset, rate of progression, and symptoms, depending on the specific disorder. These diseases manifest through a range of symptoms, including memory loss, impaired motor function, altered speech, and changes in behavior and personality. The causes of neurodegenerative diseases are complex and multifactorial and involve a combination of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. While some diseases, such as Huntington's disease, are clearly linked to genetic mutations, others such as Alzheimer's disease, involve a mix of hereditary and other risk factors [14]. The pathophysiology of this disease typically involves the accumulation of abnormal proteins, which lead to neural damage. For example, in Alzheimer's disease, amyloid plaques and tau proteinles are characteristic, while in Parkinson's disease, levels of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra is a key feature. These disorders are known for their debilitating nature, significantly impacting the quality of life and placing a considerable burden on caregivers. The progressive nature of these disorders often worsen over time, with patients eventually requiring comprehensive care. Current treatments primarily focus on managing symptoms and improving quality of life, as research continues to evolve, there is hope for a better understanding of these diseases, which could lead to more effective treatments and potentially preventive strategies [12]. This section aims to explore four major neurodegenerative disorders, providing insight into their distinct characteristics, understanding, and therapeutic approaches.
