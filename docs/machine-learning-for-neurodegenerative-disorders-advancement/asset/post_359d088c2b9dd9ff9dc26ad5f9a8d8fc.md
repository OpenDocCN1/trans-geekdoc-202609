### 8.1.1 BACKGROUND ON AUTISM SPECTRUM DISORDER

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a multifaceted and pervasive neurodevelopmental condition characterized by a wide array of challenges that predominantly affect social interactions, communication abilities, and the presence of repetitive or restrictive behaviors [1]. The term "spectrum" captures the extensive variability in symptoms, skills, and levels of impairment affecting individuals with ASD. Some individuals display social difficulties, while others experience profound communication barriers and behavioral challenges. One of the hallmark features of ASD is its early onset, typically manifesting in the first few years of life. Parents and caregivers may observe signs such as delayed development, lack of eye contact, or an absence of interest in peer interactions. However, the presentation can be subtle, leading to delays in recognition and diagnosis. This variability necessitates a comprehensive and individualized approach to assessment and intervention [2].

Over the past decades, there has been a notable and steady increase in the prevalence of ASD. Current global estimates suggest that approximately 1 in 54 children are affected by this condition [3]. Several factors contribute to this prevalence, including heightened awareness among healthcare professionals and the public, improved diagnostic criteria, and potential environmental influences. Nonetheless, the exact reasons remain a subject of ongoing research and discussion within the scientific community. The escalating prevalence of ASD accentates the critical importance of accuracy and timely detection is not merely about labeling; it serves as the gateway to intervention strategies that can significantly influence developmental trajectories. Interventions initiated during the early developmental windows, such as behavioral therapies, speech and language therapy, and social skills training, have demonstrated efficacy in mitigating some of the challenges associated with ASD [4]. These interventions can enhance communication abilities, foster social engagement, and reduce maladaptive behaviors, thereby improving the quality of life for individuals with ASD and their families.

### 8.1.2 CHALLENGES IN DIAGNOSING ASD

Diagnosing ASD is a complex and challenging process due to the disorder's highly variable presentation and the absence of definitive methods. The term “spectrum” reflects the wide range of symptoms, behaviors, and levels of functioning associated with ASD. Some individuals exhibit subtle signs, as difficulties with social interactions or mild communication challenges, others have more pronounced symptoms, including severe language deficits, repetitive behaviors, or sensory sensitivities. This variability makes it difficult to establish a one-size-fits-all diagnostic criteria, and as ASD diagnosing ASD is the lack of a reliable medical test or marker that can definitively identify the condition. Unlike medical conditions that can be diagnosed through blood tests, imaging, or genetic markers, ASD is diagnosed primarily on the basis of behavioral observations and developmental history. The reliance on clinical assessment means that the diagnostic process can be highly subjective. Clinicians must interpret a range of behaviors and milestones, which can vary depending on the environment, cultural background, and individual.

Furthermore, the process of diagnosing ASD often involves evaluations by different specialists, including pediatricians, psychologists, and speech-language pathologists. Each specialist may differ in the evaluation, which can contribute to inconsistencies in diagnosis. The absence of standardized tools also contributes to delays in diagnosing ASD. Early signs of the disorder can be subtle and may not be recognized by parents or caregivers, especially if they are not familiar with pivotal milestones. When concerns are raised, the process of obtaining a formal diagnosis can be lengthy, involving long waiting times for specialist assessments. During this time, child may miss out on early services that could significantly improve their outcomes.

Moreover, there is the risk of misdiagnosis. Some symptoms of ASD, such as difficulties with social communication or repetitive behaviors, can overlap with other developmental disorders, including attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or language disorders. This overlap can lead to incorrect diagnoses, where a child is either diagnosed with ASD or another condition, which can affect the type of support and interventions they receive. Misdiagnosis can have long-term implications, as the interventions for developmental disorders are often distinct.

In addition to these challenges, there are also disparities in the diagnosis of ASD across different populations. Research has shown that minority groups and children from lower socioeconomic backgrounds are often diagnosed later than their peers, if at all. These disparities can be attributed to a variety of factors, including lack of access to healthcare, cultural differences in recognizing symptoms, and biases within the healthcare system. Late or missed diagnoses in these populations can exacerbate inequalities in early intervention and support services.

### 8.1.3 ROLE OF DATA SCIENCE IN ASD PREDICTION

In recent years, the field of data science has become increasingly pivotal in advancing the diagnosis and understanding of ASD. The integration of data-driven techniques has opened up new possibilities for enhancing the precision, speed, and overall effectiveness of ASD diagnosis. By leveraging large-scale datasets and sophisticated algorithms, data science can uncover patterns and correlations that might be imperceptible through traditional diagnostic methods. One of the most promising aspects of ASD diagnosis is the use of ensemble models, particularly algorithms like voting and AdaBoost. These models are designed to combine the strengths of multiple learning algorithms to produce more accurate predictions than any single algorithm could achieve on its own.
