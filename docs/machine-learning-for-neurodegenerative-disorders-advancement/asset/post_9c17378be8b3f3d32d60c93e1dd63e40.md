## 2.3.2 APPLICATIONS IN NEUROIMAGING

Neuroimaging has found diverse and impactful applications across various domains, revolutionizing our understanding of the brain's structure and function. One prominent application lies in the realm of disease diagnosis and monitoring. Techniques such as MRI and PET are pivotal in detecting and characterizing disorders like Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and multiple sclerosis. The ability to visualize structural abnormalities, track disease progression, and assess treatment has significantly advanced clinical practice [13].

Functional neuroimaging, particularly with methods like fMRI, enables the study of brain activity and connectivity during cognitive tasks or rest. This has profound implications for cognitive neuroscience, offering insights into how the brain processes information, perceives stimuli, and forms networks. In the field of psychology, fMRI is employed to investigate neural correlates of mental processes, emotions, and psychiatric conditions.

Neuroimaging also plays a crucial role in research, facilitating the exploration of brain development and plasticity [10]. Longitudinal studies employing techniques like DTI and structural MRI contribute to our understanding of how the brain changes over the lifespan and adapts to various experiences.

Moreover, neuroimaging has become a valuable tool in the field of neuropharmacology, aiding in the development and evaluation of pharmaceutical interventions. Functional imaging techniques like PET are employed to study neurotransmitter systems, providing insights into drug mechanisms and guiding the development of novel therapies for psychiatric and neurological disorders.

As the field advances, emerging applications include the integration of neuroimaging with ML for predictive, personalized medicine, and the identification of biomarkers for early detection. The interdisciplinary nature of neuroimaging continues to foster collaborations between neuroscience, computer science, and clinical medicine, driving innovation and expanding the range of applications in this dynamic field [14].

## 2.4 PREPROCESSING TECHNIQUES

### 2.4.1 SEGMENTATION

In the domain of neuroimaging, preprocessing techniques play a pivotal role in enhancing the quality and interpretability of imaging data. Among these techniques, segmentation is a fundamental step, involving the partitioning of the brain into anatomical or functional regions. Segmentation is crucial in delineating brain structures, allowing for the isolation of regions of interest (ROIs) and subsequent quantitative analysis. Various neuroimaging modalities, such as MRI and PET, benefit from segmentation approaches to extract meaningful information about brain anatomy and function.

A comprehensive review by Ardekani et al. [15] discusses the importance of segmentation in neuroimaging and highlights the challenges associated with accurate and reliable segmentation. Challenges include variations in imaging protocols, image artifacts, and the inherent complexity of brain structures. The review emphasizes the significance of incorporating advanced computational methods, such as algorithms, to improve the accuracy of segmentation and address these challenges effectively [15].

Furthermore, the work of Fisch [16] presents an influential approach to cortical and subcortical segmentation using FreeSurfer, a widely used software in neuroimaging research. FreeSurfer employs a combination of probabilistic and atlas-based methods to perform automated segmentation of brain structures, providing detailed and accurate reconstructions.

In conclusion, segmentation as a preprocessing technique in neuroimaging is indispensable for extracting meaningful information from complex imaging data. The continuous development of advanced computational methods, as highlighted in the referenced works, contributes to improving the accuracy and reliability of segmentation techniques, ultimately enhancing our understanding of the intricate structures and functions of the brain [16].

### 2.4.1.1 Traditional Methods

Traditional methods in neuroimaging refer to the conventional techniques that have been foundational in studying brain structure and function. MRI and CT are classic examples of medical imaging. MRI, based on the principle of nuclear resonance, provides high-resolution images of soft tissues, allowing for detailed visualization of the brain’s anatomy. CT, on the other hand, employs X-rays to generate cross-sectional images, offering valuable information about structures and certain brain pathologies. These traditional methods have been instrumental in clinical diagnostics, aiding in the detection and characterization of various neurological disorders [17].

The work of Lauterbur and Damadian [17, 18] made a seminal contribution to the development of MRI, for they were later awarded the Nobel Prize in physiology or medicine. Their groundbreaking research laid the foundation for the non-invasive imaging of brain structures, revolutionizing diagnostics and neuroscience. Additionally, CT, pioneered by Hounsfield [19], represented a significant leap in imaging technology, for three-dimensional visualization of the brain and the identification of structural abnormalities.

While traditional neuroimaging methods have been indispensable in clinical practice, they are limited in their ability to capture dynamic brain processes [18]; MRI, an innovation that emerged in the 1990s, introduced a paradigm shift by studying the brain activity in real time, complementing the information provided by traditional methods.

In summary, traditional neuroimaging methods have played a crucial role in our understanding of brain structure and pathology. The foundational contributions of Lauterbur, Damadian, Hounsfield, and others have paved the way for subsequent developments, the integration of traditional methods with more advanced techniques, contributing to a comprehensive approach in studying the brain [19].

### 2.4.1.2 Advancements with CNNs

Recent advancements in neuroimaging have been significantly influenced by the integration of CNNs, a class of deep learning architectures, excel in automatically learning hierarchical features from data, making them particularly well suited for tasks such as image recognition and segmentation. In the context of neuroimaging, CNNs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in tasks ranging from anatomical segmentation in MRI to the identification of pathological regions in PET scans.

A study by Pawan and Montana [20] showcased the potential of CNNs in medical image analysis, demonstrating their effectiveness in segmenting brain tumor regions from MRI. This work highlighted the ability of CNNs to automatically learn discriminative features, eliminating the need for handcrafted features in the segmentation process. Furthermore, the research of Hosseini et al. [21] applied CNNs to automatically classify Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment, emphasizing the network’s power in capturing subtle patterns of neurological disorders [20].

The application of CNNs in functional neuroimaging has also been notable. For instance, CNNs have been employed to decode cognitive states from fMRI data, providing insights into the neural representation of various cognitive processes. Additionally, the integration of CNNs with multi-modal imaging approaches has expanded their utility in capturing complex relationships within heterogeneous datasets.

These advancements underscore the potential of CNNs in automating and improving the efficiency of neuroimaging analyses. As the field continues to evolve, CNNs are expected to play a crucial role in unraveling the complexities of brain structure and function, offering new avenues for personalized medicine, early disease detection, and treatment monitoring [21].
