## 2.5.3.2 Transfer Learning

Disease identification and classification in neuroimaging has witnessed significant advancements through the application of deep learning approaches, particularly transfer learning. Transfer learning leverages pre-trained network models on large datasets and adapts them to smaller datasets [40]. This is particularly beneficial in neuroimaging, acquiring labeled data for deep learning is often limited due to sample sizes. The authors utilized a pre-trained 3D CNN on a large dataset and fine-tuned it for Alzheimer’s disease prediction, achieving superior performance. Transfer learning has also been successfully applied to tasks such as brain tumor classification in MRI, as shown in the work by Zhou et al. [41]. By transferring knowledge from large-scale image datasets, these approaches enable more accurate and robust disease identification, with limited labeled neuroimaging data [41].

## 2.6 REGRESSION AND PREDICTIVE MODELING

### 2.6.1 Linear REGRESSION MODELS

Linear regression models, a fundamental technique in ML, have found valuable applications in neuroimaging analysis and interpretation. Linear regression enables the modeling of the relationship between independent variables and a dependent variable, allowing researchers to quantify the impact of specific features on brain-related outcomes. In neuroimaging studies, linear regression has been employed for tasks such as identifying associations between brain structure and function and clinical variables [42]. For example, in the work by Holmes et al. [42], linear regression was used to investigate the relationship between cortical thickness and cognitive performance in individuals with multiple sclerosis. Another notable application is the study by Yendi et al. [42], where linear regression was utilized to examine the association between white matter integrity and cognitive abilities. These examples illustrate the versatility of linear regression models in elucidating neuroimaging features and establishing connections between brain measures and clinical or cognitive variables [43].

### 2.6.3 TIME-SERIES ANALYSIS IN fMRI

Time-series analysis in fMRI is a crucial aspect of neuroimaging that involves the examination of brain activity over time [10]. fMRI captures the blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal, providing a temporal record of neural activity. Time-series analysis techniques aim to extract meaningful information from these temporal signals, enabling the investigation of dynamic patterns and connectivity within the brain.

One common approach in time-series analysis is seed-based correlation analysis, where the correlation between this seed and other brain regions is computed [30]. This method provides insights into functional patterns and has widely used to explore resting-state networks. Another technique is ICA, which decomposes fMRI data into spatially and temporally independent components, revealing distinct networks of activated brain regions [45].

Dynamic connectivity analysis goes beyond static connectivity by examining how patterns change over time. This is particularly relevant for understanding the temporal dynamics of brain networks during cognitive tasks or resting. Additionally, graph-based analyses assess the brain's functional connectivity as a complex network, characterizing the organization of nodes (brain regions) and edges (connectivity) over time.

Time-series analysis in fMRI plays a crucial role in unveiling the temporal dynamics of brain function and connectivity. These techniques contribute to our understanding of cognitive processes, neural plasticity, and the altered patterns associated with neurological and psychiatric disorder [46].

#### 2.6.3.1 Dynamic Time Warping

Dynamic time warping (DTW) is a powerful technique in time-series analysis that allows for the comparison and alignment of time-varying sequences, even when they exhibit temporal distortions. Originally introduced in the context of speech recognition, DTW has widespread applications in various fields, including neuroscience and neuroimaging. DTW addresses the challenge of comparing time-series data with different patterns, making it particularly relevant for fMRI.

In neuroimaging, DTW has been used to align time-series, making it particularly relevant for fMRI. For instance, the work by Liao et al. [47] used DTW to align time-series, providing valuable insights into functional connectivity. One of the strengths of DTW is its flexibility to handle variable lengths, showcasing its application in EEG and MEG [48].

While DTW is powerful, it can be computationally intensive. Nevertheless, it remains an essential tool for analyzing time-series data, contributing to our understanding of brain dynamics [48].

#### 2.6.3.2 Recurrent Neural Networks

Recurrent neural networks represent a class of neural network architectures designed for sequential data, capable of capturing temporal dependencies. Unlike feedforward neural networks, RNNs have connections that loop back on themselves, allowing them to maintain a memory of previous inputs. This architecture makes RNNs well-suited for tasks involving time-series and sequential data.

In neuroimaging, RNNs have been employed to analyze and model sequential activity, leading to improved performance in various applications [44]. However, traditional RNNs face challenges such as vanishing or exploding gradients, limiting their effectiveness in capturing long-term dependencies. Gated recurrent units (GRUs) have been introduced as specialized neural architectures with improved memory and gradient flow. These variants have enhanced the capability of RNNs to capture sequential dependencies [38].

## 2.7 CONNECTIVITY ANALYSIS AND GRAPH THEORY

### 2.7.1 The Brain As a Network

Connectivity analysis and graph theory have become pivotal in understanding the organization of the brain as a complex network. The brain is not only an intricate graph of specialized regions but also a network of interconnected nodes, where each node represents a distinct brain region, and edges signify the functional or structural connections between them [46]. Connectivity analysis, often employed in neuroimaging studies using techniques like fMRI or DTI, allows researchers to investigate how different brain regions communicate and collaborate. Graph theory provides a mathematical framework to analyze and quantify the topological properties of these networks, revealing crucial insights into the brain’s structural and functional organization [10].

The application of connectivity analysis and graph theory has greatly enhanced our understanding of normal brain function, cognitive processes, and neurological disorders. Studies using these techniques have investigated diverse topics, including the identification of hubs in the brain network associated with higher cognitive functions and the alterations in network properties in conditions Alzheimer’s disease or schizophrenia. Overall, connectivity analysis and graph theory provide a powerful framework to explore the brain’s intricate architecture and offer valuable insights into both healthy and pathological states [51].
