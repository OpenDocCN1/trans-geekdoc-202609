In parallel, logistic regression emerges as another cornerstone in the machine-learning arsenal for Alzheimer's detection. Similar to SVMs, logistic regression undergoes fine-tuning procedures, typically involving application of L2 penalty and varying regularization parameters to refine model behavior. Logistic regression, with its simplicity and interpretability, provides a complementary approach to SVMs, offering insights into the relationships between predictor variables and the likelihood of AD occurrence. Decision trees are foundational components of ML algorithms, widely applied in various domains, including AD detection. In this context, decision trees are initially trained without tuning, allowing the algorithm to create a tree structure based on features extracted from the dataset. Subsequently, grid search techniques are employed to optimize parameters such as tree depth and evaluation criterion, which may include the Gini index or entropy. Decision trees offer interpretability but are susceptible to overfitting, requiring pruning and parameter optimization to enhance generalization and model robustness. Despite these limitations, decision trees provide valuable insights into the salient features contributing to AD diagnosis, in the development of effective diagnostic models [22].

Random forests represent an ensemble learning technique designed to address the limitations of individual decision trees. The aggregating predictions from multiple decision trees improve performance and stability. Each decision tree in the ensemble is trained independently, and the final prediction is determined through voting or averaging mechanisms. This approach mitigates overfitting and variance issues enhances robustness of the model. Random forests incorporate grid search and cross-validation techniques to handle large and high-dimensional datasets, random forests are well suited for AD detection tasks, where identifying patterns in complex data is crucial for accurate diagnosis. In conclusion, random forests offer a powerful framework for developing reliable and effective models for AD diagnosis, facilitating early detection and intervention to improve patient outcomes.

Deep learning architectures represent cutting-edge methodologies for analyzing complex datasets, including those pertaining to AD detection. DL frameworks, constructed by multiple layers of interconnected neural networks, facilitate the extraction of intricate patterns and relationships within heterogeneous data modalities. In the context of AD research, DL architectures offer a powerful framework for processing diverse datasets encompassing neuroimaging scans, scan genetic markers, and clinical records. These architectures leverage convolutional, pooling, fully connected, and normalization layers to learn hierarchical representations of data, enabling effective classification and diagnostic inference [23].

CNNs have been extensively applied in AD research, with various architectures including LeNet-5, VGG19, and custom-designed models tailored to neuroimaging datasets. LeNet-5, originally developed for handwritten digit recognition, has been repurposed for binary classification tasks involving AD and healthy controls. VGG19, a deeper CNN architecture comprising multiple convolutional layers, exhibits superior performance in tasks requiring classification among multiple groups. Custom-designed CNN models leverage domain-specific knowledge and data augmentation techniques to enhance diagnostic accuracy and generalization capabilities. Overall, CNNs represent a versatile effective tool for AD detection, leveraging the potential to utilize novel biomarkers and advance our understanding of disease mechanisms [5]. Convolutional autoencoders represent a specialized variant of autoencoder architectures tailored for image data and feature extraction. In the task of AD detection, convolutional autoencoders are adept at capturing intricate patterns and spatial relationships with neuroimaging data, such as MRI and PET images. Convolutional autoencoders hold significant promise in AD research due to their ability to learn meaningful representations of neuroimaging data without supervision. By minimizing reconstruction error, convolutional autoencoders enable researchers to identify subtle anomalies and biomarkers with AD progression. Additionally, the unsupervised nature of convolutional autoencoders enables the discovery of novel imaging biomarkers and patterns that may elude supervised learning approaches [24].

The architecture of LeNet-5 enables the efficient extraction of spatial and temporal features of AD pathology. Its hierarchical structure allows for the hierarchical abstraction of features, enabling the model to capture both local and global patterns relevant to disease diagnosis. LeNet-5 has been successfully applied in binary classification tasks involving AD and normal control subjects, demonstrating its efficacy making a valuable tool for uncovering functional brain networks associated with AD progression [25, 26].

VGG19 is a CNN architecture renowned for its depth and effectiveness in image classification. Comprising 19 layers, including multiple convolutional and fully connected layers, VGG99 offers a powerful framework for analyzing complex image data, including neuroimaging scans relevant to AD detection. In the context of Alzheimer's research, VGG19 has been leveraged to classify neuroimaging data derived from MRI and PET scans, facilitating the identification of patterns indicative of AD pathology [24].

Fully connected layers at the end of the network integrate extracted features and perform classification. VGG19's depth and complexity make it well-suited for capturing intricate patterns in neuroimaging data, enhancing diagnostic accuracy, and enabling early detection of AD [27, 28].

Recurrent networks represent a class of neural architectures designed to process sequential data, making them particularly well suited for time series analysis and natural language processing tasks. In the task of AD detection, RNNs offer a powerful framework for analyzing longitudinal patient data, including cognitive assessments, genetic markers, and clinical records. RNNs can exploit temporal dependencies within sequential data, enabling the identification of patterns and trends indicate disease progression. The architecture of RNNs includes recurrent connections that allow information to persist over time, enabling the model to capture temporal dynamics and long-term dependencies within sequential data. This characteristic makes RNNs effective for modeling AD progression, where changes in cognitive function and clinical symptoms evolve over time [29].
