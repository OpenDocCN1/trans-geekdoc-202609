# 6.1 INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a common neurological illness that can be fatal. It is marked by a gradual deterioration in cognitive function, memory loss, and behavioral abnormalities. Neurotic plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, and the degradation of nerve cells are its hallmarks [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) and other organizations report that the prevalence of AD is startlingly high and is on the rise as a result of aging populations. To highlight the need for efficient diagnostic techniques, we use the most recent statistics. These organizations estimate that 50 million people globally suffer from AD, and that figure rise to 152 million by 2050. More than 5 million people in the US alone suffer from AD, which is higher in women than in men. This disease has a significant financial and psychological impact on individuals, families, and healthcare systems; the global expenses are estimated to be $1 trillion [2, 3]. Figure 6.1 shows Medicare and Medicaid estimates for the cost of AD (in millions of US dollars) in women during the upcoming 2050s. Estimated on variable prevalence documented in several large studies, and although gender distribution is not supported by all studies, the prevalence of AD is higher in women than men. In the age group 65–69, 0.7% of women and 0.6% of men suffer from the disease, increasing frequencies of 14.2% and 8.8% in individuals aged 85–89 years.

With a person with AD typically has five years to live after diagnosis, it is critical to establish efficient diagnosis and treatment plans. In order to enhance early diagnosis and treatment results, this project seeks to perform a thorough assessment of the literature on the detection of AD with a particular focus on the integration of modern imaging techniques and artificial intelligence (AI) methodology [4, 5]. Prior review articles have mostly focused on particular datasets and traditional diagnostic techniques, frequently ignoring the examination of AI-driven strategies and the possible incorporation of genomics. In order to fill these gaps, we offer a thorough analysis of deep learning (DL) and machine learning (ML) models for AD diagnosis. Additionally, we investigate the potential benefits of merging genomic and imaging data .
