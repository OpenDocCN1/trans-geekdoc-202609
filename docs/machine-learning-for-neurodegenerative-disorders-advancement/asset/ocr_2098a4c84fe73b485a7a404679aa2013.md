The exact cause of ALS is not well understood, and it is believed to involve a combination of genetic and environmental factors. In approximately 10% of cases ALS is inherited, and in these familial cases, mutations in several genes have been identified [77]. The most common genetic cause is a mutation in the C9orf72 gene, followed by mutations in the SOD1, TARDBP, and F genes [78]. The cause of the majority of ALS cases, which are sporadic, remains unknown. Researchers are investigating a multitude of potential causes, including chemical imbalances, disrupted immune responses, and mishandling of proteins within nerve cells [79]. One of the features observed in both familial and sporadic ALS is the abnormal accumulation of proteins in motor neurons, which leads to cell death [80].

There is no cure for ALS, and treatment focuses on managing symptoms and maintaining quality of life. Table 4.4 provides a comprehensive overview of the enhanced pharmacological approaches for treating ALS. Riluzole, which targets glutamate release, is a significant therapeutic approach; the first drug approved for ALS, has shown efficacy in slightly prolonging patient survival by reducing damage [73, 81]. Antioxidant therapy, with edaravone as a key example, targets oxidative stress, a factor believed to contribute to ALS progression. This therapy has been shown to potentially solve functional decline in the early stages of ALS [82]. Table 4.4 also highlights ongoing research into anti-inflammatory agents (e.g., quercetin) aimed at mitigating inflammation with ALS, which could slow progression [83]. Gene therapy represents a cutting-edge approach, focusing on specific genetic mutations found in familial forms of ALS. ASOs targeting such as SOD1 or C9orf72 are in experimental stages, reflecting the move toward personalized medicine in ALS treatment.
