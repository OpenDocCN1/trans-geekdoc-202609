The accumulation of Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in the brain leads to the dysfunction and death of neurons, which in turn causes memory failure, problems carrying daily activities, and other symptoms of Alzheimer's disease [21]. The exact cause of Alzheimer's disease is still largely unknown, it is believed to result from a combination of genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors that affect the brain over time [22].

There is no known cure for Alzheimer's disease, but treatments for symptoms are available, and research continues [10]. Current treatment focuses on helping people maintain mental function, manage behavioral symptoms, and control certain symptoms of Alzheimer's disease [23]. Many drugs used to treat it are cholinesterase inhibitors, which work by boosting the levels of cell-to-cell communication in the brain [24]. The therapeutic landscape for Alzheimer's disease is limited, with only four acetylcholterase inhibitors (AIs) receiving approval for its treatment. These include donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, and taurine. The primary mechanism of action for these ACEHis is to increase the concentration of acetylcholine by inhibiting the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, responsible for the breakdown of acetylcholine. This neurotransmitter is crucial for memory and learning functions, which are significantly impaired in Alzheimer's disease patients [24]. Another drug, is used to treat more severe Alzheimer's disease. It works by regulation of glutamate, another communication channel that can be overproduced in the brain, leading to cell death [25]. Apart from pharmacological interventions, lifestyle interventions such as exercise, mental stimulation, and a balanced diet might also play a role in managing this condition [31]. Research is also ongoing on the role of neuroinflammation and the immune system in Alzheimer's disease, with the hope that future treatments might better target these aspects of the disease [32]. Despite the current limitations in treatment, research on Alzheimer's disease is a rapidly evolving field, and new therapies are currently being investigated.

# 4.2.1.2 Parkinson's disease

Parkinson's disease is a chronic and progressive movement disorder whose symptoms continue to worsen over time [37]. It primarily affects the motor system, and its symptoms are the result of loss of dopamine-producing cells in the substantia nigra, a region of the midbrain [38]. The four primary symptoms of Parkinson's disease are tremors in the hands, arms, legs, and face; rigidity of the limbs and trunk; bradykinesia; slowness of movement; and postural instability, which leads to impaired balance and coordination [39]. As these symptoms become pronounced, patients may have difficulty walking, talking, or completing simple tasks [40].
