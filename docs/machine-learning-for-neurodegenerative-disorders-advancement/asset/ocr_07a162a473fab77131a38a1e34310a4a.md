While DTW has proven valuable in neuroimaging, it is essential to consider computational-complexity and parameter tuning in application. Nevertheless, DTW remains a valuable tool for exploring temporal dependencies in time-series data, contributing to a deeper understanding of dynamic processes in the brain [48].

# 2.6.3.2 Recurrent Neural Networks

Recurrent neural networks represent a class of neural network architectures designed to capture sequential dependencies in data, which may be particularly suited for tasks involving time-series analysis and sequential data. Unlike traditional feedforward neural networks, RNNs have connections that loop back on themselves, allowing them to maintain a memory of previous inputs. This architecture makes RNNs well-suited for capturing temporal dynamics and dependencies, making them valuable in applications such as natural language processing, speech recognition, and neuroimaging.

In neuroimaging, RNNs have been employed to analyze and model sequential brain activity, in the example of MRI for the modeling of dynamic patterns of brain function over time, facilitating the exploration of temporal aspects of neural activity during cognitive tasks or resting states [44]. The ability of RNNs to capture sequential dependencies has also been beneficial in tasks like predicting neurodegenerative disease progression from longitudinal imaging.

However, traditional RNNs face challenges such as vanishing or exploding gradients, limiting their effectiveness in capturing long-term dependencies. Gated recurrent units (GRUs) have been introduced as specialized neural architectures with improved memory and gradient flow. These variants have enhanced the capability of RNNs to model and interpret complex sequential patterns, leading to improved performance in various neuroimaging applications [38].

# 2.7 CONNECTIVITY ANALYSIS AND GRAPH THEORY

# 2.7.1 The Brain As a Network

Connectivity analysis and graph theory have become pivotal in understanding the organization of the brain as a complex network. The brain is not only an intricate graph of specialized regions but also a network of interconnected nodes, where each node represents a distinct brain region, and edges signify the functional or structural connections between them [46]. Connectivity analysis, often employed in neuroimaging studies using techniques like fMRI or DTI, allows researchers to investigate how different brain regions communicate and collaborate. Graph theory provides a mathematical framework to analyze and quantify the topological properties of these networks, revealing crucial insights into the brain’s structural and functional organization [10].

Functional connectivity assesses the temporal correlations between brain regions, elucidating patterns activity and resting-state networks [49]. Structural connectivity, on the other hand, examines the anatomical links between regions, often inferred from white matter tractography through DTI. Graph theory metrics such as centrality, cluster-coefficient, and modularity quantify to measure the network properties of the brain [50].

The application of connectivity analysis and graph theory has greatly enhanced our understanding of normal brain function, cognitive processes, and neurological disorders. Studies using these techniques have investigated diverse topics, including the identification of hubs in the brain network associated with higher cognitive functions and the alterations in network properties in conditions Alzheimer’s disease or schizophrenia. Overall, connectivity analysis and graph theory provide a powerful framework to explore the brain’s intricate architecture and offer valuable insights into both healthy and pathological states [51].
