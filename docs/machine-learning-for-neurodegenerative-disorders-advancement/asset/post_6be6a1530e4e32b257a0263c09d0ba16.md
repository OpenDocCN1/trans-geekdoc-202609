## 2.4.2 REGISTRATION

Image registration is a fundamental process in neuroimaging that involves alignment and spatially mapping images obtained from different modalities or at different times. This technique is essential for integrating information from multiple sources and facilitating meaningful comparisons across datasets. Registration is particularly crucial in studies involving longitudinal imaging, where it enables the tracking of structural or functional changes in the brain over time [22].

One common application of image registration is the alignment of structural and functional images from MRI. For instance, the work of Jenkins and Smith [22] introduced the FMRIB's Linear Image Registration Tool (FLIRT), a widely used tool for linear registration in neuroimaging. FLIRT allows for the precise alignment of different MRI modalities, facilitating the combination of anatomical and functional information for comprehensive analyses.

In the realm of PET and CT, registration techniques are essential for co-registering functional and anatomical images. The work by Woods et al. [23] outlined the mathematical principles of image registration and the Automated Image Registration (AIR) software, contributing significantly to the field. The AIR has been instrumental in aligning PET and CT images, enabling more accurate location of functional activities within the anatomical context.

The significance of image registration extends beyond anatomical alignment, encompassing applications such as inter-subject registration for population studies and spatial normalization for group analyses. As neuroimaging research advances, the development of sophisticated registration algorithms, such as nonlinear and deformable registration techniques, continues to refine the accuracy and reliability of image alignment, enhancing the interpretability of complex datasets [23].

## 2.4.3 DIMENSIONAL REDUCTION

Dimensionality reduction is a critical technique in neuroimaging that addresses the challenges associated with high-dimensional datasets, where the number of variables or features is significantly larger than the number of observations. The vast amount of information generated by neuroimaging modalities, such as MRI and fMRI, necessitates efficient methods for selection and extraction. Dimensionality reduction methods aim to preserve the essential information while reducing the dataset's complexity, facilitating straightforward analyses and interpretation.

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a widely employed dimensionality reduction technique in neuroimaging. By transforming the original variables into a new set of uncorrelated variables (principal components), PCA captures the most significant sources of variance in the data. This technique has been applied to tasks such as fMRI data analysis, where it aids identifying patterns of brain activity [24].

The work by Varquaux et al. [26] introduces the use of dictionary learning for dimensionality reduction in neuroimaging. By representing the data as a sparse combination of learned basis functions, dictionary learning captures essential features while discarding redundant information. While commonly in single-cell RNA sequencing, its application has been extended to neuroimaging studies for visualizing complex brain connectivity patterns [25].

In conclusion, dimensionality reduction methods play a crucial role in enhancing the interpretability and ability of neuroimaging. While PCA, t-SNE, and dictionary learning represent just a few examples, ongoing research continues to explore and develop advanced techniques tailored to the complexities of high-dimensional neuroimaging data [26].

### 2.4.3.1 PCA and ICA

Principal component analysis and independent component analysis (ICA) are two prominent dimensionality reduction techniques widely applied in neuroimaging to extract meaningful information from complex datasets. PCA, introduced by [27] is a linear transformation method that identifies the principal components (PCs) capturing the maximum variance in the data. In neuroimaging, PCA has been used for tasks such as reducing the dimensionality of fMRI data, uncovering latent structures, and enhancing signal-to-noise ratios [24]. By transforming the original variables into orthogonal components, PCA aids in simplifying the representation of neuroimaging data while retaining critical information.

On the other hand, ICA, proposed by [28] is a more recent and advanced technique that seeks to decompose a multivariate signal into statistically independent components. ICA proven particularly valuable in uncovering spatial and temporally distinct sources of neural activity in MRI data [29]. ICA assumes that the observed signals are mixtures of independent sources, enabling the separation of brain networks with unique functional roles. In the realm of neuroimaging, ICA has been applied to uncover resting-state networks, providing insights into the intrinsic functional architecture of the brain.

While PCA and ICA are powerful tools for dimensionality, they differ in their underlying assumptions and applications. PCA focuses on capturing variance, making well suited for denoising and feature extraction, while ICA emphasizes the stability of sources, enabling the identification of spatial and temporally distinct functional networks. The choice between PCA and ICA in neuroimaging studies depends on the specific goals and characteristics of the data [30].

### 2.4.3.2 Autoencoders

Autoencoders represent a powerful class of neural architectures widely employed in neuroimaging for unsupervised feature learning and dimensionality reduction. The fundamental idea behind autoencoders is to compress high-dimensional data into a lower-dimensional representation and then reconstruct the input from this compressed representation. This process enables the network to learn a compact and informative representation of the data. In the context of neuroimaging, autoencoders have been applied to various modalities, including MRI and fMRI.

The work of Hinton and Salakhutdin [31] introduced the concept of deep autoencoders, paving the way for the development of deep learning architectures. Deep autoencoders consist of multiple layers of encoding and decoding units, allowing the learning of hierarchical and abstract representations of complex neuroimaging data. Applications of deep autoencoders in neuroimaging include tasks such as denoising, feature extraction, and anomaly detection [32].

Furthermore, autoencoders have been integrated into generative models for the synthesis of realistic neuroimaging data. The variational autoencoder (VAE) combines autoencoders with probabilistic modeling, enabling the generation of diverse and realistic samples from the latent space. VAEs have been utilized in the generation of synthetic brain images, facilitating data augmentation and enhancing the training of latent models.

The versatility of autoencoders makes them valuable tools in neuroimaging research, providing a mean to learn representations of complex data.

## 2.5 Random Forests

Disease identification and classification in neuroimaging have been significantly advanced through the application of ML techniques, and random forests (RFs) stand out as a robust algorithm. Random forest, an ensemble learning method, consists of an ensemble of decision trees trained on random subsets of the data. An illustrative example of the successful application of RFs in disease identification is found in the work by Kher et al. [36]. The study employed RF to discriminate between individuals with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), mild cognitive impairment (CI), and healthy controls using multimodal neuroimaging data. The results demonstrated the efficacy of RFs in accurately classifying different cognitive states, showcasing its potential as a valuable tool for early diagnosis.

## 2.5.3 Deep Learning Approaches

Disease identification and classification in neuroimaging have been revolutionized by the advent of deep learning approaches. Deep learning, particularly CNNs and RNNs, has demonstrated remarkable capabilities in automatically learning patterns and representations from complex neuroimaging data. CNNs, with their ability to capture hierarchical features, have been extensively applied to tasks such as brain tumor classification in MRI [37]. RNNs, on the other hand, are well suited for sequential data and have found applications in tasks like predicting neurological disease progression over longitudinal data [38].
