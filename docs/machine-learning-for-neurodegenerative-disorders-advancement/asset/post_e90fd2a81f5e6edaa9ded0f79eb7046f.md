## 4.2 BACKGROUND

### 4.2.1 NEURODEGENERATIVE DISORDERS

Neurodegenerative disorders are a group of diseases characterized by gradual degeneration of the structure and function of the nervous system. They are known for their progressive nature and typically result in various cognitive and physical impairments [12]. This degenerative process can vary in its onset, rate of progression, and symptoms, depending on the specific disorder. These diseases manifest through a range of symptoms, including memory loss, impaired motor function, altered speech, and changes in behavior and personality. The causes of neurodegenerative diseases are complex and multifactorial and involve a combination of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. While some diseases, such as Huntington's disease, are clearly linked to genetic mutations, others such as Alzheimer's disease, involve a mix of hereditary and other risk factors [14]. The pathophysiology of this disease typically involves the accumulation of abnormal proteins, which lead to neural damage. For example, in Alzheimer's disease, amyloid plaques and tau tangles are characteristic, while in Parkinson's disease, levels of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra is a key feature. These disorders are known for their debilitating nature, significantly impacting the quality of life and placing a considerable burden on caregivers. The progressive nature of these disorders often worsen over time, with patients eventually requiring comprehensive care. Current treatments primarily focus on managing symptoms and improving quality of life, as research continues to evolve, there is hope for a better understanding of these diseases, which could lead to more effective treatments and potentially preventive strategies [12]. This section aims to explore four major neurodegenerative disorders, providing insight into their distinct characteristics, understanding, and therapeutic approaches.

#### 4.2.1.1 Alzheimer's Disease

Alzheimer's disease, initially recognized by Alois Alzheimer in 1907, is a progressive neurodegenerative condition that is a major public health concern. Although Alzheimer's disease is widespread and has profound effects, there is still no conclusive diagnosis available before death of disease development. It is projected by 2050, the number of Alzheimer's disease patients in the United States could reach approximately 13.8 million [15]. Alzheimer's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that primarily affects older adults, although early-onset cases do exist [10]. It is the most common cause of dementia and a general term for a decline in mental ability severe enough to interfere with daily life [16]. Alzheimer's disease is characterized by a gradual deterioration of cognitive functions, including memory, thinking, orientation, comprehension, calculation, learning capacity, language, and judgment [17]. However, consciousness is typically not affected. The disease begins with mild symptoms, such as forgetfulness and difficulty in remembering recent events or conversations. As the disease progresses, patients may experience memory loss; disorientation; mood and behavior changes; deepening confusion about events, time, and place; unfounded suspicions about family, friends, and professional caregivers; more serious memory loss and behavioral changes; and difficult speaking, swallowing, and walking [18].

The pathology of Alzheimer's disease is defined by two abnormalities (Figure 4.1): amyloid beta (β) protein plaques and neurofibrillary tangles. Amyloid beta is a fragment of a larger protein called amyloid precursor protein, which, when misfolded, forms plaques between neurons and disrupts cell function [19]. These proteins are natural products of brain cell activity. Glial cells usually manage small to moderate amounts of Aβ without issue. However, excessive production and accumulation of Aβ disrupt neuronal function. Originating from the amyloid precursor protein, AB peptides consist of 36–43 amino acids. The most common forms contain 40 and 42 amino acids (AB40 and A42), with the 42 and 43 amino acid variants showing a greater propensity for clustering together. These variants are primarily responsible for neuronal damage in Alzheimer's disease [19]. In contrast, neurofibrillary tangles are twisted fibers of another protein tau build up inside cells [20]. The accumulation of Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in the brain leads to the dysfunction and death of neurons, which in turn causes memory failure, problems carrying daily activities, and other symptoms of Alzheimer's disease [21]. The exact cause of Alzheimer's disease is still largely unknown, it is believed to result from a combination of genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors that affect the brain over time [22].

There is no known cure for Alzheimer's disease, but treatments for symptoms are available, and research continues [10]. Current treatment focuses on helping people maintain mental function, manage behavioral symptoms, and control certain symptoms of Alzheimer's disease [23]. Many drugs used to treat it are cholinesterase inhibitors, which work by boosting the levels of cell-to-cell communication in the brain [24]. The therapeutic landscape for Alzheimer's disease is limited, with only four acetylcholterase inhibitors (AChIs) receiving approval for its treatment. These include donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, and taurine. The primary mechanism of action for these AChIs is to increase the concentration of acetylcholine by inhibiting the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, responsible for the breakdown of acetylcholine. This neurotransmitter is crucial for memory and learning functions, which are significantly impaired in Alzheimer's disease patients [24]. Similarly, tau targeting agents inhibit the aggregation of tau proteins, which leads to neurofibrillary tangles, another key feature of Alzheimer's disease; for example, leuco-methylthionium bisomethylsulfone (LMTM) has been researched to disrupt these tangles [27]. Research is also ongoing on the role of neuroinflammation and the immune system in Alzheimer's disease, with the hope that future treatments might better target these aspects of the disease [32]. Despite the current limitations in treatment, research on Alzheimer's disease is a rapidly evolving field, and new therapies are currently being investigated.

#### 4.2.1.2 Parkinson's disease

Parkinson's disease is a chronic and progressive movement disorder whose symptoms continue to worsen over time [37]. It primarily affects the motor system, and its symptoms are the result of loss of dopamine-producing cells in the substantia nigra, a region of the midbrain [38]. The four primary symptoms of Parkinson's disease are tremors in the hands, arms, legs, and face; rigidity of the limbs and trunk; bradykinesia; slowness of movement; and postural instability, which leads to impaired balance and coordination [39]. As these symptoms become pronounced, patients may have difficulty walking, talking, or completing simple tasks [40].
