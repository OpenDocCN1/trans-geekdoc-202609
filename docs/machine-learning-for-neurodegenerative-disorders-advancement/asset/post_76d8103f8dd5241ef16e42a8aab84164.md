
## 4.3.2 TARGET IDENTIFICATION

The identification of appropriate targets, such as specific proteins, RNA molecules, or signaling pathways, is a foundational step in the drug discovery process. The efficacy of a drug largely depends on its ability to interact effectively with its intended target, which is typically a key component in the pathology of a disease [98]. In traditional drug discovery approaches, target identification is often labor intensive and time consuming and relies heavily on experimental biology and a deep understanding of disease mechanisms. However, with the integration of AI technologies, the landscape of drug target identification is undergoing a significant transformation [9].

Machine learning algorithms in AI are particularly adept at extracting vast amounts of genetic and molecular data to predict potential drug targets. These algorithms can analyze complex datasets that include genetic expression profile, protein-protein interaction network information, and information on various aspects of molecular pathways. AI can suggest novel targets that have been overlooked by traditional methods [99]. From genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to genes linked to specific diseases, genes or the proteins could be potential targets for new drugs [100]. In addition to revealing new targets, AI can also evaluate existing targets, perhaps identifying additional misses or suggesting new ways to modify them. This revelation could lead to drug repurposing, where existing drugs are applied to new therapeutic areas [12].

The power of AI target identification lies not only in its ability to analyze data but also in its potential to predict the outcomes of drug-target interactions [101]. For example, sophisticated AI models can model how molecules interact with a target at the atomic level, providing insights into the potential efficiency and specificity of a drug. These predictions can help prioritize which targets to pursue in the early stages of drug development, reducing time and resources required for experimental validation [7]. Moreover, AI can identify targets for complex diseases such as cancer or neurodegenerative disorders, where multiple genes and pathways may be involved [102]. By analyzing data from various sources, including patient-derived data such as genetic profiles and clinical histories, AI models can identify key molecular or targets within these complex networks. These insights offer opportunities for more effective and personalized therapies [103]. As AI technologies continue to evolve and integrate with biomedicine, they have the potential to revolutionize the discovery and development of novel, more effective therapeutic agents.

## 4.3.3 MOLECULAR DOCKING SIMULATIONS

Molecular docking simulations are crucial in the drug discovery process, allowing scientists to predict how small molecules, such as drug candidates, interact with a biological target, typically a protein. The core objective of molecular docking is to determine how well a molecule fits into a receptor binding site, finding the correct key for a lock. This process involves not only identifying the orientation of the molecule (the pose) but also estimating how strongly the molecule binds to the target (the binding affinity) [104]. Traditionally, molecular docking has been a computationally intensive task, often requiring significant time and resources. This process involves scanning vast conformational spaces to predict the optimal binding pose and affinity, which necessitates significant computational power and sophisticated algorithms [105].

The advent of AI-driven approaches, especially deep learning models, has led to transformative changes in molecular docking simulations. These AI models are trained on large datasets of protein-ligand interactions and use this training to predict binding poses and affinities for new drug candidates. The strength of AI, particularly in terms of deep learning, lies in its ability to process and learn from complex, high-dimensional data, enabling it to predict molecular interactions with a high degree of accuracy [106]. For example, a deep learning model can analyze structural data from thousands of protein-ligand complexes and learn patterns that dictate how molecules bind to proteins. By applying these patterns, AI models can predict the binding orientations and affinities of new drug candidates, often more quickly than traditional computational docking methods. This method is particularly beneficial in high-throughput screening scenarios, where thousands of compounds need to be evaluated in a short time [89].

Moreover, AI-driven molecular docking simulations often equal quality or better than traditional methods. This improvement is partly due to the ability of learning models to consider a broader range of interactions and factors that influence binding, including subtle changes in proteins and dynamic aspects of ligand binding [106]. Additionally, AI models incorporate advanced techniques such as reinforcement learning, where the system learns the best docking strategies through a process of trial and error [13]. This approach allows the development of novel molecular strategies for AI that have not been considered by traditional methods. Importantly, these AI-driven simulations can reveal potential off-target effects, where a drug might bind to unintended proteins, leading to side effects [107]. AI models aid in the design of more selective and safe drugs by predicting these off-target interactions. This advantage not only speeds up the drug discovery but also enhances the likelihood of identifying effective and safe drug candidates, potentially reducing the time and cost associated with bringing new drugs to market.

## 4.3.4 Generative Models for Drug Design

Generative models in AI, particularly generative adversarial networks (GANs) and variational autoencoders (VAEs), represent a revolutionary approach in the field of drug design. These models are unsupervised models in which the system is trained to generate new data that are similar to the input data [103]. In the context of drug discovery, generative models are trained on vast libraries of molecular structures and their properties, enabling them to generate new, drug-like molecules with desired characteristics. GANs achieve this through this unique architecture comprising two networks, a generator and a discriminator, which compete against each other. The generator creates new molecular structures, while the discriminator evaluates the authenticity of the structures against real data. This competition drives the generator to produce increasingly accurate and realistic structures [108].

VAEs offer another approach for generating new molecular structures. VAEs are trained to encode molecules into a low-dimensional latent space and decode this representation back into molecular structures. By exploring this latent space, VAEs can be interpolated and extrapolated from known molecules to create novel structures that possess desired chemical or biological activities that are relevant for treating neurodegenerative disorders, such as blood–brain barrier permeability, receptor binding affinity or metabolic stability. This targeted design enables the creation of drug candidates that are not only effective but also provide an improved safety profile [110].

The use of generative models for drug discovery is particularly for addressing the complex and multifaceted nature of neurodegenerative disorders. These diseases often require drugs that can interact with multiple targets or pathways simultaneously. Generative models can be trained to take into account multiple pharmacological factors, enabling the design of multarget drugs [103]. For instance, a model can be trained on data from compounds to modulate specific pathways involved in Alzheimer’s disease, allowing it to generate molecules that are likely to have a therapeutic effect on the disease's progression. AI-driven models can be used to modify the molecular structures of existing drugs to improve their efficacy or reduce side effects, a process known as molecular optimization [111]. Furthermore, their ability to precisely target molecules for specific therapeutic targets opens new avenues in the design of more effective and safe medications. As these models continue to evolve and improve, they are set to transform the future of pharmaceutical research and development.
