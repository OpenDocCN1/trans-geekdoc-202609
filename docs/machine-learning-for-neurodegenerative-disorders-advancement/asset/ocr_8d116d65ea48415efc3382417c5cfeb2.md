# 4.3.4 Generative Models for Drug Design

Generative models in AI, particularly generative adversarial networks (GANs) and variational autoencoders (VAEs), represent a revolutionary approach in the field of drug design. These models are unsupervised models in which the system is trained to generate new data that are similar to the input data [103]. In the context of drug discovery, generative models are trained on vast libraries of molecular structures and their properties, enabling them to generate new, drug-like molecules with desired characteristics. GANs achieve this through this unique architecture comprising two networks, a generator and a discriminator, which compete against each other. The generator creates new molecular structures, while the discriminator evaluates the authenticity of the structures against real data. This competition drives the generator to produce increasingly accurate and realistic structures [108].

VAEs offer another approach for generating new molecular structures. VAEs are trained to encode molecules into a low-dimensional latent space and decode this representation back into molecular structures. By exploring this latent space, VAEs can be interpolated and extrapolated from known molecules to create novel structures that possess desired chemical or biological activities that are relevant for treating neurodegenerative disorders, such as blood–brain barrier permeability, receptor binding affinity or metabolic stability. This targeted design enables the creation of drug candidates that are not only effective but also provide an improved safety profile [110].

The use of generative models for drug discovery is particularly for addressing the complex andmultif nature of neurodegenerative disorders. These diseases often require drugs that can interact with multiple targets or pathways simultaneously. Generative models can be trained to take into account multiple pharmacological factors, enabling the design of multarget drugs [103]. For instance, a model can be trained on data from compounds to modulate specific pathways involved in Alzheimer’s disease, allowing it to generate molecules that are likely to have a therapeutic effect on the disease's progression. AI-driven models can be used to modify the molecular structures of existing drugs to improve their efficacy or reduce side effects, a process known as molecular optimization [111]. Furthermore, their ability to precisely target molecules for specific therapeutic targets opens new avenues in the design of more effective and safe medications. As these models continue to evolve and improve, they are set to transform the future of pharmaceutical research and development.
