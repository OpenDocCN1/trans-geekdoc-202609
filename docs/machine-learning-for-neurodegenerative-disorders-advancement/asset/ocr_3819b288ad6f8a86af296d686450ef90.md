4.2.1.4 Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, also known as Lou Gehrig's disease, is a progressive neurodegenerative disease that affects nerve cells in the brain and the spinal cord. First identified in 1824, ALS is responsible for nearly 30,000 deaths annually worldwide [1]. Projections indicate a significant increase in the incidence of ALS, with an expected increase of 69% by the year 2040 [73]. The hallmark of ALS is the gradual degradation and death of motor neurons, which are essential for controlling muscle movement [74]. As both the upper motor neurons (located in the brain) and the lower motor neurons (located in the spinal cord) die. This loss of neurons leads to a reduction in the brain's ability to initiate and control movement, resulting in progressive weakness and atrophy of muscles. The onset of ALS can be subtle, which progressively patients experience muscle weakness, twitching, and cramping. As speaking, swallowing, and breathing [75]. The progression of ALS varies among individuals, with the average life expectancy ranging from two to five years from the time of diagnosis. However, some individuals with ALS live ten or more years [76, 77].
