FIGURE 4.3 Pathophysiology of Huntington's disease.

As demonstrated in Figure 4.3, Huntington's disease is caused by a genetic mutation in the huntingtin gene (HTT), which leads to the production of an abnormal version of the huntingtin protein. This mutation involves an expanded guanine-adenine-guanine (GAG) nucleotide repeat in the gene. Normally, this is repeated 10 to 35 times within the gene, but with Huntington's disease, it is repeated 36 to more than 120 times [62, 63]. As result, an abnormal version of the huntingtin protein is produced, which accumulates in the brain, causing the degeneration of neurons. This degeneration primarily affects the basal ganglia and cerebral cortex, leading to impairments in movement, cognition, and behavior associated with the disease [64]. Huntington's syndrome is unique among neurodegenerative disorders due to its genetic predictability; children of a parent with Huntington's disease have a 50% chance of inheriting the faulty gene [65]. There is currently no cure for Huntington's disease, and there is no way to slow or stop the brain it causes. However, there are treatments available to help manage symptoms of the disease and improving quality of life [66].
Treatment for Huntington's disease focuses on managing symptoms and improving quality of life [67]. Medications are often employed to manage mood disorders associated with Huntington's disease. Table 4.3 presents an overview of current pharmacological strategies for treating Huntington's disease. Several therapeutic approaches targeting various aspects of the disease's symptoms and underlying causes are highlighted. Dopamine receptor blockers are common in Huntington's although they can lead to side effects such as dystonia and psychosis [68]. Glutamate modulators, including memantine, aim to modulate glutamate, reducing excitotoxicity and offering neuroprotective benefits [69]. Specifically selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), such as fluoxetine; and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), such as duloxetine, also manage the mood disorders and depression frequently associated with Huntington's disease [70, 71]. Tetrabenazine, which targets vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2), is employed to control chorea and can be used for depression [72]. Innovative gene silencing therapies, such as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), with tomersen as a prime example, represent a novel approach that targets the genetic root of the disease. These therapies work by reducing the production of mutant huntingtin protein and are currently under clinical investigation, potentially offering a way to modify the progression of the disease.
