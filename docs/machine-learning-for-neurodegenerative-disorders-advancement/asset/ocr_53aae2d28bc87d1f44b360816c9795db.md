# 2.5.3.1 Convolutional Neural Networks

Disease identification and classification in neuroimaging have undergone a transformative shift with the integration of CNNs within deep learning frameworks. CNNs, designed to automatically learn hierarchical features from data, have demonstrated exceptional capabilities in capturing patterns within neuroimaging datasets. In the realm of brain disorders, CNNs have been applied to tasks such as brain tumor classification in MRI [37]. Notable studies, such as the work by Hawaii et al. [37], show the efficacy of CNNs in accurately segmenting and classifying tumors, providing valuable support for medical professionals in diagnosis and treatment planning. Moreover, CNNs have been instrumental in the automated analysis of functional MRI (fMRI) data, enabling the identification of abnormal functional connectivity patterns associated with neurological and psychiatric conditions. # 2.5.3.2 Transfer Learning

Disease identification and classification in neuroimaging has witnessed significant advancements through the application of deep learning approaches, particularly transfer learning. Transfer learning leverages pre-trained network models on large datasets and adapts them to smaller datasets [40]. This is particularly beneficial in neuroimaging, acquiring labeled data for deep learning is often limited due to sample sizes. The authors utilized a pre-trained 3D CNN on a large dataset and fine-tuned it for Alzheimer’s disease prediction, achieving superior performance. Transfer learning has also been successfully applied to tasks such as brain tumor classification in MRI, as shown in the work by Zhou et al. [41]. By transferring knowledge from large-scale image datasets, these approaches enable more accurate and robust disease identification, with limited labeled neuroimaging data [41].

# 2.6.1 Linear REGRESSION MODELS

Linear regression models, a fundamental technique in ML, have found valuable applications in neuroimaging analysis and interpretation. Linear regression enables the modeling of the relationship between independent variables and a dependent variable, allowing researchers to quantify the impact of specific features on brain-related outcomes. In neuroimaging studies, linear regression has been employed for tasks such as identifying associations between brain structure and function and clinical variables [42]. For example, in the work by Holmes et al. [42], linear regression was used to investigate the relationship between cortical thickness and cognitive performance in individuals with multiple sclerosis. Another notable application is the study by Yendi et al. [42], where linear regression was utilized to examine the association between white matter integrity and cognitive abilities. These examples illustrate the versatility of linear regression models in elucidating neuroimaging features and establishing connections between brain measures and clinical or cognitive variables [43].
