Driving while drowsy can result in consequences comparable to operating a vehicle under the influence of alcohol. Drowsiness shares similarities with alcohol in compromising capabilities by diminishing alertness, delaying reaction times, and impeding decision-making skills. Unfortunately, identifying drowsiness can be challenging for individual drivers, as some may not realize they have dozed off the wheel, even after a few minutes of sleep. It's important to note that drowsiness can impair safety, driving leaves drivers unable before falling asleep. A recent study by the AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety revealed that an estimated 6% of all crashes involving a towed vehicle, 7% with treated injuries, 13% resulting in hospitalization, and 21% in fatalities were linked to drowsiness [4]. This highlights the gravity of drowsy driving as a significant public health concern, prompting the Academy of Sleep Medicine (AAME) to adopt a position statement aimed at educating the public and healthcare providers about the risks and countermeasures against drowsy driving.

Three major areas of data consumption have been identified in recent studies on driver sleepiness: vehicle-based, behavioral, and physiological measures. Physiological or bio-signal-based measures have been found to outperform vehicle and behavioral measures because they provide fewer false positives and are less affected by environmental and road conditions. Physiological or bio-signal-based measures are more accurate and reliable. There are three primary signals employed in this regard to detect drowsiness: EEG, electrooculography (EOG), and electrocardiography (ECG).

Over the 2020's, our EEG-based research studies have explored the integration of machine learning (ML) in medical diagnosis. These efforts have been particularly significant in developing classification models for drowsiness. Such advancements have helped the growing synergy between EEG and ML techniques in healthcare applications. However, various drawbacks arise in ML applications, the requirement for a large dataset to train, restrictions on the predictions made, the requirement for a middle step in feature representation, and the requirement to draw conclusions in order to identify anomalies. Electric potentials in the brain linked to various states of arousal, including wakefulness, sleepiness, and sleep, are measured using EEG. To detect tiredness, a number of features in the temporal and frequency domains can be derived from EEG data.
