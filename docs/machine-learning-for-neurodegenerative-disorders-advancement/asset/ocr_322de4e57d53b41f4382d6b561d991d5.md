# 2.4.1.1 Traditional Methods

Traditional methods in neuroimaging referring to the conventional techniques that have been foundational in studying brain structure and function. MRI and CT are classic examples of medical imaging. MRI, based on the principle of nuclear resonance, provides high-resolution images of soft tissues, allowing for detailed visualization of the brain’s anatomy. CT, on the other hand, employs X-rays to generate cross-sectional images, offering valuable information about structures and certain brain pathologies. These traditional methods have been instrumental in clinical diagnostics, aiding in the detection and characterization of various neurological disorders [17].

The work of Lauterbur and Damadian [17, 18] made a seminal contribution to the development of MRI, for they were later awarded the Nobel Prize in physiology or medicine. Their groundbreaking research laid the foundation for the non-invasive imaging of brain structures, revolutionizing diagnostics and neuroscience. Additionally, CT, pioneered by Hounsfield [19], represented a significant leap in imaging technology, for three-dimensional visualization of the brain and the identification of structural abnormalities.

While traditional neuroimaging methods have been indispensable in clinical practice, they are limited in their ability to capture dynamic brain processes [18]; MRI, an innovation that emerged in the 1990s, introduced a paradigm shift by studying the brain activity in real time, complementing the information provided by traditional methods.

In summary, traditional neuroimaging methods have played a crucial role in our understanding of brain structure and pathology. The foundational contributions of Lauterbur, Damadian, Hounsfield, and others have paved the way for subsequent developments, the integration of traditional methods with more advanced techniques, contributing to a comprehensive approach in studying the brain [19].

# 2.4.1.2 Advancements with CNNs

Recent advancements in neuroimaging have been significantly influenced by the integration of CNNs, a class of deep learning architectures, excel in automatically learning hierarchical features from data, making them particularly well suited for tasks such as image recognition and segmentation. In the context of neuroimaging, CNNs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in tasks ranging from anatomical segmentation in MRI to the identification of pathological regions in PET scans.

A study by Pawan and Montana [20] showcased the potential of CNNs in medical image analysis, demonstrating their effectiveness in segmenting brain tumor regions from MRI. This work highlighted the ability of CNNs to automatically learn discriminative features, eliminating the need for handcrafted features in the segmentation process. Furthermore, the research of Hosseini et al. [21] applied CNNs to automatically classify Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment, emphasizing the network’s power in capturing subtle patterns of neurological disorders [20].

The application of CNNs in functional neuroimaging has also been notable. For instance, CNNs have been employed to decode cognitive states from fMRI data, providing insights into the neural representation of various cognitive processes. Additionally, the integration of CNNs with multi-modal imaging approaches has expanded their utility in capturing complex relationships within heterogeneous datasets.

These advancements underscore the potential of CNNs in automating and improving the efficiency of neuroimaging analyses. As the field continues to evolve, CNNs are expected to play a crucial role in unraveling the complexities of brain structure and function, offering new avenues for personalized medicine, early disease detection, and treatment monitoring [21].
