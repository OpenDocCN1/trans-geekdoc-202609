## 1.2 BASICS OF MACHINE LEARNING

This section defines ML and its core concepts, including supervised, unsupervised, and RL. The section also explains standard ML algorithms as decision trees, support vector machines, neural networks, and deep learning.

## 1.2.1 MACHINE LEARNING CORE CONCEPTS

ML is a subset of artificial intelligence (AI) that involves the development of algorithms and models that allow computers to learn from data and improve their performance on a specific task without being explicitly programmed (Pavarino et al., 2023). ML enables systems to automatically adapt and improve based on experience, making it particularly useful for tasks that involve complex tasks, large datasets, and where explicit rule-based programming is challenging. The core concepts of ML includes the following.

### 1.2.1.1 Data

Data are the foundation where devices are built and foster human operations. They include all kinds of information, structured or unstructured; algorithms can process to extract meaningful patterns. Structured data are organized into rows and columns, normally in data sources or spreadsheets, and it is much easier to evaluate. Data are the raw information that is used to train and test ML algorithms. Disorganized information, such as text, pictures, and video clips, which do not have a predefined layout, allowing for more innovative preprocessing methods (Pavarino et al., 2023). High-quality data are essential as they directly influence the efficiency of ML designs. They include raw inputs, historical records, sensor results, and any other relevant details that inform the knowing process. The quality, quantity, and variety of the information considerably the design's capability to generalize and make precise predictions. It can encompass various formats, such as numerical values, video, images, or audio (Choi et al., 2023). In supervised learning, data consists of pairs of input features and their corresponding labels (outputs). In unsupervised learning, the data may consist solely of input features without labeled outcomes.

### 1.2.1.2 Features

Features are individual attributes or characteristics of the data that are used as inputs to the ML algorithm. They represent the information that the algorithm will use to make predictions or classifications. Features can vary in type; they could be numerical (for example, temperature or age), categorical (for instance, gender or color), or more complex in types (such as text or images). The input variables used by ML models to discover patterns and make (Zheng et al., 2023). Attributes can be numerical, specific, or binary, they must be thoroughly chosen and crafted to guarantee the model's effectiveness. Feature option involves picking one of the most pertinent features from the dataset. In contrast, the quality and relevance of functions directly affect the design's performance, as irrelevant attributes can introduce noise.

### 1.2.1.3 Label or Target

In supervised learning, the label (target) is the output or outcome the algorithm aims to predict. For example, in a medical diagnosis scenario, the label could indicate whether a patient has a disease (Moreno-Rodríguez et al., 2023). During training, the algorithm learns the relationship between the input features and the labels. In a binary category, tags could be “0” or “1”, showing the lack of visibility of a specific condition. Labels can be categorical, numerical, or binary.

### 1.2.1.4 Algorithm

An algorithm is a set of mathematical and computational rules that the ML system follows to learn from the data (Schmidgall & Hays, 2023). Algorithms are designed to discover patterns, relationships and relations within, enabling the model to make decisions. Algorithms can be categorized by the kind of learning they assist in, such as supervised, unsupervised, or semi-supervised. Each algorithm has its strengths and weaknesses, and the choice of algorithm relies on the details of the problem being handled, the nature of the data, and the preferred result (Jaison et al., 2023).

### 1.2.1.5 Training

Training exposes the ML model to a dataset containing input features and corresponding labels. During training, the algorithm adjusts its internal parameters to learn the patterns and labels that enable accurate predictions (Jiang, 2023). The algorithm aims to generalize its learning to unseen data. During training, the model iteratively changes its criteria to lessen the mistakes between its predictions and the actual labels. This entails making use of a loss function to gauge the discrepancy and optimization, such as gradient descent, to upgrade the version's parameters. Training calls for a classified dataset (in supervised learning) and often splitting the data right into training and validation sets to review efficiency and avoid overfitting (Zarr & Brown, 2023).

### 1.2.1.6 Model

The model is the learned representation of the relationships between input features and labels. It is the output of the training process and encapsulates the features and information the algorithm has learned from the training data (Lockhart, 2023). The model is used to make predictions.
