# Data

MMIC-III (v.1,2016) is a publicly available database that comprises de-identified data from 46,520 patients and 58,976 admissions recorded between June 1, 2001, and October 31, 2012, the Beth Israel Deacon Medical Center in Boston, MA [27]. The data includes demographics, admission, and discharge notes, International Classification of Diseases 1999 (ICD9) codes for diagnoses, physiological findings, survival data, visit records, and medication history. Physiological wavelet data was collected in a subset of patients. We decided to model ICF from ABP, ECG, and PPG. By using these waveforms, we had three modes of predicting ICF and contributions of each waveforms.

# 4.1. Pre-processing, cohort selection, and data methods

Patients who were at 18 years and old and had admission, ABP, PPG, and ECG records for at least 5 minutes in this database has been provided to us. We used only ECG lead II [28]. Patients who had more than 5 minutes of recorded PPG waveform data were excluded. This was because less than 5 minutes is frequently used as the typical duration for the clinical finding when determining which period of ICF is clinically significant.

From the remaining patients, waveform data (synchronous or asynchronous) were extracted. Five-minute sequences that do not contain any non-zero values (NA) or physiologically impossible values (ICF < -100 mmHg or 200 mmHg; PPG < 20 mmHg or 300 mmHg; heart rate mean) from each waveform were extracted for waveform prediction. To address potential issues with CVD evaluation, an interpolation method and bandpass filter were applied to the signals to remove high-frequency and low-frequency waveforms that could lead to inaccuracies in the data.

For this study, an episode of intracranial hypertension was defined as an ICF > 20 mmHg [29]. Recent traumatic brain injury guidelines suggests a threshold of 20 mmHg, however, our study includes a heterogenous population of patients who were admitted to the ICU, which is responsible for both pediatric and geriatric patients. A significant number of intracranial hypertension events were reported to be present in the patients, who had an aggregate duration of intracranial hypertension for > 60 minutes. Finally, patients with less than 5 minutes of recorded ECG data were excluded [30]. Each step of the pre-processing is reported in Supplementary Table-IA.

# Related works

Our research presents a novel approach to predicting the ICF waveforms which differs significantly from what is reported in other literature. We leverage deep learning to predict ICF using high-frequency (125 Hz) ABP, PPG, and ECG data in real time. Other groups have developed to build physics-based simulations and statistical models to interpret ICF, data-driven methods to predict ICF [1, 14-17]. Some groups have developed a potential-learning algorithm for intracranial hypertension [18,19]. We have attempted to offer a relative alternative in a limited number. A number of studies focus on predicting ICF using extracranial physiological features, however, they have not used deep learning [12, 13]. Deep learning models are able to learn the features of the waveforms directly from the data. Can deep learning help capture hierarchical and non-linear relationships between the input waveforms (extracranial) and the output waveforms (intracranial) for feature engineering.

A report on a study to compare ICF and blood pressure [20]. We used ECG, ABP, and blood flow velocity (derived from transcranial Doppler (TCD)) waveforms to compute ICF. A method that is used for data from TCD waveforms [23] has been applied similarly to ABP, ECG, and PPG. Additionally, other studies have used the monitored intracranial PPG signal for ICF in this study [24].
