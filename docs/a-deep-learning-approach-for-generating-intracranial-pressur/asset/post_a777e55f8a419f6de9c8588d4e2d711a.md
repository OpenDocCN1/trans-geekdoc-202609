Intracranial pressure (ICP) is commonly monitored to treat patients with brain disorders such as traumatic brain injury (TBI). Established methods to assess ICP are resource intensive and highly invasive. We obtained ICP waveforms continuously from three signals acquired in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU): arterial blood pressure (ABP), plethysmography (PPG), and electrocardiogram (EEG) over 600 h of high-frequency (125 Hz) simultaneously acquired ICP, ABP, and PPG wavelet data from 10 patients. We trained deep learning (DL) models to reconstruct ICP waveforms. The predictive performance of different DL models was evaluated in single-patient and multi-patient tests. The mean absolute error (MAE) of the best-performing models was 1.34 mmHg in the single-patient test and 2.11 mmHg in the multi-patient test. Analysis was conducted to compare statistically independent performances across the models (SD = 0.63 ± 0.75, 0.66 ± 0.96, for EEG, PPG, and ABP; p = 0.42). The results demonstrate the potential of non-invasive continuous monitoring using electrophysiological waveforms. With refinement and validation, this method could provide a more accessible and alternative to invasive ICP, enabling stable assessment and treatment in resource-constrained settings.

Elevations in ICP are life-threatening events which may expand intracranial damage, cerebral edema, or death. # 4.1. Pre-processing, cohort selection, and data methods

Patients who were at 18 years and old and had admission, ABP, PPG, and ECG records for at least 5 minutes in this database has been provided to us. We used only ECG lead II [28]. Patients who had more than 5 minutes of recorded PPG waveform data were excluded. This was because less than 5 minutes is frequently used as the typical duration for the clinical finding when determining which period of ICP is clinically significant.

From the remaining patients, waveform data (synchronous or asynchronous) were extracted. Five-minute sequences that do not contain any non-zero values (NA) or physiologically impossible values (ICP < -100 mmHg or 200 mmHg; PPG < 20 mmHg or 300 mmHg; heart rate mean) from each waveform were extracted for waveform prediction. To address potential issues with CVD evaluation, an interpolation method and bandpass filter were applied to the signals to remove high-frequency and low-frequency waveforms that could lead to inaccuracies in the data.

For this study, an episode of intracranial hypertension was defined as an ICP > 20 mmHg [29]. Recent traumatic brain injury guidelines suggest a threshold of 20 mmHg, however, our study includes a heterogenous population of patients who were admitted to the ICU, which is responsible for both pediatric and geriatric patients. A significant number of intracranial hypertension events were reported to be present in the patients, who had an aggregate duration of intracranial hypertension for > 60 minutes. Finally, patients with less than 5 minutes of recorded ECG data were excluded [30]. Each step of the pre-processing is reported in Supplementary Table-IA.

# Related works

Our research presents a novel approach to predicting the ICP waveforms which differs significantly from what is reported in other literature. We leverage deep learning to predict ICP using high-frequency (125 Hz) ABP, PPG, and ECG data in real time. Other groups have developed to build physics-based simulations and statistical models to interpret ICP, data-driven methods to predict ICP [1, 14-17]. Some groups have developed a potential-learning algorithm for intracranial hypertension [18,19]. We have attempted to offer a relative alternative in a limited number. A number of studies focus on predicting ICP using extracranial physiological features, however, they have not deep learning [12, 13]. Deep learning models are able to learn the features of the waveforms directly from the data. Can deep learning help capture hierarchical and non-linear relationships between the input waveforms (extracranial) and the output waveforms (intracranial) for feature engineering?

A report on a study to compare ICP and blood pressure [20]. We used ECG, ABP, and blood flow velocity (derived from transcranial Doppler (TCD)) waveforms to compute ICP. A method that is used for data from TCD waveforms [23] has been applied similarly to ABP, ECG, and PPG. Additionally, other studies have used the monitored intracranial PPG signal for ICP in this study [24]. Research suggests that intracranial PPG signals may change or in conjunction with changes in ICP [25,26]. Here we investigate PPG as a potential correlate of ICP.
