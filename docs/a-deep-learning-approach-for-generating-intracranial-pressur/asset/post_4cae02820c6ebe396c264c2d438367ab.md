# 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Key results

We present a framework for continuous ICP waveform that leverages deep learning to fuse PPG waveform data measured in ICU. The best predicted ICP with an MAE of 5.5 mmHg and limited systematic bias show that PPG is an important signal in predicting ICP, while the waveform provides more comprehensive evidence. Overall, the findings support our hypothesis that information on the ICP waveform can be extracted from non-invasive PPG and is possible to accurately reconstruct an ICP waveform from extracranial data, the real-time and non-invasive nature of our algorithms could be integrated into the continuous flow of ICP monitoring, which would represent a significant advantage with respect to current invasive approaches.

We compare our findings with recent studies that employed deep learning algorithms trained on TCD-derived blood flow velocity, ABP, and ICP in ICU. The reported mean absolute error of their methods were 3.88 ± 3.26 mmHg and 3.44 ± 1.71 mmHg, However, TCD used in many settings cannot be monitored continuously, suggesting that this would represent an inadvertent assessment of ICP, the approach in this study provided a numerical computation instead of re-forming the waveform as is the case in the current work.

## 6. Strengths

By focusing on non-invasive ICP, our research has potential to significantly advance the monitoring and treatment of patients with critical brain injuries, in resource-limited settings. Real time waveform reconstruction using deep learning could be integrated into the current monitoring workflows as a safer, more accurate alternative in ICP-generation scenarios, expanding the potential of patients who would benefit from non-invasive ICP for the treatment of acute brain injuries.

Our best results performed with similar accuracy to invasive ICP show an error of 5.5 mmHg [52]. We can also compare our algorithms' performance to technologies such as TCD-ultrasoundography, where ICP estimation has an error of range of 12–55 mmHg [52], and, do not support continuous evaluation [53]. Other non-invasive ICP techniques have an error range of 15–55 mmHg and require a probe to be inserted into the ear of the brain [8].

## 6.3. Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations. This research is a proof-of-concept study with a sample size that was small, leaving justifiable concerns about statistical power and generalizability. Our results are reflective of the method used and indicating that the model will need further validation before considered clinical implementation. Furthermore, the current dataset contains fields that were uncertain, potentially affecting performance due to variations in input signals. Our application of a pre-processing scheme, absence of PPG and ECG detection system can help filter out noisy quality data.

## 6.4. Future direction

Ongoing and future research will concentrate on aggregating the sample size of evaluated patients and examining differences in performance. We plan to expand our work by including more de-identified waveforms (e.g., continuous monitoring) and adding demographics, thereby enhancing patient representation and improving generalizability. We will also test the models on an external dataset outside of MIMIC III Database to assess the generalizability of model performance. Ideally, we intend to externally validate our data on a more homogeneous group of TBI patients enrolled in a larger prospective database. Finally, we will tune our independent deep learning models and methods for aggregating the outputs of each into a ‘super-ensemble’ learner to enhance performance.

# 7. Conclusions

In conclusion, the results of this study indicate that algorithmic prediction of ICP from discrete set of continuous signals is possible and yields accuracies that are higher than most other techniques and on par with methods like ICP. With further refinement and validation, this could provide an accessible alternative to ICP measurements, enabling prompt treatment of severe brain injury in lower resource settings.
