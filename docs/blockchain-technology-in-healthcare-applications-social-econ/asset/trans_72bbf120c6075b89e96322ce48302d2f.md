
Accurate diagnosis results lead to successful treatment of diseases. Patients can use blockchain to share their information with their chosen service providers while keeping their information safe and reliable. Credentials prove who owns the medical records. They act as a security check against forgery [67](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_67). A recent study by Business Intelligence and Strategy Research (BIS) states that the healthcare industry can save up to $100 billion per year by 2025 through the implementation of blockchain [68](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_68). In addition to savings in data breaches, operations, IT, misappropriation fraud, and insurance fraud, these savings will improve the overall quality of life. The report indicates that approximately $6 billion in revenue could be generated by 2025 [68](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_68).

## 13.5 Healthcare Supply Chain

Supply chain logistics in healthcare involve multiple procedures, different teams, and the flow of medicines, surgical kits, and various other products used daily by healthcare providers. By exploring vulnerabilities in the healthcare industry supply chain, healthcare managers can propose measures to reduce these vulnerabilities. By identifying weak links and focusing resources on targeted health outcomes, global health investments can be increased. The healthcare supply chain offers numerous benefits, such as improved processes, efficient resource utilization, staff service, and effective patient treatment. To achieve these goals, hospitals should use integrated supply chains to ensure that hospital operations and departments are fully connected within the supply chain. An example of a supply chain is a backend computer program that helps integrate all the different steps in the manufacturing process. Supply chain implementation ensures that medicines and products are available at the right time, inventory is minimized, patients receive maximum care, and all departments coordinate to minimize human and medication errors.

### 13.5.1 Blockchain for Supply Chain

Blockchain technology allows pharmaceutical companies to track raw materials, materials, or compounds from the source at every transition point to the patient [69](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_69). Healthcare and health-related supply chains can also leverage blockchain to protect pharmaceutical research data and information. Blockchain improves supply chain security by enhancing the ability to identify the source and authenticity of medical products [70](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_70), [71](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_71), [72](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_72). Furthermore, blockchain allows us to track and detect the full range of difficulties associated with pharmaceutical treatments, which have become more prevalent in recent decades. Pharmaceutical industry data is shared in a more trusted manner through blockchain-based technology. Additionally, [Modum.io](http://Modum.io), a Swiss company with sensor and blockchain technology, improves supply chain productivity [73](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_73). The `MODSense` system ensures accurate readings of recorded temperatures and ensures stakeholders receive actual handling information for specific products during transport [73](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_73). Besides medical supplies, vaccine transport, clinical trials, drug samples, and perishable foods, blockchain solutions can benefit a wide range of other products.

The pharmaceutical supply chain claims that the solution achieves a higher level of efficiency and safety by identifying product locations faster than ever before [74](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_74). As an authenticating agent for sharing data and information, blockchain has the potential to convince supply chain managers to adopt this technology for supply chain management. Blockchain research related to supply chain management is still in its infancy; therefore, investigating the possible applications of this technology warrants further discussion. Companies must quantitatively demonstrate what advantages can be gained before implementing blockchain-enabled supply chains in the industry. In this regard, trust in blockchain still relies on trust in partner companies. Data sent to the blockchain by companies can still be pre-forged or tampered with. For blockchain to provide high performance, accurate or non-forged data regarding demand and inventory must be shared among participants in the supply chain.

### 13.5.2 Drug Traceability Using Blockchain

To reduce data breaches related to costs, procedures, and IT, traditional solutions for tracking drugs in the supply chain are usually centralized and expensive, and reduce the number of counterfeit drugs. Due to a lack of transparency among supply chain participants, central authorities can modify data without notifying other stakeholders. On the other hand, blockchain-based solutions provide data security, transparency, immutability, traceability, and transaction record authentication.

Blockchain technology facilitates information transactions across a decentralized, immutable ledger used to record various business transactions. Although the terms "transparent" and "traceable" are often used interchangeably, they serve different purposes. Supply chain transparency refers to the openness of top-level information. A detailed supply chain map would include product components, facility locations, and supplier names. Traceability, on the other hand, involves selecting specific components to track, defining common standards for communication with partners, determining appropriate procedures for generating and collecting accurate information, selecting a data accumulation platform, and making necessary decisions on data sharing mechanisms. Despite the differences between transparency and traceability, the two concepts are interrelated because accessing detailed information requires a comprehensive study of the supply chain. In this regard, several existing pharmaceutical drug tracking and tracing solutions leverage the cryptographic properties of blockchain.

Ingraham et al. [75](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_75) outlined healthcare problems and described a blockchain-based approach, although without providing details or examples. Nageswar et al. [76](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_76) discussed how blockchain technology can be used for the pharmaceutical supply chain. Dindarian et al. [77](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_77) designed a traceability system using Ethereum as an anti-counterfeiting tool. The proposed solution used smart contracts but lacked implementation or evaluation, making its contribution unclear. Stevens et al. [78](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_78) developed an integrated, secure drug traceability system—Drug Ledger (`DL`)—which simulates real transactions managing the supply chain while maintaining the identity and confidentiality of stakeholders' traceability information without compromising system security.

Using the data structure of Unspent Transaction Outputs (`UTXOs`), including packaging, repackaging, and unpacking, `DL` completes its workflow. Research indicates that the `UTXO` data structure is prone to bugs, has high storage costs, and low state space utilization [79](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_79). Jamil et al. [80](#C_018_c13.xhtml#r13_80) proposed a solution using Hyperledger technology to track drugs in the healthcare supply chain, a more efficient system with slightly increased latency but improved throughput and less capital lock-up. However, their solution has not undergone systematic testing and has only been applied in a small-scale environment.
