Let `T`: `l²`→`l²`![Equation](Images/F000138si69.png) over the complex field. For any `v` in `l²`,![Equation](Images/F000138si70.png) use the mapping, `Tv`=`T(v1, v2, v3, …)`=`(v1, v2/2, v3/3, …)`. To be in `l²`![Equation](Images/F000138si71.png) means that the sum $\\sum\_{n = 1}^{\\infty}\\left( {vn} \\right)^{2}$![Equation](Images/F000138si72.png) converges. Consider `w` in `l²`![Equation](Images/F000138si73.png), where `w`=`(w1, w2,… )`, and then the inner product `<w, Tv>`=`w1∗v1+w2∗v2/ 2+w3∗v3/3+….` The definition of the adjoint operator involving the inner product is `<T∗w, v>`=`w1′∗v1+w2′∗v2+w3′∗v3 +….` Here, `wj′∗` is `T∗wj`. Then to see if there exists a relation between `T` and `T∗`, equate coefficients involving `vn`. This gives `w1′∗`=`w1∗`, `w2∗′`=`w2∗/2`, `w3′∗`=`w3∗/3….` As a consequence, `T∗w1`=`w1`, `T∗w2`=`w2/2`, `T∗w3`=`w3/ 3`, …. Accordingly it follows that `T∗`=`T`, and so the operator `T` is self-adjoint.

### Example 13.9

Let `T`: `l²`→`l²`![Equation](Images/F000138si74.png) over the complex field. For any `v`=`(v0, v1, v2, v3, …)`, in `l²`![Equation](Images/F000138si75.png) say that `Tv`=`(v1, v2/2, v3/3, …)`. To find `T∗`, notice that for `w` in `l²`![Equation](Images/F000138si76.png), `<w, Tv>`=`w0∗v1+w1∗v2/2+….` Also forming `<T∗w, v>`=`w0′∗v0+w1′∗ v1+….` Then equating the two inner product expressions together and then setting the coefficients of `vn` equal to each other yields: `w0′∗`=`0`, `w1′∗`=`w0∗`, `w2′∗`=`w1∗/2`, …. As a consequence, `T∗w`=`(0, w0, w1/2, w2/3, …)`. So in this example `T∗` does not equal `T`, so it is not self-adjoint. Notice, also that `<T∗w, v>`=`0+w0∗v1+w1∗v2/2+….` Accordingly, `<T∗w, v>` does not equal `<w, Tv>`, and so `T` and `T∗` are not even symmetric, which is a must in this case.

In the previous example since the operator `T` is not self-adjoint, it automatically follows that the operator is not symmetric. The result does not hold for unbounded operators that in fact are the most important in quantum. For a not necessarily bounded operator on Hilbert space `H`, the domain of `T`, `domT`, is a subset of `H`. The adjoint `T∗` has domain `domT∗`, which is also a subspace of `H` with vectors `w` such that there is a vector `v` in `H` such that `<v, Tu>`=`<w, u>`. By letting `T∗w`=`v`, define the adjoint operator `T∗`.

The graph of `T` is `graph(T)`=`{(v, Tv) such that v is in domT}`. An operator `S` extends `T` means that `graph(T)` is a subset of `graph(S)`. For a symmetric operator `T`, the `graph(T)` is a subset of the `graph(T∗)`. The operator `T` is self-adjoint when `graph(T)`=`graph(T∗)`. In this case, `domT`=`domT∗` and `T`=`T∗`. A symmetric operator having `domT`=`dom T∗` is self-adjoint.

A key theorem for quantum is the Hellinger-Toeplitz theorem stating that when `T` is defined on a Hilbert space for all `v`, `w` in `H` such that `<Tv, w>`=`<v, T w>`, then `T` must be bounded ([Teschl, 2009](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib17)). Since an everywhere-defined symmetric operator on `H` is also self-adjoint, then it follows that for a self-adjoint nonbounded operator `T`, its domain must be a proper subset of `H`. The operator `T` in this case must be a partially defined operator on `H`.

In several areas of quantum, operators are sometimes defined on a subset of a Hilbert, or a Banach space. This most often occurs for the adjoint operation, as well as for the closed operation. First, for the adjoint, `T` is defined on `dom(T)`, which is a subset of Hilbert space `H`[1]![Equation](Images/F000138si77.png), and `T`: `dom{T)`→`H`[2]![Equation](Images/F000138si78.png). `H`[2]![Equation](Images/F000138si79.png) is another Hilbert space. In this case, `T∗` is defined by `T∗(u)`=`v`, which means `<v, w>`=`<u, T w>`, for all `w` in the `dom(T)`. In the above inner products, the first inner product is in `H`[1]![Equation](Images/F000138si80.png) and the second is taken in `H`[2]![Equation](Images/F000138si81.png). It is assumed that `T∗` is a closed operator and that `dom(T)` is dense in `H`[1]![Equation](Images/F000138si82.png). When this is true, `<u, T v>`=`<T∗ u, v>`, for `v` in `dom(T)` and `u` in `dom(T∗)`. Similarly, assume that `B`[1]![Equation](Images/F000138si83.png) and `B`[2]![Equation](Images/F000138si84.png) are Banach spaces, and consider `T` in `L (B[1],B[2])`![Equation](Images/F000138si85.png). `T` is said to be a closed operator on `dom(T)`, which is a subspace of `B1` when given that `vn` converges to `v` in `dom (T)`, and given that `Tvn` converges to `w` in `B`[2]![Equation](Images/F000138si86.png), then `w`=`Tv`.

The operator `T` is said to be densely defined when `dom(T)` is dense in `B`[1]![Equation](Images/F000138si87.png). `T` is fully defined when `dom(T)`=`B`[1]![Equation](Images/F000138si88.png). Closure of an operator `T` means that there exists a closed operator `S`, such that `dom(T)` is a subset of `dom(S)`, and `S`=`T` on `dom(T)`. Also, the graph norm on `dom(T)` is given for `v` in `dom(T)`,‖`v`‖=`‖v‖+‖T v‖`; the first norm is taken in `dom(T)`, the second in `B`[1]![Equation](Images/F000138si89.png), and the third in `B`[2]![Equation](Images/F000138si90.png).

For a nonbounded operator `T` on a Hilbert space `H`, it is a closed operator when its graph is closed. The graph of `T` is a subset of the Cartesian product `H x H`. It is a vector space. The graph of `T` is `Gr(T)`=`{<v, T v>|v is in dom(T)}`. The closed graph theorem says that an everywhere-defined linear map with a closed graph is continuous. See Appendix A.6. A partially defined linear map with a closed graph can be not continuous.

### Example 13.10

Consider the Hilbert space `H`=`l²`![Equation](Images/F000138si91.png), that is, all complex-valued absolute sum squared sequences that converge. Let `dom(T)` be equal to all sequences such that the sum ∑|`n` `v`n|²![Equation](Images/F000138si92.png) converges. Let `T`: `dom (T)`→`H` by `Tv`=`T (v1, v2, v3, …)`=`(v1, 2v2, 3v3, …)`. The closure of `dom(T)`=`H`, so `dom(T)` is dense in `H`. This follows since `dom(T)` contains all sequences that have at most a finitely many nonzero terms. `T` is not one to one due to the finite number of nonzero terms. Two identical finite vector tuple strings in the range correspond to an infinite number of different terms in the domain whose image is the same finite tuple sequence. Additionally, `T` is unbounded, but it is self-adjoint, since `<T∗w, v>`=`w1′∗v1+w2′∗v2+…` and `<w, Tv>`=`w1∗v1+w2∗2v2 +….` Here `wk′∗` are tuples from `T∗w`. Then equating coefficients of `vk` gives `wk′∗`=`wk/k`. This means that `T∗w`=`(w1, 2w2, 3w3, …)`. Moreover, the spectrum for `T` is the point spectrum: `sppT`=`{1, 2, 3, …}` since `Tvn`=`n vn`.

### Example 13.11

For the unbounded operator `T`, having an adjoint `T∗` in the Hilbert space `H` with `T`: `dom(T)`→`H`. Let `c∗` be in `sppT∗`. That is, `c∗` is in the point spectrum of `T∗`. It will be seen that `c` will be in the union of `sppT` with `sprT`, specifically, that is, it will be in one of these. Since `c∗` is an eigenvalue for `T∗`, there is an eigenvector `v` in `dom(T∗)` such that `T∗v`=`c∗ v`. For all `w` in `dom(T)`, it follows that `<v, Tw>`=`<T∗v, w>`=`<c∗v, w>`=`c <v, w>`. So, `<v, c w>`−`<v, Tw>`=`0`, and because this holds for all `w`, this means that `<v, (cI−T) w>`=`0` for all `w` in `dom(T)`. Consequently, it follows that the `ran(cI−T)` is not dense, and so the spectrum cannot be continuous.

### Example 13.12

Even though the position operation `Q` in `H`=`L²(R)`![Equation](Images/F000138si93.png) is unbounded, it is, however, self-adjoint. The inner product for `f` in `H` and the product `x·g` in `H`, where `g` is also in `H`, are given by the integral ∫[ − ∞]^∞![Equation](Images/F000138si94.png) `f∗·(x·g) dx)`=`<f, x·g>`. It follows that this quantity equals `<x·f, g>`. The latter inner product is the integral ∫[ − ∞]^∞![Equation](Images/F000138si95.png) `(x·f∗)·g dx)`=`<x·f, g>`. So `Q(f)`=`x·f`, and so the multiplication operator `Q` is self-adjoint.
