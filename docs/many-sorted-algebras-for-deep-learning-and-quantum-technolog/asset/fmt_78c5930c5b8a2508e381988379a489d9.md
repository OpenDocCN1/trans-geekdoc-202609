
Also in this paper, a tensor product is given involving the centroid qubit and the data qubit. An ancillary qubit `|0>` is also involved. These are inputted into the circuit in order `|0 Ψϕ>`. A `Hadamard` gate is applied to the ancillary qubit, thus giving `1/2^(1/2)` `[|0 Ψϕ> + |1 Ψϕ>]`. This is followed by a `controlled swap` gate to assign each state to its nearest cluster. In conclusion, comparable results were achieved between classical and quantum means approaches. However, less execution time was experienced for the quantum system.

## 18.8 Support vector classifiers

Support vector classification (`SVC`) is a binary classification procedure that can be extended to multiclass classification. In terms of machine language, a training set consists of `n` pairs of vectors: {(`v[1]`, `y[1]`), (`v[2]`, `y[2]`), …, (`v[*n*]`, `y[*n*]`)}, where `v[*i*]` are in `H=R^(*N*)` and `y[*i*]` are in {−1, 1}. The objective is to classify the `v[*i*]` by finding a linear decision hyperplane `A`, along with a margin. Technically, a margin consists of a pair of hyperplanes each parallel to `A` and each equidistant from `A`. These margins are also called gutters or curbs. Additionally, the area between them is thought of as a street, with `A` as the center line. Points lying on the curb are called support vectors, and this is how SPV machines acquire their name. SPV machines are often called maximum margin classifiers, usually when the data is separable. Another name for this machine is a hard margin classifier. Soft margin classifiers allow for some miss-classification-type errors to occur. This is allowed because they employ cross-validation. This results in better overall classification.

Mathematically, the affine classifying hyperplane is `A=min(|<w, v[*i*]>+b|)` for `i=1, 2, …, n`. Let `y[*i*]=1` whenever it is `y[*i*]` in `A^+`= `{v[*i*]![Equation](Images/F000163si50.png) in H, such that <w, v[*i*]![Equation](Images/F000163si51.png)>+b is greater than or equal to zero}`. Likewise, let `y[*i*]![Equation](Images/F000163si52.png)` equal −1, when `y[*i*]![Equation](Images/F000163si53.png)` is in `A^-`![Equation](Images/F000163si54.png)`={v[*i*]![Equation](Images/F000163si55.png) in H, such that <w, v[*i*]![Equation](Images/F000163si56.png)>+b is less than zero}`. Normalization can be employed in separable circumstances. Assume that the scaling is adjusted and that normalization occurs in the following. Recall that `A={v in H, such that <w, v>+b=0}`. Now the two gutters, that is, the parallel affine spaces one unit from `A`, are given by: `A^+`![Equation](Images/F000163si57.png)`={v in H, such that <w, v>+b=1}` and `A^-`![Equation](Images/F000163si58.png)`={v in H, such that <w, v>+b=−1}`. Also, by the scaling, the minimization is `|<w, v[*i*]>+b|=1`. By requiring the margin to be as large as possible, this can be converted into a maximization problem. First, by noticing that for `y[*i*] (<w, v[*i*]>+b)`, it is greater than or equal to one for all `y[*i*]` in `A^+`![Equation](Images/F000163si59.png)` as well as in `A^-`![Equation](Images/F000163si60.png)`.

Using the aforementioned facts, solving a constrained convex maximization problem will find `A` as well as the maximum margin. In the following, it is assumed that the data is linearly separable. If this is not the case, the method fails. Moreover, the method is called a hard margin classifier. Finally, the Lagrange multipliers technique will be used to maximize or minimize an objective function with constraints. This will lead to a quadratic optimization that almost always needs an approximate numerical solution. However, a very simple example is provided below, but even here a lot of computation is required.

To begin, finding the max `||w||^(−1)`![Equation](Images/F000163si61.png)` with constraints: `y[*i*](<w,v[*i*]>+b)`![Equation](Images/F000163si62.png)` is greater than or equal to one, for all `y[*i*]`![Equation](Images/F000163si63.png)`. This maximization problem can be replaced by finding the min `||w||²/2`![Equation](Images/F000163si64.png)`, with the same constraints. So, the first step is to form the Lagrangian: `L=1/2 <w, w>−∑`![Equation](Images/F000163si65.png)` a[*i*][y[*i*]`![Equation](Images/F000163si66.png)` (<w, v[*i*]`![Equation](Images/F000163si67.png)`>+b)−1]`. Here, the `a[*i*]`![Equation](Images/F000163si68.png)` are called the Lagrange multipliers; they also need to be found. Next, take the partials derivatives or the gradient with respect to `w`, `b`, as well as all the `a[*i*]`![Equation](Images/F000163si69.png)`, and then set them all equal to zero. Do this first with `∂`![Equation](Images/F000163si70.png)` L/∂w=w−∑`![Equation](Images/F000163si71.png)` a[*i*] y[*i*] v[*i*]=0`, and then, `∂`![Equation](Images/F000163si72.png)` L/∂b=∑`![Equation](Images/F000163si73.png)` a[*i*] y[*i*]=0`. Substituting the quantity, `w=∑`![Equation](Images/F000163si74.png)` a[*i*] y[*i*] v[*i*]`, back into `L` gives the long expression `L=1/2 <∑`![Equation](Images/F000163si75.png)` a[*i*] y[*i*] v[*i*], ∑a[*j*]y[*j*]v[*j*]> − ∑a[*i*]y[*i*][<∑a[*j*]y[*j*]v[*j*], v[*i*]>] − ∑a[*i*]y[*i*]b + ∑a[*i*]`![Equation](Images/F000163si76.png)`. Next using `∑a[*i*]y[*i*]=0`![Equation](Images/F000163si77.png)` and simplifying the equation gives `L = − ∑a[*i*] − 1/2∑∑a[*i*]a[*j*]y[*j*]y[*i*] <v[*i*], v[*j*]>`![Equation](Images/F000163si78.png)`. Now using the decision rule with `w`, `<w, v>+b`, that is, its relationship with one, `w = ∑a[*i*]y[*i*]v[*i*]`, `∑a[*i*]y[*i*] <v[*i*], u> + b`. `L = − ∑a[*i*] − 1/2∑∑a[*i*]a[*j*]y[*j*]y[*i*](<v[*i*], v[*j*]>)`![Equation](Images/F000163si79.png)`.

### Example 18.8

Consider the training set: {(`v[1]`, `y[1]`), (`v[2]`, `y[2]`), (`v[3]`, `y[3]`)}, where `v[*i*]` are in `H=R²` and `y[*i*]` are in {1, −1}. To find the `SVC` `A`, where `A={v in H, such that <w, v>+b=0}`. Above `A` should be those vectors with `y[*i*]=1`, and below `A` should be those vectors such that `y[*i*]= −1`. Say that the vectors `v[*i*]` with `y[*i*]=1` are `v[1]=(−1 0)′` and `v[2]=(0 1)′`, and the one with `y[*i*]= −1` is `v[3]=(0–1)′`. Form the inner product Gram matrix `G`, with entries `<v[*i*], v[*j*]>`, `i=1, 2, 3` as row indicators, and `j=1, 2, 3` as column indicators. The matrix is given as follows:

$\\begin{matrix} {|1} & 0 & {0|} \\\\ {|0} & 1 & {{- 1}|} \\\\ {|0} & {- 1} & {1|} \\\\ \\end{matrix}$

![Equation](Images/F000163si80.png)

The Lagrangian for this problem is `L=− ∑`![Equation](Images/F000163si81.png)` a[*i*]−1/2 ∑∑`![Equation](Images/F000163si82.png)` a[*i*] a[*j*] y[*j*] y[*i*] (<v[*i*], v[*j*]>)`. In particular, expanding out provides `L = − (a[1]+a[2]+a[3]) − 1/2(a[1]²+a[2]²+a[2]a[3]+a[3]a[2]+a[3]²)`![Equation](Images/F000163si83.png)`. Here, the inner products `<v[*i*], v[*j*]>` are substituted using values from the Gram matrix `G`. Taking the partial derivatives of `L`: `∂L/∂a[1]=−1−a[1]=0`; `∂L/∂a[2]=−1 – a[2] – a[3]`; `∂L/∂a[3]=−1 – a[3] – a[2]`. From this, it is seen that `a[1]=−1`. The other two equations are dependent, `a[2]+a[3]=−1`. However, using the constraint, `∑`![Equation](Images/F000163si84.png)`a[*i*]y[*i*]=0`, this implies that `a[2]−a[3]=1`. Consequently, `a[2]=0` and `a[3]=−1`. Finally, using `w=∑`![Equation](Images/F000163si85.png)` a[*i*] y[*i*] v[*i*]`, it follows that `w=−v[1]+v[3]=(1 0)′+(0–1)′=(1–1)′`. Therefore, the affine line classifier happens to be a subspace of `R²`![Equation](Images/F000163si86.png)`, in x-y coordinates; it is `y=x`. The margin here is `1/2^(1/2)`![Equation](Images/F000163si87.png)`, and support vectors are `v[1]=(−1 0)′` and `v[3]=(0–1)′`.
