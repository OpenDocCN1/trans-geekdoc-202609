
The momentum operator is also unbounded, but it too is self-adjoint in `H` = `L²(R)`![Equation](Images/F000138si96.png). The inner product in this case is `<f, -i d/dx g>`; it is the integral `∫[-∞]^∞`![Equation](Images/F000138si97.png) `f*·(-i d/dx g) dx`. Here it is assumed that the derivatives are integrable. Now integrating by parts gives `(f*·g)` evaluated between minus infinity to plus infinity, along with the integral `∫[-∞]^∞`![Equation](Images/F000138si98.png) `(i d/dx f*)·g dx`. Assume that the product `(f*·g)` goes to zero; then this result in terms of the inner product is `<-i d/dx f, g>`, thus showing that this operator is also self-adjoint.

## 13.4 Bounded operators and numerical range

As mentioned in [Section 7.1](#Ch007_131-144_B9780443136979000205.xhtml#s0010), the numerical range is very useful in characterizing the types of spectra associated with bounded operators on Hilbert space. To see this, consider the operator `T` in the Banach space or actually the C∗ algebra, `B(H)`. The numerical range for an operator `T` is `sup[‖v‖=1]`![Equation](Images/F000138si99.png) `<v, T v>`. Assume that `cI - T` is not invertible, and it is not even one to one. Then, there exists `v` nonzero in `H` with `(T - I c) v = 0`. Use this identity, `T v = c v`, and form the inner product with `v`, `<v, T v> = c <v, v>`, where `‖v‖ = 1`. This shows that the eigenvalue `c` is in the numerical range. As a consequence, the point spectrum `sppT` is a subset of the numerical range.

Now assume that the range of `(c - T)` is not dense in `H`; this means that the closure `clos(c - T)` is a proper subset `A` of `H`. Then there exists a unit vector `w` in `A^⊥`![Equation](Images/F000138si100.png), such that the inner product `<w, (T - c) w> = 0`, that is, `<w, T w> = c`. So again `c` is in the numerical range. Thus, the residual spectrum `sprT` is part of the numerical range of `T`.

Lastly, assume that the range of `(T - c)` is dense in `H`, but not equal to `H`. In this situation, there exists a sequence of normalized vectors, `w[n]`![Equation](Images/F000138si101.png) in `H` such that `(T - c) w[n] → 0`![Equation](Images/F000138si102.png). A sequence going to zero has to exist. Otherwise, `(T - c)` would be bounded from below, and as seen in [Section 7.6](#Ch007_131-144_B9780443136979000205.xhtml#s0035) and earlier in this chapter, dense and bounded from below implies `(T - c)` is invertible. Using the inner product with the sequence `w[n]`![Equation](Images/F000138si103.png) gives `<w[n]`![Equation](Images/F000138si104.png), `(T - c) w[n]>` = `<w[n]`![Equation](Images/F000138si105.png), `T w[n]`![Equation](Images/F000138si106.png) > − `c` < `w[n]`![Equation](Images/F000138si107.png), `w[n]` > → 0![Equation](Images/F000138si108.png). As a consequence, this time `c` is in the closure of the numerical range. In this case, the continuous spectrum `spcT` is a subset of the closure of the numerical range.

A list of properties for the numerical range `W(T)` for `n` by `n` complex -valued matrix are provided as follows ([Gustafson and Rao, 1997](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib5)):
1. `W(T)` is a compact subset of `C`.
2. `W(T)` is a convex set in `C`, by the Hausdorff-Toeplitz theorem.
3. `W(T)` contains the spectrum of `T`.
4. If `T = T*`, then `W(T)` is the closed interval on the real line. Moreover, it consists of end points that are two eigenvalues of `T`.

The next example illustrates the use of eigenvalues for finding the numerical range. It also illustrates Property 4). This same example was solved by numerical simulation in Example 7.3 where a most general qubit in `C²`![Equation](Images/F000138si109.png) was inserted into the numerical range formula.

### Example 13.14

The eigenvalues for the self-adjoint matrix `T =`
```
\begin{matrix}
{|2} & {i|} \\
{|{- i}} & {1|} \\
\end{matrix}
```
![Equation](Images/F000138si110.png)
are found from the solution of the characteristic equation: `t² − 3t + 1 = 0`![Equation](Images/F000138si111.png). The eigenvalues are `(3 + -5^(1/2))/2. = .382, 2.618`![Equation](Images/F000138si112.png). The numerical range is `W(T) = [.382, 2.618]`.

More generally, for an operator `T` on Hilbert space `H`, several other properties hold for the numerical range ([Shapiro, 2004](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib15)).
1. `W(T)` is invariant under unitary similarity.
2. `W(T)` lies in the closed disk of radius `‖T‖` centered at the origin.
3. `W(T*) = {c* for all c in W(T)}`.
4. `W(I) = {1}`.
5. For `a, b` in `C`, and `T` in `B(H)`, `W(a T + b I) = a W(T) + b`.
6. If `T` is nilpotent, that is, `T^(n+1) = 0`![Equation](Images/F000138si113.png), then the spectrum of `T` is zero in `clos W(T)`.

### Example 13.15

Use as the carrier set the algebra of 2 by 2 complex-valued matrices `T`. If `T` is the matrix with all zeros except for the first row second column entry, here, let entry `T[1,2]`![Equation](Images/F000138si114.png) be equal to one, `T =`
```
\begin{matrix}
{|0} & {1|} \\
{|0} & {0|} \\
\end{matrix}
```
![Equation](Images/F000138si115.png)
Then `sp(T) = 0` and `‖T‖ = 1`. Additionally, `T` is nilpotent order two since `T² = 0`.

For unbounded operators, relations between the spectrum and the numerical range are the following: ([StackExchange, 2015](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib16)).
1. If `T` is normal, then the whole spectrum of `T` lies in the `clos W(T*)`.
2. If `T` is symmetric, contained in `clos T` contained in `T*`, then the spectrum need not be real, but `clos W(T)` is a subset of `R`.
3. If `T` is not a closed operator, and `T` is a subset of `clos T = T**`, then the spectrum is complex and it contains the `clos W(T)`.

For a self-adjoint bounded operator `T` on `H`, there is a relationship between the numerical range `W(T)` and an eigenvalue of `T`. When `a = inf <v, T v>` for all `‖v‖ = 1`, then `a` is in `sp T` ([Gustafson and Rao, 1997](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib5)).

As mentioned earlier, the real part of the dissipative operator `T` in a Hilbert space `H` is one such that for all `v` in `H`, the real part of the numerical range is nonpositive. That is, `Re <v, T v>` is nonpositive, with `‖v‖ = 1`. The next few examples will illustrate this concept.

### Example 13.16

Let the carrier set for the Hilbert space be `H = R^(n)`![Equation](Images/F000138si116.png), with the usual inner product if `T = -I`, that is the negative of the diagonal identity matrix; then for all `v` in `H`, `<v, T v> = -‖v‖²`![Equation](Images/F000138si117.png), as such `T` is dissipative.

### Example 13.17

Another instance of a dissipating operator `T` this time in `L²(R)`![Equation](Images/F000138si118.png) intersects `C_o¹(R)`![Equation](Images/F000138si119.png). This is the space consisting of continuously differentiable functions of compact support, which are absolutely square integrable. Let `T` be a momentum type operator, `T = d/dt`.

Then `<T f, f> = integral, ∫[t = -∞]^∞`![Equation](Images/F000138si120.png) `f′* f dt`, where the prime stands for derivative. Now integrating by parts gives `<T f, f> = ∫[t = -∞]^∞`![Equation](Images/F000138si121.png) `-f* f′ dt = - <f, T f> = - <T f, f> *`. So the real part of `<T f, f> = 0`, because when `z = - z*`, `z` is pure imaginary. Accordingly, `T` is dissipative.

In a Banach space `A`, the definition of an operator `T` being dissipative is extended because there need not be an inner product. In this case, if for all `c > 0` and all `v` in `dom(T)`, `‖(c - T) v‖` is greater than or equal to `c ‖v‖`; then `T` is dissipative. A quite complete compilation of dissipative operators in a Banach space is in [Lumer and Philips (1961)](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib9) and [Engel and Nangel (2000)](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib4). These properties are needed in proving the Lumer-Phillips theorem previously mentioned relating the spectrum of an operator with the spectrum of its dual operator.
