
# Banach Space `l¹` and Its Dual

The Banach space `l¹` has a dual space `l^∞`; additionally, there is a continuous isomorphism from `l^∞` into the dual space of `l¹`. Consider a continuous linear operator `ϕ`![Equation](Images/F000138si15.png), `ϕ`![Equation](Images/F000138si16.png): `l^∞`→`C`. Let `en`, `n`=0, 1, 2, …, in `l¹` be a basis where `en`=(0, 0, …0, 1, 0, …) and has one in the nth tuple and zero elsewhere. For `v` in `l¹`, where `v`=(v0, v1, v2, …), the norm of the difference $\\left\\| {v - \\sum\_{n = 0}^{N}\\left( {{vn} \\cdot {en}} \\right)} \\right\\|$![Equation](Images/F000138si17.png) is less than or equal to $\\sum\_{N + 1}^{\\infty}\\left| {vn} \\right|$![Equation](Images/F000138si18.png), which goes to zero as `N` goes to infinity, since `vn` is absolute summable. Now using `ϕ`![Equation](Images/F000138si19.png) and then employing the continuity condition gives $\\phi\\left( v \\right) = \\lim\_{N\\rightarrow\\infty}\\phi\\left. \\lbrack{\\sum\_{n = 0}^{N}\\left( {{vn} \\cdot {en}} \\right)} \\right\\rbrack = \\lim\_{N\\rightarrow\\infty}\\sum\_{n = 0}^{N}{\\left( {{vn} \\cdot \\phi\\left\\lbrack {en} \\right\\rbrack} \\right) =}\\sum\_{n = 0}^{\\infty}\\left( {{vn} \\cdot \\phi\\left\\lbrack {en} \\right\\rbrack} \\right)$![Equation](Images/F000138si20.png). Since `ϕ`![Equation](Images/F000138si21.png) is continuous for all `w` in `l¹` and since `ϕ`![Equation](Images/F000138si22.png) is bounded, ‖`ϕ`![Equation](Images/F000138si23.png)‖ is less than or equal to `c`, where `c`> 0. Also,|`ϕ`(`w`)|is less than or equal to ‖`ϕ`![Equation](Images/F000138si24.png)‖ ‖`w`‖. In particular,|`ϕ`(`en`)|is less than or equal to ‖`ϕ`![Equation](Images/F000138si25.png)‖ ‖`en`‖=`c`. So `ϕ`(`en`) is bounded, and as a consequence, the series for $\\phi\\left( v \\right) = \\sum\_{n = 0}^{\\infty}\\left( {{vn} \\cdot \\phi\\left\\lbrack {en} \\right\\rbrack} \\right)$![Equation](Images/F000138si26.png) is absolutely converging; $\\sum\_{n = 0}^{\\infty}\\left| {{vn} \\cdot \\phi\\left\\lbrack {en} \\right\\rbrack} \\right|$![Equation](Images/F000138si27.png) is less than or equal to `c` $\\sum\_{n = 0}^{\\infty}\\left| {vn} \\right|$![Equation](Images/F000138si28.png). It follows that ∥`ϕ`∥![Equation](Images/F000138si29.png) is less than ∥`ϕ`(`e` `n`)∥![Equation](Images/F000138si30.png) because a bounded operator norm is the infimum of the Lipschitz constant `M`, where ∥`ϕ`(`v`)∥![Equation](Images/F000138si31.png) is less than or equal to `M`‖`v`‖. This implies that ∥`ϕ`∥ = ∥`ϕ`(`e` `n`)∥![Equation](Images/F000138si32.png).

Several examples that follow ([He’lein, 2014](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib21)) involve the Banach space `l¹` and its dual space `l^∞`. In the following, the range and image of an operator `T` are used interchangeably. That is, `ranT`=`imT`; they are the same. Additionally, the closure of an operator is denoted by `closT`.

### Example 13.4
Consider the Banach space `l¹` of all complex-valued sequences `v`=(v1, v2, …), which are absolutely summable. Let `L` be the left-shift operator `L` (`v`)=(v2, v3, …). The dual operator is denoted by `L∗` and not symbolized as `L′` for readability purposes. It operates in the dual space `I^∞`, the space of bounded sequences `w`=(w1, w2, …). Even though the inner product is not applicable, it will be employed as an operational calculus device to quickly find the adjoint. So formally, taking the inner product <`w`, `L` (`v`)>=w1∗v2+w2∗v3+…. Next, to find `L∗`, use <`L∗` (`w`), `v`> and then equate this result to the sum written earlier as <`L∗` (`w`), `v`>=w1′∗v1+w2′∗v2+w3′∗v3+…, where the prime, that is, wn′∗, indicates the nth tuple of `L∗`(`w`). Equating coefficients of `vk` involving <`L∗` (`w`), `v`> and <`w`, `L` (`v`)>, and solving for wk′∗, gives w1′∗=0, w2′∗=w1∗, w3′∗=w2∗, …. This shows that the dual operator `L∗` (`w`)=(0, w1, w2, …). The resulting operation is the right shift.

Note that ∥`L`^(`n`)∥ = ∥(`L*`)^(`n`)∥ = 1![Equation](Images/F000138si33.png), and the norm equals the spectral radius for both `L` and `L∗`. Gelfand’s formula from [Section 8.10](#Ch008_145-164_B9780443136979000059.xhtml#s0055), shows this. Here the limit `n`→∞, of ∥`L`^(`n`)∥^(1/`n`)\=*r*(`L`)=1=*l**i**m**i**t* `n`→∞![Equation](Images/F000138si34.png), of ∥(`L*`)^(`n`)∥^(1/`n`) = *r*(`L*`) = 1![Equation](Images/F000138si35.png). Because of this result, the spectra of `L` and `L∗` lie in the closure of an open ball centered at the origin, and of radius one. The ball is denoted `clos(O(0,1))`. Where `clos` represents the closure operation. All the points of the open ball are eigenvalues, that is, `O(0,1)` is a subset of `sppL`. If `v`=(1, 0, 0, …), then `v` is an eigenvector of `L` with `c`=0 as an eigenvalue, because `L`(`v`)=0 `v`. Other points `c` in `O(0,1)` are also in `sppL`. To see this, let `v` = (1,`c`,`c`²,…)![Equation](Images/F000138si36.png) then `L`(`v`) = (`c`,`c`²,`c`³,…) = `c`(1,`c`,…) = `c` `v`![Equation](Images/F000138si37.png). Accordingly, the results mentioned earlier show that the open unit ball consists of eigenvalues for `L`.*

### Example 13.5
Again consider the Banach space `l¹`, as in [Example 13.4](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#enun4). This space consists of all complex-valued sequences `v`=(x1, x2, …), which are absolutely summable with `L` the left-shift operator `L` (`v`)=(x2, x3, …). It was seen in that example that the dual operator `L∗` operates in the dual space `l^∞`![Equation](Images/F000138si38.png), the space of bounded sequences `w`=(y1, y2, …). Moreover, the dual operator `L∗` (`w`)=(0, y1, y2, …) is the right-shift operator. It was seen that the spectra of `L` and `L∗` lie in the closure of an open ball centered at the origin with radius one, `clos(O(0,1))`. The inclusions hold: `O(0,1)` is contained in `sppL`, which itself is contained in `spL`, and this is contained in `clos(O(0,1))`. Then, since `spL` is compact, `spL`=`clos(O(0,1))`=`spL∗`, by the Lume-Phillips theorem. In [Example 13.4](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#enun4), it is shown that `O(0,1)` is a subset of `sppL`. Ultimately, it will be seen that `O(0,1)`=`sppL`, that is, the open unit ball is the point spectrum for `L`.

First note that the boundary of `O(0,1)` is not in `sppL`. That is, the boundary points are not eigenvalues. The boundary consists of {`c`, such that|`c`|=`1`}. Say that `c` is in `ker(c−L)` and not equal to {0}. This is proof by contradiction. Let `v`=(x1, x2, …) and then (`c`−`L`) `v`=0. That is, (`c` x1, `c` x2, …)=(x2, x3, …). A result of this identity is a recursive set of equations. Specifically, the recursion must hold, x(n+1)=`c` xn, `n`=1, 2,…. Solving, by using backward substitution, gives `v` = `c` `x`1(1,`c`,`c`²,…)![Equation](Images/F000138si39.png). Since|`c`|=`1`, `v` is not in `I¹`![Equation](Images/F000138si40.png), unless x1 is zero. It follows that the boundary is not in `sppL`, and therefore, the whole point spectrum is `sppL`=`O(0,1)`.

Next, it will be seen that the point spectrum of `L∗` is empty. Again assume not, that is, there is a complex value `c` such that (`c`−`L∗`) `w`=0 for a vector `w`=(y1, y2, …) in `l^∞`![Equation](Images/F000138si41.png). That is (`c` y1, `c` y2, …)=(0, y1, y2, …); this also leads to a set of equations, namely: `c` y1=0, `c` yk=y(k-1), `k`=2, 3, …. When `c`=0, then `w`=0 and cannot be an eigenvector. Otherwise, when `c` is not zero, then from the equations mentioned earlier, y1=0, and then by recursion, yk=0 for all `k`=2, 3, …. This implies that `w`=0, and it cannot be an eigenvector. Thus, `sppL∗` is empty, and all that is known so far is that for the adjoint; the closed ball centered at the origin is the spectrum of `L`.*
