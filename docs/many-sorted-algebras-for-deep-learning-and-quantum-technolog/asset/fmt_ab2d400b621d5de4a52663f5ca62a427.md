The spectrum of unbounded operators will be considered. First recall, for bounded operators, that in a Banach algebra the resolvent set is open. Also, the resolvent is analytic. ## 13.12 Brief descriptions of spectral measures and spectral theorems
There are numerous versions of the spectral theorem. To begin with a most powerful version, [Haase (2018)](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib6) called the multiplicative version for a normal operator `T` on a Hilbert space `H`. In this case, `T` is unitarily equivalent to a multiplication operator on some `L²`![Equation](Images/F000138si230.png) space in a semifinite measure space. Semifinite measures are described in Appendix A.2. As a quick review, a measure is said to be semifinite if for each `E` in a measure space, `M` with measure, `μ`![Equation](Images/F000138si231.png) (`E`)=∞![Equation](Images/F000138si232.png), there exists a measurable set, `F` a subset of `E` such that `0 < μ(F) < ∞`![Equation](Images/F000138si233.png). A sigma finite measure is always a semifinite measure.
The spectral theorem for a normal operator `T`, in Hilbert space `H`, says there exists a unitary operator `U`, such that `T:H→L²(S), T*=U^(−1) M U`![Equation](Images/F000138si234.png). Here, `S` is a semifinite measure space that could be a Radon measure in a locally compact space. `M` represents a continuous multiplication operation in `L²(S)`![Equation](Images/F000138si235.png). This spectral theorem shows that normal operators in Hilbert spaces have a multiplicative representation in `L²`![Equation](Images/F000138si236.png); however, they are not unique. For a self-adjoint operator `T`, there is a real-valued measurable function `f`, corresponding to multiplication by a unitary operator: `U:H→L²`![Equation](Images/F000138si237.png), involving measure space `M`, where `U T U^(−1)`![Equation](Images/F000138si238.png). This multiplication operator involves `f` whose domain is `{g in L²`![Equation](Images/F000138si239.png), such that `f g in L²}`. When `H` is separable, as assumed unless stated otherwise, a finite measure space can be employed.
A simpler version of the spectral theorem will briefly be described for a bounded self-adjoint operator `T`, `T: H→H`, where `H` is a Hilbert space over `C`. Here, `T` is given by a projective-valued measure, `PVM`. `T=∫ λ dE[λ]`![Equation](Images/F000138si240.png). In this case, the integral is understood as a Stieltjes integral ([Apostle, 1974](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib1); [Rudin, 1987](#Ch013_231-253_B9780443136979000138.xhtml#bib22)), here, `E[λ]`![Equation](Images/F000138si241.png) is called the integrator and is a projection onto the null space consisting of the positive part of `T[λ]^+ = 1/2{(T−λI)+[(T−λI)*(T−λI)]^(1/2)}`![Equation](Images/F000138si242.png). Since `T` is self-adjoint, the positive square root of the square `(T−λI)²`![Equation](Images/F000138si243.png) could appear, in place of the bracketed portion. Finally, the limits of integration are from the lower limit, equaling the minimum of the smallest eigenvalue `m`, or infimum of the continuous spectrum to the upper limit, the larger of the biggest eigenvalue, `M`, and the supremum of the continuous spectrum. Additionally, using functional calculus integrals of the form `f(T)=∫ f(λ) dE[λ]`![Equation](Images/F000138si244.png) will be considered in [Chapter 19](#Ch019_349-364_B9780443136979000278.xhtml#Ch019_349-364_B9780443136979000278).
