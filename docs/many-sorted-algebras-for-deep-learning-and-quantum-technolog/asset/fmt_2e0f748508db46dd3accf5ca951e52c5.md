
# Spectral Theorem

Again to motivate the spectral theorem involving `T`, a self-adjoint operator in `B(H,H)`, consider the carrier set `R^n`![Equation](Images/F000278si239.png). Additionally, let `T` be a matrix over `H` with distinct eigenvalues, that is, `T` has a nondegenerate discrete spectrum. Assume that values within `sppT` are ordered, that is, `m = λ[1] < λ[2] < … < λ[n] = M`. The corresponding eigenvectors should be normalized, thereby forming an `ON` basis. Define a projection operator `P[j]`![Equation](Images/F000278si240.png), such that `P[j] v = < v[j], v > v[j] = a[j] v[j]`![Equation](Images/F000278si241.png), where `v[j]`![Equation](Images/F000278si242.png) is the jth eigenvector for `T` and `a[j]`![Equation](Images/F000278si243.png) is in `R`. Since any vector in `H` is a superposition of the basis element, then $v = \\overset{n}{\\underset{j = 1}{\\sum^{}}}a\_{j}v\_{j}$![Equation](Images/F000278si244.png), and so $v = \\overset{n}{\\underset{j = 1}{\\sum^{}}}P\_{j}v$![Equation](Images/F000278si245.png). As a consequence, $\\overset{n}{\\underset{j = 1}{\\sum^{}}}P\_{j}$![Equation](Images/F000278si246.png) can be thought to be equal to the identity element `I`. The sum of projections in this case is often called the resolution of the identity. The operation `T v` can be written as ${Tv} = \\overset{n}{\\underset{j = 1}{\\sum^{}}}\\lambda\_{j}P\_{j}v$![Equation](Images/F000278si247.png). Accordingly, when `v` is nonzero, the operator $T = \\overset{n}{\\underset{j = 1}{\\sum^{}}}\\lambda\_{j}P\_{j}$![Equation](Images/F000278si248.png).

At this juncture, it is time to provide the essence of spectral theories from an engineering perspective. Basically in this case, it will be explained what is meant by integrating on the spectrum `spT`. First, forget the projections for a moment, and consider delta functions, located at each eigenvalue, `δ(t−λ[j])` for `j=1, 2,…, n`. For a continuous function `f` on the real line, a type of spectral integral for `f` is $\\int\_{- \\infty}^{\\infty}{f\\left( t \\right)\\sum\_{j = 1}^{n}{\\delta{({t - \\lambda\_{j}})}}}{dt}$![Equation](Images/F000278si249.png). Here, the integral goes from minus infinity to plus infinity. It could have instead only went to a finite upper limit, say `x` in `R`. In that case, only those eigenvalues less than or equal to `x` would be included in the integral. This integral operation maps from the reals into the reals; specifically, it maps the spectrum of `T` into `R`.

Continuing with the projection operators, these operators are used in mapping the spectrum of `T` into `B(H,H)`. Consider the staircase-type functions consisting of sums of projections, $E\_{\\lambda} = \\underset{\\lambda > = \\lambda\_{j}}{\\sum^{}}P\_{j}$![Equation](Images/F000278si262.png). Notice that `E[λ]=0` for `λ < λ[1]`, and for `λ`![Equation](Images/F000278si263.png) in the interval `[λ[1],λ[2])`, `E[λ] = P[1]`![Equation](Images/F000278si264.png); also for `λ`![Equation](Images/F000278si265.png) in the interval `[λ[2], λ[3])`, `E[λ] = P[1] + P[2]`![Equation](Images/F000278si266.png). The operator `E[λ]`![Equation](Images/F000278si270.png) can be thought to be a staircase-type function, continuous from the right with saltus `s[j]`![Equation](Images/F000278si271.png), at each eigenvalue.
