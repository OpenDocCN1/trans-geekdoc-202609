
For a Hilbert space `H`, over the field `C`, an associated projective space `PH` can be defined. It is the set of all rays `L`, in `H` where `PH={L, such that L is a complex one-dimensional subspace of H}`. Consider `H − {0}`, with its induced topology from `H`, and let `T: (H − {0})→ PH`. Then, choosing a single nonzero point on each ray `L` shows that `T` is an onto map. Additionally, choosing the final topology for `PH` with respect to `T` makes `PH` a topological space. Here, in the final topology, open sets `U` in `PH` are those for which `T^(−1) (U)` are open in `H − {0}`. For `v`, `w` in `H − {0}`, an equivalence relation can be defined by `v ~ w` means `v=aw` for `a`, nonzero in `C`. Equivalence classes were illustrated in [Example 4.19](#Ch004_57-81_B9780443136979000011.xhtml#enun19). Denote the quotient space, `(H − {0})/~` by `H~`, and define the quotient map `T~: (H − {0}) → H~`. This produces a unique homeomorphism `M`, `M: H~ → PH`. Elements in `H − {0}` are often referred to as state vectors, whereas elements `L` of `PH` are called the rays. When there exists a unit norm, they are referred to as pure states.

The projective Hilbert space inherits an inner product from `H`. In this case, the binary function `IN: PH x PH → [0, 1]`, and for `v`, `w` in `H − {0}`, then `IN(v,w)=|<v,w>|/(‖v‖‖w|)|`.

### Example 4.21
When a quantum system is prepared to be in state `v` in `PH` for measurement and the state of the observable is `w` in `PH`, then the probability of the event is interpreted to be `IN(v,w)²`.

When the dimension of `H` is greater than one, the `PH` is a complete semi-metric space with semi-metric given by `d: PH x PH→ [0, infinity]`, where for `L`, `K` in `PH`, the distance `d(L,K)=inf ‖v − w‖`, where `v` is in `L`, `w` is in `K`, and `‖v‖=‖w‖=1`.

### Example 4.22
If `H=C²`, the projective space `PH` is the set of all straight lines in the complex plane `C`, which go through the origin. In particular, if `L` is the y-axis and `K` is the x-axis, then `d(L,K)=2^(1/2)`. This follows, since unit lengths are employed, and the distance from point `(1, 0)` to point `(0, 1)` is `2^(1/2)`.

The Fubini-Study metric `D` is defined on a projective Hilbert space ([Sakai, 1995](#Ch004_57-81_B9780443136979000011.xhtml#bib7)). It is an equivalent metric to `d`, in the previous example. The Fubini-Study metric is such that `D: PH x PH → [0, infinity]`, where `D(L,K)=arccos(|L,K|)`. Equivalency is shown by the inequalities: For all `L` and `K` in `PH`, `d(L,K)` is less than or equal to `D(L,K)` that is less than or equal to `2^(1/2) d(L,K)`.

Isometries can be defined in projective spaces. If `PH1` and `PH2` are projective spaces, then `A: PH1 → PH2` is called an isometry whenever it is distance preserving, `|<A(L), A(K)>|=|<L, K>|`, for all `L` and `K` in `PH1`. An isometric automorphism is a mapping `A`, where `A: PH → PH` when `A` is a 1–1, onto isometry. This is also called a Wigner automorphism or symmetry of the quantum system ([Chevalier, 2007](#Ch004_57-81_B9780443136979000011.xhtml#bib2)). There are many types of isometries such as translations, rotations, and reflections as well as glide reflections, which are reflections about a line followed by translations. These symmetries form a group. Distinct isometries are described as being linear whenever `A` obeys:
1. 1) Additive: `A (L + K)=A (L) + A (K)`.
2. 2) Homogeneous: `A(aL)=a A(L)`, for `a` in `C`.
However, if (1) holds and
* 2′) Conjugate Homogeneous: `A(aL)=a\* A(L)`,
then the isometry is said to be conjugate linear. A theorem of Wigner’s; involves isometries in a Hilbert space of dimension greater than one. For an isometry `A: PH → PH`, there exists a linear or conjugate linear isometry `S` such that `S: H → H`. Moreover, `A=S′`, which is an isometry on `PH` mapping the ray `cv` into the ray `cSv`, for `v` in `H−{0}`.

It is important to mention a related topic, namely antiunitary or conjugate linear unitary operations. In particular, for a bijection `T: H → H` satisfying the three conditions below, the operator is called an antiunitary map; here `a` and `b` are in `C` and `v` and `w` are in `H`:
1. 1) Adjoint Inverse: `T T*=T* T=I`.
2. 2) Additive: `T(v + w)=T(v) + T(w)`.
3. 3) Conjugate Homogeneous: `T(a v)=a\* T(v)`.
In terms of inner products, this is often condensed into:
4. 4) Inner product, `<T(v), T(w)>=< v, w>*`.
However, the last representation is useful, but care must be taken because all operators in this document are assumed to be linear, unless specified otherwise.

### Example 4.23
If `T` is antilinear, then so also is `T^(−1)`. For a non-zero, `T^(−1)(av) = w`, implies that `av = T(w)`, or `v = 1/a T(w) = T(1/a\* w)`. From this `T^(−1)(v) = 1/a\* w`, or `w = a\* T^(−1)(v)`, thus showing the inverse is also antilinear. It also follows that `T^(−1) (i) T=−i T^(−1)T=−i`.

In this chapter, several operations involving matrices are specified, along with an MSA polyadic graph description of the sorts and operations involved.
