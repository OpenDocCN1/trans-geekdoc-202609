
The bound state solution is found by solving the time-independent Schrödinger equation: `−h²/(2 m)d²ψ/dx² = E ψ`![Equation](Images/F000217si42.png). Here, `V=0` to the left and right of the origin. Also `E < 0`. Make a substitution `d²ψ/dx² = k² ψ`![Equation](Images/F000217si43.png), where `k = (−2 m E)^(1/2)/h`![Equation](Images/F000217si44.png), and do not forget that `E < 0`. In this case, two negatives make a positive. Solving in general, `ψ(x)=A e^( − k x)`![Equation](Images/F000217si45.png) + `B e^(k x)`![Equation](Images/F000217si46.png). The delta function exists at the origin; to the left of the origin, the solution is `ψ`[1]![Equation](Images/F000217si47.png); and to the right, the solution is `ψ`[2]![Equation](Images/F000217si48.png). The delta function at the origin is `−a δ(x)`. The quantity `B` must be zero for `x>0`, since it provides an unbounded solution. For `x<0`, the scalar `A` must be zero.

Since the waveform must be continuous, boundary matching is applied. So, for continuity `ψ`[1](x)=`B e^(k x)`![Equation](Images/F000217si49.png) must be equal to `ψ`[2].(x)=`A e^( − k x)`![Equation](Images/F000217si50.png) for `x=0`; this implies that the two constants `A` and `B` are equal, that is, `A=B`.

Derivative matching fails because of the delta function infinite potential. Instead, integrate the time-independent Schrödinger equation from minus epsilon to epsilon. And let epsilon go to zero. Using `V(x)=−a δ(x)`, then `∫[ − ϵ ]^(ϵ) −h²/(2m) d²ψ/dx²dx − ∫[ − ϵ ]^(ϵ) a δ(x) ψ(x)dx = ∫[ − ϵ ]^(ϵ) E ψ(x)dx`. Integrating and preparing to substitute limits of integration gives `− h²/(2 m) dΨ/dx |[ − ε ]^(ε) − a ψ(0) + E ψ(x) |[ − ε ]^(ε) = − h²/(2 m) dΨ/dx |[ − ε ]^(ε) − a Ψ(0) + 0`![Equation](Images/F000217si57.png). The last zero occurs from the integral mean value theorem. So `dψ/dx |[ − ϵ ]^(ϵ)`![Equation](Images/F000217si58.png) =`−2 m a/h² ψ(0)`. Considering the left and the right solutions and differentiating gives `dψ[1](x)/dx |[ − ϵ ]`![Equation](Images/F000217si59.png) =`B k e^(−k ϵ)` and `dψ[2].(x)/dx |^(ϵ)`![Equation](Images/F000217si60.png) =`−B k e^(−k ϵ)`. Subtracting the lower limit from the upper limit and letting `ϵ→0` yields `− 2 B k = −2 m a/h² ψ(0)`. Let `ψ(0)=B` and then cancel out `B`, so `k = m a/h²`, but `k² = −2 m E/h`, and this shows quantization and allows solving for the quantized energy `E = − m a²/(2 h²)`![Equation](Images/F000217si61.png).

Finally, normalize the wave function `∫[ − ∞ ]^∞ ψ * ψ dx =1`, using the fact that this is a Gaussian-type kernel and the area under the curve is one. This determines `B = (m a)^(1/2)`![Equation](Images/F000217si63.png). The normalized bound state solution is described by `ψ(x) = (m a)^(1/2) e^( − m a |x| / h^(1/2))`, `E = − m a²/(2 h²)`![Equation](Images/F000217si64.png). This solution is illustrated in [Fig. 6.1C](#Ch006_103-128_B9780443136979000217.xhtml#f0010).

The next example below illustrates the free particle solution and demonstrates the tunneling process. In this situation, the energy `E` is greater than zero.

### Example 6.6

Scatter the free particle wave solution for the case `E>0`. The value `V` is zero everywhere except at the origin. The time-independent Schrödinger equation is `–h²/(2 m)d²ψ/dx² = E ψ`. This time, since the energy is positive, let `k² =2 m E/h²`; then `d²ψ/dx² = −k² ψ`. Again, there are two separate solutions one to the left of the delta function and one to the right. To the left of the origin, the solution is `ψ[1](x)=A e^(i k x)`![Equation](Images/F000217si65.png) + `B e^( − i k x)`![Equation](Images/F000217si66.png), and to the right of the origin, the solution is `ψ[2](x)=D e^(i k x)`![Equation](Images/F000217si67.png) + `F e^( − i k x)`![Equation](Images/F000217si68.png). Using continuity of the wave function solutions amounts to setting the left-hand solution equal to the right-hand solutions at the origin. This shows that `A + B = D + F`.

As mentioned in the previous example, the derivative need not be continuous at the origin since there exists a delta function potential. So as before, integrate the Schrödinger equation. As in the case of a bound state, the limits form a small neighborhood about the origin. Thus, the integral is `∫[ − ϵ ]^(ϵ) −h²/(2m) d²ψ/dx²dx − ∫[ − ϵ ]^(ϵ) a δ(x) ψ dx = ∫[ − ϵ ]^(ϵ) E ψ dx`. Applying limits gives `−h²/(2 m) dψ/dx |[ − ϵ ]^(ϵ) − a ψ(0) + E ψ(x) |[ − ϵ ]^(ϵ) = − h²/(2m) dψ/dx |[ − ϵ ]^(ϵ) − a ψ(0) + 0`. Again, the last zero results from the mean value theorem for integrals. Differentiating and substituting in for the left- and right-hand solutions shows that `i k (D − F) − i k (A − B) = −2 m a/h² (A + B)`, where `ψ(0)` was arbitrarily chosen to be equal to `ψ[1](0)=A + B`. Define a constant to compress the solution, where `β = m a/(k h²)`. So, `D − F = A (1 + 2 i β) − B (1 − 2 i β)`.

Now, heuristics can be applied. Say that scattering occurs only from the left. Assume that a wave `ψ`![Equation](Images/F000217si83.png) is transmitted from left to right. Therefore, it can be assumed that the constant `A` is known. So all solutions will involve `A`. Also, in this case, `B` can be thought of as being a coefficient for a reflected wave. Since the wave is going from left to right, `F` should be set to zero because it cannot be reflected. However, `D` can be considered as the coefficient for a tunneled wave. Therefore, the overall solution will be given in terms of `A`. Solving for `B` and `D` results in `B = i β/(1 − i β) A` and `D = 1/(1 − i β) A`.

The reflection coefficients are defined using `R = |B|²/|A|² = β²/(1+β²)`. Similarly the transmission coefficient, that is, the tunneling coefficient, is `T = |D|²/|A|² 1/(1+β²)`. Replacing `β` by `m a/(k h²)` results in `R = 1/(1 +2 h² E/(m a²))` and `T = 1/(1 + m a²/(2 h² E))`. Moreover, `F + T =1`. To illustrate quantum tunneling, as `E→∞`, `T→1`, that is, the greater the energy the more likely the particle will tunnel through. On the other hand, the closer the energy is to zero, the more likely that the particle will be reflected. In the nonquantum situation, that is, classically, these values are equal to `T=1` and `R=0` for the potential well. Also these values are `T=0` and `R=1` for the potential barrier. However, in quantum theory, for the delta function situation, they have the same value. That is, if a barrier was used in this problem instead of a quantum well, the solutions would be the same. That is, `T = 1/(1+β²)`, and `R = β²/(1+β²)`. Again, these quantities sum to one when added together, and they are interpreted as being probabilities.
