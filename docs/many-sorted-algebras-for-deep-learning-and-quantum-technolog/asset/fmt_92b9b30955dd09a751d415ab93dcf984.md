
# Outer Product in C²

Refer to `Example 4.6` where the fundamental Hilbert space setting is `C²`. That is, the carrier set for `VECTOR` is the set of all 2 by 1 complex column vectors identified with kets. The outer product of the two kets `|r>=(a b)′` with `|s>=(c d)′` is a 2 by 2 matrix `E`. Entries of `E` are the following: `E11=ac∗`, `E12=ad∗`, `E21=bc∗`, and `E22=bd∗`. The same result could have been found by multiplying the 2 by 1 column vector `|r>=(a b)′` by the 1 by 2 row vector `<s|=(c∗ d∗)`, corresponding to a bra representation for `|s>`. Again, `|r><s|=` 
$\\begin{matrix} {|{\\text{ac} \\ast}} & {{\\text{ad} \\ast}|} \\\\ {|{\\text{bc} \\ast}} & {{{\\text{bd} \\ast}|}.} \\\\ \\end{matrix}$
![Equation](Images/F000084si8.png)

Consider any three vectors `u`, `v`, and `w` in `C²`. The inner product `<w, v>` times the ket `|u>` equals the outer product `|u> ⊗`![Equation](Images/F000084si9.png) `w>)|` times ket `v`. That is, `<w|v>|u>=(|u> ⊗`![Equation](Images/F000084si10.png) `w>)|v>`. This is the usual definition of an outer product as a rank one operator. This identity will now be illustrated: for instance, let `u=(a b)′`, `v=(e f)′`, and `w=(c d)′`. Then forming the left hand side gives the 2 by 1 column vector, `<w|v>|u>=((c∗e +d∗f) a (c∗e + d∗f) b)′`.

Next, forming the right hand side (`|u>⊗`![Equation](Images/F000084si11.png)`|w>)|v>`, it is given below by the two by two matrix (`|u>⊗`![Equation](Images/F000084si12.png)`|w>`), followed by the column vector `(e f)′`,
$\\begin{array}{lll} \\left. \\middle| \\text{ac∗} \\right. & \\left. \\text{ad∗} \\middle| \\right. & \\left| e \\right| \\\\ \\left. \\middle| \\text{bc∗} \\right. & \\left. \\text{bd∗} \\middle| \\right. & {\\left| f \\right|.} \\\\ \\end{array}$
![Equation](Images/F000084si13.png)

Multiplying the 2 by 2 matrix times the 2 by 1 column vector gives a new 2 by 1 vector:
$\\begin{array}{l} {|\\left( {\\text{ac} \\ast e + \\text{ad} \\ast f} \\right)|} \\\\ {{|\\left( {\\text{bc} \\ast e + \\text{bd} \\ast f} \\right)|}.} \\\\ \\end{array}$
![Equation](Images/F000084si14.png)

From this, the identity (`|u>⊗`![Equation](Images/F000084si15.png)`|w>)|v>=<w|v>|u>` is seen to hold.#

More generally, any column vector (n by 1) multiplying a row vector (1 by n), in that order, corresponds to the outer product. When using bras and kets, the conjugate must be employed in the row representation of a bra. In any case, the results of the multiplication are an n by n matrix. Arbitrary ket-bra multiplications each of the same degree provide outer products in an n-dimensional Hilbert space.

The following examples continue with Hilbert space `C²`, but this time the origin is not included because states can never be zero, and arbitrary phase factors are ignored. In quantum areas, overall phase differences cannot be detected. Numerous representations and lower level views of simple (pure) states within the MSA are provided next. As far as quantum applications are concerned, the following representation is most important; it is that of Poincare or Bloch sphere. See [Fig. 5.3](#Ch005_83-101_B9780443136979000084.xhtml#f0020). It generalizes to Lie groups. In particular, the special unitary group, `SU(2,C)`, is a 2 by 2 matrix representation for manipulating qubits. This involves the Pauli basis as well as the commutator operation and the antisymmetric Levi-Civita symbol. Additionally, in the orthogonal group `O(3, R)`, the pure state is given by a 3 x 1 column vector with an invariant unit norm. High views of the algebraic and topological foundations of Lie groups and Lie algebras are provided in a forthcoming chapter.

### Example 5.3

With the exception of arbitrary phase, the most general single qubit is described in this example. It is the 2 by 1 vector: `|q>=(cos (t /2) e^(*ip*) sin (t 2))′`, where `p` is a real number in \[0, 2*π*) and `t` is real valued in \[0, *π*\]. Correspondingly, it can be written using a linear combination of kets: `|q>=(cos (t /2)|0> + e^(*ip*) sin (t 2)|1>`. Making use of the outer product or projection operation: `|q><q|`, this results in the 2 by 2 self-adjoint matrix. It is positive definite, of trace one, with the nonnegative determinant. It is a representation of `|q>`, called the density matrix, `ρ`=
$\\begin{array}{ll} {|{\\qquad\\cos^{2}\\left( {t/2} \\right)}} & {{\\sin\\left( {t/2} \\right)\\cos\\left( {t/2} \\right)e^{- ip}}|} \\\\ {|{\\sin\\left( {t/2} \\right)\\cos\\left( {t/2} \\right)e^{ip}}} & {{{\\qquad\\sin^{2}\\left( {t/2} \\right)\\mspace{52mu}}|}.\\#} \\\\ \\end{array}$
![Equation](Images/F000084si16.png)

The aforementioned example provided a Hilbert space representation of a pure state, a single qubit. Previously mentioned, the most general qubit was illustrated; it is given by `e^(*is*) |q>`, where `s` is a real number in \[0, 2*π*) and `|q>` is given earlier. A most intuitive, as well as a most important, representation of a single qubit is given next. It ignores the global phase `e^(*is*)`, but involves the Bloch sphere and is described next.

A point on the surface of the Bloch sphere is always a pure state. The point is given by the 3 by 1 vector, `q`, in the Lie group `O(3)`. It is obtained using spherical coordinates or using normalized Stokes parameters needed in polarization identification. This representation for the single qubit is called the spherical representation of the qubit and is the 3 by 1 column vector: `q=(sin (t) cos (p) sin (t) sin (p)cos (t))′`. Here, `t` denotes an (elevation, latitude type) angle in \[0, *π*\], and `p` is a (longitude type) angle in \[0, 2 *π*). Of special importance are the north and south poles. These occur by using `t=0` and `t=*π*`, `p=0`, respectively, thus obtaining kets `|0>` and `|1>`, respectively. See [Fig. 5.3](#Ch005_83-101_B9780443136979000084.xhtml#f0020). So, for instance, using `|q>=(cos (t/2) e^(*ip*) sin (t 2))′`, with `t=0`, gives `|0>=(cos (0 /2) e^(*ip*) sin (0 2))′=(1 0)′` in `C²`. Additionally, the spherical representation of this qubit is `q=(sin (0) cos (p) sin (0) sin (p) cos (0))′=(0 0 1)′`. In three dimensions, this is x=0, y=0, and z=1; it represents the north pole. A similar representation can be given for `|1>`. In this case, the representation on the Bloch sphere is (0 0–1), and this is the south pole.

The next representation involves the three Pauli matrices: `s1`, `s2`, and `s3`. Along with the 2 by 2 identity matrix, these four matrices form an orthogonal basis for all 2 by 2 complex self-adjoint matrix Hilbert space. So, for any self-adjoint matrix `T`, that is, `T=T∗`, it follows that `T=n[1] I + n[x] s1 + n[y] s2+ n[z] s3`, where all the `n[j]` are real numbers.

The Pauli matrices are given below in order, `s1`, `s2`, and `s3`:
$\\begin{array}{lll} {|{0 1}|} & {|{0 - i}|} & {|{1 0}|} \\\\ {|{1 0}|} & {|{i 0}|} & {|{0 - 1}|} \\\\ \\end{array}$
![Equation](Images/F000084si17.png)

The self-adjoint matrix `T` mentioned earlier, when the Pauli matrices are substituted, is given by:
$\\begin{matrix} {|{n_{1} + n_{z}}} & {{n_{x} - {in}_{y}}|} \\\\ {|{n_{x} + {in}_{y}}} & {{{n_{1} - n_{z}}|}.} \\\\ \\end{matrix}$
