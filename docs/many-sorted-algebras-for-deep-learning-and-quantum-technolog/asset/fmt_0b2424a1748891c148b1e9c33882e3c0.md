
# Hilbert Space Operators

## Trace class operators

Let `H` be a finite, `n`-dimensional Hilbert space. For any `n` by `n` matrix `M`, the trace defined earlier agrees with the usual trace operation on matrices. No matter which `ON` basis is employed, it must follow that `Tr(T)` always yields `Trace(M)`. For instance, use the carrier set `H=C²`. Let the basis set be `{i|0>, −1 |1>}={(i 0)′, (0 –1)′}`; then using these 2 by 1 vectors along with `M`=

```
\begin{matrix} {|{4 - i}} & {{- 2}|} \\ {|3\mspace{27mu}} & {{- 2}|} \\ \end{matrix}
```

![Equation](Images/F000205si110.png)

gives `Tr(M)=<(i 0)′, M (i 0)′> + <(0 −1)′, M (0 −1)′ >`. So: `Tr(M)=<(i 0)′, (4i+1 3i)′> + <(0 –1)′, (2 2)′>`. Now recall that the inner product is conjugate linear and so the left operand should be conjugated. Therefore, `Tr(M)=4 − i − 2=2 − i`. By inspection, the sum of the diagonal elements of `M` is the trace of `M`. The result agrees with `2 − i`.

A generalization of this example is that any bounded linear operator with a finite-dimensional range, that is, whose codomain is of finite cardinality, is of trace class. However, in general, infinite-dimensional situations can be different. The next example is for an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, `H`. The operator in this example is not of trace class even though it gives a finite trace for a single `ON` basis, but not for all `ON` bases for `H`.

### Example 7.13

Let `B={e0, e1, e2, …}` be an `ON` basis for `H`. It will be shown that an operator `T` has trace equal to zero with respect to the basis `B`, but it has infinite value with respect to basis `F`. Let `F={(1/ 2^(1/2)) (e1 − e0), (e2 − e1), (e3 − e2), …}`; it is also an `ON` basis. This can be seen by forming the inner product: `<fi, fj>=(1/ 2) <ei+1 − ei, ej +1 − ej>=1` when `i=j` and `0` otherwise. Next, let `T: H → H`, where `T` sends the basis `B` into itself by transposing adjacent elements. It exchanges even entries for odd ones next to each other and vice versa, that is, `T(e2n)=e2n+1`, `T(e2n+1)=e2n`, `n=0, 1, 3, …`. Note that the infinite sum `∑_i` ![Equation](Images/F000205si111.png) `<(ei), T ei>=0`. But the infinite sum employing basis `F` is `∑_n` ![Equation](Images/F000205si112.png) `<((1/ 2)^(1/2))(e2n+1 − e2n), (1/ 2)^(1/2)T(e2n+1 − e2n)>=` the infinite sum `(1/ 2) ∑_n` ![Equation](Images/F000205si113.png) `<e2n+1 − e2n, e2n - e2n+1>=` infinite sum `1/ 2 ∑_n` ![Equation](Images/F000205si114.png) `− (‖e2n‖² + ‖e2n+1‖²)= −∞`.

Trace class operators are similar to Hilbert-Schmidt operators defined in the next section.

## 7.6 Hilbert-Schmidt operators

This section begins with one of the most important inequalities in Hilbert spaces. It is the Cauchy-Bunyakovsky-Schwarz inequality, and throughout the document, it is abbreviated as `CBS` inequality. This inequality holds for any vectors `v` and `w` in a Hilbert space. However, a similar inequality exists for positive sesquilinear forms in any vector space.

The `CBS` inequality is `|<v, w>|`, which is always less than or equal to the product `‖v‖ ‖w‖`. This result follows by considering the nonnegative inner product: `N=<v + a t w, v + a t w>`. Here, `a` is in `C` and `t` is in `R`. To see that this inequality holds, expand the inner product yielding `N=‖v‖² + a* t <w, v> + a t <v, w> + |a|² t² ‖w‖²`. Next, by expressing the complex constant `a`, as `a=<w, v>`, then if this value is substituted in the aforementioned expression for `N`, it follows that `N=‖v‖² + 2 t | <v, w>|² + |<v, w>|² t² ‖w‖²`. Observe that `N` is a quadratic polynomial; it is a parabola in `t`. Because it is nonnegative, it touches the x-axis at most once. Accordingly, its discriminant is negative or zero. So, the discriminant, `4 | <v, w> |⁴–4 ‖v‖² | <v, w>|² ‖w‖²`, is negative or zero. Notice that if `<v, w>=0`, there is nothing to do. Otherwise, factoring out `|<v, w>|²` shows that the `CBS` inequality holds. Moreover, from the initial representation of `N`, the equality sign holds if and only if `v` and `w` are linearly dependent. An application of the `CBS` inequality follows.

### Example 7.14

Let `v` and `w` be vectors in the Hilbert space `H`. Using the operator norm type of notation, it will be shown that `‖v‖=sup |<v, w>|` where `‖w‖=1`. If `v` is zero, the result follows immediately. Otherwise, let `b=sup |<v, w>|` where `‖w‖=1`. By the `CBS` inequality, it is seen that `|<v, w>|` is less than or equal to `‖v‖`, and so `b` is less than or equal to `‖v‖`. Next, it will be shown that `b` is greater than or equal to `‖v‖`. Letting `w=a v`, then `|<a v, v> |=| a | ‖v‖²`. Since `b` is found utilizing the supremum of `|<v, w>|`, the result is that `b` is greater than or equal to `|a| ‖v‖²`. Finally, since `w=a v`, `|a|=‖w‖/‖v‖=1/‖v‖`, and `‖v‖` is not zero, it is found that `b` is greater than or equal to `‖v‖`, and so `b=‖v‖`.

The following operators are similar to the trace class operators mentioned previously. Here, the Hilbert-Schmidt bounded operators `T` are defined in the Hilbert space `H` when `n` is any integer and `vn` is an `ON` basis. In this case, `A = ∑_{n = - ∞}^{∞}‖T vn‖²` ![Equation](Images/F000205si115.png) converges. Let `wk` and `vk` be the same `ON` set, and notice that the Hilbert-Schmidt norm for bounded `T` is given by `‖T‖ = [∑_{n = - ∞}^{∞}‖T vn‖²]^{1/2}` ![Equation](Images/F000205si116.png). This norm was used for matrix norms earlier and is also called the Frobenius norm. See Section 5.1. When `T` is only a linear operator, the defining sum could be positive infinity. Including the point at infinity with `[0, ∞)` results in a pointing set.

Additionally, the identity holds, `‖T‖=‖T*‖`. This can be seen by first expressing the norm `‖T v‖` for the operator `T` involving the inner product using an `ON` set of vectors `wn`, in `H`. Accordingly, this results in `‖T v‖²` equaling the sum over all the integers of `∑_n |< wn, T v >|²` ![Equation](Images/F000205si117.png). In the following, the sum will always be from `-∞` to `∞`, unless otherwise specified, `‖T‖² = ∑_n` ![Equation](Images/F000205si118.png) `∑_k` ![Equation](Images/F000205si119.png) `| < vk, T wn> |² = ∑_n` ![Equation](Images/F000205si120.png) `∑_k` ![Equation](Images/F000205si121.png) `| < T* vk, wn > |² = ∑_k ∑_n` ![Equation](Images/F000205si122.png) `| <T* vk, wn > |² = ∑_k` ![Equation](Images/F000205si123.png) `‖ T* vk ‖² = ‖T*‖²`. Thus, it follows that `‖T‖=‖T*‖`.

Notice also that from the `CBS` inequality, it can be seen that `‖T v‖²` is less than or equal to `‖T‖² ‖v‖² = ‖T*‖² ‖v‖²`.

Again, employing the `CBS` inequality will show that a bounded Hilbert-Schmidt operator is also compact. Let `Tn` be a sequence of finite rank operators. As such, each `T vn` is represented for any `v` in `H` as a finite sum: `k=1, 2, 3, …, N` of `<wk, Tn v> wk`, with `wk`, as before being an `ON` basis in `H`. Form the quantity `‖(T − TN)v‖²`, notice that from it is the truncated infinite sum `k=N+1, N+2, …` of `|< wk, T v>|²`. Then, using the `CBS` inequality, `‖(T −TN) v‖²` is less than or equal to the tail of the series of `‖T* wk‖² ‖v‖²`, but for `k=N+1, N+2, …`, the sum of `‖T* wk‖²` goes to zero.

The set of all Hilbert-Schmidt operators, `S` and `T`, form a vector space, a subspace of the bounded operators. Moreover, the following inner product can be used and is substantiated using `CBS` inequality: `<S, T>=∑_n` ![Equation](Images/F000205si124.png) `<S vn, T vn>`, where `vn` is an `ON` basis in `H`. Additionally, the vector space of all Hilbert-Schmidt operators forms a Hilbert space ([Conway, 1990](#Ch007_131-144_B9780443136979000205.xhtml#bib2)).
