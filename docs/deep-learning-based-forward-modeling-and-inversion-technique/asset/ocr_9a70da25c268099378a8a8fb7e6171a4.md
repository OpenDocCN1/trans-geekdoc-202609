With the temperature increases, the mean path is restricted by the phonons, thus the thermal conductivity tends to reduce. In fact, the thermal conductivity $k$ and the electrical conductivity $\sigma$ has the following relationship:

$L = \frac{k^2}{\sigma T} \frac{k_B}{e} = 2.44 \times 10^{-8} W\Omega K^{-1}$ (4.3)

Where $e$ is the charge quantum of an electron. For non-metals, the heat transfer is accomplished through the vibrations of the lattice (or phonons in other words). The thermal conductivity of these materials lacks appropriate theory, hence has to be measured experimentally. Figure 4.1 shows the thermal conductivity information of some common solids.

Broadly speaking, the experimental approaches to determine the thermal conductivity can be divided into two categories, namely the steady-state and transient ones. For steady-state methods, the thermal conductivity can be inferred from measurements of the steady state temperature, while transient techniques rely on the temperature history.
