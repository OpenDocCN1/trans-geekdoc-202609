## 3.2 Progress in Inversion Research

### 3.2.1 Conventional Approach

As a typical ill-problem [4], the inverse problem of the heat transfer is extremely sensitive to the disturbance of the definite solution data [5]. In recent decades, scholars have carried out relevant research through various methods. Among all kinds of algorithms, the analytical method is the most accurate and has no theoretical error [6, 7]. Taking a one-dimensional flat plate as an example [8], the series expansion method can be used to reconstruct the boundary heat flux and temperature.

Suppose there is a uniform infinite plate, and a heat flux is applied to its outer surface. The temperature and heat flow of the outer surface can be obtained from the time domain temperature $T_0(t)$ and heat flux values $g_0(t)$ measured on the inner surface. Obviously, all points of the plate satisfy the passive heat conduction equation:

$$\rho C_p \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \kappa \nabla^2 T (3.1)$$

The heat flux on the outer surface satisfies:

$$q = -\kappa \frac{\partial T}{\partial x} (3.2)$$

The $n$-th derivative of both sides of Equation (3.1) at the same time, it can be obtained:

$$\frac{\partial^n T}{\partial t^n} = \alpha \nabla^{2n} T (3.3)$$

If the surface heat flux can be expressed separately in the form of space and time, the temperature of any point in the plate can be expressed in infinite series.

$$T(x,t) = T_0(t) + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{x^2}{\alpha} \right)^n \frac{d^n T_0}{d t^n}}{(2n)!} (3.4)$$

$$\frac{x}{\kappa} \left[ q_0(t) + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{x^2}{\alpha} \right)^n \frac{d^n g_0}{d t^n} \right] (2n+1)!$$

Applying Equation 3.2, the heat flux on the outer surface can be obtained as:

$$q_0(t) = -\rho C_{\rho} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2n-1)!} \left(\frac{\delta^2}{\alpha}\right)^{n-1} \frac{dT_0}{dt^n} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2n)!} \left(\frac{\delta^2}{\alpha}\right)^{n} \frac{dg_0}{dt^n} (3.5)$$

In practical application, only the first few terms of the infinite series expansion are usually taken as approximations. For example, when the thickness is small:

$$q_0(t) \approx -\rho C_{\rho} \frac{dT_0}{dt} + q_0(t) (3.6)$$

O.R. Burggraaf [8] extended Equation 3.6 to cylinders and spheres, and gave examples to verify it. It is worth noting that although the analytical method is accurate in calculation and low resource consumption, its application scope is not wide. It is difficult to find that the method is only applicable to very simple geometric conditions, and requires that the known temperature and heat flows are separated in time and space infinitely differentiable. When faced with two-dimensional or three-dimensional situations, it will be difficult to obtain explicit solutions, so the introduction of numerical methods becomes necessary.

The traditional numerical methods usually include offline methods and online methods. For example, the offline method needs to integrate the data all time to obtain the unknown heat flux, while the online method only needs the temperature measurement data for a short period of time. The common-line methods are all functional-based optimization processes, which can be further divided into deterministic and stochastic optimization. Deterministic optimization depends on the gradient value of the function. In each iteration, the forward heat problem is calculated with the current parameters, while the error between the calculated temperature value and the measured temperature value is obtained. The iterative process is constantly updated to finally reach the minimum value of cost function. Researchers have applied a variety of gradient algorithms to heat flux reconstruction. Figure 3.1 displays an example of gradient-based method.

For instance, Zhou [9] have introduced the conjugate gradient (CG) algorithm to reconstruct the spatially or temporally related boundary conditions of two-dimensional boundary. The results show that developed method can be adopted to various situations with considerable accuracy. In 1992, Taler [10] employed the Newton-Raphson method to retrieve the unknown heat flux through transient temperature sections. Afterwards in 2009 [11], they integrated the Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) algorithm with ANSYS and attained precision estimation of heat flux, convection transfer coefficient and temperature [12]. Moreover, Ngo [13] implemented the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) method to forecast the time-dependent heat flux and heat transfer coefficient of metal.

The gradient-based method has not artificially set parameters, and inversion accuracy is fairly high, so it has strong universality. However, such algorithms are all affected by initial value, so they are easy to fall into local optimum. In contrast, stochastic optimization has certain advantages in finding global optimum solution. Nowadays, scholars have introduced several global algorithms for heat flux reconstruction. For instance, Kane et al. [14] utilized an improvement version of genetic algorithm (GA) to retrieve surface heat flux via transient temperature, whereas Liu [15] implemented the particle swarm optimization (PSO) to reconstruct the unknown heat flux on the base of temperature measurements. Although these methods are more likely to jump out the local optimum, they are arduous to be applied for inversion of heat loads on complex structures due to tremendous amount of calculation and intolerable time costs. Besides, since these algorithms can reconstruct merely a small number of parameters at one time, they are inappropriate for real-time scenarios.

Unsteady state conduction exhibits features of diffusion, which has damping property and delay property. The damping property shows that the change of boundary heat flux has a greater influence on the temperature near the boundary, and the influence will gradually decrease with increase of distance from the boundary. The delay property is that the reaction of internal temperature to boundary heat flux is delayed in time. Therefore, most effective method to retrieve surface heat flux is to estimate it in time sequence rather than in the whole time domain.

To this end, Beck introduced the sequential function method (SFM) in 1968 [16], then described it in detail in 1985. The sequential function specification algorithm was also applied in the research of Huang et al. [17] to reconstruct the temporal and spatial related heat flux by assuming variants are linear during every time step. It is noted that the sensitivity coefficients were acquired by the finite element method (FEM). The SFM presumes a specified formula for the heat flux and predicted the current unknown heat flux by future time measurements. By this method:

$$J_p = \sum_{n=0}^{r-1} \sum_{m=1}^{M} [T(r_m, t^{p+n}) - \tilde{T}(r_m, t^{p+n})]^2$$

Assuming that the heat flux in the selected time window is linear, the heat flux estimate of the $[i+1]$-th iteration is:

$$Q_{i+1}^p = Q_i^p - \frac{\sum_{n=0}^{r-1} \sum_{m=1}^{M} [T(r_m, t^{p+n}) - \tilde{T}(r_m, t^{p+n})] \frac{\partial G_n}{\partial Q^p}}{\sum_{n=0}^{r-1} \sum_{m=1}^{M} [\frac{\partial G_n}{\partial Q^p}]^2}$$

where $\partial T/\partial Q^p$ is the heat flux at $G_n$, which is recorded as $G_n$. It satisfies:

$$\rho C_p \frac{\partial G_n}{\partial t} = n\nabla^2 G_n, t \in [t^{p-1}, t^{p+r-1}]$$

$$\kappa \frac{\partial G_n}{\partial t^{p+n}} = -n + 1$$

During the calculation, the temperature history $T(r_m, t^{1+n}), (n = 0, \dots, r-1)$ can be calculated measuring each point from the full field heat distribution at the initial time and the preliminary estimated $Q_i^p (n = 0, \dots, r-1)$ using the heat conduction equation, and the sensitivity $G_n^p$ by applying Equation 3.8. After that, it is feasible to use $Q_1$ and $T(r_m, t^{2+n}), (n = 0, \dots, r-1)$ to reconstruct $Q_2$ by the time step following the same method.

In addition to the sequential function method, Al-Khality [18] adopted the digital 1D filtering HCPs which achieved high stability without scattering effect.
