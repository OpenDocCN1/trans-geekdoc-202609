1.  Foreword

Bitcoin - a topic that divides opinion. For some, Bitcoin is not only a digital currency, but a revolution against the entire financial system. Others retort that Bitcoin has no intrinsic value and you'd better sell your holdings quickly because it will soon be worthless.

Although both sides make valid points, and regardless of how one feels about blockchain technology, Bitcoin has defied all opponents in recent years. The price of a Bitcoin has soared several times, and over time Bitcoin, just like all other cryptocurrencies, has seen tremendous growth, so it at least makes sense to investigate the issue. Blockchain technology is being researched or already being used in many areas, that can no longer be denied. The revolution ahead of us is comparable to the revolution of digitalization. Who has benefited from digitalization? The people who got to grips with the matter at an early stage. Who has not benefited from digitalization? The people who were stuck on their old principles and were not open to new technologies. So it really makes sense to get to grips with new technologies like blockchain or bitcoin at an early stage.

 

What is Bitcoin? Is Bitcoin the same as blockchain? And what other cryptocurrencies are there?

Bitcoin is on everyone's lips, whether on public television or in private circles. A lot of half-knowledge is prevalent, and many terms are confused or used in the completely wrong context.

This is not surprising—after all, the technology is new and relatively complex. A layman is not expected to read up to the deepest level of programming. This requires several years of computer science studies.

Nevertheless, the topic of blockchain and bitcoin is by no means as complex as it might first appear. Only a certain technical understanding and enthusiasm for the topic are necessary to understand the mechanisms.

 

Since you bought this book, you seem to have the enthusiasm for the subject. You want to understand the basics and the structures around Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies. Maybe you are thinking about investing in Bitcoin but want to explore the technical background first. Maybe you just want to have a say the next time Bitcoin or Blockchain is mentioned at the regulars' table. Or maybe you're a critic looking for weaknesses in the technology - because Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies have those too!

In any case, you will not regret having dealt with the matter.

Books on blockchain technology are mostly either purely focused on the monetary aspect of Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies or written in such a technical way that they require several years of computer science studies to follow the book content.

However, these books are completely unsuitable for newcomers: page-long programme lines that you forget after a week. The result is confusion. This demotivates and quickly causes many interested people to close their minds to the subject. This is not because of the readers, but because they simply do not belong to the target group of the book. You don't have to be an Einstein to understand the technology behind Bitcoin, you just have to be motivated to deal with the matter.

And it is precisely from this way of thinking that this book has emerged.

It provides a beginner's guide for inquisitive people who want to learn the basics of blockchain, bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies without much prior knowledge.

2.  Entry and basic knowledge

The monetary system as we use it today determines almost all areas of our lives, whether it's choosing our career path, financing our home or taking a summer holiday.

Of course, money alone does not make people happy, but there are numerous studies that show that money problems cause increased stress levels. That is why it is important to deal with the money system at an early age. Where does money come from? What is the function of money? How do banks earn their money?

Unfortunately, there is a huge gap in financial education. Economic contexts are usually not part of the school curriculum and are therefore only insufficiently taught. Yet fundamental mechanisms are at work in the market that have a significant influence on everyone's everyday life.

It is therefore important to know what this money is, how it has developed historically and what problems are associated with it.

To get an overview of the current system and its strengths and weaknesses, let's start by taking a look at the history of our money.

2.1.         The history of money

The history of money goes back to the origin of mankind.

Until about 2000 years before Christ, it was common for goods and commodities to be exchanged directly. At markets or shops, all yields were brought together and exchanged. The farmer gave part of his surplus grain for a few woolen blankets, the cobbler a pair of shoes for fresh milk and fish, and so on. Relatively quickly it became clear that it made more sense not to trade goods directly, but to trade a substitute product that unified the value of the different goods. At the same time, this substitute must be smaller, more manageable and remain stable for longer, i.e. it must not become mouldy or worn out.

The aim was to be able to store the manpower that is in the products in the long term.

Relatively quickly, shells, diamonds and precious metals such as gold and silver were identified as potential stores of value. In the end, precious metals such as gold, silver and bronze compounds stood out.

Gold was the primary, stable currency until the 16th century. But why, with so many possible commodities, did the choice fall on precious metals of all things? To understand this, let's first take a look at the tasks of money or a currency. What properties must it possess, what problems does it solve?

2.2.         What is money?

We have already seen that it makes sense to introduce a time-independent store of value in the form of a currency.

![](Image00000.jpg)For the value of the currency to be maintained, it must be rare. Rare means that the commodity is only available in limited quantities. If the currency can be increased, the value decreases.

This is the case, for example, with (hyper-) inflation. The amount of money is increased rapidly and as a result the purchasing power of the currency decreases.

![Picture 4](Image00001.jpg)Strictly speaking, other factors such as economic growth also play a decisive role. However, within the required precision of this book, we can neglect these aspects.

The second elementary property of money is that it must be difficult to produce . This primarily serves to protect against counterfeiting. This is also the case with gold. It is very costly to recover gold from the earth's crust and to mint coins.

This brings us directly to the next essential point of a functioning currency: substitutability . Substitutability means that several objects within a currency class have the same value. A €10 note is worth the same as another €10 note. This is not the case with shells or diamonds, and that is why they have not become accepted as a means of payment. With diamonds, for example, there are different degrees of value. Two diamonds that look the same can still have a different value. Only an expert can determine the true value.

Gold, on the other hand, can be melted and pressed into coins of the same size and weight - this ensures replaceability. In addition, you can decide for yourself how big the coins should be.

![](Image00002.jpg)A currency must be transportable , divisible and counterfeit-proof in order to ensure smooth trade.

The last criterion is also the most important criterion for a stable currency and will be discussed more often in this book: We are talking about trust .

Trust is the central building block of the monetary system, and gold has earned this trust over thousands of years as a safe means of payment. Gold is therefore often referred to as a "safe" investment or "safe haven". Gold itself has no intrinsic value. You cannot eat it or make clothes out of it. A piece of jewellery looks visually appealing, but nowadays it is largely independent of the current gold price.

The mere confidence that we will receive goods from a third party for a piece of gold makes it possible to use gold as a means of payment.

However, gold and gold coins also have disadvantages. For one thing, they are relatively difficult to produce. This plays into the hands of counterfeit protection; however, this has turned out to be very impractical from today's perspective. In addition, the divisibility or resolution is limited. Today, a fine ounce of gold (1 oz tr. = 31.1 g) is around 1500-2000 USD. If we would like to get one eurocent of change at the cash register, we would get back 0.0002 g of gold. Therefore, this is not feasible.

![](Image00000.jpg)Therefore, the idea has come up to keep the gold as a basis of exchange, but no longer to exchange directly with the gold, but to create a kind of promissory note that can be traded more easily.

What is important in this context is that behind each of these promissory notes ![Picture 4](Image00003.jpg)or paper notes there was a piece of physical gold that could be exchanged for the note at any time. Central banks issued the notes and stored the deposited gold.

This concept of a currency being backed by a commodity such as gold or silver is called the gold standard.

The confidence that we will get our piece of gold back for our promissory note is guaranteed by the bank.

This system has many advantages. Paper is light and can be produced in large but also small quantities. Different banknotes represent different divisions.

But why did the idea suddenly arise that this system and the currencies as we have them are not suitable as long-term means of payment and store of value? To find out, let's take a closer look at the problems with our paper money system. It all started with the abandonment of the gold standard.

2.3.         Abandonment of the gold standard

We have already learned about the gold standard. It means that a currency, be it the euro, the Japanese yen or the dollar, was backed by a commodity such as gold, silver or even oil. This gold standard was gradually dissolved. For example, because the gold reserves in the First World War were not sufficient to finance the costs of the war, paper money was unceremoniously decoupled from the price of gold.

All currencies today are unbacked currencies. They are also called fiat currencies.

What are the characteristics of this new, unbacked currency?

The money is still just as transportable and divisible as before. However, money can be multiplied at will by the people responsible for controlling the money press. This task is taken over by the central banks.

If a central bank prints 10% new banknotes per year without destroying old banknotes, each existing banknote is only worth about 90%. Of course, the value on the banknote itself does not change. A €10 banknote will remain a €10 banknote forever. However, the purchasing power, i.e. what we get for our money, decreases. As a result, the prices of food, real estate or material assets follow suit.

This does not automatically have to be bad - as long as the standard of living is also adjusted. Optimally, rising salaries offset this effect.

In any case, we see that the property of storing value is controlled solely by a central entity. We trust this entity to use its power over the monetary system wisely. As long as it continues to do so, there is little to oppose the unbacked fiat money system. So much for the theory.

But what does this theoretical view look like in reality?

Even if the money monopoly is used sensibly, one major problem always remains: trust in a central institution like the state and central banks.

![](Image00004.jpg)We must trust that what we use as money will also be accepted as money by other people and that we will get other goods in return.

If this is not the case, we are stuck with the currency and it is worthless. So where does the confidence come from that our currency, i.e. our euros, Swiss francs or dollars, will still be accepted by other participants in the monetary system tomorrow or in ten years' time? Because the abandonment of the gold standard means that it is no longer backed by gold.

It goes without saying that we will be able to buy food in the supermarket tomorrow with our euro notes, doesn't it?

Yes and no. This system requires a central institution to ensure that we get value for the coloured notes we use as money. This is the task of the state and the central banks , which watch over the money. We trust that the bank has really "put away" €10 for our €10 note.

![](Image00004.jpg)In stable times, this centralised banking system works wonderfully. However, when a crisis arises and confidence in the state dwindles, confidence in the currency often dwindles at the same time.

There is a direct correlation between the stability of a government and the value of the currency.

There have been plenty of negative examples in the past, for example the Greek crisis, when people ran in droves to the ATMs but were not allowed to withdraw their own money.

Another example is the economic crisis in Venezuela, where trust in the government and the currency was almost completely lost, resulting in an inflation rate of over 130,000% in 2018, according to the International Monetary Fund. As a result, people secured themselves with goods such as oil, dollars or cryptocurrencies.

The latter example also shows us the big problem of all fiat currencies: The fact that they are no longer backed and can be printed in any mass by a central administrative body means that the currency is not protected against inflation. The European Central Bank (ECB), the Federal Reserve (FET) or the Swiss and Japanese central banks can theoretically create billions in new money overnight. And that is how it is done! In principle, inflation is not automatically good or bad. Economists argue about whether slight inflation keeps economic growth high and whether this would also be possible with a deflationary currency.

Spending money or taking on debt is also not reprehensible per se but ensures growth.

In a healthy economic cycle, loans are one of the most effective means of strengthening the economy. When an entrepreneur borrows money to create new jobs and pays the money back with interest in a few years, it has a positive effect for everyone in the system: both for the workers who can feed their families, for the company that can expand, for the consumers who can buy products, and for the bank that gets interest on its capital.

Unfortunately, in the past, investments were not made in strong companies, but in ailing states.

It is a fact that billions of new Dollars have been printed in recent years to buy government bonds of indebted countries like Italy or Greece. This new money is not matched by any countervalue in the form of a country's economic performance. These states do not put the money to good use, but burn it, and merely ensure that more money is in circulation. This in turn devalues the purchasing power of the remaining money.

As an example, let's look at the absolute money supply in the US over the last 25 years.

![](Image00005.jpg)

Figure 1 Development of the absolute money supply in the USA

In 2020 alone, one third of all money was printed.

Whether this will be healthy and sustainable can only be determined retrospectively. Many economists criticise this development and see it as shifting the problem rather than solving the basic problem.

Another problem of the current monetary system is the banking system itself. This system is associated with slow and outdated structures.

Although we have arrived in the digital age, it still takes two working days for a transfer to be made, to transfer abroad it takes even longer, and no transfers are made at all over the weekend. The diversions via an intermediary always takes longer than the direct exchange.

Technically, however, faster transactions are quite easily possible.

And it is precisely this concept that has made companies like PayPal or instant bank transfers so successful.

The structures, for example in credit intermediation with several middlemen, are also neither effective nor cost- or time-saving.

At this point, it should be mentioned that banks have recognised this problem themselves and real-time transfers are becoming more and more established.

The problems mentioned have taken on even greater proportions in recent years due to the digitalisation of money.

2.4.         Digitisation of money

The digitisation of money also initially brings many advantages. For example, we save enormous storage costs and can move the money faster and easier. In addition, we can divide it as we like. Digitally, we can send €20 from €50 credit and keep €30, whereas we cannot divide a €50 note into 2x €20 and 1x €10.

It has also become easier to multiply money. If a central bank wants to print new money, no large money printing plants must be started up. No money transporters must arrive to distribute the money. No need to pay for printer's ink either. With a few mouse clicks on the PC, money in the billions can be created out of thin air within seconds.

The combination of centralised structures that form money monopolies and digitalisation has facilitated the multiplication and devaluation of money.

Cryptocurrencies address all the issues of trust, centralisation and outdated structures. They solve these problems through a simple but ingenious substitution.

Cryptocurrencies are based on mathematics . 2+2 = 4. We don't have to be afraid that tomorrow 2+2= 5.

Cryptocurrencies continue to have a decentralised structure. This means that the data is not stored on a server, but on a distributed network consisting of thousands of computers.

Before we look at cryptocurrencies, Bitcoin and blockchain technology in detail, let's look at the basics of cryptography. The entire blockchain technology is based on these fundamentals and algorithms. Cryptography lets us understand the ingenious mechanisms behind cryptocurrencies. Don't worry: the entire exercise will be kept simple so that it remains easy to understand.

2.5.         Basics Cryptography

![](Image00004.jpg)Cryptography is a science that deals with encryption and the creation of cryptosystems. A cryptocurrency is a digital currency that has a cryptosystem i.e., various encryption algorithms, as its basis.

Transactions are encrypted, executed and confirmed using well-known, tried and tested encryption algorithms. Let's take a systematic look at the individual steps and start with how a message can be encrypted in the first place.

The fact that good encryption is considered secure and is one of the few means left in the fight against hackers or malicious central institutes was also recognised by the well-known whistleblower Edward Snowden.

"Encryption works. Properly implemented strong cryptosystems are one of the few things we can rely on." - Edward Snowden

With the advance of digitalisation, encryption algorithms have become indispensable because the integrity of data is essential for communication across continents.

![](Image00006.jpg)Data integrity says that we can be sure that the data we receive arrives unaltered. This means that no one can change, intercept or delete it along the way.

To ensure this, all communication systems such as the internet, mobile radio or even sensors in industrial plants use encryption algorithms . But how does encryption actually work and what types of encryption are there?

To answer these questions, it is first necessary to define some of the terms and objectives of encryption. We will now look at a model that is often used in cryptography.

Two people, let's call them Alice and Bob, want to communicate with each other in encrypted form. To do this, Alice writes a message in plain text . This means that the message can be read by anyone.

The primary goal of encryption is to keep the message secret , so that it cannot be read along the way. Therefore, Alice cannot simply send the message in plain text, but must encrypt it by means of a procedure so that the message becomes unreadable. Bob, on the other hand, can decrypt the message and receives the plain text back. But how can you encrypt a message? A simple but insecure method is the so-called Caesar shift cipher or Caesar encryption . Here, all letters of the plaintext are shifted by a certain number in the alphabet. As an example, we shift each letter three places backwards in the alphabet. The A becomes a D, the B becomes an E and so on:

 

 
| Letter || A || B || C || D || E || F || G || H || I || … || V || W || X || Y || Z |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Letter+3 || D || E || F || G || H || I || J || K || l || … || Y || Z || A || B || C |

The number three is called the key , because with the help of this number and the algorithm, an encrypted message is created from the plaintext.

![](Image00000.jpg)For example, if Alice encrypts the message "Bitcoin is awesome!" with the key three , the result is the message "Elwfrlq lv dzhvrph! “

(You are welcome to check!)

![](Image00007.jpg)

Figure 2 Symmetric encryption of a message

![](Image00000.jpg)The message is transmitted in encrypted form. Bob receives the message and decrypts the message by shifting all the letters back three places .

For this, Bob must know which method is to be used for encryption and what the key is (in our case, the Caesar shift cipher and the number three). This information must be known beforehand, for example, it must have been agreed upon in a personal meeting.

It is obvious that the procedure is very insecure, because there are a maximum of ![Picture 4](Image00003.jpg)26 different letters in the alphabet, and thus only 26 different keys with which the message can be encrypted. A hacker would therefore have to make a maximum of 26 attempts to crack the encryption.

The Caesar shift cipher is an example of a symmetric encryption method . Symmetric means that the same key is used for both encryption and decryption. For this, both parties must exchange the key without a third person knowing about it.

Another system that will still be of great importance to us is asymmetric encryption.

2.6.         Asymmetric encryption

![](Image00004.jpg)In an asymmetric encryption method , the same key is not used for encryption and decryption, but two different keys . One key is used to encrypt the message, the other to decrypt the message.

The key used for encryption can be openly accessible. It does not have to be kept secret and is therefore also called a public key . The key for decryption, on the other hand, must be kept secret. It is called a private key .

![](Image00008.jpg)

Figure 3 Asymmetric encryption of a message

For this kind of encryption, however, we need a function that is not equally easy to solve in both directions.

![](Image00009.jpg)

Figure 4 Function of a mathematical one-way function

![Picture 4](Image00001.jpg)The public key and the private key are related via mathematical functions. However, it is not possible to infer the private key from the public key.

We cannot easily infer the input of the function from the output of the function, but we can infer the input of the function from the output of the function.

As a simple example we take the function ![](Image00010.jpg).

This equation is relatively simple to solve. On the way back, however, the fourth root would have to be drawn: ![](Image00011.jpg). This is a bit more complicated than pure multiplication. More complex functions and huge numbers are used for asymmetric encryption.

These mathematical functions are highly complex and would go beyond the scope of this book.

In mathematics, this is called a hard problem. In our context, it is only important to understand that the private key and the public key are not independent of each other, but that the public key is related to the private key.

We will look at one more concrete example in this book, but due to the size of the book we will not look at the full background or even the mathematical proofs.

These were already the basics of secrecy or confidentiality by means of encryption procedures. Now we know how an encrypted message is created from a message in plain text.

Summary:

Public Key : Key, freely accessible. This is used to encrypt the message.

Private Key : Key that must be kept secret. This is used to decrypt the message.

Private key and public key are related via complex mathematical functions.

Next, we ask ourselves how we can be sure that Alice really is the sender of the message and that the encrypted message has not been intercepted and changed in transit. These aspects are summarised under the terms: integrity and authenticity.

To ensure the integrity of the message, Alice can "encrypt" the message with her private key. This process is called signing .

We can imagine digital signing like a medieval letter seal.

![](Image00012.jpg)

Figure 5 Signing in the Middle Ages

A sealed letter proves that it has not yet been opened and accordingly cannot have been falsified. The seal, in turn, confirms that a certain person, for example the king at the time, must have sent this letter.

The "encrypted" message represents a digital signature . Bob, on the other hand, can use Alice's public key to decrypt the message. Since only Alice has her private key, the message must have come from Alice.

![](Image00013.jpg)

Figure 6 Digital signature of a message

The signature itself does not constitute encryption. After all, the public key is accessible to everyone. Anyone can check whether the message ![Picture 4](Image00003.jpg)came from Alice or not. If we want to both encrypt and sign the message, we have to combine the two.

Alice encrypts the message with Bob's public key so that only Bob can decrypt the message. Alice then signs the already encrypted message with her private key.

![](Image00014.jpg)

Figure 7 Digitally signing and then encrypting a message

![](Image00004.jpg)When Bob receives the message, he can use Alice's public key to confirm that the message came from Alice. He obtains the content by decrypting the message with his private key.

With this, we have already discussed the basics of asymmetric encryption and digital signatures.

In the next step, we look at the algorithms that are used for verification. These take on the function of a "watchdog" and confirm that, for example in the case of Bitcoin, a transaction, i.e. the sending of Bitcoins back and forth, has taken place according to the rules.

In a centralised system, the central unit (for example a bank), acts as a guardian. The bank checks whether we have enough money in the account, whether the money is directed to a valid address, etc. In a decentralised system, there is no entity that performs these functions. That is why we need cryptographic algorithms.

There are various algorithms that are used nowadays. These are also called auxiliary functions of encryption .

The function or algorithm is called a hash function or hashing algorithm.

Hash functions must have various properties which we will now take a closer look at. We have already learned most of them.

![](Image00015.jpg)Hash functions are one-way functions . This means that calculating the output of the function is simple. However, it is almost impossible to determine the input using the output. Hash functions are used as auxiliary functions in cryptocurrencies.

Different cryptocurrencies are based on different algorithms. We will take a look at the algorithm underlying Bitcoin as an example.

Bitcoin has two algorithms. One algorithm generates our public key from our private key. The other algorithm is responsible for the security of the network and the stored transactions.

Bitcoin uses, among other things, an elliptic curve with the functional equation to generate the public key. ![](Image00016.jpg).

![](Image00017.jpg)

Figure 8 Diagram of an elliptical curve

This complex one-way function uses our password (private key) to create a public key from which our Bitcoin address can be derived. We will take a closer look at how this is implemented in practice later. First, we will look at the algorithm that is responsible for the security of the blockchain.

This algorithm is called the SHA256 algorithm. SHA stands for Secure Hash Algorithm . The function is relatively simple from the user's point of view.

The SHA256 algorithm guarantees Bitcoin's security. We will take a closer look at how Bitcoin implements the algorithm later. The application of the algorithm is always the same:

We pass a plain text to the function. Within the function, the letters are shifted, swapped, compressed or added in several stages.

![](Image00018.jpg)

Figure 9 Sequence of a hash function

How we have to shift the letters, compress them, and so on is fixed by the SHA256 algorithm.

After several stages, we get an "encrypted message" as output. The output of the SHA256 function is called a hash . A hash is a kind of checksum , control or security number . However, it depends very much on the context.

![](Image00004.jpg)The performance or application of a hashing function is comparable to the application of a function to a variable, as they are known from school lessons. Theoretically, we can calculate the SHA256 hash of a text on a piece of paper and a pencil.

The number 256 of the hashing algorithm indicates the length of the output, i.e., the calculated, encrypted message. The output characters are called bits and can only be one or zero.

The output of the SHA256 algorithm is always exactly 256 bits long. It does not matter whether the input consists of a single character or hundreds of thousands.

![](Image00020.jpg)The input of a hash function is arbitrarily large, the output is independent of it and always constant, in the case of SHA256 it is exactly 256 bits long.

![](Image00021.jpg)

Figure 11 Simplified representation of the SHA256 hash function

Since the message only consists of 1 and 0, it is neither legible nor does it follow a system.

Often, for better clarity, four of these bits are combined and displayed as a number or letter. The character string is then shortened to 80 or 64 characters instead of 256.

![](Image00020.jpg)The hashing algorithms are freely accessible. Anyone can load them onto their computer and confirm the result.

It is also important that if we change just one character of our message, the resulting hash changes not just slightly, but fundamentally.

For example, if we only change the spelling of the word Bitcoin to Bit-coin, we get different hashes.

"Bit-coin is awesome!" , on the other hand, results in the hash

![](Image00004.jpg)The hashing algorithms are, as already mentioned, openly accessible and anyone can see how they work. However, due to the complexity, it is not possible to conclude from the output to the input.

We will see how a hash function is used by looking at how passwords are stored.

When we set a new password in our PC, in for example our e-mail account or the online shop of our choice, it would be very insecure if the passwords were stored in plain text on the providers' servers.

Instead, the PC generates a hash of the password and stores it.

![](Image00004.jpg)In the case of a password query, the password entered is again converted into a hash. If it corresponds to the stored hash, the password is correct, otherwise the password entered was not the same.

Now we also understand why we cannot get the password from our provider in plain text. The provider does not have the password itself, but only the hash of the password, which does not allow us to draw any conclusions about the password. If we have forgotten our password, we can only reset it, but not display it.

If a hacker attacks the provider's server, he can optimally only steal the hash, but not the password in plain text.

Hashing algorithms thus guarantee a certain degree of security. Furthermore, hashing algorithms can help to ensure the integrity already mentioned.

If we want to be sure that a message has not been tampered with, we can ask the sender to convert the message into a hash and make it publicly available. The hash only matches that message. If we change just one letter, the hash changes completely unpredictably.

Using the hashes, no one can decrypt the message, because back-calculating the hashing function is almost impossible.

However, we can also convert the received message into a hash and compare it with the public hash. If the message has been changed, the hash values no longer match.

3.3.         Consensus building and mining

![](Image00004.jpg)Mining is the English word for mining, extraction or production and is often used in connection with commodities such as gold, gold mining . Analogously, the term bitcoin mining, i.e., the production or mining of bitcoin, has been established.

Cryptographically, mining describes the process of solving a mathematical problem such as the calculation of the SHA256 algorithm.

When the miners have done their work, the information is ready to become part of the consensus. Consensus describes the agreement of the participants as to what they consider "true" and what they consider "false". Since there is no central institution, the community decides if and when, for example, a transaction can take place and whether they will adopt the transaction into their blockchain.

To better understand this process, let's look at the different participants in a decentralised system. In addition to the miners, there are other participants, for example the full nodes already mentioned. A user can also act in several roles at the same time.

For easier understanding, we take the building of a house as an analogy to the validation process of the individual participants. Using this example, we assign tasks to the participants.

Miner: Miners are the users who do the work, in other words, the mining. The new information, i.e. the digitally signed transactions, are sent to them.

Analogously, miners are the construction workers who build the house with their labour. The blueprint of what the house should look like are the rules of the blockchain.

The miners calculate a potential consensus in the network, which they forward to the nodes for validation.

The miners naturally want to be paid accordingly for their work.

![](Image00004.jpg)Miners are allocated several transactions in a block and create consensus, for example by making their computing power available. Users have to pay a fee for this. These transaction costs are distributed to the miners.

Miners are those who do work to ensure the safety of the consensus.

Full nodes: Full nodes are users who have downloaded the entire blockchain onto their computer and update it regularly. They synchronise with other nodes and form the pillars of the decentralised system. They also validate the information sent to them by the miners.

We can become a full node by running the Bitcoin Full Node programme on our computer. To do this, the computer must be running continuously and connected to the internet. Finally, the transaction history updates every ten minutes.

3.4.         Sending a transaction

To understand how a transaction can be sent on the blockchain, the basics of cryptography are again used.

Alice announces that she wants to send Bob a Bitcoin (1 BTC). First, the intention of the transaction is recorded.

This request is signed with Alice's private key, ensuring that she really is the sender she claims to be and accordingly owns the credit she wants to send.

We remember: With the help of the signed transaction and Alice's public key, it is possible to check whether Alice is the sender.

This signed transaction is sent to all surrounding miners and nodes. Everyone in the network can see this transaction.

In reality, sending the request is again done by the wallet you use to access your bitcoin address.

The miners and nodes in turn send the information to the surrounding miners and nodes, and so on, until every participant in the network has received the information. In reality, this only takes a few seconds.

The nodes check whether the sender has enough coins in his possession by checking whether the signature is correct. To do this, they use the public key, i.e., the sender's address. The confirmed transactions are collected in a block. In Bitcoin, about 4,200 transactions fit into one block. The miners secure this block with their computing power and send the secured block to all nodes. The transaction has been confirmed. If an error occurs, the transaction is rejected.

3.5.         Proof of Work and Double Spending

So far, we have learned that miners provide their computing power to secure transactions. This is the so-called Proof of Work (POW) principle.

![](Image00004.jpg)Proof of work means that work must be done in the form of computing power to secure the transactions so that they cannot be falsified. This concept is the oldest and most widespread. Almost all major cryptocurrencies rely on this principle.

The miners must solve a puzzle. In practice, this means that the miners have to calculate different algorithms, such as the SHA256 hashing algorithm.

This is why the computing power provided by a Miner is often referred to as hashing power .

But why is it necessary for miners to do work and calculate hashing algorithms in the first place? How is security generated from this? For this, we look at a problem that is ruled out by this.

Until 2008, there was a problem that was previously considered unsolvable: the double-spending problem.

Let's imagine we are a fraudster and send our money in our bank account via instant transfer to two other accounts almost simultaneously.

With a normal account, it is not possible to send money to two people at the same time to spend more in total than we actually have. The bank controls the balance and blocks one of the transactions so that we can never overdraw our account. If Alice wants to send money to Bob and Charles at the same time, the later transaction is blocked by the bank.

In a decentralised system, however, it is possible to send your money first to one person and then a millisecond later to another person. We have seen that the miners and the full nodes have to synchronise first. By the time all the nodes on the entire planet have synchronised, the money has already been spent several times.

![](Image00004.jpg)When funds are sent from one account to two or more accounts almost simultaneously, this process is called double spending.

Until 2008, it was dogma that the double-spending problem could not be prevented without a central unit.

But how was this problem solved in Bitcoin and other decentralised networks? We have already learned the answer: mining. For a better understanding, let's look at how mining combines the individual blocks containing the transactions into a blockchain. We start by looking at which transactions are primarily processed, and which must wait.

3.6.         Sequence of transactions

A decentralised network, like that of bitcoin, now has millions of users who permanently send thousands of transactions to the blockchain. Since the miners only have a limited capacity to process and validate the transactions, they can be given a voluntary bonus. This is a kind of tip that encourages the miners to process the transaction sooner. In Bitcoin, for example, only 4,200 transactions fit into a block of ten minutes. If you want the transaction to be transferred immediately to the current block, you have to be one of the 4,200 "highest bidders".

We will learn more about the setting of fees in the later chapters.

Theoretically, we don't have to pay a fee at all, but then the transaction will probably never be processed because there is always someone who will pay at least a few cents to have their transaction favoured.

The amount and speed of the transaction costs depend on the load of the network. If many people want to send a transaction quickly, they outbid each other.

![](Image00035.jpg)

Figure 21 Historical transaction fees per bitcoin transaction

This can also be seen in the chart above with the example of Bitcoin. In late 2017 and early 2018, Bitcoin had reached a new all-time high of 20,000 USD. In parallel, the average transaction fees for a transaction rose from under one dollar to over 50 USD, as the demand for Bitcoin led to an extreme utilisation of the network - which of course pleased the miners.

The transactions are thus selected according to a bidding system, and from these, blocks of up to 4200 transactions are in turn combined. The next step is to validate and combine the blocks.

4.  Structure of the blockchain

The blockchain consists of blocks. The first entry of a block is always a reference to the previous block. This establishes the link to the previous block and the blocks link together to form a chain.

In addition, the current block contains the transactions that have occurred in the corresponding period. The entire data of the block is entered into a SHA256 algorithm. As a result, we get a hash of the current block.

![](Image00036.jpg)

Figure 22 Hash generation from the data of a block

We have learned that this hash has a length of 256 bits and is unpredictable. If we change just one character of the entire block, the hash changes completely and unpredictably. And exactly this fact is as simple as it is ingenious. A single change in just one transaction would change the entire hash of the block and thus all other blocks .

This is comparable to a cinema film in which a time traveller changes only one small thing in the past, but the future becomes completely different because of this small change.

Thus, the hash of the current block is also a unique reference to this block. The hash of the current block is used as the first entry of the following block. This creates a sequence of blocks.

![](Image00037.jpg)

Figure 23 Stringing blocks together

The miners calculate the hash of the current block.

However, we still don't know how the miners ensure security.

Now, the trick with blockchain technology is that there is a rule that the hash of the block must start with a certain number of zeros. Otherwise, the block is not accepted.

Since the hash can only be changed if the data of the block is changed, another element is added to the block, called a nonce . This part is variable and does not depend on the transactions. By changing the nonce, the hash of the block is changed. The nonce has no other function than that the hash can be adjusted by changing the nonce.

![](Image00038.jpg)

Figure 24 Hash generation from the data of a block incl. the additional variable "Nonce".

Next, the miners' computing power comes into play. The miners do not have to calculate the hash, but they should find a number so that the hash of the entire block starts with a certain number of zeros. There is only one way to solve this problem: the miners have to try different possibilities for the nonce.

We can think of a block of the blockchain as a big puzzle with one piece missing. All transactions represent pieces of the puzzle and miners have to find the missing, completing piece of the puzzle through trial and error.

If they have found a nonce, the block is accepted.

To illustrate this complex process, let's look at a simplified example and create our own, very trimmed-down block. This contains only the hash of the previous block , a transaction and a nonce . In our case, we start with the zero . Furthermore, our requirement is that the resulting hash must start with a zero.

Using an online SHA256 generator, we obtain the hash that belongs to our data.

![](Image00039.jpg)

Figure 25 Example of hash generation using an online hash generator  
Source: https://emn178.github.io/online-tools/sha256.html

We see that with the data entered, the hash 48c6..... a19 is created. Since this does not start with a zero, the block would not be allowed. We increase the nonce by one until we get a hash that starts with a zero.

In this example, this is the case for the first time for the value 16.

![](Image00040.jpg)

Figure 26 After 16 trials, the resulting hash starts with a zero

We had to calculate a SHA256 algorithm 16 times to arrive at a valid hash. In reality, significantly more zeros are required, so the algorithm has to be run significantly more often.

In the example, we tried the nonce by hand and changed it until the desired result was output. In the Bitcoin network, this is exactly the work of the miners. They try out billions of different nonces per second and calculate the resulting hash each time. When it starts with the required number of zeros, the miner reports, according to the motto: "I've found the solution!" He sends the solved block including his found nonce to all full nodes. These check whether a hash with the required number of zeros is created. If this is the case, the block is appended to the blockchain, and the miner receives his reward.

But why do we set the miners the task of finding the matching nonce? Why don't we just attach the block directly to the blockchain without solving a puzzle first? On the surface, looking for the nonce is a waste of energy, isn't it?

This system that we are looking for a number that makes the hash of the data start with zeros has no deeper meaning. That's not the point either. All that needs to be ensured is that it is a very difficult puzzle to solve.

To understand the meaning of mining, let's imagine that a malicious attacker wants to change the transaction history. In doing so, he not only changes a transaction, but also the hash of the block that contains the transaction. And not only that: since this hash in turn becomes the first entry of the next block, he also changes this block and the next and the next.

![](Image00004.jpg)The malicious attacker would therefore have to recalculate/guess all the nonce numbers on his own. However, he is in competition with all the honest miners who have more computing power and have already found the next block or two.

Analogously, we can imagine the blockchain like a big castle protected by many castle walls. The castle walls are like onion skins around the castle. An attacker (evil miner) has to overcome one wall after the other. The good miners, on the other hand, defend the castle and are already working on ever new castle walls on the inside. The attacker would have to tear down the castle walls faster than the good miners reinforce them.

Analogous to the example: If the attacker solves an old block in the blockchain, the miners have already found several new ones.

It is a race of computing power that the hacker will always lose against the honest miners. It is assumed that the community, the good miners, will always have the absolute majority of hashing power and can thus defeat attacks by individuals, companies or even entire states. This is also the solution to the double-spending problem.

Therefore, the following network rule applies: The longest blockchain , or the blockchain with the most validated blocks, is always the "true" blockchain. Most of the work was done to create this blockchain.

![](Image00004.jpg)It is therefore possible for a person to spend money twice. The transactions are allocated to different blocks and sent to different miners. The block for which the matching nonce was found first is appended and applies as the consensus for the entire network.

It can also happen that parallel chains are created for a short time, that miners work at different ends.

However, one chain will always prevail, and all miners will immediately jump on the bandwagon with the longest chain. For this reason, a transaction is not automatically completed when a miner has integrated it into a block. Only when the next block and the block after that have also been confirmed can we be sure that we are on the main blockchain. For Bitcoin, this is usually three to six blocks, for other cryptocurrencies correspondingly more or less.

But is this effort worthwhile for the miners at all? After all, miners have to buy equipment, consume electricity and maintain the equipment. Let's take a look at mining from the miner's perspective.

When guessing the nonce numbers, which is also referred to as solving the block , the miners all work simultaneously and all against each other. The miner who finds the correct number first and thus completely solves the current block receives the entire transaction fees . ( Transaction fees ).

The other miners go away empty-handed. After that, all the miners immediately rush to the next block and start looking for the right nonce.

![](Image00041.jpg)But that's not all: in addition to the transaction fees, the miner who solves the block receives a reward that is anchored in the programme code and varies depending on the cryptocurrency. This reward is called a block subsidy .

Miner reward = subsidy + transaction fees

Block Rewards = Block Subsidy+ Transaction Fees

For Bitcoin, the initial value of the Block Subsidy is set at 50 Bitcoins per block found.

About every ten minutes, 50 new bitcoins are distributed to the miners in addition to the transaction fees.

 

We have just seen how bitcoins are generated. Bitcoins have not always existed, they are issued through mining and distributed to miners. But doesn't that mean that Bitcoin also involves a kind of inflation? After all, new Bitcoins are created out of nothing. That's exactly what should be prevented, isn't it?

Indeed, this would be the case if 50 bitcoins per block were always distributed, and that is why the Bitcoin protocol has limited the number of distributed coins. The number of distributed coins of 50 bitcoins per block becomes less and less over time. More precisely, the value is halved every 210,000 blocks. This means: The first 210,000 blocks of the blockchain distribute 50 Bitcoins, the next 210,000 blocks only 25 Bitcoins per block and so on.

Since one block is equal to 10 minutes, 210,000 blocks are pretty much equal to 4 years.

So currently, every ten minutes 6.25 BTC is paid out to the person who solves the current block: the person who finds the matching nonce for the block.

![](Image00004.jpg)There will never be more than 21 million Bitcoins. The limit is anchored in the programme code and cannot be changed. This is how Bitcoin protects itself from inflation.

At the end of the chapter, a question should be clarified that some readers have probably already asked themselves. We have seen that a block always contains the hash of the previous block.

But then what was the first entry of the very first block? After all, there is no previous block from which a hash can be generated.

The first block is called the Genesis block . It has two tasks:

It starts the blockchain. The content of the first entry is completely insignificant.

However, the other function that the first entry fulfils is enormously important: it must prove that the blockchain was newly created.

Let's imagine that Satoshi Nakamoto invented the Bitcoin blockchain not in 2008, but already in 2000. However, he did not publish it, but secretly calculated block by block and created a blockchain. In the process, he collected all the block subsidies himself, initially 50 BTC per block. In addition, his computer was offline during the entire period so that no one would know what he was doing.

This calculated blockchain would of course be significantly longer than all blockchains that have only been calculated since 2008. After all, Satoshi would have an eight-year head start. If he suddenly goes online with his blockchain in 2020, every miner, every node and every user will accept his blockchain as the longest and thus the "true" blockchain. All previous mining, all transactions and all created Bitcoins would no longer be valid.

To prevent this, it must be proven that he has not mined a longer blockchain before. But how do you prove that something has only existed for a short time?

The solution is as simple as it is ingenious. Satoshi Nakamoto took the headline of a newspaper and wrote the hash of the headline in the first block. This proved that the blockchain could not have been created before the headline appeared. How else could Satoshi have known the headline?

Satoshi could have taken any headline, but he deliberately chose a symbolic headline that refers to the financial crisis and thus highlights the problems of monetary policy. The headline is from the British Times on 3 January 2009.

![](Image00043.jpg)

Figure 28 Front page of the Times of 3 January 2009

The article titled Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks is about the then British Chancellor of the Exchequer Alistair Darling . He tried in vain to stimulate the British economy. With the choice of headline, the message is obvious: the old system is not working, it is time for something new. It is time for Bitcoin.

![](Image00044.jpg)

Figure 29 Excerpt from the Bitcoins programme code

The heading is therefore found in the source code (programme code) of Bitcoin. It is used to create the first hash, which forms the very first entry of the Genesis block. Among Bitcoin enthusiasts, this headline is well known.

4.1.         Mining Difficulty

What we have seen so far: A block is solved when the matching nonce has been found, i.e., when a miner has found a number that, together with the transaction data, happens to find a hash that starts with a certain number of zeros.

However, it has often been stated that a new block is found every ten minutes. If several miners connect to the network and thus many people mine and make their computing power available, a suitable nonce can be found much faster.

Analogously, we can imagine solving a puzzle:

If ten people try to solve a puzzle, even if only by guessing, they will arrive at the result about ten times faster than a single person.

This is only half the truth, because if the puzzle is also ten times as complex, the time required is again about the same.

Of course, a person can be lucky and guess the right number directly by chance. However, this evens out over time.

If we adjust the difficulty of the puzzle to the number of players, the time needed to solve the puzzle remains about the same.

This principle has also been adopted in the Bitcoin blockchain.

A block is appended to the blockchain when a miner finds the matching nonce. The frequency with which this happens depends on the difficulty of finding a matching nonce. To adjust the difficulty, we need to adjust the conditions on the nonce or the resulting hash.

If we specify that the resulting hash must not only start with a zero, but with two zeros, the solving becomes much more difficult.

![](Image00004.jpg)By increasing the number of initial zeros of the resulting hash, finding the correct nonce becomes more complicated. Solving a block takes longer on average.

This has another side effect: since the puzzle has automatically become more difficult, the resulting hash also contains more computing power or energy that had to be expended to solve it. This in turn means that an attacker also needs more computing power. The network became more secure by making the puzzle more difficult!

![](Image00004.jpg)The more participants connect to the network, the more computing power is in the network and the more difficult it becomes to find the matching nonce. Thus, the entire network becomes more and more secure with each additional participant.

The complexity of finding a matching number is also called the mining difficulty. The mining difficulty is adjusted every 2,016 blocks. This corresponds to a period of about two weeks. If the miners needed more than two weeks to solve the last 2,016 blocks, the mining difficulty was too high and is therefore reduced. If, on the other hand, too much computing power was available in the network and solving the blocks took less than two weeks, the mining difficulty is increased and solving the puzzle becomes more difficult. For the network, it is unimportant how much absolute computing power is available. It automatically adjusts every fortnight so that the blocks take an average of ten minutes to solve.

4.2.         Economic aspects of mining

For each block found, bitcoins are paid out to the miner who guessed the right nonce. At today's (2021) 6.25 BTC, this adds up to several thousand euros. This lucrative incentive attracts people who want to earn money with mining. There is nothing wrong with this, after all, they support the network, expand decentralisation and make it more difficult to "hack" the blockchain.

Since the mining difficulty adjusts to the total hashing power, or the computing power of the network, the mining difficulty increases with increasing popularity and higher bitcoin price. After all, it is more lucrative to mine Bitcoins when the price is not €100 but €100,000. This is because the amount of bitcoin distributed is already prescribed by the source code and is completely independent of the price of a coin.

4.3.         The 51 % attack

A 51% attack or a 51% attack means that a group of miners can take complete control of a network.

If a group owns 51% or more of the total hashing power of a network, it can freely decide the rules of the emerging blockchain. It can confirm or reject transactions and even double-spend.

The reason for this is that 51% is the largest hashing power of the network in absolute terms. This allows the miners to find the required nonces and solve blocks the fastest. Consequently, the chain of blocks of the "attackers" is longer than all others and is taken as consensus by all nodes.

 

In reality, this kind of attack is completely unfounded on the Bitcoin blockchain and many other major cryptocurrencies. It is almost impossible to amass over 50% of the total hashing power. The network is so widespread that no group of people could centre enough resources and energy to get their hands on over 50% of the computing power. However, smaller cryptocurrencies that are not as widespread have seen this kind of attack very often.

For this, we again take the largest cryptocurrency: Bitcoin. Bitcoin uses the SHA-256 algorithm. Another cryptocurrency called Bitcoin Cash uses the same algorithm but is only about 1% the size of Bitcoin.

One percent of the Bitcoin hashing power is therefore equal to 100% of the Bitcoin Cash hashing power. If a group that has only 1% of the hashing power of the entire Bitcoin network decides to attack the Bitcoin Cash Blockchain, it already has 50% of the hashing power and could therefore almost drive a 51% attack.

This has happened frequently in the past and happens again and again with smaller coins. Although it is not possible to change the blockchain retroactively, it is possible for the community to no longer recognise the blockchain that has been created. If such a 51% attack were to occur, the community has a very simple way to get rid of the attackers. We are talking about a fork.

4.4.         Forking a Blockchain

![](Image00046.jpg)Forking describes the process of splitting a blockchain into two (or more) blockchains. ![](Image00048.jpg)

Figure 32 Forking of a blockchain

The Bitcoin blockchain has already been forked several times, for example in Bitcoin SV, Bitcoin Cash or Bitcoin Gold.

![](Image00049.jpg)      ![Bitcoin Gold (BTG) Logo Vector](Image00050.jpg)    ![File:Bsv-icon-small.png](Image00051.jpg)

Figure 33 From left to right: Bitcoin Cash BCH , Bitcoin Gold BTG , Bitcoin SV BSV

This is why the Bitcoin Blockchain is often referred to as Bitcoin Core or Bitcoin Main , to clearly distinguish it from other forged Bitcoin Blockchains.

A fork is when a part of the community decides to change the underlying protocol (the source code) from a certain block of the blockchain onwards.

Actually, the source code is considered "sacred" and should never be changed. Nevertheless, a fork can be useful in certain situations. Let's illustrate forking again with the help of an analogy.

Let's say the blockchain is a dirt road we can drive along. The miners are the road builders who work with excavators and jackhammers instead of computing power. Piece by piece, they build a long road, which we call Bitcoin core, and on which we can move.

Suddenly a group of people come along and see weaknesses in the construction of the road. It is not wide enough, not enough cars can pass through, the construction is not stable and so on. They see the need to continue building the road in a different way and to change the building rules. However, not everyone agrees with this. For the other group, the road is perfectly adequate, or even sees it as better.

As a result, the road splits and a new parallel road called Bitcoin Cash is created. Both roads continue to be built in parallel.

A miner/road builder also has to decide which blockchain he will make his computing power available to. After all, he cannot build the Bitcoin Cash Road and the Bitcoin Core Road at the same time.

![](Image00052.jpg)

Figure 34 Bitcoin Cash Fork

When the blockchain splits into two separate chains, this process is also called a hard fork . The streets are not compatible with each other. At one point, however, the comparison is flawed: as users, we can continue to use both blockchains. After the Bitcoin Cash Blockchain was split off from the Bitcoin Blockchain on 1 August 2017, the miners had to decide, but the users did not.

It gets even better: If we own coins of the original currency, in this case Bitcoin, at the time of a fork, then we automatically own coins of the new blockchain.

This means: If we had 1 BTC on our Bitcoin account on 1 August 2017, we would still have 1 BTC on our account after the fork, but additionally 1 BCH (Bitcoin Cash) on our new BCH account. This account has the same address, and we can access our coins through the same private key, after all it has only been a copy of the previous Bitcoin Blockchain. This may sound a bit confusing, but we need to remember first and foremost that we do not own our coins, only our private key. In a fork, all addresses, i.e. all public keys and all private keys, are copied 1:1 to the new blockchain.

Why isn't Bitcoin forked more often? After all, everyone who owns Bitcoin also gets coins of the new currency for free every time, right?

In principle, this is correct, but first there must be a demand for the new currency. In addition, enough people must be willing to make their computing power available to mine the new coin. In reality, 99% of all forks of Bitcoin have disappeared into insignificance so far.

To name a few examples: Bitcoin Gold, Bitcoin Platinum, BIG Bitcoin, Bitcore, super Bitcoin, etc.

Another example of a "hacker attack" that forced a hard fork has taken place on the cryptocurrency Ethereum.

Ethereum is the second largest cryptocurrency after Bitcoin. The currency is denominated in Ether (ETH).

Just like Bitcoin, Ethereum uses Proof of Work to build consensus.

There was an "inaccuracy" in the Ethereum blockchain that a hacker was able to exploit in 2016. He managed to steal 3.5 million ETH (equivalent to 60 million euros at the time) from part of the community. Great chaos broke out - there had never been anything like it before. After the initial fog had settled, the community was faced with the question: Should we initiate a hard fork and "ban" the hacker from the network?

The idea behind this is that it would also undo the hack and the hacker would no longer have control over the coins. However, there was also a group of people who felt that it did not make sense, as such an action shows that the code can be changed too easily. According to the principle, "the source code is law," they held on to the blockchain with the hacked coins.

![](Image00054.jpg)

Figure 36 Should the blockchain be forked after the hacker attack?

The groups could not agree and, ironically, even the hacker himself got involved in the discussion. He insisted that he had done nothing illegal, as he had merely exploited a vulnerability in the Ethereum blockchain. He had even consulted his lawyers.

In the end, however, a hard fork occurred anyway. The blockchain that did not "undo" the hack was named Ethereum Classic . Ethereum, which is present today and at the same time the second largest cryptocurrency, reversed the hack.

The miners also had to decide: Do they make their computing power available to the Ethereum or the Ethereum Classic network? To date, the majority have opted for the Ethereum network.

On the Ethereum Blockchain, the hacker was not able to steal any coins because his hack was neutralised and the vulnerability was fixed. On the Ethereum Classic Blockchain, on the other hand, he kept the "stolen" coins.

The moral of the story: new technologies are always associated with risks. This story also shows that even a hack can be overcome by the decentralised community. Many people claim that this trial has even strengthened Ethereum and shown that no hacker stands a chance against the blockchain and the community - even if a mistake is discovered.

Ethereum Classic, on the other hand, has become increasingly unpopular over the years. When Ethereum is mentioned, this explicitly refers to Ethereum and not Ethereum Classic.

So far, we have seen the most radical type of fork. Overall, there are several ways to fork a blockchain, such as a source for k .

In a source fork, the existing source code of a cryptocurrency is copied and changed in some places. The blockchain is then started from scratch. Since there are no matching blocks, a source fork is not considered a real fork of a specific blockchain. We also do not receive any coins if, for example, we already own Bitcoins before the fork.

An example of a source fork of Bitcoin is the cryptocurrency Litecoin .

![](Image00056.jpg)

Figure 38 Litecoin logo

Litecoin (abbreviation LTC) is often referred to as Bitcoin's little brother and is technically almost 1:1 identical to the Bitcoin system. Only the name and some parameters have been changed. For example, the targeted block time was reduced from ten minutes to 2.5 minutes. This means that four times as many transactions can take place. At the same time, four times as many Litecoins are distributed, so that not 21 million but 84 million LTC form the upper limit of all Litcoins that ever occur. Litecoin has also recognised the problem that the SHA256 algorithm can be calculated very efficiently using ASICs. An ASIC is as powerful as hundreds of computers. This makes it easier to centre the computing power of the network. Significantly fewer resources and energies are needed.

That's why Litecoin chose a different algorithm that was created specifically for "commodity hardware" to prevent centralisation of computing power. Litecoin's scrypt algorithm cannot be calculated more efficiently by ASICs. Since the network as a whole is much smaller, an attack here is nevertheless much easier than with Bitcoin.

The last fork method to be mentioned is the soft fork. As the name suggests, this is not a hard fork but a soft transition. The fork is more of an upgrade of the previous blockchain than a split. The advantage is that a soft fork is often backwards compatible. Analogous to updating a programme on your computer or smartphone, you can still use the old or the new version.

This is because energy generation from solar and wind power is cheaper than electricity from coal or oil. Bitcoin does not stand in the way of green energy but promotes it.

Bitcoin supporters also state that bitcoin stands for a free financial system . It stands for a fundamental right of the people .

Global telecommunications and consumer electronics, such as Netflix, video games and the like, consume many times more resources but make a much smaller contribution to a free society.

However, there is also a technical solution that requires less electricity. For this, however, we will need to make other compromises. We have already learned about proof of work mining. Another way to create consensus is the so-called Proof of Stake procedure.

4.6.         Proof of Stake

Proof of Stake , POS can best be translated as proof of entitlement/possession.

In contrast to POW, the security of a network is not guaranteed by providing computing power, but by requiring participants who validate a block to deposit credit in the form of coins.

![](Image00004.jpg)We can think of it as if everyone involved in solving a block were to deposit their credit as insurance. If someone has a bad intention, wants to falsify a block and is caught, he loses his credit. Therefore, no one has an incentive to step out of line.

Everyone who has money is given the right to vote . Then, through a democratic vote, the blocks are verified. The more assets a person owns, the more voting rights he or she has. The rewards for solving the blocks (the transaction fees and block subsidies) are in turn paid proportionately to everyone who participated in the consensus-building process.

This procedure does not require energy-hungry calculating machines. In addition, we can see at any time which account has how many voting rights. The proof-of-stake algorithm is also advantageous for the holders of the coins. They receive a return for providing their coins in the form of the distributed coins. They do not need expensive calculators or cheap electricity prices.

Even if the proof of stake procedure only offers advantages at first glance, there are some serious problems associated with it. For one thing, it is a capitalist system in which money or wealth automatically brings with it a certain claim to power.

Moreover, those who already have the most wealth, even in absolute terms, receive the most rewards for solving a block. Gradually, monopolies form and gain more and more influence. We already see in our current world that the money supply becomes more and more centralised over time. Over 50% of the world's money supply is held by just 1% of the people. Applied to the proof-of-stake approach, this means that if this 1% of people got together, they would have enough voting power to change the rules of the blockchain. Of course, this is again a very hypothetical assumption, and there would be other mechanisms to prevent such a conglomeration as well. For example, it is the case that a fundamental system decision within the network requires 90% approval instead of 51%. A system decision would be, for example, the question of how the stalking rewards, i.e. the "dividend", should be distributed.

It should be noted that all blockchains based on POS show surprisingly few problems with monopolies.

With the proof-of-work concept, on the other hand, such approaches are not possible in principle.

Another technical disadvantage that has not yet been fully resolved is the possibility of forking the blockchain. Since in a fork the coins remain on both blockchains, the owners of the coins do not have to decide on which blockchain they "mine” but can continue their power on both blockchains.

| --- | --- | --- |

| Behaviour during a hardfork || The coins are present on both blockchains || The coins are present on both blockchains |
| Consensus building || Mining || Validators (asset owners) |

As we can now see, one is not automatically better than the other or vice versa.

Some cryptocurrencies rely on POW, others on POS. Only time will tell what will prevail in the long term for a particular field of application.

5.  Transparency and anonymity

If we want to find out how transparent or anonymous a blockchain is, we first have to clearly separate the terms. This is because many terms such as transparency and anonymity are often thrown around or used incorrectly in everyday language.

5.1.         Transparency of the blockchain

Transparency describes the property that a third person can easily recognise and understand an action within a system. In terms of money flows, this means that a person can see the flow of money from one account to another, even if it is not their own. And this is exactly the case with Bitcoin and most cryptocurrencies. The blockchain is the transaction history that contains all former transactions. It is freely viewable and accessible to everyone.

![](Image00004.jpg)The blockchain is 100 % transparent. Any person can see all account balances and money flows that have ever taken place at any time. We can also easily look at Satoshi Nakamoto's account, provided we know which account belongs to him.

Compared to blockchain technology, today's banking systems are not transparent. We cannot see any other money flows except our own. Even though the blockchain is transparent, it is also completely anonymous. We need to clearly separate the transparency of a system from anonymity.

5.2.         Anonymity of the blockchain

The word anonymity is derived from the Greek. It can be translated as "without a name" or "no name".

One can have full anonymity within the blockchain. We have already seen in the first chapters of this book that all we need to do to "open" a Bitcoin address (public address) is to think up a private key with a length of 256 characters. No one can stop us from doing this. Moreover, no one can deduce the name, age, origin or gender of the person from the public key. Bitcoin and blockchain technology are therefore 100% anonymous and at the same time 100% transparent.

Even if both are true in theory, in reality, anonymity is not completely given. This effect is also called pseudo-anonymity.

5.3.         Pseudo-anonymity

Pseudo-anonymity describes that the anonymity in an actually 100% anonymous system is automatically dissolved by the high transparency.

![](Image00004.jpg)As soon as we can clearly assign a Bitcoin address to a small group of people, we can infer the owners of other addresses based on the transactions of this address.

If we send a certain amount of money to a person every month, on the 1st of the month, the associated account probably belongs to a close relative, our landlord or a bank from which we have borrowed. Since the points of entry and exit are not anonymous, the anonymity gradually dissolves.

![](Image00004.jpg)This is not only a theoretical consideration of anonymity, but also common practice. According to current estimates, 80-90% of all Bitcoin addresses can be clearly assigned to their real owners.

One reason why this is possible is because the entry and exit points are monitored. Often, when buying Bitcoins or other cryptocurrencies, you have to confirm your personal details so that the address can be clearly assigned before a transaction has even taken place.

De facto, we can state that there is little to no privacy in the Bitcoin network. We will take a closer look at the effects this has on the everyday usability of cryptocurrencies later. First, we look at cryptocurrencies that are nevertheless completely anonymous through various tricks.

5.4.         Private Coins

So far, we have only got to know the blockchain technology, which allows all inferences to be made about transactions. However, there are also cryptocurrencies where a block is not completely transparent.

![](Image00004.jpg)These cryptocurrencies are called private coins or privacy coins . These blockchains do not allow any precise conclusions to be drawn about who has received how much money from whom.

One of the first cryptocurrencies to emerge as a private coin is Monero (XMR).

![](Image00058.jpg)

Figure 40 Monero logo

In the blockchains we have looked at so far, each participant signs their own transaction. This makes it easy to see who has sent money to whom.

![](Image00059.jpg)

Figure 41 Digital signature of a message

From a technical point of view, private coins increase privacy by having several people sign not only their own transaction, but also other transactions. This is then referred to as a ring signature. ![](Image00060.jpg)

Figure 42 Digital ring signature

The participants sign a block or their transactions in the block together.

All participants in the ring signature can confirm that the transactions are permissible, but it is no longer concretely visible from the outside from where and to where the money has flowed.

For a more tangible comparison, imagine a group of people pooling money. For example, Alice wants to transfer €200, Bob wants to transfer €100 and Charlie wants to transfer €50 to other people. They meet and confirm that the other has the necessary money. Alice confirms (signs) that Bob's transaction is OK, Bob confirms Charlie's and Charlie confirms Alice's transaction intention.

They then deposit €350 in the bank together and send the money wherever they want. The bank does not see how much Alice, Bob or Charlie have deposited individually. All the bank sees is a transaction with the total amount.

It is no longer clear whether ring signatures like Monero's are still sufficiently anonymous. However, the Monero community is already working on the implementation of another algorithm that should further increase the anonymity of users.

There are other cryptocurrencies that also guarantee complete anonymity. However, these are currently rather niche products.

Other cryptocurrencies such as Ethereum, Bitcoin Cash or Monero have also been mentioned. Now let's take a closer look at some of these alternative cryptocurrencies. 6.1.         Stablecoins

In the early days of the crypto market, there were still some problems. The crypto exchange Bitfinex , for example, recognised the problem that every purchase and sale of cryptocurrencies had to go through a bank.

![](Image00065.jpg)

Figure 45 Bitfinex logo

If we want to buy Bitcoins, we have to deposit money into a bank account. In return, we receive Bitcoins. With blockchain technology, we already have the perfect method with which we can circumvent exactly that.

To remove this middleman from the buying and selling process, the company Bitfinex has created a cryptocurrency that replicates the value of one US dollar.

For this, one billion US dollars were deposited in a bank account and one billion so-called tethers (USDT) were offered for purchase.

![](Image00066.jpg)

Figure 46 Tether logo

Bitfinex is an exchange for cryptocurrencies. The exchange has a great financial incentive to offer a "digital US dollar" for trade. Every time USDT is exchanged for Bitcoin or other cryptocurrencies, the exchange now earns and not the bank.

Instead of selling your Bitcoins for "real" money, you can exchange them for USDT. The company behind Tether guarantees that we will also get paid one US dollar for the Tether coin if needed. Actually, the price of the currency would fluctuate, just like all other cryptocurrencies. Tether actively counteracts this.

If the demand for coins is too low, Tether compensates for the price fluctuations by buying the coin. If, on the other hand, the demand for coins is too high, Tether counteracts this by issuing new coins so that the nominal value of 1 USD can be maintained.

![](Image00067.jpg)

![](Image00004.jpg)Because these types of coins, like Tether, have a stable value, they are called stablecoins .

Stablecoins are an important component of the cryptosystem. They form a kind of bridge from gold or fiat currencies, like the euro, the dollar, the yen or the Swiss franc, into the crypto world. Stablecoins are coins that are linked to a comparative value and backed by a real equivalent value.

Another aspect regarding the issuance of the Tether Coin was that these cryptocurrencies were not considered currency by financial authorities, and, as a result, many regulatory hurdles for the Bitfinex exchange were removed.

As in the paper money system, it is important for stablecoins that the currency is backed. Covered means that for each Tether coin, a corresponding US dollar is also frozen in a bank account. This is the only way to ensure the value of Tether. Let's imagine that there are a billion Tether-Coins, but only one million dollars are deposited in a bank account. If all the holders wanted to exchange their Tether Coin for fiat money at the same time, it wouldn't work. In our banking system, this is commonplace. The banks usually own less than 5% of the money they lend. Now, if people want to physically withdraw their money, as in the Greek crisis, it is simply not possible because the bank does not have enough funds.

Analogous to the printing of money by the central banks, a multiplication of tethers without a real countervalue being deposited would be a devaluation of the existing money.

It is precisely regarding this aspect that there has been criticism of Tether for a long time. The company behind Tether cannot prove beyond doubt that it really owns the money. However, this is essential for the stabilisation of the USDT.

Bitfinex is currently facing lawsuits in relation to Tether. The New York Attorney General's Office accuses Bitfinex of creating Tether out of thin air and manipulating the Bitcoin price through its enormous market power by buying or selling Bitcoins with Tether.

As soon as a central institution has a monopoly over the money supply, the creation and abuse of money is tempting.

For this reason, other exchanges have removed USDT and issued their own stablecoins.

6.2.         Ethereum

Ethereum has been the second largest cryptocurrency for years. We have already touched on Ethereum in the chapter on mining and hard forks. In the following, we want to explore the idea behind Ethereum, because unlike Bitcoin, Ethereum is no ordinary cryptocurrency.

Ethereum was first introduced in 2013 and implemented in 2015. Unlike Bitcoin, the founders are known and in constant exchange with the community. The developers Vitalik Buterin and Gavin Wood are the face of the Ethereum project. Vitalik Buterin in particular is often in the spotlight and is considered the figurehead of Ethereum. He developed Ethereum at the age of only 19, has an IQ of 257 and is the most famous person in the crypto field.

Meanwhile, the Ethereum project is also being further developed by the Ethereum Foundation, which is based in Switzerland.

A foundation is a community of people who, unlike a company, do not (have to) make a profit, but dedicate themselves to a project, in this case the development of the Ethereum network, free of charge. Of course, the developers profit indirectly from their work, because all developers also own Ethereum coins themselves and therefore profit from rising prices and the increase in Ethereum's level of awareness.

The developers are constantly working on improvements. Improvement proposals, so-called EIPs (Ethereum improvement proposals), are regularly presented and implemented after consultation with the community.

![](Image00004.jpg)Ethereum is denominated in Ether (ETH) . Just as a Bitcoin can be divided into mBTC or Satoshi, an Ether can be divided into smaller units, the Wei. One billion Wei forms a Giga-Wei Gwei and one billion Gwei forms an ETH.

![](Image00068.jpg)

Ethereum also uses the proof-of-work algorithm . This means that the transactions are confirmed by decentralised miners and computing power must be used for this. However, the block duration for Ethereum is only about 13 seconds. As a reminder, it takes ten minutes for Bitcoin. Furthermore, Ethereum does not use the SHA256 algorithm to secure the blockchain, but the ETHash algorithm . This can be efficiently calculated by graphics processors. The idea behind this was that graphics cards usually have more power than a processor and that therefore more computing power and more security is available to the network. In addition, the centralisation of computing power by ASICs should be counteracted.

![](Image00004.jpg)Ethereum, unlike Bitcoin, is unlimited. With every block, two new ethers are distributed to the miners. The number is not reduced in the process. Ethereum is therefore an inflationary system.

If we want to send an Ether transaction, we have to pay the fee in gas . The price for the gas is given in Gwei. The terminology is often a bit confusing for newcomers. Gwei, just like Ether (ETH), is Ethereum's currency.

We can think of the gas as fuel for our transaction. The faster we want the transaction confirmed, the more fuel we need and the more gas we have to buy Gwei.

If the transaction becomes larger or more complex, we need more gas accordingly. Usually 21,000-40,000 gas is sufficient for a transaction. We can often manually set the maximum amount of gas we want to use.

![](Image00004.jpg)Ethereum, like Bitcoin, is a highest bid system. The transaction for which the most fees (gas) are paid is charged first. Only as much gas as is necessary is ever used. The rest is paid back to our account.

![](Image00069.jpg)

Figure 47 Example fee calculation of an Ethereum transaction

Example: We want to send an ETH. The gas price for a quick transaction is 134 Gwei at the time of writing. We set the maximum gas consumption to 21,000 gas. This results in a maximum transaction fee of ![](Image00070.jpg). To arrive at the amount in Ether and dollars, we need to convert Gwei to ETH. To do this, we need to divide by 1 billion. ![](Image00071.jpg).

At an ETH rate of 1,900 USD, this results in a transaction fee of about 5.27 USD, i.e. about 4.50 €.

If we buy a car for €5,000, transaction costs of €4.50 are tolerable, but to pay for a coffee, these costs are completely unsuitable. One solution is Ethereum 2.0, which is a conversion of the current protocol that we will also look at.

Ethereum and Bitcoin have a very similar protocol, but the two cryptocurrencies differ in one respect:

![](Image00004.jpg)In contrast to Bitcoin, Ethereum is not limited to the mere sending and receiving of coins but positions itself as a decentralised super-computer on which decentralised apps ( DApps ) can run. The basis is again a decentralised blockchain.

Each full node not only stores the blockchain, but also runs the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM). All nodes together form a network of computers - a virtual, decentralised supercomputer.

![](Image00072.jpg)

Figure 48 The Ethereum network consists of a blockchain and the virtual machine

We can think of this supercomputer as a giant operating system on which independent developers can install programmes that anyone can then use. The programmes will run on any computer that runs a full node.

For example, investor funds can be collected, purchase contracts concluded or even games played via the Ethereum platform. The focus is mostly on financial applications , although there is no limit to the scope of application.

The DApps are realised through so-called smart contracts.

6.3.         Smart contracts and ERC20 tokens

Bitcoin was designed as a means of payment and a store of value. Ethereum, on the other hand, has another function, namely the aforementioned function as a supercomputer. So-called smart contracts can be executed on this decentralised supercomputer.

A smart contract can be compared to an ordinary contract. There are two contracting parties who agree to exchange goods or services.

![](Image00004.jpg)A smart contract is a contract that is stored on the Ethereum blockchain or executed on the virtual computer of the Ethereum network.

![](Image00073.jpg)

Figure 49 Smart contract on the blockchain

The smart contract is automatically triggered when a predefined condition occurs.

Since the idea of smart contracts is very abstract, we take a concrete example to help.

Alice wants to sell her house to Bob. They have negotiated a purchase price of 1000 ETH. Normally, the two would need an intermediary, be it a bank or a notary, whom they both trust. This is cumbersome, time-consuming and costly. Instead, they set up a smart contract that automatically forwards the deed of the house to Bob as soon as the previously determined sum of 1000 ETH has been received in Alice's account.

![](Image00074.jpg)

Figure 50 Setting up a smart contract

As soon as Bob has sent the 1000 ETH to the smart contract, he receives the deed. In return, Alice automatically gets the money paid out to her Ethereum address. For this, the two have paid a few cents to euros in transaction fees. In a central system, hundreds of euros would have been due for a middleman. The time savings are not even considered here.

![](Image00004.jpg)No middleman is needed any more and the execution of the smart contract only costs small transaction fees on the blockchain.

Other examples where smart contracts can be used are elections. These can be transparent, but at the same time anonymous. Election fraud would in fact not be possible.

![](Image00004.jpg)If an event is to be executed as soon as a condition is fulfilled, middlemen can be bypassed with the help of a smart contract. For this, the condition and the event must be represented digitally.

A smart contract replaces trust in an intermediary with ![Picture 4](Image00003.jpg)trust in programming. Conversely, this also means that problems can arise if the smart contract has not been written correctly.

Because once a smart contract has been written, it is unchangeable.

In addition, the smart contract is openly visible (open source) and executes itself automatically as soon as the condition has occurred.

Smart contracts are executed on the Ethereum blockchain and are therefore decentralised and at the same time relatively cheap, depending on the network load.

A decentralised network is slower than a classic centralised network because of the many participants. Due to increasingly faster internet connections, the difference is usually negligible. Real-time applications, on the other hand, can only be implemented to a limited extent with a decentralised system.

Simple contracts are not the limit of smart contracts. Smart contracts can be used in many ways. For example, they can be used to create tokens .

A token is comparable to a coin of a cryptocurrency, but tokens can also represent a share, deeds, certificates and much more. A token is not just a currency, but a general, virtual object.

The purpose of a token is to represent an object from the real world in the digital world. A "VW Golf token" or a "Siemens share token" are conceivable, each reflecting the real-world object.

To receive the tokens, we first set up a smart contract. Then, we have to send Ether to the smart contract. In return, we receive a certain number of tokens.

![](Image00075.jpg)

Figure 51 Token issuance through a smart contract

The smart contract is not only responsible for issuing the tokens, but also for using the tokens to another address or monitoring the quantity of tokens.

It is important that we understand the difference between a coin and a token.

A coin like Ether or Bitcoin is the currency of the underlying cryptocurrency. A token, on the other hand, only exists within a smart ![Picture 4](Image00003.jpg)contract on the Ethereum blockchain (or other blockchains that support smart contracts). A token does not have its own blockchain or address. We can access the token by using the corresponding smart contract on the Ethereum blockchain.

Tokens are not usually mined but rather issued by the smart contract. Tokens are often created with a specific application goal. We will get to know some more concrete examples in the coming chapters.

Any programmer with prior knowledge can set up an individual smart contract and use it to create tokens. Over time, many different contracts and tokens were set up and issued. Many of them had little or no use.

![](Image00004.jpg)It quickly became apparent that there must be a standard for tokens that serves as a guideline for the creation of a token. This is how the ERC20 standard (Ethereum Request for Comments) came into being. Tokens created according to this standard are called ERC20 tokens .

The ERC20 standard can be compared to a basic framework for a programmer. It specifies that every ERC20 token must have the same properties and functions at the lowest level. This includes, for example, that the token has a name and an abbreviation (symbol) and that a maximum number of tokens must be defined. If the maximum number is exceeded, no new tokens are issued. There must also be a possibility in the smart contract that makes it possible to send the token from one address to another.

This requirement makes it easier to offer standardised interfaces for these tokens so that they can be traded or sent more easily.

![](Image00004.jpg)The creation of ERC20 tokens is standardised and very easy to realise. There are now over 100,000 different ERC20 tokens on the market.

However, this makes it very difficult to see how much value a new token project offers. Almost anyone, even without programming skills, can create an ERC20 token with an idea and a little internet research.

This was also the case in the crypto hype of 2017-2018, when numerous new tokens were created. Many of these tokens were not based on any real concept or use case. Instead, with the help of branding ![Picture 4](Image00003.jpg)and many promises, investors (often private individuals) were convinced to invest in these tokens. A total volume of over 4 billion euros flowed into these ICOs (Initial Coin Offerings) projects.

The ICO bubble burst in early 2018, with many projects losing over 90% of their peak to date.

Often the promised project was never realised, and the founders merely enriched themselves.

Of course, not all ERC20 projects are automatically bad, but it is important to inform yourself sufficiently about whether the promises made can actually be kept.

Besides Ethereum, there are numerous other cryptocurrencies that can be used as a blockchain for the creation of smart contracts and tokens, for example the cryptocurrency NEO. NEO is also referred to as Chinese Ethereum. It follows the same approach as Ethereum. NEO wants to transfer all data to the blockchain and represent it through tokens. There is now talk of tokenising   the world . We will take a closer look at other cryptocurrencies that follow the same approach in the course of the Ethereum alternatives. However, Ethereum has by far the largest number of active users. Therefore, the Ethereum Blockchain is by far the most used for the creation of tokens and applications based on smart contracts.

Having looked at tokens and smart contracts, we now turn to the question of why we need smart contracts and tokens in the first place. 

The smart contracts and tokens based on the Ethereum blockchain form the basis for decentralised applications .

6.4.         DApps

A smart contract, as we have come to know it so far, can mostly execute a simple function. It is an instruction according to the principle: "If X happens, then execute Y."

However, a smart contract can become much more complex. As already mentioned, programming skills are required to write a smart contract yourself. The smart contracts based on the Ethereum blockchain are programmed in a specially developed programming language called Solidity . In the meantime, there are also interfaces to more common programming languages, so that significantly more programmers can participate in the Ethereum system. Even without prior knowledge of the Solidity programming language, it has become relatively simple for a programmer to set up a smart contract.

However, a smart contract is only a relatively simple construct. However, we can build more complex applications from these simple building blocks. This works by the smart contracts triggering each other and is comparable to a domino chain reaction.

![](Image00076.jpg)

Figure 52 Multiple smart contracts interlock

All this takes place on the decentralised computer and is stored on the blockchain.

![](Image00004.jpg)Since the application consisting of the individual smart contracts is executed de-centrally on the blockchain, we speak of decentralised applications, or Dapps for short. A Dapp can be anything, for example an online game, a social media website or a huge database.

Everything we can bring to the digital world can be realised through smart contracts and Dapps.

An example of a decentralised game that runs completely autonomously on the Ethereum Virtual Machine are the crypto kitties .

On the website [https://www.cryptokitties.co/](https://www.cryptokitties.co/) we can collect virtual cats, exchange them and breed them. It is a classic collecting game, comparable to digital Pokémon cards.

![](Image00077.jpg)

Figure 53 Crypto Kitties start page

To participate in the game, we have to send Ether to our cat account and can then buy one or more cats.

The cats we can collect can also mate with each other. This, in turn, gives rise to new cats that look similar to their parents, and yet each cat is unique.

We can doubt the meaningfulness of this game, but for the Ethereum network the crypto kitties were the first real, decentralised application.

But what advantage do we now have from the fact that the game runs de-centrally on the blockchain and not, like every other game, on a central server of the game developers?

Let's consider at a story from the childhood of the inventor of Ethereum, Vitalik Buterin.

Vitalik used to play the game World of Warcraft (WoW). World of Warcraft is an open-world game where you can meet other players in a virtual world, complete tasks and collect items. It was Vitalik's absolute favourite game.

But one day, the game developer Blizzard removed one of his favourite items. That day, Vitalik says he cried himself to sleep and realised how unfair centralisation can be.

"I played World of Warcraft in 2007-2010 and was happy with the game, but one day Blizzard removed the damage component from my beloved Warlock's life drain spell. I cried myself to sleep and realised that day what horrors centralised services can bring. " - Vitalik Buterin

Even though losing a virtual ability in WoW doesn't seem that tragic, the crypto kitties show how to do it better. Just like our cryptocurrencies, the Kitties are 100% our own. There is no institution that can take them away from us, exclude us from trading or close the account. The community decides on the rules, improvements, etc.

Strictly speaking, even a DApp is not 100% decentralised. This is because ![Picture 4](Image00003.jpg)in order to run the application on our computer, we need programmes that enable the content to be displayed. This is similar to how we need an internet browser to access the internet. These programmes run on central servers.

The advantage is that we can also access the decentralised network from any other server at any time. If the PC or the browser is defective, we can access the internet via another computer.

The crypto kitties have made something else clear: every purchase, exchange and reproduction of the crypto kitties is realised through a smart contract. This maps transactions on the Ethereum blockchain.

This means that every time two cats mate or we swap the virtual cats, a transaction is triggered on the Ethereum blockchain. This costs the player money and increases the network load.

At its peak in 2017, the Ethereum network was almost at full capacity because of the crypto kitties. Over 13% of all transactions were due to the collection game alone. This also shows that the network still has to overcome scaling difficulties.

In the future, DApps, just like the entire Ethereum network, will continue to develop. DApps will become much more important.

However, games are not the only area of application for DApps. DAOs are another possible application.

6.5.         DAOs

We have already seen an example of an application on the Ethereum blockchain. With crypto kitties, the focus was on the fun and collection factor. With DAOs, the focus is on economic enterprises that can be mapped on the blockchain.

A Decentralised Autonomous Organizations DAO is an organisation that has no board of directors or decision-making staff. Instead, all rules are written down through smart contracts.

![](Image00004.jpg)A DAO runs completely autonomously on the Ethereum blockchain. Every process within the company is stored in the Ethereum blockchain as a transaction.

The procedure for setting up a DAO is always the same:

First, the company is "founded." In the process, the rules are written in smart contracts. Anyone can see these.

Afterwards, a crowdfunding is launched where future tokens can be secured. This is important so that the company builds up start-up capital.

By sending money to the company, for example in the form of Ether, we can become part of the company. In return, we receive a token. This is comparable to buying a share in the company. If you choose a good company, you can increase your money as a "shareholder". However, it can also happen that the company cannot arouse interest for potential investors, and you thus lose your invested money completely.

![](Image00004.jpg)As soon as the DAO has been funded, it will be launched. From now on, it runs completely autonomously. All decisions are made by the community, i.e., the holders of the DAO tokens. The community can suggest what the capital is used for.

In decision-making processes within the company, the tokens are voted on democratically.

For example, the smart contracts may stipulate that a change is only adopted if at least 60% of the shareholders—the owners of the tokens—vote in favour of it.

Every employee or investor can invest in the company and has the same voting rights as a percentage of their shares. There are no hierarchical structures like in a central company.

![](Image00004.jpg)Because the blockchain is decentralised, no central decision-making bodies are needed. Instead, the entire consensus-building process is decentralised on the Ethereum blockchain.

One example of such a DAO is Steemit .

![](Image00078.jpg)

Figure 54 Logo steemit.com

Founded in 2016, Steemit is a decentralised social media and blogging platform. It is based on the Steem blockchain.

We can access the decentralised forum at the URL https://steemit.com/. Users can create content, read it and exchange ideas with each other. In addition, users are rewarded for good content. Not a central company, but the users themselves decide what is considered good.

As is so often the case, the trust factor is completely taken out of the equation. The rules by which the company acts are laid down in the public smart contracts.

Another example of a DAO is an investment fund or company. Everyone who invests in the company and thus pays in capital receives voting rights in the form of a token. Then we can regularly vote on which shares, which real estate or other asset classes the company should invest in, when it should buy and when it should sell. In doing so, we can set all the rules ourselves or submit new proposals, which are then also voted on. For example, an automatic bonus to all investors is possible if an investment has done well. We also do not have to trust a central stock exchange and do not need a cost-intensive fund manager.

The fields of application of DAOs are manifold.

We must be aware that the company's launch rules (the smart contracts) were programmed by humans and may contain errors that an end user without programming knowledge cannot easily understand (not even experienced programmers or security companies can always do this beyond doubt).

This is shown by a very popular example: The DAO .

The DAO

One often reads about an implementation called The DAO . This was one of the first and largest DAO projects. The DAO project recognised the DAO hype and also named its cryptocurrency the DAO. It's like a company calling itself “artificial intelligence" just to automatically look more attractive to investors.

The DAO was developed in 2016 by the company Slock.it and executed on the Ethereum blockchain. It was a kind of investment company.

Here you could exchange your Ether for DAO tokens. You could then use your DAO tokens to take part in votes on how the decentralised, autonomous company should be managed.

The DAO was an open-source project . This is because anyone can read the smart contracts that serve as the foundation of a DAO. The DAO gained enormous popularity within a few months. However, the DAO smart contract had a security vulnerability:

It was possible to issue DAO tokens for Ether and reclaim them afterwards. An attacker discovered this vulnerability on 17 June 2016 and was able to capture 3.6 million Ether in a short time. At the time, the value was around 70 million euros.

The hacker could spend the tokens, reclaim the ETH and so on. Theoretically, this would be possible indefinitely, but he made do with 3.6 million ETH.

In response, the price of Th -DAO token fell by over 70% within a few hours.

![](Image00079.jpg)

Figure 55 Price development of "The DAO Coin

Fortunately, there was a security measure in place that prevented the hacker from leaving with the coins: Before the refunded coins could be spent, there was a hold period of 28 days. The community quickly noticed the incident and from then on had 28 days to decide how to deal with this problem.

In response to the incident, a discussion was sparked, and the attacker defended himself. Strictly speaking, it was not a hack, because the attacker did nothing illegal. He merely recognised a weakness in the smart contract and exploited it for his own benefit.

He stressed in an official statement that he is legally entitled to the coins, that he has behaved legally and that a hard or soft fork would immensely damage the reputation of Ethereum. One could no longer trust that the code cannot be changed arbitrarily. Any kind of hard or soft fork would be the end of Ethereum.

Whether that was really his opinion, or whether he simply wanted to save the value of the coins he had captured, is anyone's guess. In any case, the majority of the community did not listen to him and initiated a hardfork.

Doesn't this attack sound familiar? Haven't we already addressed a similar scenario? Exactly: In response to this attack, the hardfork was triggered, from which Ethereum Classic emerged. Now we also understand the background behind the attack, which has since become known as the DAO hack. We also understand that it was not a bug in the Ethereum blockchain, but a bug in a smart contract that only ran on the blockchain and was programmed by humans.

Ethereum founder Vitalik Buterin also saw it that way and initially stayed out of the entire discussion surrounding the upcoming fork.

Since over 15% of the total ETH was in the DAO network, the founder finally dealt with this issue and worked out the fork, in which the attacker's address is put on a kind of "blacklist" so that he cannot access the coins.

Even though it was probably the right decision, this example shows that the protocol is not set in stone and can be changed by the community.

Despite this unfortunate event, we can state that smart contracts bring with them unimagined possibilities. DApps, tokens or DAOs. Decentralised applications whose rules are visible to everyone will be of great relevance in the future.

6.7.         EIP1559 and Ethereum 2.0

Ethereum itself is now aware of this problem.

For this reason, EIP1559 will be implemented in July 2021. This improvement proposal, also known as the London Update, was proposed by Vitalik Buterin himself in 2018. In simple terms, EIP1559 means that transaction fees will no longer be distributed to miners as before but instead will be burned . The miners will only receive the block subsidy of two ETH per solved block . The transaction fee will be replaced by a standard transaction fee. Although this depends on the network load, it is significantly lower than the previous "highest bidder system" that is normally the case with Proof of Work. However, one can still send a small donation to the miners, the Miner Tip or also called Inclusion Fee .

A concrete example:

Before EIP1559:

Mining Reward = Block Subsidy + Transaction Fees

User costs for the transactions (transaction fee): 5 ETH

Mining Reward: 2 ETH + 5 ETH = 7 ETH

The distributed Ether creates an inflation of 2 ETH

After EIP1559:

Mining Reward = Block Subsidy + Miner Tip

\-> Base fees are burnt

User costs for the transactions (base fee): 2.5 ETH

Additionally 0.7 ETH Mining Tips will be donated

Mining Reward: 2 ETH + 0.7 ETH = 2.7 ETH

In addition, the base fee of 2.5 ETH is burned. This results in a slight deflation of 0.5 ETH (2.5 ETH base fee minus 2 ETH block subsidy).

Depending on the network load, the transaction fees per block can b higher than the two ethers distributed per block. This means that, for example, two Ether are distributed while three are burned at the same time. Thus, Ether could change from an inflationary to a deflationary system.

The majority of miners do not like this proposal at all. After all, according to current estimates, they receive up to 60 % less ether per solved block. Nevertheless, the proposal was implemented by the developers. Theoretically, the miners could initiate a hardfork, but this would very likely leave a mark on the stability and trust of the Ethereum network, so they would be harming themselves. Apart from that, it is a big step forward for the system on a technological level.

Another technical aspect of the proposed improvement is that the size of a block adjusts to the load on the network. This makes the network faster and cheaper for all users. This step is important for scalability, which is why most of the community is behind this proposal. They reason that miners, due to rising popularity and rising Ether price, can still mine profitably. Although they will only receive two Ether per block, these will be worth more in fiat currency terms. This is what many miners are hoping for. There are already other solutions to compensate the miners, but in the long run such an update is inevitable to be able to use Ethereum on a large scale.

In any case, it is also clear that Ethereum mining will end with Ethereum 2.0, which is expected to be launched in 2022 or 2023 at the latest. By that time, the proof-of-stake concept will have been fully implemented and the miners will have been replaced by validators.

Depending on when you read this, the 1559 proposal has already been implemented or Ethereum is already running on Proof of Stake. Perhaps the impact on scalability on the Ethereum price has already become visible.

The example of EIP1559 and Ethereum 2.0 shows that the technology is developing rapidly and sometimes fundamentally, and major changes are being introduced all the time.

7.2.         Store coins safely for the long term

The disadvantage of the exchange lies in its centralisation. This is an ordinary company whose servers can be hacked. We ourselves often do not have access to our private keys.

An example of a fatal hack is the history of the Mt. Gox exchange . By 2013, over 60% of the world's bitcoin exchanges were transacted through this marketplace. Through security gaps (or, possibly, planned insider trading), over 850,000 BTC could be stolen. This corresponded to a value of half a billion euros at the time. A year later, a wave of lawsuits began. The exchange went bankrupt in 2014. Some injured parties received a partial refund, but in fiat money at the then BTC price of 2013. Others are still waiting for their money today. Most of the victims would be millionaires today if they had not lost their Bitcoins.

This risk is still present today, but much lower than it was in 2013, for example. Just as with any other technology company, a hack can also occur with a stock exchange. Other companies like Apple, Microsoft and Co. have also been hacked several times. The companies have learned from the security breaches and, for example, divide the data and credit balances between different virtual wallets so that in the event of a hack, not everything can be captured at once, but only a small part.

7.2.1. Software Wallets

![](Image00004.jpg)A wallet is an app where our private keys are stored. Our coins are stored on the blockchain, and we don't keep the coins, but the proof that we are the owner of the coins in the form of our private key.

This type of wallet is called a software wallet .

When opening an account with a wallet provider, as with the exchanges, care was taken to ensure that it was designed to be user-friendly and that no prior knowledge or background knowledge of the blockchain was required. We will go into more detail about the different types of wallets later when it comes to storing the coins or private keys. But here, too, there is a potential risk of encountering a dubious source. In practice, this is unlikely, but there are also one hundred percent secure methods of creating an address.

Instead, it makes sense to send the coins to an address that we have created ourselves, whose private key only we know. No coin tossing is necessary for this. A random generator on the PC can be sufficient. There are many softwares and algorithms that create a 256-bit long random sequence of ones and zeros.

Important: The PC must be offline. We should never create our private key online. This random private key can easily be saved by the operator in order to steal the coins later. This private key should be written unaltered on a piece of paper and stored, for example in a safe or safe deposit box. This type of wallet is called a paper wallet.

A cold wallet is any type of wallet that is not connected to the internet and therefore has no contact with the outside world and is considered relatively secure. Private keys can also be stored using so-called seeds, which we will look at in more detail in the next sub-chapter.

7.3.         Seeds and Master Keys

The basis for Seeds is an update to the Bitcoin system, update number 32, called BIP32 (Bitcoin Improvement Proposal). A BIP is an improvement proposal issued by developers to the community. The proposed change is discussed, and if the proposal is deemed good, it is adopted into the Bitcoin protocol. In this way, further improvements are always possible.

As we already know, the Bitcoin network is very transparent, and anyone can see the balance of a Bitcoin address. If we don't want anyone to be able to see our entire assets, we have to create a new wallet. In other words, we must save two private keys.

![](Image00095.jpg)

Figure 66 Generation of two addresses by two private keys

If we want to open 100 addresses on the blockchain, we have to store 100 private keys. That is time-consuming. That’s where the proposed improvement comes in.

BIP32 proposed the introduction of Hierarchic Determinist Wallets (HD Wallets).

They solve the problem described and simplify the operation of wallets. However, it helps to increase privacy at the same time.

With HD wallets, a master key is introduced. This looks very similar to a private key and also consists of a cryptic sequence of numbers and letters.

Any number of private keys can then be generated from the master key.

 

![](Image00096.jpg)

Figure 67 Generating any number of addresses using a master key

A private key is generated from the master key, from which another private key can be generated and so on.

Deterministic functions are used for this purpose. Deterministic means that an output can be clearly assigned to an input.

Similar to the SHA256 algorithm, one-way functions are also used that can easily generate one or more private keys from the master key and thus subsequently generate a public address. On the other hand, it is not possible to infer from a private key to another private key or to the master key.

When we want to receive money, we create a new private key and the corresponding address. For the next transaction, we create a new private key and so on.

 

![](Image00004.jpg)Although the coins are distributed over many addresses, we can access all coins through a single master key. However, a third person cannot tell how much credit we have in total, because no one can trace which addresses belong to us.

We can see that the BIP32 extension is a great help for privacy. The problem, however, is that we still have to remember a cryptographic sequence of numbers.

Anyone who has ever tried to enter a private key on their PC will see how impractical it can be. It is easy to mistype or lose the thread. Even storing the key is not intuitive. That is why so-called seeds have been introduced. This extension has been introduced as BIP39 .

With the introduction of the seeds, we no longer have to remember a cryptic private key, but English words that are in plain text.

A total of 2,048 different English words were selected for this purpose.

The list of words is publicly available.

[https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/english.txt](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/english.txt)

![](Image00097.jpg)

Figure 68 Extract from the BIP39 word list

From these 2,048 words, we select twelve or 24 random words. These twelve or 24 words are called the seed.

![](Image00004.jpg)Nowadays, wallet providers do that for us. When we create a wallet, we are assigned twelve or 24 random words.

An example of a seed, twelve random words:

"couple copy shy they vintage during avoid champion little join better code"

We can't do much with these words ourselves at first, but we can convert the words into a Master Key and then into several Private Keys ![](Image00098.jpg)

Figure 69 Generating any number of addresses using a seed (12 words)

![](Image00004.jpg)With the BIP39, keeping private keys has become much more mass-produced. Twelve or 24 words are more tangible than a cryptic password with 80 or 256 characters.

Until now, seeds and multiple addresses have been limited to multiple bitcoin addresses. While it was possible to store hundreds of Bitcoin addresses in just one seed, more and more cryptocurrencies entered the market and the demand for user-friendly storage of multiple cryptocurrencies has increased. The fact that a seed can only be used for one cryptocurrency has changed with another BIP.

BIP44 enables the creation of private keys for various cryptocurrencies with the help of just one seed. Not only private keys for Bitcoin addresses are generated from the master key, but also other private keys, for example for Ethereum, Bitcoin Cash and thousands of other coins.

These private keys can in turn be used to calculate public addresses, i.e. accounts, on the respective blockchain.

![](Image00099.jpg)

Figure 70 Generating any number of addresses of different cryptocurrencies using a seed

The extensions made it possible to offer an all-in-one solution so that the end user only has to write down 12 or 24 words. They can choose these themselves, just as they did with the private key.

HD wallets query the words, create a master key and run through all addresses of all coins until several addresses in a row no longer contain any coins. In most cases, another 100 addresses are checked for security, so that no coins are forgotten.

Cold wallets such as the Ledger-Nano are also able to select a seed, i.e. twelve or 24 words, from the BIP39 word list through an integrated random generator.

What we have learned in this chapter: Crypto exchanges offer advantages and disadvantages. We can get our money quickly and exchange it for USDT or "real" USD, for example. However, we are putting our money in foreign hands. We have further seen that both the long-term, safe storage of coins and the ownership of different cryptocurrencies are facilitated by seeds and HD wallets. With just twelve or 24 words, we can simultaneously create and store countless addresses of different cryptocurrencies such as Ethereum, Bitcoin, Litecoin or Ripple. If we want to store all these coins in the long term, we no longer need to open a separate address for each coin.

7.4.         What should we pay attention to?

Especially when you are new to cryptocurrencies, there are always obstacles in your way, which we will look at in more detail in this subsection so that your enthusiasm does not turn into frustration.

Comparison of fees

When buying a share or financing a property, different banks and exchanges have different costs. It is therefore advisable to compare different stock exchanges and banks. Two to three percent fees might not sound like very much, but at the latest when financing a house, it is better to think twice.

It is similar with the purchase of cryptocurrencies. Some exchanges have percentage buying and selling fees (such as Binance ), others have a fixed price (such as eToro ) and still others have a hybrid model, for example Coinbase . To keep costs as low as possible, we need to compare the different price directories.

In addition, there may be hidden fees, for example due to price differences. With one provider, a BTC costs only €10,000, while the Bitcoin is listed for €10,500 with the other provider.

This is especially the case with cryptocurrencies, for example when we want to exchange Bitcoin for Ethereum.

It therefore makes sense to compare the prices with the current prices on coinmarketcap or other portals before buying. Especially with smaller providers and unpopular coins, you should pay attention to this.

Transaction fees

We have already seen that sending Bitcoins and other cryptocurrencies involves a fee for the miners. The lower the fee, the longer it takes to confirm our transaction. In the worst case, the transaction is not processed. Our coins are then not lost, but remain in our account, but in the worst case the transaction hangs in a queue for days before it is rejected.

![](Image00004.jpg)The transaction fees for sending coins are based on the load of the network and are not a percentage of the number of coins sent, but of the data size of the transaction. This means: If we want to send one BTC, the transaction does not cost significantly more than if we want to send 100 Satoshi.

When the price of a currency rises or falls in a short time, it means that many people are buying or selling BTC. Accordingly, the networks are busy, and transactions are higher. As described in the chapter Mining Difficulty , the transaction cost for Bitcoin was over 40 USD at peak times. This means: If we wanted to send BTC for €10, we had to pay almost four times the cost just for the transaction!

These costs are displayed in a wallet or the exchange before a transaction is sent. If we are not in a hurry, we can set a lower transaction fee. If this is not possible, it may make sense to wait a few hours or days to make the transaction until the network has calmed down.

If our transaction fees are set too low, ![Picture 4](Image00003.jpg)the miners will not forward the transaction to the network but put it in a "queue." This means that in the worst case our transaction is trapped in a queue for a fortnight (in the case of Bitcoin) before it is finally rejected.

The transaction fee we choose should always be in the normal range and fit the load of the network. Wallet providers know about this issue and often suggest a suitable transaction fee.

If you are interested in the current network load, you can look up the required transaction fees at [https://bitcoinfees.earn.com/](https://bitcoinfees.earn.com/) , for example. There, it is indicated what the fees are and how long the transaction time is.

Scams

Even though Bitcoin has existed for over ten years, the crypto market is still in its infancy. There is a great deal of ignorance or half-knowledge in many areas. As always when a market is still very unregulated, fraudsters are attracted who want to enrich themselves through nasty tricks. To avoid becoming a victim ourselves, there are a few things to keep in mind.

Becoming victim to fraud is called a scam .

A pyramid scheme, i.e. a scheme that has no real product and can only grow by attracting new customers, is a typical example of a scam.

An example of one of the biggest scams in the history of cryptocurrencies, which cost many people a fortune, was the website Bitconnect or the associated coin Bitconnect BCC. The site went online in spring 2016.

Bitconnect promised customers 0.25-2% daily  return on their assets if they exchanged their Bitcoins for Bitconnect coins.

When larger amounts such as USD 5,000, USD 10,000 or USD 100,000 were deposited, there were additional returns of 0.25% up to 1%.

How was this possible? According to Bitconnect, it had developed an artificial intelligence that buys and sells Bitcoins at the right time by observing price movements on the crypto market and thus makes a profit.

It was noticeable that Bitconnect spent a lot of money to acquire new customers. They paid influencers with several million followers to aggressively promote the Bitconnect site. Each person who recruited new customers sometimes received up to 10% of the new customers' deposits.

It was propagated that one could increase one's money by 37 times within one year. Although common sense tells us that such a principal can never have a legitimate basis, Bitconnect collected millions in investor money.

In reality, it was a pyramid scheme. The new customers' money was distributed to the older customers to create a legitimate impression. The early customers and influencers publicly showed that the money was actually received.

This pyramid scheme became so popular in the meantime that the Bitconnect coin rose to become the fourth largest cryptocurrency, with a market capitalisation of USD 2.6 billion.

Despite warnings from Ethereum founder Vitalik Buterin, for example, the greed of many investors was greater than their intellect.

In 2017, state authorities stepped in and threatened to take the site offline if the legality of the business model was not proven. Initially, all allegations were denied and the marketing department of Bitconnect did its best not to let the tarnished reputation break completely.

Gradually, however, the obvious could no longer be countered. From one day to the next, a statement was made that Bitconnect would no longer exist. In January 2018, Bitconnect disappeared from the scene. Reasons for this were allegedly the bad press and ongoing hacker attacks on the website.

The Bitconnect Coin lost over 99% of its value until it was completely suspended from trading in August 2018. All investors who had invested lost virtually all their deposits.

9.1.         Bitcoin can be banned

Let's start with an argument that you hear again and again in the news or from stock market gurus.

![](Image00004.jpg)It is often mentioned that Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies are doing well as long as it is still a niche for nerds. As soon as Bitcoin becomes bigger, the state will intervene and ban the cryptocurrency.

Even the head of the European Central Bank, Christine Lagarde , mentioned in an interview that Bitcoin needs to be regulated, maybe even banned. Bitcoin is a danger and is used for illegal activities.

We have already discussed in earlier chapters that Bitcoin is not used for illegal purposes and that it would be completely nonsensical due to the transparency and the factually non-existent anonymity. That the head of the European Central Bank makes such a statement is also no longer surprising to us. She symbolically stands for the unlimited creation of fiat money, while Bitcoin is seen as a still very volatile, but limited and decentralised currency. It is merely an attempt at Bitcoin character assassination, which has worked well in recent years. For a long time, Bitcoin was considered evil and criminal ; however, the truth is gradually spreading. Bitcoin is not used for criminal business but is an alternative to the centralised, inflationary monetary system.

Although one might not think it at first glance, Lagarde's statement is nevertheless a positive signal for the crypto market. A person like the head of the European Central Bank would not be involved with Bitcoin if it was still the same niche product it was in 2010. Instead, the statement that Bitcoin needs to be regulated and controlled is a sign of how much popularity and value Bitcoin has gained.

But what could a ban on the possession of Bitcoin look like? Is that even possible?

To answer these questions, let's review what we need to do to get Bitcoins in our wallet: We need to create a randomly chosen private key that is mapped to a public key. Owning Bitcoin, conversely, means that we have generated a randomly chosen number with 256 digits, which we call a private key. How is generating a random number to be prohibited?

In fact, mining cannot be banned either. Mining is nothing more than the execution of hashing algorithms by a computer, i.e., the computer solves a mathematical problem. The same SHA256 algorithm is also used in numerous other applications. The secret service, the military: they all use this algorithm.

How one would want to prohibit such a thing in a legal text is beyond the imagination of any lawyer in the world.

![](Image00004.jpg)It is factually impossible to ban the blockchain or Bitcoin. The basis is provided by cryptographic algorithms, which cannot be banned. You cannot ban mathematics.

Banning the possession of Bitcoin would be like banning the flip of a coin 256 times. Now, we see how absurd the subject of a ban is.

What can be banned or severely restricted, on the other hand, are the access points and framework conditions. This means that official institutions such as crypto exchanges are prohibited from acquiring new customers.

![](Image00004.jpg)We must always keep in mind that the Bitcoin network cannot be banned. A state can only cut itself off from the Bitcoin network. That would only make sense if all the states in the world decided at the same time to block all access points.

Even then, the price would initially drop, but trading would go underground over time, where control would be lost even more. Therefore, a complete blockade of the buying and selling of Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies is rather pointless. It is more likely, however, that states will put more and more hurdles in the way of buying and holding Bitcoin.

And that is exactly the effort we are already seeing. A few years ago, it was easy to open an account on a crypto exchange and buy bitcoins with just an email address. Nowadays, you often must prove your identity through a verification process before you can deposit a cent into your account. This procedure is also called KYC (know your customer).

In addition, states can put further obstacles in the way of cryptocurrencies. For example, the presentation of a certificate of good conduct before the purchase of cryptocurrencies, special taxes or special annual crypto levies, comparable to property taxation.

In order for the states to retain control over the monetary system, numerous "tricks" ![Picture 4](Image00003.jpg)will still come to light. Every new sanction is a positive sign for cryptocurrencies. Even if the price will collapse briefly after such statements, the value has risen in the long term.

Furthermore, environmental protection can be used as an excuse to make mining illegal, even though we have seen that mining is already producing green energy today.

Again, we need to understand that mining (just like owning Bitcoin) cannot technically be blocked. However, it is questionable how many citizens would accept the risk, for example of a house search, just because they own Bitcoins. The majority would instead "bend" to the requirements in order not to come into conflict with the law, even if the ban is neither sensible nor in the interests of the citizens. It is human nature to avoid confrontations with higher authorities.

We see this, for example, in an authoritarian system like the People's Republic of China. A large part of the population is largely passive in accepting the encroachment of freedom and personal rights, even though in recent years there has been an increase in uprisings and counter-movements.

In Europe and in the USA, the mentality of citizens is very reserved when it comes to resistance against the state apparatus. This is perfectly understandable, because our laws and regulations are not arbitrary, but have a concrete purpose, even if the implementation or execution is not always optimal.

Accordingly, it is not so much a technical prohibition that can threaten Bitcoin, but a game-theoretical consideration that involves all participants in the financial system, as well as the herd behaviour of people.

To completely eliminate Bitcoin in this way, the majority of states in the world would have to resort to totalitarian measures at the same time. History has shown that no totalitarian state can prevail in the long term. Moreover, we see that Bitcoin can also be interesting for states to make themselves independent of the world's reserve currency, the dollar.

Another possibility is to offer your own cryptocurrency, a state cryptocurrency. In some ways, this would be effective because the trust in the state is still higher among the masses than in an "unknown" cryptocurrency.

9.2.         Bitcoin is a bubble

By far the most common argument used against Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies is that Bitcoin is pure speculation and that it is just a bubble. Comparisons are often drawn to previous bubbles such as the tulip bubble or the dotcom bubble in 2000.

But what is a bubble anyway?

A speculative bubble is a [market situation in](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markt_\(Wirtschaftswissenschaft\)) which the prices of one or more [traded goods](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handelsware) (such as [shares](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aktie) or [bonds) are](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anleihe) above their intrinsic value.

When someone talks about a Bitcoin bubble, they mean that the price of a Bitcoin is far above its intrinsic value. The reason is usually given at the same time, namely that Bitcoin has no intrinsic value.

This is because Bitcoin is not matched by any economic performance, as is the case with stock companies, nor by any physical substance, as is the case with gold or real estate. In conclusion, Bitcoin is worthless and thus a bubble.

Intrinsic value is a very abstract concept and cannot be described by physical attributes. Before we look at how we can measure bitcoin value, let's consider the arguments in favour of a bubble in the crypto market:

A lot of capital is flowing into the crypto market, especially from people who do not have a long-term investment horizon but want to make "quick money." Here again, the non-existent regulation of the market plays into their hands.

The result is extreme volatility. Rapid rises and falls in the price of currencies are the result.

Another argument for a bubble is the fact that Bitcoin is limited to 21 million coins. Once a good is limited, a bubble can develop as demand rises, with people paying unlimited prices to get a share of the limited good.

The final element is euphoria when the price rises rapidly. The fear of being the last not to get rich with Bitcoin leads uninformed market participants to invest in the crypto market without analysing the fundamental data. This effect is called FOMO (fear of missing out).

As soon as expectations are not fulfilled, they are sold (often at a loss).

All these reasons form temporary "bubbles" in the crypto market, which we have seen again and again in recent years. Within a few months, cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin multiplied and then crashed again by up to 90%.

However, there were two major differences to a "normal" bubble: When a bubble burst, as was the case with the dotcom bubble, the prices of goods fell below their pre-bubble price. Shares like the German Company Telekom share have not recovered from this to this day.

If you held your coins for the entire period of the rise and fall, you multiplied them compared to the price before the bubble.

In comparison, in the case of a real bubble, as was the case with the Telekom share, one has suffered a loss with one's investment to date.

9.3.         Bitcoin can be hacked

The next argument that Bitcoin is worthless is that it only takes one attack by a hacker to destroy all the trust that Bitcoin has built up over the last few years. In principle, this is completely correct, but the thesis is wrongly formulated. Nevertheless, the media and high-ranking celebrities keep spreading it.

"Cyber Theft

Blockchain hackers have stolen more than US$13.6 billion".

Source: [https://www.internetworld.de/sonstiges/bitcoin/blockchain-hacker-13-6-milliarden-us-dollar-gestohlen-2600349.html](https://www.internetworld.de/sonstiges/bitcoin/blockchain-hacker-13-6-milliarden-us-dollar-gestohlen-2600349.html)

We have seen that it is virtually impossible to guess someone else's private key, but how secure is the blockchain itself? After all, we keep reading that Bitcoin has been hacked and billions of dollars in assets have been stolen.

The blockchain is a decentralised system, which means that there is no central point of reference that hackers can use, unlike in a safe deposit box, a bank branch or a checkout in a supermarket. When there is talk of "hacked Bitcoin accounts," it is usually the private key that has been stolen, for example because it was unprotected on the PC and the person concerned caught a virus. There are also known cases of people being blackmailed into handing over their private key.

Another scam is insecure or fraudulent wallets that pretend to generate a random private key but store it themselves and thus have access to the account. For this reason, it is extremely important to use a secure, proven provider.

The last way Bitcoin has been "hacked" so far is that a central institution, such as an exchange, has been hacked. The servers were hacked and the private keys stolen, as was the case with the Mt.Gox hack of 2013.

![](Image00004.jpg)The blockchain technology behind Bitcoin has nothing whatsoever to do with all these facts. They are always central points of attack that a hacker can exploit, be it a crypto exchange or a private PC.

Therefore, we should never leave coins lying around unnecessarily on an exchange platform (especially if larger sums are involved), but always send them to our address, of which only we have the private key. After all, otherwise the control lies with a central institution again.

The principle is: " Not your key - not your coins ".

Technically, the blockchain is not hackable.

To hack Bitcoin, proven principles such as the elliptic curve or the SHA256 algorithm would have to be hacked. This is considered practically impossible. Credit card companies or government institutions like the US military also rely on these techniques. The SHA256 algorithm has been considered unhackable for 20 years.

Having cleared up this myth, let's look at the next aspect, which is often completely misinterpreted.

9.4.         Bitcoin is inefficient

Bitcoin can currently write about seven to eight transactions per second to the blockchain. In comparison, payment providers such as Visa or Master Card make several 10,000 transactions per second.

The conclusion: Bitcoin is inefficient! Huge amounts of electricity and resources have to be spent to be able to carry out a few transactions.

These are valid objections, but when we hear these arguments, we know that the speaker has not studied Bitcoin technology or understood the meaning of Bitcoin.

To do this, we first need to understand what efficiency means in the first place. Because why does Bitcoin need so much electricity? Where does all the "consumed" energy go?

Contrary to common understanding, it does not mean that we convert electrical energy into transactions. That was never the primary goal of Bitcoin. A central entity like a bank is many times more efficient in terms of this comparison.

![](Image00004.jpg)Bitcoin was created to establish a decentralised, secure system that is unassailable and in which no participant can be blocked or censored by a central authority. Complex mathematical algorithms are calculated through mining. The results are stored in the blockchain. The more energy is used, the more difficult it is to solve the algorithms and the more secure the network becomes.

Bitcoin converts the lowest level of life, energy, into security. Mining is the most efficient way to generate decentralised security. With all other consensus methods like Proof of Stake, the electricity is wasted. With mining, it flows 100% into security.

As more miners connect to the network and more energy is consumed, the nonce requirements adjust using the Mining Difficulty. It automatically becomes more difficult to solve a block. This means that the result—the verified block—is all the more secure. An attacker would have to put more energy into the system to "crack" the blockchain in the form of a 51% attack.

We also see that there is no other cryptocurrency and no other consensus algorithm that can guarantee this security.

As an example, let's take Ethereum, which can guarantee significantly more transactions. A block does not take ten minutes, but only ten to twenty seconds. Is it therefore more efficient? If we take the pure energy per transaction as a yardstick, then: definitely. But as we have seen, that was and is never the goal of Bitcoin.

![](Image00004.jpg)Bitcoin was deliberately created to be slow and sluggish because this is the only way to ensure optimal security.

The fact that Bitcoin's blocks are smaller and slower means that more computing power is required. This in turn means that more security is created for the same number of blocks/transactions.

It is also often argued that the technology is outdated and that there are much better, newer and faster cryptocurrencies. These can handle many more transactions and still support great features at the same time. This is also a misconception.

![](Image00114.jpg)

Figure 81 Crypto trilemma

As always in life, in cryptography we have to make a trade-off of centrality, security, speed and much more. It is not a dilemma where one has to determine a compromise between two things, but a trilemma . In the trilemma of decentralisation, one cannot achieve at least one of the three goals (decentralisation, security and speed).

![](Image00004.jpg)Some altcoins are significantly more centralised than Bitcoin and can carry out significantly more transactions per second. They are therefore not more modern, do not have better technology or are not superior to Bitcoin in any other way. They have merely opted for a different compromise.

Once you understand the point of a decentralised, indestructible store of value, you realise that Bitcoin is neither obsolete nor inefficient.

And although Bitcoin was deliberately built to be slow and sluggish, it can still be used as a means of payment. We'll get to the bottom of the payment myth next.

9.5.         Bitcoin is unsuitable as a means of payment

The next point has many overlaps with the previous point that Bitcoin is inefficient. Due to the fact that Bitcoin is still very slow with currently seven to eight transactions per second, the myth arises that Bitcoin can never establish itself as a means of payment.

![Bitcoin, Europa, Geld, Finanzen, Münzen, Euro, Btc](Image00115.jpg)

Figure 82 Bitcoin as a physical means of payment conceivable?

That is absolutely correct. We have already seen that the intention was never to enable as many transactions as possible on the blockchain, but to make the existing ones as secure as possible! Bitcoin is unassailable. No state or conglomerate in the world can take over this network.

And yet, through technical advancement, it is possible to use Bitcoin as a means of payment and still maintain core security.

There are already technical solutions, for example by compressing the size of the transactions and thus putting more transactions into a block, for example through an extension called Segwit .

Normally, a transaction consists of half data and half signature. Segwit reduces the need for the signature, so that almost twice as many transactions fit into one block. This means that 14 transactions per second can already take place on the Bitcoin blockchain. Fourteen transactions are already more than seven or eight, but we have already seen that it would have to be tens of thousands for Bitcoin to be used as a large-scale means of payment.

![](Image00004.jpg)Since the Bitcoin Blockchain primarily focuses on ensuring the security and invulnerability of the network, it will most likely never be possible to run all transactions directly in the Blockchain. For small amounts, it would also be completely uneconomical due to the high transaction costs.

As the demand for transactions on the Bitcoin Blockchain increases, the transaction costs rise at the same time. In a few years, hundreds of euros per transaction could be normal.

Does this mean that Bitcoin is not an option as a means of payment?

![](Image00004.jpg)Bitcoin is a worldwide movement with a huge community and thousands of active programmers who work on the further development of the network every day. It would be naïve to believe that there is no technical solution to Bitcoin's challenges today. One example that has been developed enormously in recent years is so-called second-layer solutions.

Second Layer Solutions

Second layer models are based on the idea that the blockchain serves as a stable, lowest layer, but that we can build further layers on top of this layer. The blockchain is described as the main layer.

This is comparable to the internet. The basis for our internet is the Ethernet protocol. Further layers are built on this lowest protocol, for example the IP addresses that each computer receives. Further protocols, such as the internet browsers with HTTPS protocols, build on the IP addresses. Each new layer brings new features. For example, the HTTPS layer provides security for data transmission. We all use the HTTPS protocol when we surf the Internet and see the lock symbol in our browser.

![](Image00116.jpg)

Figure 83 HTTPS abbreviation for a secure internet connection

As we have already seen, the blockchain does not need a security update. However, a second layer protocol can help us solve the speed problem.

By building more layers (new technologies) on top of the blockchain, the number of transactions can be scaled to thousands of transactions per second.

So far, there are many different approaches as to what exactly the second layer models can look like. For example, we are looking at a very promising technology: the Lightning network.

Lightning network

The Lightning network is an independent network. Bitcoin can be sent within this network. The name ‘lightning’ was chosen because the transactions can be transmitted at the speed of light.

The Lightning network makes sense for small payments, for example when we want to buy a coffee for €2 or a bread roll for 40 cents. The effort to initiate a separate transaction for this, which is stored forever in the blockchain, is out of proportion.

The Lightning network is as simple as it is ingenious. We send our Bitcoin from the Bitcoin Blockchain to the Lightning Network. To do this, we lock our Bitcoins on the Bitcoin Blockchain so that they can no longer be used. To do this, we need a one-time transaction on the Bitcoin Blockchain.

We can send the blocked bitcoins on the Lightning network and can receive bitcoin from other Lightning users that the other users have previously blocked on the main blockchain.

![](Image00004.jpg)The transactions within the Lightning network are only stored by parties in the Lightning network. The Bitcoin blockchain is not aware of this. A transaction is only created when a party enters or leaves the Bitcoin Blockchain.

![](Image00117.jpg)

Figure 84 Transferring the bitcoins to the Lightning network

We can think of the system as if we had a bank account (Bitcoin account on the Bitcoin blockchain) from which we withdraw cash. We transfer our money from one network (bank account) to another network (cash system).

With cash, we can trade with anyone who also has cash. This costs no fee for a transfer and is usually quicker than if we must wait a day for the transfer.

If we want to use our Bitcoins again on the Bitcoin Blockchain, we have to deposit the same amount of Bitcoin that was "withdrawn" again.

The difference of all transactions in the Lightning network is stored as one transaction on the Bitcoin blockchain.

![](Image00004.jpg)The transaction history within the Lightning network is not stored. This has a positive side effect: because the transactions are not stored, it is no longer so easy to trace which person sent how many bitcoins to whom.

From outside, only the overall balance is that no Bitcoins are missing.

The Lightning network (like many technologies) is still in its infancy. At the moment, a lot of technical knowledge is still needed to be able to take Bitcoin out of the network and send it on the Lightning network, for example.

Thousands of users around the world are already using the Lightning network, and more are joining every day.

Over time, the technology will be developed further and become suitable for mass use. In the future, a simple smartphone app may make it possible to transfer bitcoins cheaply in near real time.

9.6.         Quantum computers destroy the Bitcoin network

People who are somewhat tech-savvy often throw in the argument that a quantum computer can easily crack Bitcoin's encryption, causing the entire network to collapse. As a layman, this sounds like a valid argument. However, this is not the full truth either. To understand to what extent quantum computers can pose a threat, let's take a look at what quantum computers actually are, how they work and what impact this has on Bitcoin.

We learned about one-way functions in the basics of cryptography. These are functions that are very simple to solve in one direction, but not so simple in reverse. Applied to the Bitcoin network, this means that we can very easily infer the public address from the private key, as we already discussed in "Bitcoin can be hacked."

This function, like the elliptic curve, is called a mathematical hard problem , and it is precisely these that quantum computers can calculate much faster than conventional computers. A quantum computer does not calculate with 1 and 0 like an ordinary computer, but with very small parts, so-called quantum particles or qubits . These can be electrons or light particles. The special feature of these particles is that their properties allow us to calculate several calculations simultaneously instead of one after the other.

This means that a quantum computer requires significantly fewer calculations to obtain the same number of results as a conventional computer.

This is both an attack on the SHA256 algorithm and on the elliptic curve method, which generates the public address from the private key.

![](Image00004.jpg)In any case, it is correct that there is the theoretical possibility of cracking the two methods. However, we are still very far away from this at the present time. Some estimates suggest that the computing power of a quantum computer still needs to increase hundreds to thousands of times for there to be a real danger of an attack. Quantum computers are also extremely expensive, and only a few large companies like Google are working on development.

 

![](Image00119.jpg)

Figure 86 Today's dimensions of a quantum computer

Quantum computers cannot be compared with normal computers. The quantum particles have to be meticulously processed and evaluated. They work at -270 °C and require enormous amounts of energy.

The focus of large companies like Google is certainly not on attacking cryptocurrencies during development.

The objection to this is that we have seen in the last 50 years how quickly technology can advance. Let us therefore assume that quantum computers develop into everyday devices.

As soon as it becomes apparent that quantum computers could pose a threat to cryptographic algorithms, the network will replace the previous algorithms with quantum-safe algorithms. As we have seen, this is easily done by a fork. The SHA256 algorithm can be replaced by another, quantum-safe algorithm, and the network will continue to run as before. Furthermore, we have already seen that the SHA256 algorithm is also used by governments and intelligence agencies. These face the same theoretical attack scenarios.

![](Image00004.jpg)Quantum-safe algorithms already exist. Whether quantum computers will ever pose a real threat to cryptography or whether it will remain a theoretical problem is something that even quantum physicists cannot predict beyond doubt.

In any case, it is easy for the Bitcoin network to avoid such a scenario. Again, this argument has no deeper substance that could make Bitcoin dangerous.

Now that we have dispelled many technical myths, let's take a look at some economic aspects.

9.7.         Bitcoin is used for illegal activities

You often hear in the news that Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies are used by the Mafia or terrorist organisations for illegal activities on the dark net. Especially in the early years of cryptocurrencies, this was indeed the case.

Bitcoin was considered a private coin due to its low profile and was used to buy weapons and drugs. This was possible because the Bitcoin network was not yet widespread, and there was no pseudo-anonymity. Nowadays, the network is more widespread. The entry and exit points into the Bitcoin network, i.e. the crypto exchanges, are for the most part more tightly controlled by state institutions.

Due to its transparency and the resulting pseudo-anonymity, Bitcoin is completely unsuitable to be used as a means of payment for illegal activities.

![](Image00004.jpg)Cryptocurrencies are used for less than one percent of all illegal transactions. Most of these transactions take place via fiat currencies, primarily cash. This is unlike open blockchains such as Bitcoins.

Because with Bitcoin, everyone can see the transaction history and knows exactly who has how much money and who has sent money to whom. This is so widespread that it is known in “insider circles" which addresses and which coins originate from illegal activities. These have virtually no value for the community anymore. Nobody wants to receive a coin from an address that is known to be the account of a drug or Mafia boss.

![](Image00120.jpg)The coins that can be associated with illegal activities are also called tainted coins .

Tainted coins are coins that are linked to illegal activities and are therefore almost not accepted by the community. They are worth less than other coins. This is also the case with Bitcoin due to the transparent blockchain.

As the network grows and more transactions are made, more and more coins will be tainted or partially tainted over time.

Large exchanges also keep databases in which coins are considered to be tainted. This means, for example, that an exchange will reject coins if a large part of the coin is tainted.

The problem of tainted coins can be solved by mixing tainted coins with non-tainted coins. Here again, private coins like Monero or Dash offer a solution. If a tainted coin is added to a transaction pot with 99 "clean" coins, the output is only 1% tainted on average.

On the one hand, this makes sense, as it does not further limit the money supply because some coins are worth less than others.

On the other hand, this in turn opens the door to illegal activities. However, we have already seen that it can make sense to ensure a certain degree of privacy.

A compromise must be found between security and freedom or security and privacy.

But why do we need privacy at all? Surely those who have nothing to hide won't resort to a private coin, will they?

Here we take the idea further that Bitcoin will establish itself as a means of payment and that we will make everyday payments through Bitcoins. Do we really want everyone to be able to see how much money we have? Do we really want everyone to be able to see that we paid the doctor or how much we spent on our new car?

Transparency also has its negative sides.

For this reason, extensions are already being developed to limit transparency and advance anonymisation. Extensions such as the use of seeds or the Lightning network can also help.

Another example is CoinJoins . Simply put, the transactions of several users are collected and mixed. As a result, it is no longer possible to clearly trace who sent BTC to whom.

![](Image00121.jpg)

Figure 87 Obfuscation of direct transactions using CoinJoins wallets

However, this is less well received by authorities and exchanges. Some exchanges already do not accept coins sent from "private wallets."

![](Image00004.jpg)In summary, Bitcoin is not primarily used for illegal activities and is also completely unsuitable for this due to its high transparency.

9.8.         Bitcoin is too expensive and pure speculation

Bitcoin and its price are driven up by speculators. Normal citizens can never afford a Bitcoin because no one can spend thousands of euros on a coin.

Here, too, we see parallels to other arguments, for example that Bitcoin is a bubble and has no intrinsic value.

First, we debunk the argument that a normal citizen can never afford a Bitcoin.

The argument is understandable. If Bitcoin is hypothetically going to rise to 1 million, we will get just 0.00001 BTC for €10. This acts as a deterrent as a currency, because no one wants to calculate in currency units that start with four zeros after the decimal point.

However, we have also seen that one Bitcoin itself is divisible into up to 100 million Satoshi. For our €10, we would get 1,000 satoshi in the same scenario. That sounds much more feasible, doesn't it?

The representation of the currency is therefore also not a problem with Bitcoin. A big problem at present, however, are the strong price fluctuations triggered by speculators.

On this point, we have to agree completely with the critic. At the moment, the price is unpredictable. Price drops of 70% are almost normal.

From today's perspective, the price of Bitcoin is still highly volatile. However, we have also seen that the long-term trend is upwards.

The crypto market is still very small. All cryptocurrencies together have just 10% of the market capitalisation of the gold market. Moreover, it is correct that many people have not dealt sufficiently with the fundamental, technical data. The trust in Bitcoin is not yet sufficiently developed.

In this small, unregulated market, it is easier to trigger large price fluctuations or even manipulate the market in some cases. Especially in the case of smaller cryptocurrencies, it happens time and again that several large investors join forces to influence the price of an insignificant coin upwards or downwards.

Two psychological factors play a major role here. These can be seen not only in the crypto market, but also in the gold market, the stock market or all other asset classes.

When an asset class rises rapidly in a short period of time, it stirs up a psychological fear. You don't want to be the only "idiot" who doesn't get rich while everyone else is increasing their money. For this reason, one buys, even if one is not sufficiently informed.

The aforementioned FOMO effect is also an emotional driver in these situations.

However, if the price drops sharply within a short period of time, doubts start to spread. Uncertainty spreads as to whether the investment was a wise decision, and after some pondering, one sells at a loss. Selling an asset class that did not convince is generally not wrong. You always have to put the price in relation to the fundamentals. In the case of cryptocurrencies, selling would have been the wrong decision at almost any time.

There are only two remedies against these market effects:

More long-term capital needs to flow into the crypto market. Large investors, so-called whales , and companies need to invest in the market.

This means that inflows and outflows have less of an effect in relative terms. This is exactly what we have been seeing for a few years. Large investors, fund managers or private investors invest in cryptocurrencies and the projects behind them.

Investors also need to better understand the asset class.

![](Image00004.jpg)Someone who fundamentally understands Bitcoin and is convinced of the technology and the future will not sell their Bitcoin just because the price has fallen by 70%. The value is crucial, and it is constantly growing. In the long term, the price of a good will always approach its value.

Looking at the next two, five or even ten years, the crypto market may continue to fluctuate a lot and be dominated by speculators. The more capital flows into the market and the more people are convinced of the technology, the less panic buying and selling will occur and the more stable the Bitcoin price will become.

So this myth has, at this point, a kernel of truth. Even if the price fluctuations will decrease with increasing awareness, the price is very volatile nowadays and can be regarded as speculation.

Whether Bitcoin is too expensive can only be assessed subjectively.

Lastly, let's look at an economic statement about Bitcoin.

9.9.         Deflation destroys the economy

We have already seen that our current inflationary monetary system is doomed to failure in the long run.

A central entity, such as a central bank, has the power to create money out of nothing, and can thus effectively expropriate any saver by causing the purchasing power of money to lose value.

However, one must put the blame into perspective. For the central banks only carry out what is demanded by the economy, the states and the shareholders, namely to pump new money into the market to keep the economy artificially alive. The central banks have no other options. Their only capabilities are to print new money or to destroy existing money.

That is why more and more voices are being raised, especially by Bitcoin maximalists. They state that the inequality is not due to the participants of the system, but to the inflationary monetary system in general, that it is doomed to fail.

![](Image00004.jpg)The opposite of inflation, i.e. a steady increase in the money supply, is deflation , as is the case with Bitcoin. The amount of money always remains the same or even decreases over time.

Since everything else in our world is almost unlimited, be it gold, products like mobile phones or cars, the value of money increases over time.

In the case of deflation, one year later one can buy more with one's money than today. The prices of economic goods become cheaper and cheaper and not, as we know it until now, more and more expensive.

But can this form of monetary system work?

The first argument against deflation is quite logical at first glance. Why should we still spend money if we know we can afford more of it in one, five or ten years?

It makes much more sense to save money instead of spending it on cars, holidays or smartphones. As a result, less is consumed, the economy and thus the entire system collapses. This simple example shows: Deflation cannot work. But is that really the case?

Would we no longer go on holiday at all? Would we no longer buy a smartphone or a car?

At the latest when it comes to food or housing, it becomes clear that we would still buy bread rolls or noodles and also pay rent for accommodation.

Above all, in the developed world, people continue to consume. Or do we want to stop watching Netflix after an 8-hour working day just because we would pay 10% less for it in five years? Surely not. Nor would we let our annual holiday, which we have worked hard to earn, be taken away just because we could take an extra day's holiday next year for the same money.

It is true, however, that we would only buy a new smartphone every three or five years, for example. And a high-quality one that remains functional for a long time. We would consume consciously and sustainably. The companies that produce sustainably would automatically be paid more.

![](Image00004.jpg)Our system would be trimmed for efficiency, not maximum return, in a deflationary monetary system.

If Apple, Huawei, Samsung or any other big-name smartphone manufacturer were to launch the same mobile phone every year that, technically speaking, did not feature any essential changes to the previous model, consumers would think twice about saving the money, as the money would gain purchasing power on its own.

Companies and service providers would also be competing with deflation in this scenario. They would have to offer higher quality products, as money would be worth significantly more than in an inflationary system.

It is also a fact that switching to a deflationary system would be a big change. Many companies would go bankrupt, as they would not deliver any added value to the population and would only be kept alive by the devaluation of money.

All other asset classes would automatically become less interesting than before. Perhaps it would plunge the economy into a temporary crisis, from which, however, it would recover slowly and sustainably afterwards. Many people state that we cannot deliberately switch to a system that weakens the economy for a short time. The alternative, however, is to keep a system going that has never worked at any time in our history, that is the basis for expropriation of savers and encourages waste and unsustainable consumption.

Bitcoin as a deflationary monetary system is an experiment with the potential to solve many of our problems today, an experiment worth testing.
