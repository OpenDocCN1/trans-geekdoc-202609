What is a Non-Fungible Token?

 

1.1 Definition of NFTs

The abbreviation NFT stands for non-fungible tokens, on German: non-replaceable token. NFT are certificates for unique digital objects. You certify that you own a specific file. Fungible in itself means that a thing can be replaced by something equivalent. For example, it does not matter whether you have 20 dollars in your wallet as a single note, four 5-dollar notes or 20 1-dollar notes.

However, objects that are unique by certain properties are not fungible. Such objects can exist either physically or digitally. For physical objects, such as a painting or car, it is easy to set a value. This is measured by the uniqueness of the work of art and how much people are willing to pay for it.

For digital objects, this is more complicated. After all, despite copyright and usage rights, every image, song and video can be copied and redistributed at the click of a mouse. It is difficult to determine the exact value or owner of a file. This is where the idea of NFT starts: The digital tokens contain information about a particular file that is both unique and verifiable. The blockchain technology is used to verify the authenticity of the files.

NFTs and Blockchain

Blockchain is basically a kind of database in which transactions of digital goods, such as cryptocurrencies, are stored in blocks and distributed on many computers worldwide. For example, Bitcoin is on a blockchain. Its architecture makes the blockchain tamper-proof.

Unlike cryptocurrencies, NFT in a blockchain does not represent an interchangeable asset that is transferred from one person to the next, but a concrete asset – like a unique digital work of art that is linked to an owner and a value. Simply put, by storing the NFT in the blockchain, digital goods become things that really belong to you.

Currently, the open Ethereum blockchain is the most important for NFT trading. Purchases of virtual goods are therefore usually processed in Ether, the second highest endowed cryptocurrency after Bitcoin.

 

What is the difference between fungible and not fungible?

To understand what makes NFT so special, you first need to understand the difference between fungible and not fungible. If something is fungible, it means that something is homogeneously interchangeable. Examples of this from the real world include banknotes or precious metals: one gram of pure gold is worth as much as another gram of pure gold. And if you give someone a ten-dollar note, it wouldn't matter if they didn't give back exactly the same note.

All this changes when something is not fungible. Although two items may look identical at first glance, both have unique information or features that make them irreplaceable or non-interchangeable.

An example of a non-fungible good could be a plane ticket. Airline tickets are the same at first glance, but each ticket contains a different passenger name, a different destination and a different seat number. Therefore, there could also be serious consequences if you exchange a plane ticket for another. The same is true in the digital space with NFTs. Another example would be Internet domains, as each domain can only exist once.

Non-fungible tokens can limit things in digital space and present them uniquely. Many other tokens or cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin or Ether are fungible. If you send someone an ether and get an ether back for it, you wouldn't notice any difference.

The same applies to tokens: Most tokens so far are based on Ethereum's ERC-20 standard. For the sake of simplicity, one can imagine that each of these tokens represents a 10-dollar note. If you send this token to someone and get another one back a week later, it's the same as the other.

All of this changes with non-fungible tokens. Currently, most NFT on the Ethereum blockchain use the so-called ERC-721 standard. Tokens that use this standard can be compared to Pokémon or Yu-Gi-Oh collectible cards. Each token contains unique information and a different level of rarity.

There is also another serious difference to consider. Fungible tokens are divisible, which means it is possible to send or own a fraction of Bitcoin or another ERC-20 token. Similar to cash, where you can pay with a ten-dollar note and receive change.

Non-fungible tokens cannot be shared and must be purchased or sold in their entirety. As with collectible cards, where no one would think of buying half a card.

Criticism of NFT

But there are also headwinds – and not only because of the large crypto price fluctuations. One of the criticisms concerns possible copyright violations. It becomes problematic, for example, if it is not verified whether a seller on the blockchain really owns the rights. In addition, the owner owns the crypto object only on the blockchain on which it was purchased. In most cases, this is the Ethereum blockchain. In theory, however, any object on other blockchains that support NFT could be reoffered.

NFT are also energy consuming. Since a single transaction of the Ethereum blockchain requires the computing power of many computers, the power consumption is very high. NFT consist not only of a transaction, but of a variety of them, depending on the number of bids at an auction. As a result, the energy demand increases. The digital artist Memo Akten analyzed the energy expenditure of a single NFT-sale. On average, a sale consumes 340 kilowatt hours. By comparison, an ordinary Ethereum transaction accounts for about one-tenth of that.

Just as DeFi (Decentralized Finance) protocols mimic banks' financial products, NFTs mimic the world of art galleries, shops, and museums. But without expensive and risky intermediaries.

NFTs themselves are a type of token - ERC-721 - that is non-divisible or not fungible, hence the name. Its value is based on the assurance of blockchain technology that no NFT can be falsified. 2012–2013: Coloured Coins

It can be argued that Colour coins are the first NFT's to exist. Coloured Coins consist of the small values of a BTC often as small as 1 Satoshi. Colour Coins can be used to represent a large number of assets and can have multiple use cases, including:

●Estate

●Coupons

●Ability to extract your own cryptocurrency

●Issuing shares of a company

●Subscriptions

●Access tokens

●Digital collections

Coloured Coins were an example of a big leap in Bitcoin's capabilities. The downside was that it could only represent certain values if everyone agreed on their own value. Bitcoin's coding language was never designed to enable this type of behaviour within its own network, so Colour Currencies were only as powerful as its weakest participants. For example, 3 people agree that 100 Colour Coins represent 100 company shares. If a participant decides that they no longer sync Coloured Coins to represent company shares, the whole system crashes.

The first mention of Coloured Coins appears to come from a blog post by Yoni Assia in early 2012 titled "bitcoin 2.X (aka Colour Bitcoin) – first features". In his post, he discusses Coloured Coins, but makes no reference to the various assets or their use cases. Instead, he claims that Coloured coins are unique and identifiable from normal bitcoin transactions, since they are bitcoins that are part of the "Genesis process". The potential of these new assets was apparently not explored until December 4, 2012, when Meni Rosenfeld published an article titled "Overview of Coloured Coins". A few months later, in 2013, another article titled "Coloured Coins – BitcoinX" was published.

The flaws of Coloured Coins were obvious; the system worked best in a permissible environment, which means that in some cases it is better to simply use a database. However, Colour coins opened the door for more experiments and laid most of the foundations for NFT's. The enormous potential of putting real-world assets in distributed ledgers was clear, but implementation required a more flexible blockchain.

What happens to a Hard Fork?

Unlike Soft Fork, a hard fork is a way to make changes to the blockchain that is not compatible with the old blocks. Old clients do not understand this change in the log and, if in doubt, discard the individual transactions or entire blocks. The prime example of a hard fork is a change in block size. A block larger than one megabyte on Bitcoin's blockchain is considered faulty by the current clients and is therefore discarded. Proponents of the new block size cannot simply send the large blocks to all clients but must ensure that they are accepted. This is only possible if enough participants can be found to use the appropriately adapted mining software.

Changing the size of the protocol is technically relatively easy to perform. Rather, the problem lies in convincing a large part of the community of the new block size so that it is accepted in its implementation. In the case of the Hard Fork, the new system is introduced after a specific block, which has been defined in advance. This creates two different blockchains: the first blockchain continues the old system and refuses to accept the changed blocks. The second blockchain, on the other hand, only accepts the new blocks and no longer allows the old ones. The two blockchains share a common past but go their separate ways after the split.

 

Possible Consequences of a Hard Fork

A hard fork is a difficult undertaking because it needs the support of the community. In the absence of miner support, the Hard Fork has failed because no transactions are forwarded without it. A failure would also be a lack of user support: in this scenario, all users, exchanges and traders use the old version, which would no longer make the miners' reward system profitable. Accordingly, it is in the mind of the miners that many users are behind the new system. With a large minority of miners, the "scoured" coins would only be exchanged within the small circle of proponents in fiat currencies, which would reduce demand and the price of the coins generated.

The worst-case scenario would be if both protocols are actively used and therefore run side by side. This would, for example, create a second currency besides Bitcoin. Such a case has already occurred twice, once Bitcoin Cash and once Bitcoin Gold has been created, which exist parallel to Bitcoin. In order to avoid secession, there must be consensus among both miners and users. However, achieving this in practice is difficult, as all blockchain participants are mindful of their own economic advantage and hope that Hard Fork will give them an advantage over other market participants.

What is a DAPP?

A DAPP is nothing more than a special form of an app. These are based on a blockchain, are open source and decentralized and must be able to generate their own tokens. The big advantage is that no company controls all functions, but that the open-source community continues to improve the DAPP.

There are three different types of DAPPs. On the one hand, there are Type 1 DAPPs that operate their own blockchain, such as the Bitcoin network. Now there are other DAPPs that rely on the blockchain of this Bitcoin network. Such DAPPs are called Type2. The third type is again based on type 2 DAPPs. Microsoft Azure, for example, is a Type2 DAPP that is based on the Ethereum blockchain. The company offers an infrastructure that is used by Type 3 DAPPs, in this case, for example Absolut or Heineken.

Apps and DAPPs – The Differences

Apps and DAPPs may seem relatively similar to the newcomer at first glance. Nevertheless, there are glaring differences, which will be discussed in more detail here. Apps are mostly released by companies that have full control over all the features of the respective app. For example, as a user, you can have no influence on google changing its YouTube app so that adblockers no longer work.

DAPPs, on the other hand, are open source. This means that the source code is visible to everyone and can therefore be changed at will. If you want to redesign a DAPP according to your own wishes and bring the necessary know-how with you, you can change the source code and create your own DAPP variant.

Another difference is that DAPPs are always based on a blockchain and are stored in decentralised locations. Therefore, it is almost impossible to hack a DAPP, since one would have to attack the entire blockchain behind it, including all networked computers, at the same time in order to change the structure of the DAPP. An app, on the other hand, is usually supported by the respective company's own servers. Here you would "only" have to hack the one datacenter to bring the respective app under his control.

In summary, it can be said that DAPPs are both safer and freer in their development, as no company controls them centrally. Apps, on the other hand, are clearly owned by a company that has full control. Also, the source code is not publicly visible here, so you do not get to see the structure of the respective app without further ado.

How are DAPPs Defined?

The community has agreed on a clear definition of the DAPP. First, DAPPs are not issued and maintained by a single provider. A DAPP runs distributed and decentralized over a peer-to-peer network. In addition, four criteria must be met that an application can be called a DAPP. It first must be the source code of a DAPP open source. Furthermore, it must be based on a blockchain, have cryptographic encryption and be able to generate its own tokens. Only if all these criteria are met can we speak of a DAPP.

●Open Source

The fact that a DAPP is open source does not mean that every programmer can simply decide for himself what the DAPP should look like after its own changes in the future. Rather, the community decides which modifications are accepted and which are not. Nevertheless, the source code remains visible to everyone, so that every developer knows exactly what the current state of affairs is. The fact that a DAPP is open source ensures continuous development. No individual administrator or even a company sets the future direction, but the further development of the DAPP is controlled by the entire developer community together.

Of course, as a programmer you also have the possibility to easily redesign the respective source code according to your own needs and thus get a very personal DAPP. Of course, you should master the underlying programming language and understand the subtleties of the source code. Whether your own changes will actually be applied, however, will then be decided by the entirety of the developers.

●Blockchain

The second criterion that every DAPP must meet is that it is based on a blockchain. The blockchain makes the DAPP decentralized and is stored on many different computers at the same time. As a result, it becomes almost impossible that a DAPP can be deleted or that criminals can hack into the DAPP. Well-known blockchains are, for example, the Bitcoin network or the Ethereum network. Here, all data is stored on the users' PCs and not, as for example with apps, centrally on special servers. Such data includes the source code, all transactions of the cryptocurrencies and other information necessary for the smooth running of the DAPP. The blockchain is always the heart of a DAPP. Type1 DAPPs have their own blockchain, while Type2 and Type3 DAPPs are based on it. Each block is completed and encrypted after a certain period of time.

●Cryptographic Encryption

The cryptographic encryption of each block is used to obtain and verify all information in a certain period of time. If you look at the Bitcoin network, for example, enormous computing power is spent here to verify and store all transactions. So-called Bitcoin miners take over this computing power and in return receive rewards in the form of Bitcoin and also the transaction fees. If a block is completed, this is done by generating a hash. A hash is a kind of checksum that ensures that all transactions and information made in the previous block are authentic and traceable.

However, individual trades will be difficult to identify in retrospect, as it is very time-consuming to make the previous information visible again from a hash. The cryptographic encryption thus serves both to ensure the authenticity of all information, as well as the anonymization of the individual users. Therefore, your own data is much more secure if you use a DAPP than if you make it available to the providers of apps.

●Token generation

The last criterion that must be met by a DAPP is token generation. With the Bitcoin network, such a token is simply the Bitcoin. The miners who provide their computing power to create hashes must be rewarded so that they want to fulfil this task. This is done using a reward in Bitcoin once a hash is found. Investors who speculate with cryptocurrencies know how high the Bitcoin price is today. So, each token has an equivalent value in real currency.

The token generation only serves to make it worthwhile for the miners to provide their computing power. Hash generation is important for any blockchain, as it would no longer work without it. As a result, token generation ensures that it remains attractive for miners to keep the blockchain running. If all these four criteria are met, one can speak of a DAPP.

Token Standards

All tokens on Ethereum, whether cryptocurrencies or NFTs, can be assigned to unique standards. Standards are an important building block for how tokens work. Only with suitable standards are the essential characteristics such as ownership, access and transferability guaranteed. Standards are also essential for software development. In this way, the basic functionalities and behaviours are clearly clear to all those involved.

The three most common Ethereum token standards are ERC20 for Fungible Tokens (exchangeable tokens such as cryptocurrencies) and ERC721, as well as ERC1155 for Non-Fungible Tokens (non-exchangeable tokens = NFTs).

The ERC20 standard assigns amounts to addresses. Thus, per Ethereum address, the exact number of cryptocurrencies held can always be recognized.

With the birth of CryptoKitties, the important NFT standard ERC721 was created, on which most NFTs are based to this day. ERC1155 is another NFT standard developed by Enjin. ERC1155 is a hybrid of exchangeable and non-interchangeable tokens. ERC721 maps unique IDs to owners, while ERC1155 has a nested mapping of IDs to owners to sets.

Each Crypto Punk is characterized by individual optical characteristics, so that no one is the same as the other. Crypto punks have inspired the digital art market and were the forerunner for later standards. Even though Crypto Punks were officially the first NFTs on the Ethereum blockchain, the CryptoKitties are still in the memory of most people. At the end of 2017, they became the first mainstream application of NFTs.

2017 was a year with a lot of hype around cryptocurrencies, anyway, driven by the ICO bubble on the Ethereum platform. In a short time, countless new ICO projects started and each of them had new and supposedly scarce tokens on offer. This triggered a veritable gold digger mood. More and more new cryptocurrencies on the Ethereum blockchain saw the light of day in the digital world. But one of the reasons for the high utilization was, among other things, the creation and trading with CryptoKitties. CryptoKitties are digital images of comic cats created by Dapper Labs for a blockchain computer game. Each cat is unique and thus only present once on the blockchain. The special thing about them is that in the on-chain computer game, new unique CryptoKitties can be bred by clubs of different cats. The CryptoKitties Bubble

Breeding attracted many speculators. The idea was simple – you buy a few rare CryptoKitties, breed a few new, even rarer cats with them, sell them at high prices and then do the whole thing all over again. At its peak in 2017, the trading volume was around 5000 ETH and sales of CryptoKitties achieved prices of over $100,000.

It was almost absurd to pay such high prices for digital cats. But the incredible story behind it in combination with the great hype about cryptocurrencies brought many new buyers into the market in a short time. Ethereum's network capacity at the time could not cope with this onslaught, so that trading and breeding CryptoKitties incurred horrendous fees and hours of waiting.

This mixture of hype, speculation and viral story eventually resulted in a bubble that also received appropriate attention in the press. But as expected, the bubble burst soon after. In mid-December 2017, the demand for CryptoKitties suddenly declined and prices tumbled. The entire crypto bubble began to burst shortly thereafter, and a multi-year bear market was ushered in.

6.2. FLOW: The Blockchain Built for NFTs

When CryptoKitties, brought the Ethereum blockchain to a standstill in late 2017 due to an immense overload. Developer Dapper Labs learned first-hand that blockchains of the current generation were not built for this demand.

Instead of simply finding another home for the decentralized app or wait until the Ethereum scaling solutions are mature. Dapper has decided to create a blockchain that the company and other developers can rely on.

The result is Flow, a block chain specifically designed to support things like NFT collectibles and large-scale crypto games. Flow is a blockchain designed for comprehensive scaling without using sharding techniques that enable fast and cost-effective transactions that make sense for dApps like NFT marketplaces and crypto-infused video games.

As mentioned earlier, Flow comes from Dapper Labs, who decided to solve the problem of blockchain congestion directly by developing one provided for games and other interactive experiences. Dapper now uses Flow for all of its own projects, including NBA Top Shot, but is also open to other developers.

How does Flow work?

Flow uses a pledge verification model where validators must deploy a certain number of FLOW tokens to participate in the network.

However, the way validation works is unique among blockchains in that Flow splits validation tasks into four separate node types: consensus, verification, execution, and capture. All four node types participate in the validation of each transaction.

According to Dapper, splitting tasks makes processing transactions more efficient than competing blockchains. This is an alternative option for sharding or distributing a blockchain's storage and compute requirements across numerous nodes. Flow does not use sharding. In this way, according to Dapper, Flow keeps transactions atomic, consistent, isolated and permanent (ACID) and allows developers to build on each other's work.

Flow is also updatable smart contracts that allow smart contracts to be deployed in beta and then expanded or repaired before they are completed and made immutable.

Dapper Labs convinces with a diverse network. Well-known investors joined early and co-financed the development of the Flow Blockchain. These include the Warner Music Group and star investor Andreessen Horowitz. At the same time, they are already working intensively with the American basketball league NBA. The UFC or the game developer Ubisoft are also among the partners, who also include Coinbase and Samsung.

High scalability

Among experts, the programming language is considered to be particularly developer friendly. As a result, more and more developers are turning to the Flow Blockchain and working on optimization. At the same time, the commands in the network are broken down into individual tasks, which can then be processed in parallel. This increases the efficiency of the computing capacity; the network becomes more scalable.

Users of the Dapper Wallet can use both Ethereum and Flow and get access to both networks. Dapper's current users will be the first to gain access to the now released Flow network and be able to test applications and use the latest applications there.

SpoCKs technique instead of consensus

Flow uses the new cryptographic approach Specialized Proof of Confidential Knowledge (SpoCKs). This technique was developed by the Flow team and published in a special technical documentation. With SpoCK, any number of developers of a third observation instance can prove that they each have access to the same confidential knowledge. There is no information about confidential content in the evidence and they act interactively.

Each SpoCKs is individually and specifically tailored to the developer and cannot be copied or forged by any other developer on the network. Basically, the technique asks the execution and verification nodes to prove their knowledge. This knowledge can only be obtained through the execution of all transactions assigned to them. And only when this has been clarified, they receive the reward.

Smart Contracts with ACID Guarantee

A single common status for all smart contracts ensures that each transaction complies with the following guarantees:

  1. Atomicity = atomicity stands for seclusion in transactions
  1. Consistency = Consistency means that a transaction leaves a persistent database state
  1. Isolation = isolation prevents or restricts transactions from affecting each other during execution
  1. Durability = durability guarantees the permanent storage of transactions in the database

In German also referred to as ACID properties, these four criteria are considered prerequisites for the reliability of systems.

This makes it possible for each blockchain developer to build on each other's code and therefore develop completely new products and services at a rapid pace. Technically, this is composability.

The composability approach is similar to an open-source community where developers can quickly pick up on innovations because they can ultimately access a wider range of possibilities. A platform takes up the aspect of composability (composability/compatibility) when the existing resources are used as building blocks and can be programmed in higher-order applications.

To illustrate with an example, today you can buy and buy Bitcoin or Ether by breaking it into pieces and exchange the same cryptocurrency in its own unit.
