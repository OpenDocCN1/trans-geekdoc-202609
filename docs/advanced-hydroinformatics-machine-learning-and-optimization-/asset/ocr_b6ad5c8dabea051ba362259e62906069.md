The identification of θ is typically carried out through calibration, by considering a set of realized inputs and outputs, x_i and y_i, respectively, and a performance function, P [y_i, M (x_i | θ_i)], to evaluate the closeness of simulated to actual outputs (e.g., efficiency). We remark that as result of calibration, although the transformation function, M, is deterministic, its parameters also can be considered as random, since they are inferred from the random processes x and y.

In the classical split-sample scheme and its alternatives, the realized data are obtained from observed records, x_o and y_o, a subset of which is used to calibrate the model and the rest for validating its predictive capacity against an independent data set. As already discussed, well-known shortcomings of such approaches are the potential lack of representativeness of historical data and their inability to represent long-term hydroclimatic changes (often, but not solely, associated with their length), which eventually results in poorly identified parameters. This problem is further intensified since we have to keep a significant part (e.g., half) of observations for validation purposes, thus loosing valuable information that may be contained in the associated data subset. Furthermore, when the observed records have different sizes, only their common part can be accounted for in calibration and validation, while the nonoverlapping period of data is ignored. This case occurs quite often, since rainfall data, which is a rather easily retrievable information, usually exceeds the period of runoff observations.

Here we manifest an alternative approach for parameter estimation, which follows the stochastic simulation paradigm (Efstratiadis, 2011), as depicted in Figure 2.1. Similar to the classical slit-sample scheme, this also involves a twofold strategy, yet by using long synthetic data within calibration (we apply symbols x_s and y_s for synthetic inputs and outputs, respectively), while the validation is employed against the full set of observed data, x_o and y_o. Apart from the deterministic hydrological procedure, M, and the performance function, P, the computational procedure also requires a synthetic time series generator, G. Ideally, this has to reproduce the true hydrological regime of the input and output processes, x and y, respectively. This option is offered by stochastic models, which are generally built upon the information contained in the historical data. In this respect, the synthesis procedure can be expressed as a multivariate stochastic transformation of the form:

[x_s, y_s] = G[x_o, y_o]. (2.1)
