## 2.5. Concluding Remarks

Hydrological models that are calibrated against short records may be unable to reproduce the anticipated catchment behavior in the long run, while additional drawbacks arise due to the essential sacrifice of historical data in order to keep space for validation. In an attempt to address this issue, we highlight a promising strategy, aiming at inferring the model’s parameters on the basis of synthetic rainfall-runoff data, and employ validation against the full historical sample of observations. The so-called stochastic calibration scheme is driven by a synthetic time series generation procedure, implemented within the R-package anySim, which takes advantage of the available data to represent the probabilistic properties and dependence structure of the rainfall-runoff processes across multiple scales, and the long-term fluctuations of the changing climate as well. Crucial requirements of the stochastic model are the faithful reproduction of the cross-dependencies across seasons and scales, which determines the causality between rainfall and runoff, and the fitting of suitable distribution functions to the processes of interest in order to reproduce extreme conditions that are impossible to be contained in historical data. Under this viewpoint, the proposed
method actually allows for extracting more information from short time periods.

The advantages of stochastic calibration over conventional split-sample approaches are demonstrated by means of a proof of concept, involving the calibration of a lumped conceptual model with four parameters. It is interesting to remark that while the differences in terms of efficiency are actually not so much notable among the two approaches, the optimized parameter values are. Therefore, it is expected that the two model versions will respond quite differently under combinations of inputs and states that are outside of the ones found in the historical data. Given that the stochastically calibrated model is trained over many extended hydroclimatic conditions, it is expected to ensure stronger identifiability. This is also indicated by the fact that this version exhibits more stable predictive capacity across varying time windows. On the other hand, the obvious increase of computational effort, due to the substantially larger length of calibration data, can be addressed through recent advances in global optimization, where machine learning and surrogate modeling techniques are embedded within robust evolutionary algorithms (e.g., Tsoukalas et al., 2016).

Preliminary analyses of the proposed method against a large set of catchments, and by employing models of varying level of complexity, both at the monthly and daily scales, have already provided encouraging results regarding the generality of the stochastic calibration paradigm (Kourakos et al., 2021). Future efforts will focus to the uncertainty aspect, aiming to provide a holistic framework for model calibration and uncertainty quantification based on stochastically generated data.
