The use of synthetic instead of real data is acceptable since, from a practical viewpoint, calibration is only an auxiliary stage of the overall modeling procedure. Furthermore, in order to assess the predictive capacity of the model, all available historical information is accounted for, thus the validation data set is also extended. Under this premise, the decision of which part of data should be used for calibration and which for validation makes no sense any more.

The use of stochastically generated data instead of historical samples that contain relatively few combinations of system inputs (rainfall), outputs (runoff), and states (storage conditions), and obviously miss long-term hydroclimatic fluctuations and rare events, also allows for addressing the major issue of representativeness of hydrological information from which the model parameters are inferred. It is recognized that the observed data partially only reflect the actual statistical and stochastic behavior of the input and output processes, while further uncertainties arise due to measurement and processing errors. In this respect, a well-designed synthesis procedure based on a good stochastic model can improve the information gained from the data available so far, toward at least three key directions:

1. providing wider dependency patterns among the inputs, x, and the outputs, y;
2. representing hydrometeorological extremes that are rarely appearing in short historical samples (Singh & Bárdossy, 2012); and
3. describing long-term hydroclimatic fluctuations, such as persistent wet/dry periods, which also may be induced by the changing climate.

Section 2.3 discusses in brief the specifications for the synthetic data generation approach within our stochastic calibration framework.

## 2.3. Insights to the Stochastic Simulation Procedure

The idea of substituting short historical records with synthetic ones is very popular in a wide range of hydrological design, planning, management, and forecasting applications, involving the use of deterministic models that are driven by synthetic inputs, provided by stochastic models (e.g., Koutsoyiannis & Economou, 2003). The coupling of deterministic and stochastic models allows for assessing probabilistic quantities, such as reliability, resilience, and risk, and expected values of long-term performance metrics, such as yield, costs, benefits, and deficits, on the basis of simulations with sufficiently large samples (e.g., with length of hundreds or thousands of years). On the other hand, less often is synthetic data used for evaluating the behavior of hydrological models in the long run (e.g., Nalbantis et al., 2011; Efstratiadis et al., 2015).

Cornerstone of all aforementioned approaches, belonging to the broader family of Monte Carlo simulation, is the time series generation model, which has to ensure a concise representation of the probabilistic and stochastic structure of the actual processes, x and y. For a long time, this prerequisite has been determined as the preservation of a finite set of statistical characteristics of historical data within the synthetic ones by means of low-order moments (typically the mean, variance, skewness) and low-order auto- and cross-correlations (Matalas & Wallis, 1976). However, in the context of stochastic calibration, this approach is not sufficient, since these metrics describe only partially the marginal and dependence regime of the actual processes x and y, while under some quite common conditions, unrealistic dependence patterns may appear in the synthetic data (Tsoukalas et al., 2018b).

## 2.4. Proof of Concept

### 2.4.1. Data and Model Description

In order to demonstrate our framework, we apply a lumped conceptual model to represent the monthly runoff produced upstream of Evinos dam, western Greece, which is a key component of the water supply system of Athens (Koutsoyiannis et al., 2003). The river basin area is 351.9 km², and the available hydrological data (precipitation, potential evapotranspiration, runoff) extends over 50 hydrological years (from October 1970 to September 2020). Table 2.1 summarizes the key statistical information of historical rainfall and runoff data (mean, standard deviation, skewness, auto-correlation, and cross-correlation), to be reproduced within the synthetic time series that are used in stochastic calibration. The latter have been generated via the anySim package (Tsoukalas et al., 2020), and they extend over a time horizon of 2,000 yr. The synthesis scheme ensured the generation of dependency patterns (Fig. 2.2) and long-term fluctuations (Fig. 2.3) that are much wider than the ones found in the observed data. These are expected to represent the full hydroclimatic regime of the basin, which may be hidden, as a result of the limited information embedded in the observed data. In fact, only half of this information is accounted for within the split-sample calibration approach.

Figure 2.4 depicts an outline of the model and its governing equations. The model employs a typical bucket-type scheme comprising three interconnected tanks, which gets as inputs the monthly precipitation, P, and potential evapotranspiration, PET. The upper tank has infinite capacity and represents the major hydrological processes over the terrain, namely the direct (overland) runoff, Q_D, which is considered as a portion κ of precipitation, and the direct evapotranspiration, ET_D, which is fulfilled by the water excess that is temporarily stored in the ground and the vegetation. The remaining water infiltrates to an intermediate tank of storage capacity S_max, which employs the soil moisture accounting across the unsaturated zone. This implements three fluxes, namely the evapotranspiration losses through the soil, ET_S, which are controlled by the storage ratio, S/S_max, and the evapotranspiration deficit, PET − ET_D, the percolation to the groundwater tank, W, which is controlled by a recession parameter, λ, and the saturation runoff, Q_S, which is represented as the excess water that overflows from the tank. Finally, the lower tank has infinite capacity and employs the regulation across the groundwater zone. In particular, it receives the percolation and generates baseflow, Q_B, through another recession parameter, μ. In this respect, the runoff arriving at the basin outlet is the sum of three components, namely, Q = Q_D + Q_S + Q_B.
