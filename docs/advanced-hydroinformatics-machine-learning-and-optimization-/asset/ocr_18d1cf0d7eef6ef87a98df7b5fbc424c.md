In this vein, several researchers revealed the need for taking advantage of multisource information within calibration, by means of both hard (i.e., measurements) and soft data as well as expert judgment, in order to improve model realism and parameter plausibility (e.g., Seibert & McDonnell, 2002; Efstratiadis & Koutsoyiannis, 2010; Nalbantis et al., 2011; Finger et al., 2015; Mohammed et al., 2021).

An evident drawback of the split-sample rationale is that the inferred parameter set is only conditioned on a subset of the available data, thus losing important information that may be embedded in the validation period. This has triggered researchers to provide hybrid calibration schemes across sliding windows (Coron et al., 2012, 2014; Vormoor et al., 2018) or subperiods (Gharari et al., 2013; Thirel et al., 2015a), in order to detect parameter sets that exhibit consistent performance in time. Following a rather heretic approach, Singh and Bárdossy (2012) and Arsenault et al. (2018) have manifested to entirely disregard the validation procedure under certain conditions, and use instead the full data set for parameter identifiability. To our knowledge, this approach is quite common among practitioners, who usually employ the split-sample test in order to obtain a rough picture of the model robustness and then recalibrate the model on the entire data set. Perrin et al. (2008) have also demonstrated that in poorly gauged basins, where the available flow records are very short, it is preferable to apply already optimized parameters from areas exhibiting satisfactory hydrological similarity instead of employing calibration with too limited information. On the other hand, Razavi and Tolson (2013) highlighted the issue of computational cost when calibrating time-demanding models against long data, thus proposing surrogate-based approaches to approximate the model performance on the full-length data period, based on much shorter ones.

Nevertheless, the question of how the model will perform if applied to conditions outside those used for model calibration, remains an open issue, which is also induced by the changing climate premise (Blöschl & Montanari, 2010; Kling et al., 2014; Broderick et al., 2016; Fowler et al., 2016, 2018; Dakhlaoui et al., 2017, 2019; Stephens et al., 2020). For this purpose, Klemes (1986) introduced the so-called Differential Split Sample Testing (DSST), in which the determination of calibration and validation subperiods is appropriately made to span over as much as dissimilar hydroclimatic data, which in turn allows for better evaluating model transferability across time.
