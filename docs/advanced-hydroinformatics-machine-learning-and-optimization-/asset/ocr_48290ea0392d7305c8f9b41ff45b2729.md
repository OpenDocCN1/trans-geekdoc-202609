# 7.3. Workflow

# 7.3.1. Data Acquisition and Processing

The data used were rainfall and water level from USGS EDEN (https://sofia.usgs.gov/eden/eve/index.php), discharge from SFWMD DBHYDRO database (https://www.sfwmd.gov/science-data/dbhydro), and rainfall forecasts from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) (https://apps.ecmwf.int/datasets). Data were subjected to extensive quality assurance measures by the agencies responsible for collection. The discharge data set was the only data set with missing values, which were gap-filled using two additional time series of discharge collected by the SFWMD and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. The USGS time series for discharge was the most comprehensive and was selected for use by ML models, but it also contained some missing values. To gap-fill USGS discharge, values from the other two time series were used to fill missing values of the USGS time series. Because each discharge time series represents data from the same location, values were similar and in some cases identical, confirming that this substitution method was adequate.

Once the rainfall, water-level, and discharge data were continuous with no missing values, each time series was graphed to observe trends and seasonal patterns. Cross-correlation with rainfall and autocorrelation analyses were then performed for each station, in addition to cross-correlation with discharge analyses for the stations influenced by a structure, to determine the effect of structure discharge on water level. We determined that the correlation between past rainfall and future water level was not very high (Fig. 7.3a and Fig. 7.4a). Due to the random nature of rainfall together with the influence of control structure operation, the relationship between water level and rainfall is nonlinear and may not always be captured by a linear statistic such as correlation coefficient.
