¹ Department of Agricultural Sciences, Clemson University, Clemson, South Carolina, USA
² Department of Civil Engineering, Clemson University, South Carolina, USA

The increasing rise of computational power has led to significant advances in the application of machine learning (ML) techniques in hydrological simulation. These methods are specifically developed to discover constitutive relations by implementing an unbiased implicit approach to capture unforeseen patterns in massive data sets. This study applied multiple ML approaches for daily rainfall-runoff simulation across a mixed urban-rural drainage system, the Northeast Cape Fear River Basin in North Carolina (NC), USA. Multiple ML algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Bayesian Lasso, and Random Forest (RF) models, along with the Sacramento Soil Moisture Accounting (SAC-SMA) rainfall-runoff model, were applied to predict sequential daily streamflow records based on a set of collected data from climate and streamflow gauging stations. Analysis suggests that the effects of input data on model performance, the error associated with forcing data, the amount of training data, and the correlation among different attributes of data series have strong influences on ML computation. Compared to the SAC-SMA, the Bayesian Lasso model was able to simulate the temporal dependencies among the observations and thereby was capable of accurately modeling the multivariate sequences of complex daily rainfall-runoff records across a mixed urban-rural catchment. The results provided an algorithmically informed simulation on the dynamics of daily streamflow simulation that may apply to other complex catchments and climate settings.

### 5.1. Introduction

Watershed modeling helps understand the past and current state of rainfall-runoff processes in a watershed and provides a way to explore the implications of management and planning decisions and imposed forcing function changes such as land use and climate. In complex watersheds (in this case, coastal plain watersheds), very significant modeling efforts have been conducted based on both lumped and physically based simulations (Amatya & Jha, 2011; Samadi & Meadows, 2017; Samadi et al. 2017, 2020; among others). However, rapid increase in the availability of measurement data has spurred the development of many data-driven methods or machine learning (ML) approaches for modeling and predicting rainfall-runoff dynamics. Indeed, in the realm of process-based hydrologic modeling, time-series ML algorithms have been recently utilized to improve short- and long-term streamflow predictions at various scales and domains (e.g., Solomatine & Dulal, 2003; Solomatine & Shrestha, 2009; Corzo et al., 2009; Shrestha et al., 2014; Fang et al., 2017; Kratzert et al., 2018; Shen, 2018; Shen et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2018). ML algorithms attempt to estimate the mapping function (f) from the input variables (x) to numerical or continuous output variables (y) and can learn patterns hidden in data. Different ML algorithms make different assumptions about the shape and structure of the function and how best to optimize a representation to approximate it. Several studies have demonstrated that ML models can learn complex nonlinear input-output relations (e.g., Solomatine & Dulal, 2003; Solomatine & Shrestha, 2009; Shen, 2018); thereby, they are capable of serving as a complementary alternative to other state-of-the-art techniques in rainfall-runoff applications (Yang et al., 2020).

Among various ML algorithms, regression-based models, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP), and Decision Tree (DT), have been widely applied for rainfall-runoff simulations across various domains and scales. For example, Sivapragasam and Muttil (2005) applied SVM to compute rating curve values at three gauging stations in Washington, USA. Their results indicated that SVM is well suited for rating curves extrapolation compared with widely used logarithmic and higher order polynomial fitting methods. Bui et al. (2016) proposed a new ML approach based on a neural fuzzy inference system and metaheuristic optimization for flood susceptibility modeling (MONF) over the Tuong Duong district in central Vietnam. They found that MONF outperformed other ML algorithms, including MLP and DT for flood susceptibility mapping. Sadler et al. (2018) applied RF method for the coastal urban stormwater prediction in Virginia, USA. They used quality-controlled, crowd-sourced street flooding reports ranging from 1 to 159 per storm event for 45 storm events to train and evaluate the RF model. Their results showed that RF performed better than other ML algorithms, such as Poisson regression, at predicting the number of flood reports and had a lower false-negative error rate. Despite the expanded use of ML models in streamflow simulation, very few studies have used advanced ML methods to simulate coastal rainfall-runoff processes. The closest work may be the statistical analysis of streamflow records in the United States to compute the probabilities of high and low flow events in the past several decades along with the projected changes in the coming decades (Hidalgo et al., 2009; Campbell et al., 2011; Asadieh & Krakauer, 2017).

ML approaches can be divided into three broad categories: (1) supervised learning, (2) unsupervised learning, and (3) reinforcement learning. Supervised learning maps the observations or labeled data sets to known output and generates formula based on input and output values (e.g., Simeone, 2018). On the other hand, unsupervised learning discovers implicit relationships/patterns in a given unlabeled data set (unclassified data set) and finds an association between input values to group the data. Reinforcement learning follows a trial-and-error method for each predictive step or action with no precise label or error message (e.g., Shrestha & Mahmood, 2019). This study aims to use multiple state-of-the-art regression-based ML models to predict daily streamflow across a coastal plain basin in North Carolina, USA. Coastal plain modeling is a substantial challenge as this type of simulation is hampered by the heterogeneity in basin characteristics (low-lying elevation, evapotranspiration, wide floodplains, etc.) and complexities in soil storage capacity due to extensive alluvial and nonalluvial forested wetlands and riparian storage (e.g., Shirmohammadi et al., 1986; Samadi et al., 2017). This research simulated a coastal plain rainfall-runoff process using three different regression-based ML algorithms including SVM, Bayesian Lasso, and RF. While the SVM and RF models have been widely used for rainfall-runoff simulations (see Loos & Elsenbeer, 2011; Wang et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2016, among others), Bayesian Lasso has rarely been implemented for hydrologic modeling problems (Das Bhowmik et al., 2018) and has never been performed for a coastal plain hydrologic simulation. This study is thus the first attempt to implement a Bayesian Lasso algorithm to exploit model inference via posterior distributions for a coastal plain rainfall-runoff simulation problem. This advanced algorithm, in addition to more widely used SVM and RF approaches, is employed to glean information from geospatial data and weather information and train proposed supervised learning models for daily streamflow simulation. Various ML simulations were also compared with the simulation result of a well-known conceptual rainfall-runoff model (i.e., Sacramento Soil Moisture Accounting (SAC-SMA)). It is important to note that the modeling approach presented here is general and applicable to any rainfall-runoff simulation problem, although the actual interpretations of the modeling diagnostics may vary from application to application.

Results and findings are outlined in section 5.3. A detailed discussion of ML’s potential use in rainfall-runoff applications is presented in section 5.4.
