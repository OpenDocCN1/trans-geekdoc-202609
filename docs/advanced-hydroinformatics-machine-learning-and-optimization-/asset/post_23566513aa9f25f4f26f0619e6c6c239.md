Additionally, large values of data (e.g., big data corpus) could present a challenge to computational calculations (Mikolov et al., 2013). In this sense, the Word2Vec function looks for words that occur in similar contexts to have similar embeddings, that is, represent the meaning of the word, for example:

The flood in the Bogotá River triggered emergency responses from the Colombian government.

The drought in the Bogotá River triggered remedial actions taken by the Colombian government.

The words flood and drought will have similar word vectors due to a similar context. Two architectures/algorithms are widely used to represent the “meaning” of a word according to a context: CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words) (predict the target word from the bag-of-words context) and Skip-gram (predict the context words, given the target) (Jang et al., 2019). Both implementations use neural networks, as shown in Figure 10.6, where w(t) denotes the analyzed word, and w(t±s) represents the word at a distance of s words in the sentence context.

For example:

Sentence: “Several parts in Kuala Terengganu were inundated with flood waters, following continuous rain.”

Distance: −8  −7  −6  −5  −4  −3  −2  −1  0  1  2  3  4

If w(t) = flood, then w(t+1) = waters, w(t-2) = inundated, and w(t+4) = rain. In practice, Skip-gram gives better word representations when the monolingual data are small. However, CBOW is faster and more suitable for larger datasets (Mikolov et al., 2013). In the Skip-gram approach, the objective of the neuronal network is to maximize the probability of any context word, given the current central word, mathematically formulated as follows:

$F.O. = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left( \sum_{\substack{j=-k \\ j \neq 0}}^{k} \log p\left(w_{t+j}|w_t\right) \right)$

where k denotes the size of the training window (which can be a function of the central word), and p(w_{t+j}|w_t) represents the probability of the word w(t+j), given w(t):

$p\left(w_{t+j}|w_t\right) = \frac{\exp\left(u_w^T * v_{wj}\right)}{\sum_{n=1}^{N} \exp\left(u_n^T * v_{wn}\right)}$

where N signifies the number of words in the vocabulary.

## 10.3.6. Step 6 (NLP Analysis)

Once the text had been tokenized and the Word2Vec function had been applied to embed the words in vectors, NLP tools were used to create a word cloud for each water body, so the main words linked to each article could be represented in a graph. This allowed us to obtain a better picture of the most representative elements (social, economic, environmental, and climatic) related to each water body. The Natural Language ToolKit (NLTK), Scipy, and Word Cloud libraries were used in this process.

Counting the number of news items for each time step and each water body, as well as identifying the main topics about each water body, were also part of this study. Through this information, patterns between extreme events and digital news could be explored. Later, this information was aggregated on monthly and annual levels. However, most of the NLP free libraries work only for English-language texts or have a paragraph word-count limitation for non-English languages (because of an implicit third-party translation process). This makes the application of NLP difficult in non-English regions. Nevertheless, some public packages and libraries allow NLP analysis in Spanish-language texts.

## 10.3.7. Step 7 (Sentiment Analysis)

An important analysis that could be derived from NLP tools is sentiment analysis. Sentiment analysis shows the perception of an author when writing the article, interpreting this sentiment with a number that ranges between 0 (negative sentiment) and 1 (positive sentiment). For this purpose, a neuronal network is built to classify the writer’s sentiment through a numerical value; a summary of the involved processes is shown below. This requires statistical data sampling, data normalization, input variable selection, training, and validation.

### Algorithm 10.1 Sentiment analysis pseudo code

```
Input: x=[x₁, . . . , xₙ], xₜ ∈ Rᵐ and s=[s₁, . . . , sₙ], xₜ and sₜ ∈ [0,1]
Initialize parameters: w=[w₁, . . . , wⱼ], wₜ ∈ Rⁿ
for t=1, . . . , n do
  Calculate y=w•x
  Update w
  Calculate error(y,s)
end for
Output: sₙ₊₁ ∈ [0,1]
```

In this research, we used the spanish_sentiment_analysis library (Hofman, 2017/2021), which was already trained and tested in its open source version. The library used almost one million reviews of users of the following webpages: Trip Advisor, PedidosYa, Apestan, QuejasOnline, MercadoLibre, SensaCine, OpenCine, TASS, and Twitter (Spanish-language version) for its calibration and validation. TensorFlow and Marisa-trie libraries were used for building the sentiment neuronal network. In this process, the model developed a regression between the users’ reviews of the websites and their corresponding scores on a scale from 0 to 5 (websites’ 5-star reviews). Then, this information was represented as a numerical value between 0 and 1, where a 5-star review represented a sentiment value of 1. This calibrated model was used to predict the news sentiment over the Magdalena River Basin.

The spanish_sentiment_analysis package is an open-source library built on scikit-learn and NLTK in Python. The model works as a simple pipeline that includes a vectorizer (TfidfVectorizer), a feature selector (SelectKBest), and a classifier (MultinomialNB). The feature selector will reduce the number of features generated by the vectorizer. The feature selector function selected was SelectKBest from scikit-learn, performed by checking whether a feature was relevant or irrelevant. The classifier model used was a multinomial naive Bayes with best parameter alpha = 0.03. The parameters and hyperparameters of this pipeline were found by using a GridSearch K cross-validation, with K = 10. Additionally, the Marisa-trie library was used for a static memory-efficient structure. This model was applied to the digital news information of each article. The results were compiled on monthly and annual aggregation levels. Moreover, the model was tested with reviews of users of the pages eltenedor, decathlon, filmaffinity, and ebay in their Spanish language for two data sets (positive and negative) of 50,418 reviews each one using the Mean Absolute Error (MAE).

## 10.3.8. Step 8 (Validation)

The results obtained from the NLP analysis were compared with measured physical variables (e.g., river-level flows) and observed events (social and environmental emergencies), as part of the validation process. This comparison was necessary to verify the relations between events and digital citizens’ media. For this purpose, different parameters were validated, as listed below.

- 1. Sentiment index versus monthly river levels’ range next to the Hidroituango Dam and the Bogotá River (Girardot). The monthly river-level range was used as a simple representation of a sudden change in the flow of the river (e.g., Hidroituango emergency).
- 2. Correlation of monthly climatic variables and sentiment index. The correlation was performed using the monthly sentiment index for the water bodies with information for all the time ranges (at least one article per month). Furthermore, the climatic events were extracted as the maximum and the minimum values of precipitation and temperature for the spatial area of the water body.
- 3. Confusion matrix of extreme events identified by climatic variables (precipitation and temperature) versus the number of articles over time. Extreme events were identified as monthly values smaller than 5% of the selected range values of the climatic variables. Likewise, months with values that exceeded 95% of the selected time series were classified as extremes. For this analysis, annual time ranges were selected.
