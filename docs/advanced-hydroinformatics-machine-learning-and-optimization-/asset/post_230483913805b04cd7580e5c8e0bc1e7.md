## 9.2.4 The A-trous Wavelet Transform

At the same time, if interpolations are performed, the wavelet transform and approximation coefficients are contaminated with impurities brought in by the extension method applied at the right-side limit. Errors commonly found in hydrology can be consulted in Du et al. (2017), Maheswaran and Khosa (2012), and Quilty and Adamowski (2018).

The a-trous algorithm is called in many ways by different authors, namely, redundant wavelet transform, stationary wavelet transform, a-trous algorithm, translation- or shift-invariant algorithm, or undecimated wavelet transform. Through the redundancy offered by the algorithm and at the expense of greater storage capacity, time invariance is accomplished. Another feature of the method that motivates the use of this algorithm is the fact that the wavelet coefficients are calculated from data only obtained previously in time, appropriate for a directed (time-varying) data stream. The reader is encouraged to refer to the following references to obtain greater details: Aussem et al. (1998), Kozakevicius and Schmidt (2013), Renaud et al. (2002), Renaud et al. (2003), and Shensa (1992).

Let’s proceed and briefly show the a-trous algorithm using the redundant Haar. The first approximation resolution level, φ(1), is obtained from φ(0) (original signal) by using the following equation, where c_k = (1/2, 1/2) are the scaling coefficients that satisfy the following dilation equation:

$$
\varphi(t) = \sum_k c_k \varphi(2t - k) \tag{9.5}
$$

and, for the first resolution, we never use future values. This is valid for a directed (time-varying) data stream, see below:

$$
S_{m+1,n} = \sum_k c_k S_{m,n-2^k} \tag{9.6}
$$

leads to

$$
S_{1,1} = c_1 S_{0,1-2^0} + c_2 S_{0,1-2^1} = c_1 S_{0,0} + c_2 S_{0,-1} = \frac{1}{2}(S_{0,0} + S_{0,-1}).
$$

Observe the difference with the decimated form $S_{m+1,n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \sum_k c_k S_{m,2n+k}$ (DWT algorithm), where the filter was chosen to be c = (1, 1); in this case, future values are considered (not allowed for the testing phase), and at each decomposition we are jumping two locations (2n and 2n + 1), therefore the decimation takes place. Renaud et al. (2002) define the details or wavelet transform, in this case just as the signal difference between two consecutive resolutions,

$$
T_{m,n} = S_{m-1,n} - S_{m,n} \tag{9.7}
$$

Alternatively, the wavelet function can be calculated with this relation:

$$
\frac{1}{2}\psi\left(\frac{t}{2}\right) = \varphi(t) - \frac{1}{2}\varphi\left(\frac{t}{2}\right). \tag{9.8}
$$

After the convolution is done $T_{m,n}=\langle x(t),\psi((t-b)/a)\rangle$, the same results are expected. Due to the link between decompositions at different levels, the multiresolution algorithm can be constructed as

$$
S_{0,n}=S_{M,n}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}T_{m,n}.
$$

## 9.2.5 Artificial Neural Networks

The artificial neural network is a well-established method. It had its beginnings with the threshold logic model (McCulloch & Pitts, 1943) and the model paved the way for the now known ANN. It was developed in the field of machine learning, quickly accepted, and established in applications where it is not possible to have a physically based model. ANNs are generally presented as systems of interconnected "neurons" that learn from experience as humans do. Zijderveld (2003) presents comprehensive documentation that readers not so familiar with ANN can review to delve into details. The mentioned author explains neural networks as a directed graph consisting of nodes with synaptic links and interconnection activation characterized by four properties:

1. Each neuron is represented by a set of linear synaptic links and externally applied bias.
2. The synaptic links of a neuron weight their respective input signals.
3. The weighted sum of the input signals defines the induced local field of the neuron in question.
4. The activation function squashes the induced local field of a neuron to produce an output.

The components of neural networks are weighting factors, summation, transfer, scaling, output, error, and learning functions. Once the network is ready and prepared for a particular task, the following step is the learning process. In this process, there are two approaches (supervised and unsupervised). In the first (of our interest) both the input series and the output series are presented to the model and the learning process is carried out by comparison. Optimization is carried out by taking the objective function as the difference between the modeled and the observed value. In this process, the back-propagation algorithm is in charge of adjusting the weights between nodes to find the best approximation for the objective function.

## 9.2.6 Other Data-Driven Models: Least Median Square, Linear Regressions, Pace Regression, and Model Tree

The same idea will be tested with other data-driven models, some of which offer advantages over an ANN modeling tool. One drawback of ANNs is that no additional information can be extracted due to the (black box) nature of the model. Also, if a simpler model can do the job, there is no need for a complex one. The present study does not seek to give an exhaustive analysis of wavelets with other DDMs, but to show that the same procedure could also be valid with other modeling techniques. Models used in this work are (1) LeastMedSq: Robust regression using the median rather than the mean; (2) LinearRegression: Standard linear regression; (3) PaceRegression: Linear regression models using Pace regression; and (4) M5P: Model tree. Readers can turn to Witten and Frank (2006) for details on the methods cited above; Weka software was used to develop the models in this section (http://www.cs.waikato.ac.nz/ml/weka/).

## 9.3 Methodology

### 9.3.1 Sequence of Experiments

The number of experiments was large; they are captured in the following sequence:

- Input variable selection (IVS)
  - Model-based: Forward selection
    - model1 = P(t), Q(t)
    - model2 = P(t), P(t-1), Q(t)
    - model3 = P(t), P(t-1), P(t-2), Q(t)
    - model4 = P(t), P(t-1), P(t-2), P(t-3), Q(t)
  - Model-free: Correlation and average mutual information
    - model5 = model2 = P(t), P(t-1), Q(t)
- Data splitting of best IVS (model2)
  - K-fold cross-validation; k = 10
  - Cross-validation (first 85% of the data), with the last 15% used to test the model
- Develop both models: ANN & WANN
  - For the ANN approach (model1a(1-20)-model4a(1-20)), in total 80 (20 × 4) models
    - Original time series is being used
    - An exhaustive search for the number of hidden nodes [1, 20]
  - For the WANN approach (model1b(1-220)-model4b(1-220)), in total 880 (220 × 4) models
    - Perform a-trous wavelet transform to the original time series
    - An exhaustive search for the number of hidden nodes [1, 20] and number of decompositions [1, 11]
- Evaluate models performance (model1a-model4a and model1b-model4b)
- Error metrics RMSE, Cor, NSE, PERS, and others
- Select the best fold for the ANN and the WANN model among the model1, model2, model3, and model4 models
- Once the best model is selected, de-noising by threshold is applied to the best WANN model, via optimization
  - Individual threshold values were found for all the detail coefficients via optimization, the objective function was set to the minimum RMSE

### 9.3.2 Input Variable Selection (IVS)

Forward selection, correlation, and average mutual information were implemented to select the most appropriate variables. The first consists of a linear increment search strategy in which variables are incremented one at a time until performance does not increase. The method is considered slow because the execution of a model is required. The model-free approach takes some dependency measures (linear and nonlinear between input and target) to find the right variables, attractive in terms of computational efforts because no model needs to be run. Model 2 (equivalent to model 5 suggested by correlation and AMI) described in section 9.3.1 was chosen, since it shows the best performance values. For the pragmatic purposes of the present study, the authors do not elaborate on the detailed development of this process, since the particular conditions of the case analyzed (rapid reaction basin and daily data) generate a reduced space for the selection of variables, and this prevents an objective assessment of the strengths or weaknesses of the IVS methods analyzed. For more details on the methods available for the selection of input variables, it is suggested to refer to May et al. (2011).
