
already in use by Huai River commission, is able to predict flow condition (hydrograph) at Lutaizi station using the Muskingum method from an upstream station called Hengpaitou station. The propagation time from Hengpaitou to Lutaizi is 36 hr (Pan, 2002), which can be considered as lead time for real-time operation in this study. As described in Jonoski et al. (2018), the optimization of the storage area gate operation is dependent on the simulated water level results, which are obtained from the simulation model with input of nearly 3 months discharge hydrograph (Fig. 14.2). Therefore, implementation of the optimization process with incoming real-time data is not adopted, and the use of optimal strategies obtained from preanalysis described previously (using historical data) is used in this study.

Using the database of optimized strategies obtained from the sampled hydrographs (already developed with ROPAR), the decision to be implemented is made according to the most similar hydrograph in that database to the expected real hydrograph, which is called *similarity selection*. As described previously, having forecasts about the whole hydrograph is practically impossible, given its very long duration, so the similarity assessment is restricted to the area of the hydrograph around the peaks. A threshold value of $Q_0$, 5,500 m³/s, is adopted, and only discharges above this value are considered in the similarity assessment. The proposed criteria for estimating this similarity are as follows:

- 1. The first criterion compares the volume of flood peaks where discharge is larger than 5,500 m³/s ($Q > Q_0$). This method was proposed because the volume of the upper part of a hydrograph is one of the most critical elements influencing flood severity downstream. If part of this volume is allocated to the storage areas, the flood peak(s) downstream (at Bengbu City) will be reduced. The criterion of selecting the most similar hydrograph is

$$\min FV = \left|V^R_{Q>Q_0} - V^S_{Q>Q_0}\right|,$$  
(14.14)

where, $FV$ is the notion of the flood volume method; $V_{Q>Q_0}$ is the flood volume of discharge larger than $Q_0$; $Q_0$ is a threshold value (5,500 m³/s); the superscripts $R$ and $S$ mean real hydrograph and sampled hydrograph, respectively.

- 2. The second criterion uses Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency coefficient (NSE), which gives an indicator about closeness between two hydrographs. Larger NSE means better match. Focusing again only on the upper part of the hydrograph, the equation used for similarity assessment is

$$\max NSE = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t=t_1}^{t_2}\left(Q^S_{t,Q>Q_0} - Q^R_{t,Q>Q_0}\right)^2}{\sum_{t=t_1}^{t_2}\left(Q^R_{t,Q>Q_0} - \overline{Q^R_{Q>Q_0}}\right)^2},$$  
(14.15)

where, *NSE* is Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency coefficient; $Q_t$ is the discharge at time $t$; $\overline{Q_{Q>Q_0}^R}$ is the average value of discharge larger than $Q_0$ of a real hydrograph; $t_1$ and $t_2$ are the start and ending time for discharge larger than $Q_0$, respectively; other terms are the same as in equation (14.13).

- 3. The third criterion also uses one indicator for similarity, which is the weighted root mean square error (wRMSE). A weight is added in each time step, decided by the proportion of discharge at time $t$ to the sum of discharges across all considered time steps, which is a way to introduce higher importance (weight) to higher flow discharges. The equation used, which also focuses on upper part of hydrograph, is

$$\min wRMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{t_2 - t_1}\sum_{t=t_1}^{t_2} w_t \left(Q_t^S - Q_{t,Q>Q_0}^R\right)^2}, \tag{14.16}$$

$$w_t = \frac{Q_{t,Q>Q_0}^R}{\sum_{t=t_1}^{t_2} Q_{t,Q>Q_0}^R}, \tag{14.17}$$

where, $wRMSE$ is weighted root mean square error; $w_t$ is weight at time $t$; other terms are same as equation (14.14).

- 4. The last criterion considers the factors affecting downstream risk and storage area damage. According to the two objective functions (see section 14.2.1.2), the downstream risk is affected by the flood duration (the time when the stage is larger than warning level) and peak value (difference between maximum stage and warning level) while the storage area damage is calculated with the water levels inside, which are largely influenced by peaks of hydrographs. Similarly, the last criterion is to compare the duration and flow peaks of two hydrographs. The threshold discharge is same as previously defined. Each term is normalized, using following equation:

$$\min PD = nor(PD_1) + nor(PD_2) + nor(PD_3) \tag{14.18}$$

$$nor(PD_i) = \frac{PD_i^j - \min\left(PD_i^j\right)}{\max\left(PD_i^j\right) - \min\left(PD_i^j\right)}, \tag{14.19}$$

$$PD_1^j(Q^R, Q^{S,j}) = \left|Q_{p1}^R - Q_{p1}^{S,j}\right|, \tag{14.20}$$

$$PD_2^j(Q^R, Q^{S,j}) = \left|Q_{p2}^R - Q_{p2}^{S,j}\right|, \tag{14.21}$$

$$PD_3^j(Q^R, Q^{S,j}) = \left| D\left(Q_{Q>Q_0}^R\right) - D\left(Q_{Q>Q_0}^{S,j}\right) \right|, \tag{14.22}$$

where, *PD* is the notion of the peak and duration method; *nor* is the function of normalization; *D* is duration when discharge is larger than \(Q_0\); \(Q_{p1}\) and \(Q_{p2}\) are first and second peak, respectively; \(j\) means the \(j^{th}\) sampled hydrograph; \(i\) means the \(i^{th}\) term in *PD* (\(i = 1, 2, 3\)); other symbols are same as equation (14.14).

With the four criteria presented above some similar hydrographs would be selected, for which the corresponding optimized strategies need to be identified for real-time operation. As for each inflow hydrograph, there are a number of different strategies (represented in the Pareto front), a certain level of the downstream risk needs to be first chosen, and the corresponding strategy is selected as final for implementation.

It needs to be noted that, even though by similarity selection the time-consuming process of optimization is eliminated (as mentioned above), the duration of discharge larger than 5,500 m³/s is still very long (around 20 days), much more than the 36 hr available lead time. Therefore, to generate knowledge about the real hydrograph top (to be used in the similarity assessment), additional work would be required, for example, using information about precipitation in the upstream part of the catchment, and associated rainfall-runoff modeling. The methodology presented here is based on the assumption that there is enough time to perform such analysis.

### 14.3.2. Robust Strategies Resulting From ROPAR

For three chosen downstream risk values 10, 11, and 12, the corresponding probability distribution functions (PDFs) of storage area damage were found (Fig. 14.10). It can be seen that when downstream risk is higher, there is larger likelihood of small storage area damage. That means that when making decisions to flood storage areas, it should be considered how large is the expected risk for the downstream city of Bengbu.

As described earlier when introducing the ROPAR method, in order to identify robust optimal strategies, the strategies corresponding to a certain value of downstream risk were simulated with each possible upstream inflow, and the criteria of selection were calculated. One example result is depicted in Figure 14.11, for the case of downstream risk 10. The solution with minimum value of average damage (expected value) is marked with × while the solution with minimum maximum damage (worst case) is marked with +. It is observed that a strategy leading to small expected value can often be the same as the one with small worst case, forming an asterisk mark, in which case the robust optimal strategy is often identified as being the same regardless of which criterion is used.
