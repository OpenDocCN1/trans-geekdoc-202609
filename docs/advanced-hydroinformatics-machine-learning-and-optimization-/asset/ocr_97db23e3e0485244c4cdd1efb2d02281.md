(2008), we partitioned the FDC into three different segments (1) high-flow segment (0–0.02 flow exceedance probabilities) characterizing watershed response to significant precipitation events; (2) midflow segment (0.2–0.7 flow exceedance probabilities) that specifies by flows from moderate size precipitation events and also related to the intermediate-term primary and secondary base flow relaxation response of the watershed system; and (3) low-flow segment (0.7–1.0 flow exceedance probabilities), related to the long-term sustainability of flow and controlled by the interaction of baseflow with riparian evapotranspiration (ET) during extended dry periods. A steep slope in the midsegment FDC reflects a watershed having a quick or flashy response (due to small soil storage capacity and hence larger percentage of overland flow) while flatter midsegment slopes are associated with slower and more sustained watershed response to precipitation events.

# 5.2.2. Study Region and Data

The study region is the Northeast Cape Fear River Basin (hereafter NE-CFB), North Carolina, USA (HUC 03030007), which drains 1,714.70 mi² (4,441.1 km²) of the Cape Fear River Basin (CFRB). The CFRB is one of the four river basins entirely contained within the North Carolina border. The river is formed by the Deep, Rocky, and Haw rivers, which converge in Chatham County just below the B. Everett Jordan Dam. The river ends in 32,000 acres of the estuary near Southport. The CFRB is the largest river in North Carolina, encompassing a 9,000 mi² basin that includes streams flowing within 29 of the state’s 100 counties. With about 27% of the state’s population and dozens of municipalities situated within its boundaries (including Greensboro, Burlington, Chapel Hill, Sanford, Fayetteville, Dunn, Clinton, Warsaw, Burgaw, and Wilmington), the CFRB is one of the most industrialized regions in North Carolina. These and other cities and towns rely on the river and its tributaries for freshwater, transportation, recreation, natural habitats, and other uses. The CFRB flows into the Atlantic Ocean near Cape Fear, from which it takes its name.
