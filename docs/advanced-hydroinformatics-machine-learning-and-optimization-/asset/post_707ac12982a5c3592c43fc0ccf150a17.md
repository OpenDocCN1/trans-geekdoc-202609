(2017) showed that the Strupczewski method (Strupczewski, 1964) has better suitability to both gauged and ungauged stations compared with UPO ERR Gamma function, which is also a widely used method (Baziak & Gądek, 2019). However, these two functions have complex structures. Each of them has five parameters, some of which are difficult to determine. Triangular functions were also studied in the areas that have undetermined hydrographs (Gama, 2012). The disadvantages of this approach are the limited graphs shapes and bad representation of real hydrographs, even though their parameters are much fewer and more comprehensible. On the contrary, Gamma-like function with four parameters shows good shape and is able to generate different peak values, time to peak, skewness, and base flow when sampling. The function is

```
$Q_t = Q_b + (Q_p - Q_b) \times \left(\frac{t}{T_p}\right)^{\frac{1}{S_k - 1}} \times \exp\left(\frac{1 - \left(\frac{t}{T_p}\right)}{S_k - 1}\right), \quad (14.6)$
```

where, $Q_t$ is calculated discharge at time $t$; $t$ is time (counting from 0 with unit of time step); $Q_b$ is base flow; $Q_p$ is peak discharge; $T_p$ is time to peak (the interval counting from the beginning of hydrograph to peak time); $S_k$ is the factor of skewness. However, one Gamma-like function can have only one peak, which for many flood events is not realistic. The available data of the hydrograph from 2007 (Fig. 14.1) indicates two possible subsequent flood peaks. Therefore, an alternative method was proposed, to assign different values to parameters for different periods, selected in such a way as to generate two peaks formed within one hydrograph (Fig. 14.7). In that approach, each parameter (base flow discharge $Q_b$, peak value $Q_p$, time to peak $T_p$, and skewness $S_k$) is sampled twice, and therefore there are eight parameters in total for one hydrograph: $Q_{b1}$, $Q_{b2}$, $Q_{p1}$, $Q_{p2}$, $T_{p1}$, $T_{p2}$, $S_{k1}$, and $S_{k2}$.

Another critical element for sampling is the selected probability distribution of parameters, which influences the diversity of sampled results. The aim here is that the generated hydrographs are diverse as much as possible, but still have sufficient resemblance to the real hydrographs. In other words, sampled hydrographs should cover the conditions that may happen in future, which in fact represents the uncertainty. Following these requirements, the base flow and time to peak were designed to be concentrated on a specific value (using normal distribution), while the peak discharge

The sampling method used in this study is Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) (Swiler & Wyss, 2004), which is able to sample parameters more diversely with the same quantity of sampling numbers compared with some frequently used methods such as Monte Carlo Sampling (Kapelan et al., 2005; Kang et al., 2009; Jung et al., 2013). Because each experiment of multi-objective optimization with one hydrograph would take nearly 1 day, considering time saving, the number of samples needs to be relatively small but still large enough to cover the uncertainty spectrum. In this study, it was set at 100 samples.

After the LHS, an additional operation was performed in which the flood volume of each hydrograph was modified to a value that is approximately the same as the available real hydrographs (from 2007). This was done by changing the value of the skewness parameter. This operation was introduced mainly because in the analysis the same flood volume needs to be distributed over the four storage areas and the main river. Too varying patterns (including variation of flood volume) result in some unrealistic hydrograph shapes and introduce difficulties in analyzing the effect of the synthetic hydrograph parameters to results (Zhou, 2019). That means the uncertainty studied in this research is restricted to the condition that the flood volume of all sampled hydrographs during the considered period is the same (a restriction that could be addressed in future research).

These 100 sampled hydrographs were used to develop robust strategies and in the similarity selection method for real-time operation, as will be elaborated in the subsequent sections. However, the performance assessment of the so produced strategies in different conditions needs to be carried out with hydrographs that are different from sampled ones and with different characteristics of severity. For this purpose, the Homogeneous

Multiple Enlargement method was used, which is frequently used for generating design hydrographs (Yue et al., 2002; MWR, 2006; Xiao et al., 2009; Tan et al., 2017; Yin et al., 2018; Rizwan et al., 2019). In this method, a typical hydrograph needs to be selected first, whose discharge would be amplified with a multiplier, which is calculated as the ratio of flood volume or peak discharge for a particular return period to that of the typical hydrograph. Usually, the volume ratio is used for storage areas. Considering that the use of the four storage areas in this study may last for a month, the volume is chosen as the sum of successive 30 days’ largest discharge, so that the formula of the multiplier is

$$K = \frac{V_{30,R}}{V_{30,T}}\tag{14.11}$$

where, $K$ is the multiplier; $V_{30,R}$ and $V_{30,T}$ are the 30 days’ volume for a particular return period and a typical year, respectively.

For the sake of data availability, the typical hydrograph is chosen as the 2007s. The generated hydrographs have the same shape as 2007 but with different flood volume, depending on the return period. Two most severe records were found in Chinese history, the years 1954 and 2003, with 30 and 60 return years, respectively (see Table 14.1). Their flood volumes are used for calculating the multipliers. It should be noted that with this approach the optimal strategies will be tested with very different hydrographs compared with the ones produced with the sampling. These multiplied hydrographs are presented in section 14.3.1.

In this study, the method was used to develop robust optimal strategies of storage area operation under a specific downstream risk level. It was carried out following these steps: under the inflow uncertainty quantified as described in section 14.2.5, optimal strategies for each possible hydrograph are first identified by providing the hydrographs to the simulation-optimization model and performing the optimization. This step results in 100 Pareto fronts, with two dimensions of minimum downstream risk and minimum storage area damage, corresponding to 100 inflows. When assembling all these fronts in one coordination system, a scatter diagram would be formed, in which each point contains information of optimal strategy (gate operations) and the corresponding hydrograph; then a cluster of points would be picked by a certain criterion, which in this study is that they have same downstream risk. The risk could be a spectrum (e.g., with a span of 1), and the points included in mean potential robust strategies; a probability density function (PDF) can be built by collecting the storage area damage of those points, meaning the probability of storage area inundation loss under the chosen risk level; in order to find out the most robust one among these optimal strategies, each of these potential optimal strategies is simulated with 100 sampled hydrographs. Two criteria are calculated for electing optimal ones. They are (1) worst case and (2) expected value. The functions are

$$E(X_i)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Obj_2(X_i,HG_j), \tag{14.12}$$

$$W(X_i)=\max[Obj_2(X_i,HG_j)], \tag{14.13}$$

where, $E$ is expected value; $W$ is worst case; $X_i$ is the $i^{th}$ solution; $HG_j$ is the $j^{th}$ hydrograph; $Obj_2$ is the value of storage area damage index (the objective 2); $n$ is sampling number ($n = 100$).

It can be understood that the worst case selection criterion refers to maximum damage from the 100 simulated results, and the expected value selection criterion is average damage from the same results. Therefore, each optimal strategy has two criteria to estimate its robustness. The selection of most robust strategy is done by choosing the one with either minimum worst case or minimum expected value. In many cases, the same robust strategy is chosen by two different criteria, which means the strategy can lead to minimum damage under different flood conditions no matter if it is calculated by average or maximum value.
