## 7.6. Results and Discussion

### 7.6.1. Performance of All Data-Driven Models

In comparing all data-driven models (TDNN, LSTM, linear regression and naive), the ML models generally outperformed the simpler models (linear regression and naive) (Fig. 7.6). The TDNN model performed the best in predicting water level at each lead time investigated for station BCA11. This observation is the same for station NP201 with the exception of the 10 day water-level prediction, where the naive model showed better performance than the TDNN model. Similar to station BCA11, the TDNN model for station P33 outperforms the other models at each forecast lead time. The charts of the remaining two stations show similar characteristics. The performance of the TDNN model on the short-term forecasts was similar to or better than the naive model, while it also performed better on the medium-range forecasts. For each lead time prediction for each station, the linear regression model was outperformed by both the TDNN model and the naive model, while the TDNN model generally performed better overall. The LSTM model outperformed the linear regression model in predicting the medium-range forecast horizons for each station with the exception of S12D_T. For stations BCA11 and P33, the LSTM model also performs better than the naive model. The results of the medium-range predictions of the LSTM model, and those of the TDNN model in particular, however, indicate that ML is useful in this study.

### 7.6.2. Short-Term Forecasts

Station NP201 was used as an example to present the results of the water-level forecasts. The TDNN model accurately predicts the water level 1 day ahead, over the 3 yr sequential test data, with an RMSE of 0.017 m (average water level = 1.8 m) and an R² of 0.997. The peaks and continuous changes in water level are well predicted in the first 200 days of the 3 yr prediction, particularly the peaks labeled 1 and 2 (Fig. 7.7c). Up to 3 days lead time, these peaks are well-predicted temporally as well as in magnitude, and can be linked to accurate forecasted rainfall at these points (Fig. 7.7a). Peak 3 is not as accurately predicted as peaks 1 and 2, resulting from small differences in the forecasted rainfall, particularly for the 2 day prediction. This error in the forecasted rainfall leads to a small lag, which is then propagated to the 3 day prediction. These observations show the influence of the rainfall predictors, but more important, how improved rainfall forecasts could result in more accurate predictions.

RMSE for the overall prediction for the 2 day and 3 day lead times were 0.027 m and 0.037 m, respectively, with an R² greater than 0.98 in both cases. There is some lag in the prediction to the end of the graph (Fig. 7.7c) resulting in differences between the measured water level and predictions to the magnitude of 0.25 m and 0.20 m in the case of the 3 day prediction at days 143 and 149. This error can be accounted for when considering the error in the forecasted rainfall used. Additionally, the small prediction error in the 1 day forecast would increase the error of the 2 day prediction, as well as the influence of the highly autoregressive water-level inputs, which are the main predictors in these forecasts.

At day 505, a rainfall peak of 37.8 mm resulted in a peak in the water-level prediction, which was not observed in the measured data (Fig. 7.7d). The influence of peaks in rainfall on the water-level prediction appears to be greater when the water level is indicative of a dry season (lowest values in Fig. 7.7c and d) than when the water level is higher during the wet season (indicated by the three highest periods in Fig. 7.7b). This observation also suggests that the observed rainfall was absorbed by the natural system, resulting in no major increase in the measured water level. There are a number of mechanisms by which the system can receive rainfall and not experience water-level increases. The water-control structures are designed to mitigate water levels, and drainage to the coast can vary seasonally. The major trough in the 3 yr prediction, magnified in Figure 7.7d, indicates a particularly dry period as confirmed by the 2009 South Florida Weather Year in Review report (National Weather Forecast Office, 2009) issued by the weather forecast office of the National Weather Service along with the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). The report indicated that the winter (January–April) of 2009 was the driest dry season on record up to that date.

In the 3 day water-level prediction, the water level is overpredicted on days 503 and 504 by magnitudes of 0.45 m and 0.38 m. This overprediction could be a result of rainfall forecasts for day 502 and 503 of 30.2 mm and 27.8 mm, respectively, causing two successive peaks. Note that in the dry season of 2008 (approximately days 0–130) when there is not much rainfall, the system reacts more to rainfall occurrences and the water level changes somewhat proportionally. During the wet season, this is not the case (Fig. 7.7b). This greater influence of rainfall inputs on the water level during the dry season is likely to have been the case in the training data as well, which would explain why the model may tend to predict a water-level peak where there was no measured peak (Fig. 7.7d). Given that longer-term antecedent conditions affect the response to rainfall, water levels representative of the dry season of 2009 were the lowest values presented to the model, and the characteristics of the reaction of the system during the dry season described prior could be the reason for the overprediction. Apart from this outlier in the predictions, each forecast of the 2009 dry season was good, particularly in capturing the increase in water level entering into the wet season from days 514 to 519 (Fig. 7.7d).

### 7.6.3. Medium-Range Forecasts

As expected with longer forecast horizons, the accuracy is notably reduced in the 7 day and 10 day water-level predictions. Similar to the 2 day and 3 day forecasts, peaks in medium-range predicted water level can be linked to peaks in the rainfall forecast. A combination of the error in the rainfall forecast used to predict both forecast horizons, as well as the propagation of error from the prediction at each lead time, could contribute to the larger error at these lead times. The two medium-range forecasts had RMSE values of 0.069 m and 0.094 m, and R² of 0.94 and 0.89, respectively. The general pattern of the measured data is still preserved in the forecasts despite some overprediction of the two latter peaks (Fig. 7.9b).

Even at such a long lead time, some peaks are still well predicted due to reasonably accurate rainfall forecast points. For the 7 day water-level prediction, peak 1 is delayed in comparison to the measured data. Table 7.3 indicates there is no forecasted rainfall to drive the prediction of the peak on time and as a result, it arrives late. Peak 2 occurred a day late, which coincides with a peak in forecasted rainfall being forecasted a day late. Peak 3 was well predicted due to accurate timing and comparable magnitude of the forecasted rainfall to the measured rainfall (Table 7.3). From day 139 to 200, there is significant lag, which causes point errors as large as 0.35 m at around day 143 (Fig. 7.9c). There is very little forecasted rainfall at the point where the measured water level increased and resulted in the model not predicting the increase at that point. This error would have grown from the 2 day forecast horizon, which did not have a comparable forecasted rainfall peak in the measured data. With each lead, the consequent error in the water-level prediction would have grown as a result of prediction errors due to inaccurate rainfall forecasts, and the inherent prediction errors with larger lead time forecasts.

The error of the outlier peak predicted in the dry season of 2009 propagated through each time step, resulting in point prediction errors of as high as 0.70 m at the point where the largest peak in predicted water level occurs, just before the measured water level begins to increase (7 day water-level forecast in Fig. 7.9d). There are two rainfall peaks, which result in the two predicted peaks observed. However, this error started from the 1 day prediction and is not solely due to the rainfall forecasts.
