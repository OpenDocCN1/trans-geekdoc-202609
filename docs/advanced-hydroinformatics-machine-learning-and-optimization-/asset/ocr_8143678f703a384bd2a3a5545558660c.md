The EDEN-generated values are then used by water resource scientists and managers in conducting their research assessing ecological responses to CERP (Telis et al., 2015; Conrads et al., 2014) and other restoration and management efforts. One of the important aspects of water depth, and, by extension water level as it pertains to the Everglades, is the close relationship between the timing, spatial distribution, and volume of water, and the population health of a number of species that are used as indicators of wetland ecosystem health (Brandt et al., 2016). Examples of such species are alligators (Fujisaki et al., 2009) and several wading birds (Frederick et al., 2009; Lantz et al., 2010), which are directly or indirectly affected by water levels in the wetland, whether it be through prey availability, foraging habits, or nesting habitat (Forde, 2020). The importance of water levels in the Everglades are also highlighted in Telis (2006) and SFWMD (2021).

Machine Learning is now widely applied in many aspects of modeling, in which computer models learn patterns and relations within large data sets, and are able to produce generalizable evaluations of these data sets, gaining insight, knowledge, and predictive capacity through the identification, utilization, and testing of various computational approaches (Alpaydin, 2009). These types of models, also known as data-driven models, are able to solve complex problems in systems that are challenging to describe numerically. Machine Learning has been used to solve various problems in hydrology and hydraulics, see for example, Solomatine and Dulal (2003), Bhattacharya and Solomatine (2005), Kasiviswanathan et al. (2016), Xiang et al. (2020), Kao et al. (2020), and Moreido et al. (2021). A historical overview of Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) for hydrological forecasting is provided by Abarhart et al. (2012). Additionally, there are multiple examples of using ML for water-level forecasting (e.g., Zhu et al., 2020). Cruz et al. (2018) successfully used a multilayer ANN (Aggarwal, 2018) in predicting water level using real-time rainfall, water level, and soil moisture data. Amaranto et al. (2018) demonstrated the use of ANN (multilayer perceptron) for groundwater-level forecasting. Another study by Ma et al. (2019) showed promising results using a model based on LSTM networks to investigate whether the accuracy of water-level forecasting could be improved in the Rhine River Basin in Germany. A LSTM network, one form of Recurrent Neural Networks, is a deep-learning architecture that includes memory cells that store information over long periods of time and is known to be the optimal contemporary network architecture for predicting sequences such as in time-series, speech recognition, text prediction, and others (Kratzert et al., 2018). Unlike traditional approaches for time-series forecasting like Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA), LSTM has the ability to adapt to features of the data such as seasonality (Siami-Namini, 2018).
