# *SAC-SMA Model*

The SAC-SMA model is developed by the U.S. National Weather Service (NWS) as a conceptual hydrologic simulation model. The tool utilizes a moisture accounting system to represent the catchment storage capacity. The SAC-SMA divides the basin into lower and upper soil zones at different depths, and defines the distribution of moisture, that is, tension water components (driven by evapotranspiration and diffusion) and free water components (driven by gravitational forces) in each of these two zones via a set of parameters (Burnash, 1995). The model uses climatic variables, such as precipitation and temperature, along with parameters on soil moisture states and the basin’s relative permeability, to estimate the amount of water that enters, is stored in, and leaves the basin. Thus, the model computes several key hydrologic processes, such as evapotranspiration, percolation, interflow, and different forms of runoff from a basin. These are (1) direct runoff from permanent and temporary impervious areas, (2) surface runoff due to precipitation occurring at a rate faster than percolation and interflow can take place when both upper zone storages are full, (3) interflow resulting from the lateral drainage of a temporary free water storage, (4) supplemental base flow, and (5) primary base flow (Burnash, 1995); although, proper application of the SAC-SMA requires interpretations of the primary rainfall-runoff mechanisms (precipitation, evapotranspiration, and soil moisture) in a physically reasonable manner. A distortion in any of these characteristics may produce a compensating distortion in another element. Such distortion may appear to produce an effective streamflow simulation during the fitting period, but can reduce the model performance, particularly during high and low flow events.

Several performance metrics were used in this research for model benchmarking such as Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE; Nash & Sutcliffe, 1970), the Kling-Gupta efficiency (KGE; Gupta et al. 2009), and the Box-Cox transformed root-mean-square error (TRMSE; Box & Cox, 1964).
