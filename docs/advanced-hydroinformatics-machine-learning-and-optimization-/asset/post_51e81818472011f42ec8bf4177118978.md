## 7.3. Workflow

### 7.3.1. Data Acquisition and Processing

The data used were rainfall and water level from USGS EDEN (https://sofia.usgs.gov/eden/eve/index.php), discharge from SFWMD DBHYDRO database (https://www.sfwmd.gov/science-data/dbhydro), and rainfall forecasts from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) (https://apps.ecmwf.int/datasets). Data were subjected to extensive quality assurance measures by the agencies responsible for collection. The discharge data set was the only data set with missing values, which were gap-filled using two additional time series of discharge collected by the SFWMD and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. The USGS time series for discharge was the most comprehensive and was selected for use by ML models, but it also contained some missing values. To gap-fill USGS discharge, values from the other two time series were used to fill missing values of the USGS time series. Because each discharge time series represents data from the same location, values were similar and in some cases identical, confirming that this substitution method was adequate.

Once the rainfall, water-level, and discharge data were continuous with no missing values, each time series was graphed to observe trends and seasonal patterns. Cross-correlation with rainfall and autocorrelation analyses were then performed for each station, in addition to cross-correlation with discharge analyses for the stations influenced by a structure, to determine the effect of structure discharge on water level. We determined that the correlation between past rainfall and future water level was not very high (Fig. 7.3a and Fig. 7.4a). Due to the random nature of rainfall together with the influence of control structure operation, the relationship between water level and rainfall is nonlinear and may not always be captured by a linear statistic such as correlation coefficient.

The high autocorrelation between past and future water levels was expected, because past water level largely influences future water level (Fig. 7.3b and Fig. 7.4b). The additional cross-correlation between past discharge and future water level for the stations influenced by a control structure showed a high correlation coefficient, suggesting a large influence of releases on water level at nearby stations (Fig. 7.4c). These results suggest that past and current water levels, as well as discharge (where applicable), are the dominant factors in predicting future water level. However, from the observations of rainfall and water-level peaks, rainfall inputs could prove useful in assisting the prediction of water level, particularly events.

An important step in any ML exercise is partitioning data into training, cross-validation, and testing subsets. There are various approaches for data partitioning, from simple methods like random sampling, to more complex methods aimed at optimization of the resulting models (e.g., Genetic Algorithm, Self Organizing Maps; Bowden et al., 2002). The most widely used and simplest method is random sampling, which was used in this study for the Time-Delayed Neural Network (TDNN) models. Each subset is filled with randomly selected samples from the overall data set, which avoids high bias in the performance of the model (Reitermanova, 2010). The data partitioning for the LSTM model is explained in a later section.

The NWP rainfall data were from the Interactive Grand Global Ensemble data set, and downloaded from the ECMWF (https://apps.ecmwf.int/datasets/). This download was performed using a Python script and Application Programming Interface (API), which allows for connecting to the ECMWF server and downloading requested data. The script specified the class of data preferred, the type of data set, the date required, grid size, the parameter(s) required, the time step(s) desired, and specific grid coordinates for selecting a desired region. The data were stored in gridded format and these files were processed using the software Panoply, a data viewing tool developed by National Aeronautics and Space Administration, which allows for the visualization of Network Common Data Form (NetCDF), General Regularly Distributed Information in Binary form (GRIB), and other data sets.

For building ML models, Google’s TensorFlow (Abadi et al., 2016a; Abadi et al., 2016b) was used. It is used widely for its deep learning capabilities, and the high-level API Keras (Chollet, 2015) makes building and evaluating ML models easy. The ANN was the Machine Learning technique used, and two model architectures were developed: a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) TDNN (Luk et al., 2001) as well as a LSTM (Sherstinsky, 2020) network. Both architectures were designed with a single hidden layer (see experimental setup for number of nodes), and to generate a single output, and one of each architecture was developed for each location under study. The performance indicators used to assess the models were Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Coefficient of Determination (R²). Python was employed as the main programming language.

## 7.4. Experimental Setup

### 7.4.1. Selecting Model Inputs and Data Preparation

Based on the correlation analyses and the usefulness of rainfall as one of the predictors, a number of trials were conducted to determine the most suitable combination of predictors and their lags. Models were set up to initially run using the inputs of rainfall (referred to as R) ([R_{t-1}, R_t]), water level (referred to as WL) (combinations of [WL_{t-1}, WL_t], [WL_{t-2}, WL_{t-1}, WL_t], and [WL_{t-3}, WL_{t-2}, WL_{t-1}, WL_t]), and discharge (referred to as FLOW), where applicable (combinations of [FLOW_t], and [FLOW_{t-1}, FLOW_t]. The dependent variable was [WL_{t+1}]. Similar to the correlation analyses, the tests using the WL inputs provided the best results and were used as a basis for further experiments. For the stations not influenced by a water-control structure, the rainfall inputs were added to the combinations of WL inputs initially tested, resulting in the combinations [R_{t-1}, R_t, WL_{t-1}, WL_t], [R_{t-1}, R_t, WL_{t-2}, WL_{t-1}, WL_t], and [R_{t-1}, R_t, WL_{t-3}, WL_{t-2}, WL_{t-1}, WL_t]. The test for the stations not influenced by a water-control structure were conducted first, and then the combination that provided the best model performance was used for tests for the stations influenced by a water-control structure, adding the additional input(s) of discharge. The combinations tested here were [R_{t-1}, R_t, WL_{t-2}, WL_{t-1}, WL_t, FLOW_t] and [R_{t-1}, R_t, WL_{t-2}, WL_{t-1}, WL_t, FLOW_{t-1}, FLOW_t]. Table 7.1 shows the improvement of model performance at the majority of lead times with the addition of the rainfall inputs. This improvement is observed in the RMSE values in Table 7.1. All tests were conducted using the TDNN model architecture.

Additional experiments were conducted with inputs, such as the 7 day moving average of rainfall and change in water level, but rendered no improvement in model performance and were not considered further. The final combination of inputs was decided based on the results of the tests. The models for the stations (one for each gauging station location) not directly impacted by a control structure were trained with the predictors [R_{t-1}, R_t, WL_{t-2}, WL_{t-1}, WL_t]. For those that were influenced by the discharge from a control structure [R_{t-1}, R_t, WL_{t-2}, WL_{t-1}, WL_t, FLOW_t] were the predictors used.
