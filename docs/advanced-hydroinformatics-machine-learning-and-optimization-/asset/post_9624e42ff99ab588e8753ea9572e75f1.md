## 7.5. Comparison of Measured Rainfall and NWP Rainfall Forecasts

The ECMWF rainfall forecasts were used as predictors for the models built in this study, and their accuracy (i.e., to what extent they can be assumed to represent the rainfall) is an important issue. Rainfall forecasts are prone to error due to the random nature of rainfall, particularly in the tropics where high temperatures as well as reduced influence of the Earth’s Coriolis forcing on atmospheric circulation at these latitudes, result in challenges in weather forecasting (Sobel, 2012). Despite the presence of error in daily forecasted rainfall amounts, seasonal averages provide an understanding of the comparability of the forecasted rainfall to the measured rainfall. The maps in Figure 7.5 show that the differences in average rainfall between the gauged measurements and the forecasts are not very significant. The dry season (approximately Nov–Apr) rainfall total (sum of all the totals) for the gauges used in this study was 3,883 mm over the dry periods of the 3 yr prediction period 2008–2010. The sum of the forecasted precipitation for the 4 ECMWF grid cells covering the general locations of the gauges (Fig. 7.5b) was 3,725 mm. For the wet season (approximately May–Oct), the sum of total rainfall from the gauges was 15,972 mm, while the sum for the four forecast grid cells was 14,316 mm (Fig. 7.5d).

In addition to the inherent error in rainfall forecasts, the forecasts are averaged over a ~50 × 50 km grid whereas a gauge records point rainfall. The effect of error in the daily rainfall amounts being used as model predictors will be analyzed in the water-level predictions, but it is expected that error will be greater as the forecast lead time increases.

### 7.5.1. Model Development

The architecture of the TDNN model is a multilayer perceptron feed forward network. The nodes in the input layer have the input features of rainfall and water level, but additionally has those same inputs delayed by one or more time steps. More detail on the TDNN can be found in Lingras et al. (2008). The LSTM model architecture is similar to a standard Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), but the difference is the presence of memory blocks in the LSTM allowing it to store long-term information and later access it. Within these memory blocks are multiplicative units called gates, which facilitate the storage and access of long-term information (Xiang et al. 2020). A further description of LSTM networks and memory cells can be found in Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) and Graves (2012).

For both the TDNN and the LSTM models, the nonlinear hyperbolic tangent was used as the activation function for the hidden layer, while the linear transfer function was used as the activation function for the output layer. The sigmoid function was used for the recurrent activation in the LSTM models. The number of hidden nodes for the TDNN was determined by trial and error based on the minimization of the cross-validation error, resulting in six hidden nodes. Trials were made using 6, 8, 16, and 32 nodes for the hidden layer of the LSTM models. The LSTM models for BCA11 and NP201 have 32 nodes in the hidden layer while those for the remaining stations have 8 nodes, based on the trials conducted. The Root Mean Squared Propagation (RMSProp) optimizer was used in this study, and the loss (error) function was the mean squared error. The optimizer is a gradient descent optimization algorithm, which divides the gradient by the moving average of the root mean squared gradient, first proposed and explained in an online course entitled "Neural Networks for Machine Learning" (Tieleman & Hinton, 2012), and is thought to be first used in Graves (2013). More recently, it was used in a study on stochastic optimization and was shown to be beneficial in MLP ANNs, although it is typically used in recurrent neural networks (Huk, 2020).

The LSTM was built similarly to the TDNN using the same number of nodes in the hidden layer, the same optimizer, and the same activation functions. The length of the historical sequence that the model was trained on was 10 days. Trials were made for sequence window lengths of 5, 7, 10, and 20 days.

Cross-validation was carried out using the Keras *EarlyStopping* “callback” (https://www.tensorflow.org/api_docs/python/tf/keras/callbacks/EarlyStopping) feature, which monitors the error (loss) on the cross-validation subset, and terminates training when the error no longer improves after a consecutive number of training loops or epochs. To ensure training did not stop before error was minimized, a “patience” of 100 epochs was specified. This ‘patience’ means that 100 consecutive epochs need to pass without the error of the cross-validation set improving, before training is terminated. The callback was set up to ensure that the weights that resulted in the lowest cross-validation error were restored after training is completed.

Once configured, each model could be trained and the total number of epochs specified for training was 500. After training was completed, the TDNN models were initially tested using the randomly selected test set, and then were further tested to predict the 3 yr sequential time series. The LSTM models were tested only on the 3 yr sequential time series. The simulation time step for the models is 24 hr. In order to forecast lead times of 7 and 10 days, forecasts had to be made for lead times 4–6 days and 8–9 days, respectively, in order to get the necessary WL inputs as indicated in Table 7.2.
