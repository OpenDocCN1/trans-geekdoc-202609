# 7.5. Comparison of Measured Rainfall and NWP Rainfall Forecasts

The ECMWF rainfall forecasts were used as predictors for the models built in this study, and their accuracy (i.e., to what extent they can be assumed to represent the rainfall) is an important issue. Rainfall forecasts are prone to error due to the random nature of rainfall, particularly in the tropics where high temperatures as well as reduced influence of the Earth’s Coriolis forcing on atmospheric circulation at these latitudes, result in challenges in weather forecasting (Sobel, 2012). Despite the presence of error in daily forecasted rainfall amounts, seasonal averages provide an understanding of the comparability of the forecasted rainfall to the measured rainfall. The maps in Figure 7.5 show that the differences in average rainfall between the gauged measurements and the forecasts are not very significant. The dry season (approximately Nov–Apr) rainfall total (sum of all the totals) for the gauges used in this study was 3,883 mm over the dry periods of the 3 yr prediction period 2008–2010. The sum of the forecasted precipitation for the 4 ECMWF grid cells covering the general locations of the gauges (Fig. 7.5b) was 3,725 mm. For the wet season (approximately May–Oct), the sum of total rainfall from the gauges was 15,972 mm, while the sum for the four forecast grid cells was 14,316 mm (Fig. 7.5d).

In addition to the inherent error in rainfall forecasts, the forecasts are averaged over a ~50 × 50 km grid whereas a gauge records point rainfall. The effect of error in the daily rainfall amounts being used as model predictors will be analyzed in the water-level predictions, but it is expected that error will be greater as the forecast lead time increases.

# 7.5.1. Model Development

The architecture of the TDNN model is a multilayer perceptron feed forward network. The nodes in the input layer have the input features of rainfall and water level, but additionally has those same inputs delayed by one or more time steps. More detail on the TDNN can be found in Lingras et al. (2008). The LSTM model architecture is similar to a standard Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), but the difference is the presence of memory blocks in the LSTM allowing it to store long-term information and later access it. Within these memory blocks are multiplicative units called gates, which facilitate the storage and access of long-term information (Xiang et al. 2020). A further description of LSTM networks and memory cells can be found in Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) and Graves (2012).
