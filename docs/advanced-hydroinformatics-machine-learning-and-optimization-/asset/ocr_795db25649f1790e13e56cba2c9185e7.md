Up to 3 days lead time, these peaks are well-predicted temporally as well as in magnitude, and can be linked to accurate forecasted rainfall at these points (Fig. 7.7a). Peak 3 is not as accurately predicted as peaks 1 and 2, resulting from small differences in the forecasted rainfall, particularly for the 2 day prediction. This error in the forecasted rainfall leads to a small lag, which is then propagated to the 3 day prediction. These observations show the influence of the rainfall predictors, but more important, how improved rainfall forecasts could result in more accurate predictions.

RMSE for the overall prediction for the 2 day and 3 day lead times were 0.027 m and 0.037 m, respectively, with an R² greater than 0.98 in both cases. There is some lag in the prediction to the end of the graph (Fig. 7.7c) resulting in differences between the measured water level and predictions to the magnitude of 0.25 m and 0.20 m in the case of the 3 day prediction at days 143 and 149. This error can be accounted for when considering the error in the forecasted rainfall used. Additionally, the small prediction error in the 1 day forecast would increase the error of the 2 day prediction, as well as the influence of the highly autoregressive water-level inputs, which are the main predictors in these forecasts.

At day 505, a rainfall peak of 37.8 mm resulted in a peak in the water-level prediction, which was not observed in the measured data (Fig. 7.7d). The influence of peaks in rainfall on the water-level prediction appears to be greater when the water level is indicative of a dry season (lowest values in Fig. 7.7c and d) than when the water level is higher during the wet season (indicated by the three highest periods in Fig. 7.7b). This observation also suggests that the observed rainfall was absorbed by the natural system, resulting in no major increase in the measured water level. There are a number of mechanisms by which the system can receive rainfall and not experience water-level increases. The water-control structures are designed to mitigate water levels, and drainage to the coast can vary seasonally. The major trough in the 3 yr prediction, magnified in Figure 7.7d, indicates a particularly dry period as confirmed by the 2009 South Florida Weather Year in Review report (National Weather Forecast Office, 2009) issued by the weather forecast office of the National Weather Service along with the National Oceanographic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). The report indicated that the winter (January–April) of 2009 was the driest dry season on record up to that date.
