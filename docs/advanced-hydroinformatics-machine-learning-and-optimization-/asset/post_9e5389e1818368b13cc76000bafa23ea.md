In this vein, several researchers revealed the need for taking advantage of multisource information within calibration, by means of both hard (i.e., measurements) and soft data as well as expert judgment, in order to improve model realism and parameter plausibility (e.g., Seibert & McDonnell, 2002; Efstratiadis & Koutsoyiannis, 2010; Nalbantis et al., 2011; Finger et al., 2015; Mohammed et al., 2021).

An evident drawback of the split-sample rationale is that the inferred parameter set is only conditioned on a subset of the available data, thus losing important information that may be embedded in the validation period. This has triggered researchers to provide hybrid calibration schemes across sliding windows (Coron et al., 2012, 2014; Vormoor et al., 2018) or subperiods (Gharari et al., 2013; Thirel et al., 2015a), in order to detect parameter sets that exhibit consistent performance in time. Following a rather heretical approach, Singh and Bárdossy (2012) and Arsenault et al. (2018) have manifested to entirely disregard the validation procedure under certain conditions, and use instead the full data set for parameter identifiability. To our knowledge, this approach is quite common among practitioners, who usually employ the split-sample test in order to obtain a rough picture of the model robustness and then recalibrate the model on the entire data set. Perrin et al. (2008) have also demonstrated that in poorly gauged basins, where the available flow records are very short, it is preferable to apply already optimized parameters from areas exhibiting satisfactory hydrological similarity instead of employing calibration with too limited information. On the other hand, Razavi and Tolson (2013) highlighted the issue of computational cost when calibrating time-demanding models against long data, thus proposing surrogate-based approaches to approximate the model performance on the full-length data period, based on much shorter ones.

Nevertheless, the question of how the model will perform if applied to conditions outside those used for model calibration, remains an open issue, which is also induced by the changing climate premise (Blöschl & Montanari, 2010; Kling et al., 2014; Broderick et al., 2016; Fowler et al., 2016, 2018; Dakhlaoui et al., 2017, 2019; Stephens et al., 2020). For this purpose, Klemes (1986) introduced the so-called Differential Split Sample Testing (DSST), in which the determination of calibration and validation subperiods is appropriately made to span over as much as dissimilar hydroclimatic data, which in turn allows for better evaluating model transferability across time. Yet, common experience with DSST studies indicated strong dependence of model parameters on the conditions dominating the calibration period, while it is rather questionable whether short historical data can provide sufficient analogues to represent the plausible range of highly uncertain projections for climate (and environmental, in a more general context) changes (e.g., Thirel et al., 2015b, and citations therein). Several studies found little improvement of the observed poor model performance under contrasting climate conditions, after employing longer calibration periods (e.g., Brigode et al., 2013; Coron et al., 2014; Duethmann et al., 2020). In this respect, the assumption of time-invariant parameters is disputed, and some researchers directly link parameter change with hydroclimatic variability (e.g., Merz et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2020).

The dilemma of which calibration approach is the more suitable may not make sense if long enough data were available, provided that the latter are really representative of the full regime of the modeled catchment. In order to address this issue, a well-known option is offered by stochastic models, which allow for synthesizing hydrological data to capture a wide range of plausible hydroclimatic conditions. Taking advantage of the long experience with stochastic simulation approaches in water resources planning and management studies, we reveal a novel field of application of stochastics for calibrating hydrological models. The overall idea is very simple and follows the well-known calibration-validation paradigm, where calibration is employed with synthetic data and validation against the actual (i.e., observed) ones, which are also used as guidance for recognizing the stochastic behavior of the processes of interest. In this chapter, we introduce the so-called stochastic calibration approach, which is founded on the coupling of two totally different modeling philosophies (i.e., mechanistic and stochastic), and reveal its significant advantages over conventional parameter estimation methods. Emphasis is paid to the specifications of the stochastic model, which is the most critical component of the overall procedure. The method is demonstrated by means of a proof of concept involving the fitting of conceptual model to a real-world river basin in Greece.

## 2.2. Methodology

Let $\underline{y} = \mathcal{M} (\underline{x}|\theta)$ be a transformation of the driving meteorological processes across a river basin, $\underline{x}$ (rainfall, temperature, etc.), to its response processes, $\underline{y}$ (in the typical case, runoff at its outlet), through a deterministic hydrological model of given structure, $\mathcal{M}$, and for a given set of parameter values, $\theta$. For convenience, we use the underline notation to denote a random quantity (variable or process), while the nonunderlined typeface denotes a realization of it. The identification of θ is typically carried out through calibration, by considering a set of realized inputs and outputs, x_i and y_i, respectively, and a performance function, P [y_i, M (x_i | θ_i)], to evaluate the closeness of simulated to actual outputs (e.g., efficiency). We remark that as result of calibration, although the transformation function, M, is deterministic, its parameters also can be considered as random, since they are inferred from the random processes x and y.

In the classical split-sample scheme and its alternatives, the realized data are obtained from observed records, x_o and y_o, a subset of which is used to calibrate the model and the rest for validating its predictive capacity against an independent data set. As already discussed, well-known shortcomings of such approaches are the potential lack of representativeness of historical data and their inability to represent long-term hydroclimatic changes (often, but not solely, associated with their length), which eventually results in poorly identified parameters. This problem is further intensified since we have to keep a significant part (e.g., half) of observations for validation purposes, thus losing valuable information that may be contained in the associated data subset. Furthermore, when the observed records have different sizes, only their common part can be accounted for in calibration and validation, while the nonoverlapping period of data is ignored. This case occurs quite often, since rainfall data, which is a rather easily retrievable information, usually exceeds the period of runoff observations.

Here we manifest an alternative approach for parameter estimation, which follows the stochastic simulation paradigm (Efstratiadis, 2011), as depicted in Figure 2.1. Similar to the classical split-sample scheme, this also involves a twofold strategy, yet by using long synthetic data within calibration (we apply symbols x_s and y_s for synthetic inputs and outputs, respectively), while the validation is employed against the full set of observed data, x_o and y_o. Apart from the deterministic hydrological procedure, M, and the performance function, P, the computational procedure also requires a synthetic time series generator, G. Ideally, this has to reproduce the true hydrological regime of the input and output processes, x and y, respectively. This option is offered by stochastic models, which are generally built upon the information contained in the historical data. In this respect, the synthesis procedure can be expressed as a multivariate stochastic transformation of the form:

[x_s, y_s] = G[x_o, y_o]. (2.1)
