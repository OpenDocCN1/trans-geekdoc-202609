## FORECASTING WATER LEVELS USING MACHINE (DEEP) LEARNING TO COMPLEMENT NUMERICAL MODELING IN THE SOUTHERN EVERGLADES, USA

Courtney S. Forde¹, Biswa Bhattacharya², Dimitri P. Solomatine²,³,⁴, Eric D. Swain⁵, and Nicholas G. Aumen⁶

Water level is an important guide for water resource management and wetland ecosystems, defining one of the most basic processes in hydrology. This research seeks to investigate the possibility of complementing numerical modeling with a Machine Learning (ML) model to forecast daily water levels in the southern Everglades in Florida, USA. An exact analytical solution to water level may not be possible, but using the computational methods afforded by ML, the traditional numerical techniques may be enhanced to generate more robust, scalable predictions. Five locations were chosen for application of the Time-Delayed Neural Network (TDNN) and Long-Short Term Memory Recurrent Neural Network (LSTM-RNN) ML models, which were built to estimate water level with 1, 2, 3, 7 and 10 day forecasts using a simulation step of 1 day. The results showed that rainfall forecasts from weather models could improve water-level forecasts if the accuracy and performance of the weather models can be improved. The ML models presented here improve water-level predictions from a historical hydrologic model for a 24 hour forecast horizon.

## 7.1. Introduction

Water level is widely used in flood risk management, monitoring of ground and surface water, and ecosystem and habitat monitoring and restoration in wetland environments (Forde, 2020). The Florida Everglades is considered one of the most significant wetland ecosystems in the world, particularly within Everglades National Park (ENP), the southern-most part of the Greater Everglades (Haman & Svendsen, 2006) established in 1947, which was designated as an International Biosphere Reserve in 1976, a World Heritage Site in 1979, and a Ramsar Convention Wetland of International Importance in 1987 (Maltby & Dugan, 1997). Prior to large-scale alterations of south Florida’s watershed, water flowed unhindered from the Kissimmee River into Lake Okeechobee, which periodically overflowed its southern bank during the wet season and drove sheet flow of surface water through the Everglades into the Gulf of Mexico, Florida Bay, and Biscayne Bay (Ingebritsen et al., 1999) (Fig. 7.1).

After severe flooding from hurricanes, a levee was constructed around almost all of Lake Okeechobee to prevent the overflow of water and avoid further flooding. A network of canals, levees, water control structures, and pumps was built throughout south Florida to provide water supply and flood protection to an ever-increasing population. Improvements and expansion of channel networks, development of the historical Everglades wetlands into surface reservoirs called Water Conservation Areas, and the construction of water-control structures further altered the watershed. As a result, the natural environment significantly deteriorated due to altered hydrodynamics as it relates to flow, water level, seasonal timing, and spatial distribution (Light & Dineen, 1994). The altered flows resulted in shifts in vegetation composition, as well as dramatic reductions in wading bird numbers due to habitat loss and reduced aquatic production, both of which were a result of ecosystem degradation and shortened hydroperiods (Light & Dineen, 1994).
