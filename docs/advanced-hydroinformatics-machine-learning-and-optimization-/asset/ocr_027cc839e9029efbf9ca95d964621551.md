# 7.6.3. Medium-Range Forecasts

As expected with longer forecast horizons, the accuracy is notably reduced in the 7 day and 10 day water-level predictions. Similar to the 2 day and 3 day forecasts, peaks in medium-range predicted water level can be linked to peaks in the rainfall forecast. A combination of the error in the rainfall forecast used to predict both forecast horizons, as well as the propagation of error from the prediction at each lead time, could contribute to the larger error at these lead times. The two medium-range forecasts had RMSE values of 0.069 m and 0.094 m, and R² of 0.94 and 0.89, respectively. The general pattern of the measured data is still preserved in the forecasts despite some overprediction of the two latter peaks (Fig. 7.9b).

Even at such a long lead time, some peaks are still well predicted due to reasonably accurate rainfall forecast points. For the 7 day water-level prediction, peak 1 is delayed in comparison to the measured data. Table 7.3 indicates there is no forecasted rainfall to drive the prediction of the peak on time and as a result, it arrives late. Peak 2 occurred a day late, which coincides with a peak in forecasted rainfall being forecasted a day late. Peak 3 was well predicted due to accurate timing and comparable magnitude of the forecasted rainfall to the measured rainfall (Table 7.3). From day 139 to 200, there is significant lag, which causes point errors as large as 0.35 m at around day 143 (Fig. 7.9c). There is very little forecasted rainfall at the point where the measured water level increased and resulted in the model not predicting the increase at that point. This error would have grown from the 2 day forecast horizon, which did not have a comparable forecasted rainfall peak in the measured data. With each lead, the consequent error in the water-level prediction would have grown as a result of prediction errors due to inaccurate rainfall forecasts, and the inherent prediction errors with larger lead time forecasts.

The error of the outlier peak predicted in the dry season of 2009 propagated through each time step, resulting in point prediction errors of as high as 0.70 m at the point where the largest peak in predicted water level occurs, just before the measured water level begins to increase (7 day water-level forecast in Fig. 7.9d). There are two rainfall peaks, which result in the two predicted peaks observed. However, this error started from the 1 day prediction and is not solely due to the rainfall forecasts.
