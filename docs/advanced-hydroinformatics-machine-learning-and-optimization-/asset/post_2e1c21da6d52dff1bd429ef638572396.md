
The EDEN-generated values are then used by water resource scientists and managers in conducting their research assessing ecological responses to CERP (Telis et al., 2015; Conrads et al., 2014) and other restoration and management efforts. One of the important aspects of water depth, and, by extension water level as it pertains to the Everglades, is the close relationship between the timing, spatial distribution, and volume of water, and the population health of a number of species that are used as indicators of wetland ecosystem health (Brandt et al., 2016). Examples of such species are alligators (Fujisaki et al., 2009) and several wading birds (Frederick et al., 2009; Lantz et al., 2010), which are directly or indirectly affected by water levels in the wetland, whether it be through prey availability, foraging habits, or nesting habitat (Forde, 2020). The importance of water levels in the Everglades are also highlighted in Telis (2006) and SFWMD (2021).

Machine Learning is now widely applied in many aspects of modeling, in which computer models learn patterns and relations within large data sets, and are able to produce generalizable evaluations of these data sets, gaining insight, knowledge, and predictive capacity through the identification, utilization, and testing of various computational approaches (Alpaydin, 2009). These types of models, also known as data-driven models, are able to solve complex problems in systems that are challenging to describe numerically. Machine Learning has been used to solve various problems in hydrology and hydraulics, see for example, Solomatine and Dulal (2003), Bhattacharya and Solomatine (2005), Kasiviswanathan et al. (2016), Xiang et al. (2020), Kao et al. (2020), and Moreido et al. (2021). A historical overview of Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) for hydrological forecasting is provided by Abrahart et al. (2012). Additionally, there are multiple examples of using ML for water-level forecasting (e.g., Zhu et al., 2020). Cruz et al. (2018) successfully used a multilayer ANN (Aggarwal, 2018) in predicting water level using real-time rainfall, water level, and soil moisture data. Amaranto et al. (2018) demonstrated the use of ANN (multilayer perceptron) for groundwater-level forecasting. Another study by Ma et al. (2019) showed promising results using a model based on LSTM networks to investigate whether the accuracy of water-level forecasting could be improved in the Rhine River Basin in Germany. A LSTM network, one form of Recurrent Neural Networks, is a deep-learning architecture that includes memory cells that store information over long periods of time and is known to be the optimal contemporary network architecture for predicting sequences such as in time-series, speech recognition, text prediction, and others (Kratzert et al., 2018). Unlike traditional approaches for time-series forecasting like Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA), LSTM has the ability to adapt to features of the data such as seasonality (Siami-Namini, 2018). Additionally, LSTM can utilize more hyperparameters and, because the extent of the short-term memory can be defined, LSTM tends to outperform SARIMA (Seasonal-ARIMA).

We could find only two published research papers presenting explicit water-level modeling in the Everglades. The first was a study by Conrads and Roehl (2007) where an ANN was used to hindcast water levels. The second and more applicable to this study focused on prediction boundaries and forecasting of nonlinear hydrological stage data in the A.R.M. Loxahatchee National Wildlife Refuge, the northern-most extent of the remnant Everglades (Park et al., 2005). An ANN model was developed to forecast stage over a 16 yr period from just 2 yr of training data, with forecast horizons of 1, 2, 8, and 14 days. This model was compared with linear models that forecast stages, and the results showed that the ANN model outperformed the linear models, most notably at the longer lead times. Additional methods for evaluating machine-learning models, such as visualization tools, were not applied in this study.

Apart from these examples, the models that currently exist for forecasting water level are numerical models, which simulate overall regional hydrological conditions in the entire, or specific parts, of the Everglades and are used by water managers. One of these models is the Regional Simulation Model (RSM), a surface water-groundwater model that solves for water stages and flows under natural (predrainage system) conditions as well as under controlled conditions such as the operation of water-control structures. The RSM quantifies changes in groundwater levels, groundwater and overland flow, and wetland hydroperiods, among other things (SFWMD, 2006). The RSM is solved for an irregular triangular mesh grid, which is considered an improvement on the South Florida Water Management Model (SFWMM), which has a square grid size of 3.2 x 3.2 km (SFWMD, 2008). The SFWMM simulates the hydrology and management of the water resources system from Lake Okeechobee to Florida Bay and is a tool for assessing operational changes to the complex water management system in South Florida (SFWMD, 2005).

Another regional simulation model, and the one used for comparison in this study, is the Biscayne and Southern Everglades Coastal Transport (BISECT) model, which simulates hydrologic conditions in various scenarios (Swain et al., 2019). This model uses dynamic equations so that surface water is fully represented, making it suitable for comparison in this study. One of the disadvantages of the BISECT and possibly other regional models is the averaging of inputs and outputs over each grid cell, which reduces the extent of peaks and troughs in the predictions. As a result, the BISECT model is more suitable for long-term regional analysis of hydrological conditions, than it is for local, shorter-term predictions (Swain et al., 2019).

Many ML models use real-time data in forecasting, but there is a lack of research as it relates to the use of Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP) (Pu & Kalnay, 2019) in predicting over longer forecast horizons. Additionally, from the characteristics of the numerical models mentioned as well as the issues faced by both water managers and ecologists, improved forecasts could assist operational water management and ecological assessments, particularly under the CERP.

The main objective of this study is to apply and test ML models to potentially complement conventional numerical models in an effort to improve water-level forecasting in the southern Everglades. Reaching this objective involved choosing suitable predictors for forecasting water levels. Two ML model architectures used to predict water level were tested for various forecast horizons and compared with a numerical model. A novel aspect of this study is analyzing the effect of NWP on the accuracy of the water-level predictions.

## 7.2. Methods

The southern Everglades is an important resource because ENP is one of the last remaining natural parts of the Everglades. The area just south of Tamiami Trail (Fig. 7.2) was identified as an area where water-level forecasts would be beneficial. Because restoring hydrologic conditions in the natural ecosystem is a primary objective of the CERP, water-level forecasts are essential to inform and aid restoration efforts as well as water resources management.

Five stations in the area were selected (Fig. 7.2). Station P33 was identified as an index station, considered to be representative of water levels and hydroperiods in Shark Slough, a low-lying area that is the main drainage through ENP. Station NP201 was selected from a map of the EDEN network (https://sofia.usgs.gov/eden) on the basis of its relative proximity to Tamiami Trail, as was station BCA11 (also selected to provide spatial representation along the trail). Finally, two stations influenced by control structures S12A_T and S12D_T (Fig. 7.2) were selected to allow for the consideration of discharges from water-control structures.

For each station, rainfall, evapotranspiration, and water-level data were available, while discharge data were available for the control structures S12A and S12D (vertical lift gates), all at a daily resolution. The rainfall, evapotranspiration, and water-level data had an 18 yr period of record (1 Jan 2002 to the present), 23 yr (1 Jun 1995–31 Dec 2018), and 30 years (1 Jan 1990 to the present), respectively, for all the stations. An exception was BCA11, which had a water-level record from 28 Dec 1990 to present.
