## 3.2. Method

### 3.2.1. Hydrologic Model and Its Calibration

VIC was originally developed by Liang et al. (1994, 1996). First, the user provides the model with grids of the study area, which includes meteorological data, digital elevation data, soil data, land cover data, and so on. Then the model simulates the hydrologic processes respectively on the basis of full water and energy balances in each grid cell. As a distributed hydrologic model in macroscale, the VIC model itself does not include the routing model. Therefore, the routing model based on a source-to-sink model (Lohmann et al., 1998; Lohmann et al., 1996) is utilized for runoff and base flow routing. For this application, the VIC model of version 4.2.2.d is used.

Multi-objective optimization algorithms involve more than one objective function to be optimized simultaneously. Multi-objective optimization algorithms produce a set of Pareto solutions, instead of a single optimal solution. The ε-NSGA II (Kollat & Reed, 2006) is used as a multi-objective optimization algorithm to calibrate parameters of VIC, which is updated from NSGA-II (Deb et al., 2002). Compared with NSGA-II, ε-NSGA II owns three new features, they are the ε-dominance archiving (Laumanns et al., 2002), the adaptive population sizing (Harik & Lobo, 1999), and the spontaneous termination (Reed et al., 2003). The ε-NSGA II is an effective and robust optimization algorithm (Fu et al., 2012; Hadka & Reed, 2012; Hurford et al., 2014; Kollat & Reed, 2007; Wang et al., 2014) and has been successfully applied in some previous studies (Liu et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019; Pan et al., 2018). Here we operate the ε-NSGA II algorithm in parallel with a high-performance and widely portable implementation of the Message Passing Interface (MPI) standard named MPICH.

The Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) (Nash & Sutcliffe, 1970) and relative bias (Bias) are used to mathematically quantify the performance between observed streamflow and streamflow simulated by VIC, whose detailed formulas are defined as

$$NSE = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Q_o(i)-Q_s(i))^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Q_o(i)-\overline{Q_o})^2}\tag{3.1}$$

and

$$Bias = \left|\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Q_s(i)-Q_o(i))}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_o(i)}\right|,\tag{3.2}$$

where $n$ is the length of the daily runoff series; $Q_s(i)$ and $Q_o(i)$ are the observed and simulated streamflow of the $i^{th}$ day respectively; and $\overline{Q_o}$ is the mean value of the observed streamflow. The value of NSE closer to 1 means a less difference between the observed and simulated flows, while that of Bias prefers to be 0.

The values of objective functions converge in a range around the optimization objectives with the increasing iterations of optimization. We define the range that the values of objective functions converge in as crucial range and define the remaining range outside the crucial range as trivial range.

### 3.2.2. Algorithm of Response Surface Surrogates

Adaptive boost (AdaBoost) is a boosting algorithm (Freund et al., 1996; Kearns & Valiant, 1994). This algorithm assembles several weak predictors into a strong predictor to reduce deviation and variance brought by the random generation of initial sample weights and thresholds of ANN (Schapire, 1990). In our study, the standard backpropagation neural network (BPNN) is employed as a weak learner in AdaBoost. The algorithm with AdaBoost and BPNN is called ADBP. The detailed information about ADBP (Fries & Kerkez, 2018; Zhao et al., 2014) is described as follows:

- 1. Initialize training data and network. The data of training are normalized first. $N$ sets of training data are given, where $x_i$ ($i=1,2,...,N$) is the input parameters and $y_i$ is the training objective. The initial weight of training sample $x_i$ is set as $D_1(i)=1/N$.

- 2. Train the weak learner and calculate the weight of weak learner. Here, BPNN is used as the weak learner to predict the outputs of training data. For $t=1,...,T$, where $T$ is the size of weak learners, the BPNN prediction error and the weight of the weak learner are denoted as the following:

  $e_t(i) = h_t(\mathbf{x}_i) - y_i$

  $a_t = \frac{1}{2\exp(|Err_t|)}, \text{where } Err_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N} D_t(i), \quad (3.3)$

  where $e_t(i)$ is the BPNN prediction error; $h_t(x_i)$ is the prediction value of $y_i$ with BPNN; and $a_t$ is the weight of the weak learner.

- 3. Update the weight of the training sample. If $|e_t(i)|>0.1$, the weight of the training sample would be updated as equation (3.4) or else $D_{t+1}(i)=D_t(i)$,

  $D_{t+1}(i) = 1.1 \times D_t(i). \quad (3.4)$

- 4. Output the final hypothesis. Repeat the steps above and $T$ weak learners with their weights are acquired. Then the weights are normalized and the $T$ weak learners are combined into a strong learner as the following:

  $w_t = \frac{a_t}{\sum_{j=1}^{T} a_j}$

  $h_{final} = \sum_{i=1}^{T} w_i h_i, \quad (3.5)$

  where $h_{final}$ is the ensemble prediction value of the $T$ weak learners; $w_t$ is the normalized weight of the $t^{th}$ weak learner. The inputs of SM are the adjustable parameters of VIC and the outputs are the objective functions.

#### Initial Surrogate Model Training

A two-stage model is proposed in this study to reduce the local errors in the crucial range, which can generate insufficient samples in the crucial range. The first surrogate model (SM-I) simulates approximate results to classify a set of samples into two types: samples in the trivial range and samples in the crucial range. The second surrogate model (SM-II) simulates the results in high fidelity to get a more accurate Pareto front. In order to acquire more samples in the crucial range, the samples are generated by the optimization algorithm, which can converge the range of parameters into the crucial range to ensure sufficient sample quantity in the training of SM-I and SM-II.

The algorithm of the initial SM training is shown in Figure 3.2 and described as follows:

- **Step 1:** Partition the crucial ranges and generate samples. Run ε-NSGA II with VIC for *N* generations to warm up the program. Determine the crucial ranges with the warming-up data. The input data X = [X₁, X₂, …, Xₖ] are acquired where *k* is the population size in the *N* generations of ε-NSGA II, X is a *m* × *k* matrix, and *m* is the number of the calibration parameters. The output data E = [E₁, E₂, …, Eₖ] are acquired where *k* is the initial population size of the warming-up data, E is a *n* × *k* matrix, and *n* is the number of the objective functions.

- **Step 2:** Train SM-I. After eliminating duplicates of the samples acquired in Step 1, SM-I is trained. Here, we use the hold-out cross validation to validate the training results in which generally 70% of samples are extracted randomly for the model training and the remaining 30% for the model testing.
