## Support Vector Machines

The SVM algorithm is proposed by Vapnik (2013) as a binary classification method. This algorithm uses the concept of Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension (see Vapnik, 2013), which is a measure of capacity or complexity of a machine to minimize structural risk to optimization and obtain learning and generalization ability. Given a set of N samples of {xₖ, yₖ}ₖ₌₁ᴺ, x ∈ Rᵐ, y ∈ R, where x is an input vector of size m, and y is the corresponding output value. An SVM estimator f on regression can be expressed as

$$f(x)=\omega\cdot\phi(x)+b,$$  (5.1)

where ω is a weight vector and b denotes bias. The ϕ represents a nonlinear transfer function that maps the input vectors into a high dimensional feature space (e.g., Vapnik, 2013). A convex optimization problem with an ε-insensitivity loss function is used in this model to obtain optimization (equation (5.2)):

$$\begin{aligned}
\min_{\omega,b,\xi,\xi^*}\;&\frac{1}{2}\|\omega\|^2+C\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\xi_i+\xi_i^*)\\
\text{s.t.}\;&\omega\cdot\phi(x_i)+b-y_i\le\varepsilon+\xi_i,\\
&y_i-\omega\cdot\phi(x_i)-b\le\varepsilon+\xi_i^*,\\
&\xi_i,\xi_i^*\ge0;\ i=1,2,\ldots,m.
\end{aligned}$$  (5.2)

where ξ and ξ* are slack variables, which introduce soft margin to deal with infeasible constraints; C is a positive constant to penalize training errors by the loss function over the error tolerance ε and prevent overfitting. In this algorithm, the overfitting problem is typically solved using Duality Theory and Lagrangian multipliers and imposing Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality condition (see Shevade et al., 2000; Smola, 2002).

## Bayesian Lasso

In the classic linear regression setting, the original Lasso model, proposed by Tibshirani (1996), is an L1-penalized least squares estimate that aims to resolve the following optimization problem:

$$\min_{\beta}\quad (y-X\beta)^T(y-X\beta)+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{D}|\beta_i|,$$  
(5.3)

where $X$ is a $N \times D$ matrix of standardized predictors of dimension $D$, $y$ is the mean-centered response vector, $\beta$ denotes a vector that contains the $D$ regression coefficient, and $\lambda$ is the regularization parameter, which is a constant. The coefficient estimates are the solutions of the penalized regression, and sparsity in the Lasso model is obtained because some of the estimated parameters in $\beta$ will be zero (see Efron et al., 2004; Helgoy & Li, 2019; Tibshirani, 1996). The Lasso solution can also be interpreted as a Bayesian posterior mode estimate, so-called Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) posterior estimation, when the prior for the coefficient parameters, $p(\beta)$, is a Laplace distribution or the double exponential distribution (Tibshirani, 1996). A fully Bayesian Lasso was introduced by Park and Casella (2008) with a conditional prior on $\beta$ of the form $p(\beta|\sigma^2, \lambda)$. The posterior probability distribution for the coefficient parameters can be calculated using the Gibbs sampler. The Bayesian Lasso by Park and Casella (2008) has several attractive properties when it comes to parameter and hyperparameter estimation in the Bayesian setting, that is, that the joint posterior distribution for $\beta$ and $\sigma^2$ generally has one mode instead of multiple posterior modes (Helgoy & Li, 2019). When conditioning Bayesian calculation on $\sigma^2$, it is important to guarantee a unimodal full posterior (see Park & Casella, 2008). Without this, the posterior may not be unimodal, and the convergence may slow down, making the true distribution less meaningful. Readers are referred to Tibshirani (1996) and Park and Casella (2008) for more information.

## Random Forest

RF, proposed by Breiman (2001), is one of the most popular ML techniques for regression problems. These models consist of a large number of individual decision trees that operate as an ensemble. Each individual tree in the RF outputs a class (or group) prediction, and the class with the most votes becomes the model prediction. The prerequisites for good RF performance include
- the presence of some actual signals in time series data so that the decision tree models built using those signals perform better than random guessing
- the prediction made by the individual trees need to have low correlations with each other

Decision trees are very sensitive to the data sets and a slight change in the training data can result in significantly different tree structures. However, RF took advantage of this deficiency by allowing each individual tree to randomly sample from the data set with replacement, resulting in different tree structures. This process is known as bagging. Each tree in a random forest is built using only a random subset of features. This potential limitation makes more variation among the trees in the model, and eventually leads to a lower correlation among trees and more diversification. However, one of the main problems of regression trees is that they are prone to overfitting in the training period and thus perform poorly in validation when given unseen data (e.g., Murphy, 2012). Overfitting occurs when the model is flexible and has a high capacity to memorize and learn both the actual relationship in the training data and any noise present in the data set. The RF model can overcome this deficiency by learning the structure of the decision tree that varies considerably with the training data. Instead of searching for the most important feature while splitting a node, RF searches for the best feature among a random subset of features. This results in a wide diversity that generally leads to better performance, thus avoiding overfitting of the model to the training data set. Users have the ability to make trees more random by using random thresholds for each feature, rather than searching for the best possible thresholds.

## Determination of Input Structure and Calibration Setup

Antecedent values of input variables could be considered to represent the catchment characteristics in the ML models (Solomatine & Xue, 2004; Tongal & Booij, 2018). In this study, a combination of precipitation ($P$), maximum and minimum temperatures ($T^{\mathrm{max}}$ and $T^{\mathrm{min}}$), solar radiation ($Srad$), and humidity ($H$) was used in addition to the antecedent day streamflow ($Q^s_{t-1}$) to build the rainfall-runoff simulation. The antecedent day streamflow was calculated using a Feed-Forward Neural Network (FFNN). After a number of experiments on the training data set aimed at finding the most relevant inputs, the following common input structure was selected:

$Q^s_t = f(P_t, \ldots, P_{t-3}, T^{\mathrm{max}}_t, T^{\mathrm{min}}_t, Srad_t, H_t, Q^s_{t-1})$. (5.4)

Prior to the model development, normalization was applied to all input variables as

$X_{norm} = \frac{X_0 - \mu_x}{\sigma_x}$. (5.5)

where $X_{norm}$ and $X_0$ indicate, respectively, the scaled and original data. The $\mu_x$ and $\sigma_x$ represent the mean and standard deviation of the original data, correspondingly. For each ML method used in this study, the optimal model parameters were selected by maximizing the objective function (Mean Squared Error; MSE) using training and calibration data sets. The models were developed on the training data set with a range of parameter values, and the objective function value was calculated for the calibration period. The optimal model parameters were then used to simulate streamflow for the validation period. In this study, the range of parameter values was determined by examining several hydrologic simulation studies, such as Shortridge et al. (2016), Deka (2014), Kumar et al. (2016), Naghibi et al. (2017), Worland et al. (2018), Tongal and Booij (2018), and Fotovatikhah et al. (2018).

## SAC-SMA Model

The SAC-SMA model is developed by the U.S. National Weather Service (NWS) as a conceptual hydrologic simulation model. The tool utilizes a moisture accounting system to represent the catchment storage capacity. The SAC-SMA divides the basin into lower and upper soil zones at different depths, and defines the distribution of moisture, that is, tension water components (driven by evapotranspiration and diffusion) and free water components (driven by gravitational forces) in each of these two zones via a set of parameters (Burnash, 1995). The model uses climatic variables, such as precipitation and temperature, along with parameters on soil moisture states and the basin’s relative permeability, to estimate the amount of water that enters, is stored in, and leaves the basin. Thus, the model computes several key hydrologic processes, such as evapotranspiration, percolation, interflow, and different forms of runoff from a basin. These are
- direct runoff from permanent and temporary impervious areas
- surface runoff due to precipitation occurring at a rate faster than percolation and interflow can take place when both upper zone storages are full
- interflow resulting from the lateral drainage of a temporary free water storage
- supplemental base flow
- primary base flow (Burnash, 1995)
although, proper application of the SAC-SMA requires interpretations of the primary rainfall-runoff mechanisms (precipitation, evapotranspiration, and soil moisture) in a physically reasonable manner. A distortion in any of these characteristics may produce a compensating distortion in another element. Such distortion may appear to produce an effective streamflow simulation during the fitting period, but can reduce the model performance, particularly during high and low flow events.
