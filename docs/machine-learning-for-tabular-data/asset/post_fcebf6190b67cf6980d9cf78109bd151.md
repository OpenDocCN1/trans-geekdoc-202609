## 5.1.1 Bagging and sampling

We have examined all single learning algorithms with decision trees. Ensembling
algebras of the same type is the next step that can help achieve more predictive
power on your problem. The approach is intuitive: if a single algorithm can perform
at a certain level, using the insights of multiple models or chaining them together (that one can learn from the results and errors of the other) would render even better
results. There are two ensemble strategies:

- Averaging—Differences are obtained by averaging in how the models are built, for instance by pasting, bagging, random subspaces, and random patches as we will see in this section, lead-to-
different results. The best example of ensemble models of this kind is the random forests algorithm, which is built on an approach similar to random patches.

- Boosting—Predictions are built as a weighted average of chained models, which are sequentially built upon the results of previous ones. The best example of a boosting algorithm is gradient boosting machines like XGBoost and LightGBM.

In the following subsection, we will look at random forests. Before delving into the random forests algorithm, it is necessary to spend some time on the averaging approaches, not only because the random patches approach is built upon them but also because they point out solutions that are always worth applying to tabular data when you need to reduce the variance of the estimates, hence obtaining more reliable predictions, of any machine learning model you may want to use on your data.

Pasting is the first approach to consider. Leo Breiman, the creator of the random forests algorithm, suggests that creating a set of different models trained on subsamples, obtained by sampling without replacement, of your training data. The models' predictions are pooled together by averaging in the case of a regression problem or by majority voting in the case of a classification task.

The pros of pasting

- Improvement of the results by reducing the variance of the predictions by partially increasing their bias, which is a measure of how far the predictions of model are from the true values
- Predictions that are more robust and less affected by outliers
- Reduction of the amount of data to learn at training time, thus reducing memory requirements

The cons

- Reduction of the amount of data available, which increases the bias because there is the chance of excluding important parts of the data distribution by sampling
- Very intensive with complex algorithms

The last depends on your time constraints or available resources. Historically, averaging methods have been suggested to be applied using weak models (i.e., machine learning models that are very fast to be trained because of their simplicity, such as a linear regression or k-nearest neighbors model). Practitioners observed that combining multiple weak models could beat the results of a single, more complex algorithm. However, weak models usually have a high bias problem, and by subsampling, you induce only some variance in their estimates, but their bias remains mostly untouched. Using an averaging approach has the main advantage that it reduces the variance of the estimates by trading it with a bit more bias. Since weak models inherently carry a substantial bias, they might not achieve comparable results to the same approach applied to more complex models. In situations where more significant

improvements are needed by reducing the variance of the estimates, employing an averaging strategy that can be more effective with complex models.

Bagging, also suggested by Leo Breiman as a better solution, differs from pasting because you switch to subsampling to bootstrapping. Bootstrapping consists of sampling with replacement multiple times from a sample to approximate the population distribution of a statistic. Bootstrapping is a frequently employed statistical technique that allows us to estimate the variability relative to the data from which sample has been drawn. By using the information from resamples that mimic the population behavior, bootstrapping emulates the population without explicit knowledge.

The reason for using bootstrapping in learning is to estimate the uncertainty of a model's performance. In addition, bootstrapping helps create more datasets to train. However, it has limitations if you subsample aggressively—for instance, picking up more than 50% of the data—you tend to introduce bias.

In contrast, if you subsample in a limited way, as 90% of the data, the resulting samples will tend to be correlated. Bootstrapping is more efficient because you use about 63.2% of the original data at each bootstrap. For a detailed statistical explanation of such calculated proportions, please see the detailed cross-validated analysis at https://m.bz/z20. Moreover, sampling with replacement tends to give results that mimic the original distribution of the data.

In fact, since we are building a distribution of predictions and getting the center of the distribution as our prediction, the more averaged predictions resemble a random distribution, the less the center of the distribution is biased by problems in the data (such as overfitting).

By contrast, with random subspaces, introduced by T. Ho [The Random Subspace Method for Constructing Forests, Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, 2(8), 832–844, 1998], the sampling is limited to features. This works because the model used is the decision tree, a model whose variance of the estimates is greatly reduced in the ensemble by using only part of the features for every model that is part of the. The improved result is because the models trained on a subsample of the features tend to produce uncorrelated predictions—all the decision trees overfit the data in a different way with respect to each other.

Finally, with random patches [G. Louppe and P. Geerts, "Ensembles on Random Patches," in Machine Learning and Knowledge Discovery in Databases (2012): 346–361], sampling of both samples and features is used together to achieve even more uncorrelated predictions that can be averaged more profitably.
