As for manually tuning GBMs, the models tend to work worst out of the box, so you should be aware of a few tricks of the trade. Let's begin with a 1999 paper titled "Greedy Function Approximation: A Gradient Boosting Machine" by Jerome Friedman. In this paper, Friedman discusses the tradeoff between the number of trees and the learning rate. It was observed that lower learning rates tend to result in higher optimal numbers of trees. Furthermore, it is advisable to reduce the learning rate when increasing the maximum depth of the decision trees in your model. This precautionary measure is because deep trees introduce more complexity, potentially leading to overfitting. Overfitting occurs when the model becomes excessively tailored to the training data and performs poorly on unseen data. By simultaneously reducing the learning rate, you can mitigate this risk. This is because a lower learning rate translates to smaller and more cautious updates to the model. This gradual learning process allows for finer adjustments, helping the model strike a better balance between capturing relationships and avoiding overfitting.

Another great resource for hints on manually adjusting parameters in a GBDT is Owen Zhang's talk to the NYC Data Science Academy in 2015 titled "Winning Data Science Competitions.", a top competitor on Kaggle, provided a few interesting tips:

- Decide the number of trees used based on the dataset size (usually in the range of 100 to 1,000) and keep it fixed during the optimization. Prefer fewer trees to more.

- Test the learning rate in the range from 2 to 10 divided by the number of trees. Hence, for 1,000 trees, test learning rates in the interval of 0.002 to 0.01.

- Test row sampling on 0.5, 0.75, 1.0 values.

- Test column sampling on 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0 values.

- Test max tree depth on 4, 6, 8, 10 values.

- Tune the minimum leaf weight/count as an approximate ratio of 3 over the square root of the percentage of the rarest class you to predict. Therefore, if the class need to have 10% coverage in the data, you should set the minimum leaf weight/count around 9. This figure is calculated by dividing 3 by the square root of 0.1 (since 10% coverage is 0.1 as a decimal).

## 6.4.1 Deciding between XGBoost and LightGBM

When considering gradient boosting for your data problem, XGBoost and LightGBM (along with HistGradientBoosting) are among the most popular and high-performing implementations of histogram gradient boosted machines. Despite being so powerful, in our experience, you never can a priori go for XGBoost or LightGBM or just generally favor GBDTs in regards to other classical or deep learning solutions of the free lunch theorem in machine learning: there is no universal learning algorithm that performs best for all possible problems. Hence, that “XGBoost is all you need” for tabular data is a catchy phrase, but it may not always fit your specific problem or situation with data. GBDTs often tend to overperform other solutions for tabular data. Thus, starting with, but not limited to it, is a good choice. Returning to specific implementations, while it is advisable to test any algorithm on your data and make your own decisions, there are also a few criteria to consider when deciding whether to try one first or the other. We have validated these based on our own experience. They are summarized in 6.1.

### Table 6.1 Criteria to consider when using GBDTs

| Type | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Amount of data | XGBoost works for all tabular problems; LightGBM, because of its leaf-wise splitting method that can create deeper trees, tends to overfit with smaller datasets. |
| Scalability | XGBoost is more scalable and GPU ready; LightGBM struggles more. |
| Speed of experimentation | On CPUs, LightGBM is undoubtedly faster than XGBoost. |

The availability of large amounts of data is the first criterion to consider. LightGBM uses leaf-wise (vertical) growth, which can result in overfitting. The tendency to revert the data available explains the algorithm's success in Kaggle competitions. Hence, LightGBM works better when you have a lot of data available. In contrast, XGBoost builds more robust models than LightGBM on smaller data samples.

Another criterion is to consider whether you have access to computational resources. Strong CPUs or you have plenty of resources, XGBoost is more scalable, making it a better option for use in institutional or business settings. However, if you prefer to focus on experimentation and feature engineering and cannot access GPUs, LightGBM makes sense because of its faster training time. You can use the saved training time to improve the robustness of your final model. If you have limited resources, such as a stand-alone computer, consider that the training time for XGBoost increases linearly with the sample size, while LightGBM requires a much smaller fraction of training time.
