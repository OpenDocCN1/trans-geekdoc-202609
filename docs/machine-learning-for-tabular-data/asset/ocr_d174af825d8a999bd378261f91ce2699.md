Bagging, also suggested by Leo Breiman as a better solution, differs from pasting because you switch to subsampling to bootstrapping. Bootstrapping consists of sampling with replacement multiple times from a sample to approximate the population distribution of a statistic. Bootstrapping is a frequently employed statistical technique that allows us to estimate the variability and uncertainty of statistics relative to the underlying data population from which our sample has been drawn. By using the information from the available sample through resamples that mimic the original population's behavior, bootstrapping emulates the population's behavior without requiring explicit knowledge about its statistical distribution.

The reason for using bootstrapping in learning is to estimate the uncertainty of a model's performance or to assess the. In addition, bootstrapping helps create more variations of the original dataset for training and enrolling purposes. This is based on the observation that averaging models reduces variance if the models being used are less correlated (i.e., more diverse). Subsampling creates datasets to train. However, it has limitations if you subsample aggressively—for instance, picking up more than 50% of the original data—you tend to introduce bias.

In contrast, if you subsample in a limited way, with as 90% of the data, the resulting samples will tend to be correlated. Bootstrapping is more efficient because, you use about 63.2% of the original data at each bootstrap. For a detailed statistical explanation of such calculated proportions, please see the detailed cross-validated analysis at https://m.bz/z20. Moreover, sampling with replacement tends to give results that mimic the original distribution of the data. Bootstrapping creates a set of different datasets to learn from and, consequently, a set of different predictions that can ensemble, reducing variance.

In fact, since we are building a distribution of predictions and getting the center of the distribution as our prediction, the more averaged predictions resemble a random distribution, the less the center of the distribution is biased by problems in the data (such as overfitting).

By contrast, with random subspaces, introduced by T. Ho [The Random Subspace Method for Constructing Forests, Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, 2(8), 832–844, 1998], the sampling is limited to features. This works because the model used is the decision tree, a model whose variance of the estimates is greatly reduced in the ensemble by using only part of the features for every model that is part of the i. The improved result is because the models trained on a subsample of the features tend to produce uncorrelated predictions—all the decision trees overfit the data in a different way with respect to each other.

Finally, with random patches [G. Louppe and P. Geerts, "Ensembles on Random Patches," in Machine Learning and Knowledge Discovery in Databases (2012): 346–361], sampling of both samples and features is used together to achieve even more uncorrelated predictions that can be averaged more profitably.
