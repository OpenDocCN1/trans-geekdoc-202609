CHAPTER 1 

# INTRODUCTION

![](images/chapter-1.jpg)

* * *

Have you ever had a person try to scare you with all the ‘evils’ of cyber attacks?

“*They’re going to take your data.*”

“*They’re going to destroy your company.*”

“*They’re going to ruin your life*.”

Though it is true that cyber hackers can do terrible things, living in a state of continuous fear is not the way to solve it. The reason I’m writing RELAX is because when you’re in a panic, it causes you to *react* instead of *act*.

Hackers use this fight-or-flight mindset to get you to either pay them money or have your important information exposed. In a way, cyber security companies do the same thing. It is well known in the industry that keeping a heightened sense of fear can get people to spend more money. For the sake of your pocketbook and mental health, you need to learn to relax and understand the items that you need to protect your company and your life.

Cyber security is the antithesis of everything small business: It is confusing when we are looking for straight-forward. It should be unnecessary when we have so many other things to address. It is expensive when everything needs our money. It is just wrong when we need something to go right.

As a small business owner, I understand why we feel this way. Cyber security is the result of having to spend money because bad people just cannot help being bad—and why should *we* have to pay for that?

In this book, I hope to make it fun for you to gain the necessary knowledge and information on how to reach the not-at-all-mythical “cyber security nirvana.” By sharing stories, both good and bad, I hope to help your business be better prepared for what is happening in the world today, as well as for what is to come.

I know that some people are scared to the point of inaction, while others think they are safe because it has never happened to them. I hope to get you to a point *between* those two extremes: a place that lets you be cyber safe, but also enjoy life again.

So, come along and enjoy this fun and exciting ride through the world of cyber security—with just a hint of the crazy and a dash of the dangerous!

I always like to start by sharing *my* story. I was a baby at the… ok, so maybe not *that* far back. Let’s try this again… I started out my professional career as a developer for one of the world’s largest gas and oil companies. In those days, I spent most of my time writing code that would transfer millions of dollars from one bank to another. At the time, I told myself that I was “busy changing the world,” but in all honesty, I was doing the most *boring* work.

From that “exciting” place (insert sarcasm here), I moved on to an even more exciting job of writing programs that moved inventory from one place to another. It was at this job that I learned my first lesson in disaster recovery.

I was still a youngster at 22 when I successfully (and unintentionally) destroyed the entire inventory records for one of the largest trucking and travel centers in the United States. Yes, you heard me right: *Their. Entire. Inventory. Records.* Even more crazy was the fact that I managed to make such a massive blunder in my first week of being hired!

It was a heart-dropping experience that I was sure I would never recover from. Here I was, only seven days since they had allowed me to take this wonderful job, and I typed in a delete command that just whisked away $5 million dollars in inventory. To this day, I still remember the little green cursor staring at me, almost laughing. *Flash, flash, flash.* I sat there nearly paralyzed, hoping to see the file names return, but nothing happened.

My head began racing. I was sure that after this colossal mistake, I wouldn’t be able to get a job anywhere! Who would hire a programmer that just destroyed his company’s entire inventory records? I was certain I was doomed to never work again… except maybe as a shoeshine boy. I wasn’t even good with a towel!

With great remorse and a cocky tail set firmly between my legs, I got up and slowly marched to the network administrator’s office. Going down that hall was like a death march as I awaited the lashing I knew was coming.

“*Pack up your things and get out of here! You’ll never work again!*” was the response that I expected on the other side of that door.

Entering Ken’s office, (yes, his name was also Ken), I looked down at the ground as I started my disheartened confession.

“*Ken, I think I’m fired,*” I shared.

He looked up from his technical manual and said, “*What are you talking about, Ken?*”

“*I destroyed the entire inventory system. I can go pack my things. I just wanted to make sure that you knew.*” I was certain that in a few moments, everyone would know that I destroyed the company.

And then he said it: “*You’re fired, Ken*.” After a brief pause, he laughed. “*No, you’re not. You just made a mistake; we have backups for just these occasions. I’ll have it all back within the hour*.”

My heart raced. I was saved! From that day forward, I had grown a great love of backups. I had almost destroyed an entire company, but because of these magical things called backups, I was saved!

From that point on, I began my journey of protecting what’s important. I may not have always stayed true to that path but come with me as I share stories of many bad things—and many good things—that companies endured as they faced the growing world of cyber hackers and other cyber gremlins.

Join me as I take you through three decades of cyber adventures, sharing how you can use this knowledge to protect your company and yourself.

I’ll explain ransomware, encryption, multi-factor authentication, backups, data loss prevention and many other cyber security terms you need to be aware of. Don’t worry, I’ll try to make them as fun and entertaining as possible.

Together, we can have fun as I make it easy to understand the dark and confusing world of cyber attacks and help you get ready to keep your company and your data safe. Sit back, get ready, and hold on tight!

CHAPTER 2 

# RISKY BUSINESS

![](images/chapter-2.jpg)

In respect to your business’ future, have you ever been asked the question, “*What if you get hit by a bus?*” It’s such a graphic way of saying that we need to think about the risks that face our business. We use such graphic imagery because it shocks us into taking things seriously. The “killer bus” stalking us reminds us to take note of what is important. It makes us truly focus on the *why* of what we do. Far too often, we get bogged down in the *what* and the *how,* but we forget the *why*.

I always like to start out my new client discoveries with the question “*WHY?*” The answer to this question frames all following conversations about what is important and what a business will need to do. I’ve found that many companies haven’t had the “*why”* conversation. Many times, this happens because it’s difficult, or the “daily fires” that must be addressed overwhelm that vital process.

Let me be perfectly honest with you: Cyber security is just crap! It’s expensive, frustrating, and it can feel so useless. If you don’t understand the reason *why* you’re doing it, the purpose it serves, then cyber security will feel like the greatest money-suck that you’ve ever had.

Have you ever wished that you didn’t even need to use computers? Or had the nostalgic thought*, “In the old days, people didn’t have to worry about hackers.”* When there were no computers, there were no hackers. While this *is* a true statement, do any of us really want to go back to the days of having stacks of paper covering every inch of our offices? Don’t even get me started on the miles of filing cabinets taking up most of the area you pay for in an office. We know the answer to this question is, *“No, we don’t want to go back to a time before computers.”*

Computers really are our friends. Hear me out: Like many of our friends, they can be selfish and annoying, causing us to do things we never thought we would, and cost us lots of money (you know who you are!). You may recognize the statement, “*Choose your friends wisely*.” This statement goes for your computers as well.

How is a small business going to choose their friends, their computers, wisely? In the cyber industry, it happens by doing what we call a *risk assessment*. Let me be clear here, this does *not* mean you need to do a giant, formal risk process that gets everyone involved and takes months of time and piles of money. Don’t get me wrong, those are good to get company-wide buy-in and can help large companies get a view into their “*why*,” but for small businesses like ours, this can be an informal and relaxed process. In some ways, it can even be cathartic and help you to see what potential dangers your company faces.

At this point, you may be wondering how a risk assessment gets you to your “*why*.” Let me take you through a risk assessment that I performed with a client.

I was working with a manufacturing client and brought in their President to perform a basic risk assessment. We started with some of the standard risk points: “*What files are important? What drives your production line? How do you take orders?”* This brought us to the basic situation of what files are important, and how *not* protecting them would cost thousands of dollars per day— but we didn’t stop there. Instead, I began asking deeper questions. Putting everything into dollars is useful, but there are always deeper reasons for why you do the things you do.

After getting through the base questions, I asked the President why he started the company. He answered, “*I always wanted to make the best widgets\** (\*I’ve altered his product name to “protect the innocent”).

I then asked him, “*How would you feel if you couldn’t produce your Wonder Widgets?*” He took a moment and reflected on that question.

Slowly, he said, “*I never thought about that. I just figured that I would always be making Wonder Widgets. I like to make them, so I wouldn’t be very happy with that*.”

I responded, “*Remember those threats that we talked about regarding your customer files, your inventory management, your shipping records? If you were to lose them, would you be able to continue to make your Wonder Widgets?*”

Looking deeply across the room, he replied, “*I don’t know if I could keep working. That’s a very interesting question. I just never thought about what could shut me down*.”

It’s that “eureka moment” that you want to get to with your team when performing a risk assessment. The point is to find the reasons *why* things are important, and to see if they are protected the way they should be.

I have had so many meetings with companies after-the-fact, where they say the same thing to me: *“I didn’t realize how important that file or device was to my business.”*

There are many great risk frameworks that you can use. I’ve always liked the OCTAVE Allegro from SEI, but there are hundreds to choose from. However, if you’re not looking to be that formal, just start here:

Get your key team together and ask them what they think is important and why, listing out all the items and reviewing what they say. Afterwards, ask yourself this simple question: “*If I lose this information, how can I recover?*” If you find that your answer is “*I don’t know*,” then you definitely need to rethink your recovery plan.

The purpose of any risk assessment is:

1.  To define the risks that your company faces
2.  Determine methods to reduce or mitigate those risks
3.  Design a plan of action to address the defined risks and implement the methods above

Remember: Many times, if you don’t know there’s a problem, or are not actively working to find it, they’ll usually present themselves at the worst possible time (figures, right?), and with the worst possible outcomes. So, to avoid catastrophes like this, you’ll want to take the time to perform a risk assessment of your company at least once per year.

CHAPTER 3 

# IF IT AIN’T BROKE, BETTER FIX IT

![](images/image-03.jpg)

How many times have you heard the phrase, “*If it ain’t broke, don’t fix it*”? This way of operating made sense for many companies—at least it did “way back in the olden days.” Looking back, there was a time when your father could buy a press or other device and it could be handed down for generations. In fact, many of my clients have had equipment they were still using from the 1970s and ‘80s!

For a while, it was a great plan: businesses were able to pay off hardware and continue using it for generations. But, like most things, it came with a major problem: equipment that was *that* old was hard to keep updated or working properly.

For example, we had a client in 2012 that was using a billing system that ran on Windows NT from the 1990s. Everything this company had was billed through this computer. The hardware could not be replaced, they no longer had the install disks, and most of the people didn’t even know how to run Windows NT. The cost to upgrade their system was massive.

My next example is even worse. I was a young network administrator at a drug company, and they were using an old IBM System 36 computer. For those not old enough to remember, this was known as a *mainframe*. This computer kept all the records that tracked each piece of information on the drugs they sold, including drug batch information used by the FDA in the event of a problem with a drug. Loss of this information could easily result in the closure of the pharmaceutical company.

This System 36 computer had been used for almost 20 years and had never been updated in that time. When I took over my position, I was told, “*Why change it when it has been so reliable?*” I told my superiors that it was only a matter of time before it failed and that if we didn’t act swiftly, that it could potentially collapse the company.

The solution that I found was going to cost about $4 million dollars to implement and take about three months to deploy. Top management didn’t feel that changing this “reliable system” made any sense because, at this point, it was considered free to them. They didn’t have to pay anything to keep it going, so why should they spend that money now?

Frustrated, I did everything in my power to keep that system working for as long as possible. I knew that, just like all of us, eventually it was going to die. As fate would have it, the system died on the worst day of the year, right before annual close. I remember walking in that Friday morning and having accounting pop in and ask me what happened to the System 36. After a heart-wrenching moment, I ran into the large, dark room that held this monster and there it was: the red light that I dreaded seeing each morning. “*System Fault*” flashed at me, mocking me with its angry glow.

I made the long walk down to the CEO’s office to give him the news. The System 36 was dead, and it was not coming back.

That’s when the real pain began. Because we lost the System, we had to spend time in a panic to get things back up and running as quickly as possible. There were many, many long nights with millions in accelerated costs. This $4 million project I proposed a short time ago ended up costing the company over $45 million to get back up and running after the crash. That whole “*If it ain’t broke, don’t fix it*” mindset almost destroyed the company.

I find that the inertia many companies face is one of the hardest things to overcome in moving companies towards increased cyber security. It’s getting past everyone saying, “*Why change? We’ve always done it this way,*” that poses the greatest challenge in increasing the safety and cyber security of a company and its culture. Hackers are always changing, and if you’re not keeping up with them, you’ll end up paying a terrible price.

With increased cyber security compliances and standards being implemented throughout the world, you’re going to find that outdated equipment will no longer be permitted—and for good reason! For example, if you want to work with the US federal government, it is required that old equipment be removed or isolated. Eventually, companies will see that the cost to maintain that equipment will become higher than the value it brings.

As hackers become more creative in how they launch an attack, it’s important to remember that you can’t wait for the attack to happen before you address it.

Let me share one more reason *not* to wait to upgrade. We were working with a major company that had old analog lines for connecting to their remote sites—costing $35,000 per month to maintain. The worst part was, there was only ONE man at AT&T who knew how to implement or maintain those lines because they had been discontinued almost *fifteen years* *earlier*.

One day while awaiting support on one of those lines, the client received a call from AT&T. The engineer, the only man that knew how to run those lines, had just died of a heart attack. At that moment, we started to scramble to get those lines replaced for the company as quickly as possible. It was not a pleasant experience for them, but it was a valuable lesson learned.

Constant upgrades are expensive, and we’re all tempted to just keep using what we already have. It’s a cost that we just don’t want to pay, but I urge you to remember the drug company example I shared earlier. They didn’t want to pay $4 million dollars to make the necessary upgrades, so they wound up having to pay $45 million dollars after the system completely crashed. As the old adage goes, “*An ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure*.”

CHAPTER 4 

# WHAT’S THE WORD?

![](images/chapter-4.jpg)

Passwords! Passwords! Passwords everywhere! I can’t possibly think of another one! More than eight characters, upper case, lower case, numbers, special characters… aaah! How many passwords do we *really* need in life? We’ve got passwords for our bank accounts, Netflix, HBO Max, and Disney Plus. Then there’s online services, phone services, Uber, food delivery, brand loyalty accounts… Lord only knows what else! I have over 5,000 different passwords between all my clients that I need to remember (crazy, right?), so it’s only reasonable that I would want just one password to rule them all— and of course, every cyber security person will tell you that’s a *very* bad idea.

It's just not functional or practical to try to remember the dozens (or hundreds) of passwords that you use each day. To make matters worse, many sites have auto login functionality that allows you to save (and then forget) your password, so you don’t have to enter it each day.

It’s important to remember that passwords are your first line of defense. While on one hand they keep much of your information safe, there’s also just too many to realistically remember. I remember when I worked in the pharmaceutical industry, we had a policy requiring us to change our passwords every ninety days. What made it even worse was the fact that you couldn’t use any portion of the previous passwords in the new password.

This was a great idea in theory, but a really bad one in practice. You see, people aren’t very good at remembering passwords, especially dozens (or hundreds) of them that are changing every ninety days. Ultimately, we’re going to forget them.

I still remember spending at least one full day at the end of each quarter resetting people’s passwords because they forgot what they had changed them to. What made matters even worse was that they then started writing their passwords down and sticking them on their computer screens or trying to be clever by “hiding” their password under their keyboard! Both are terrible options and should *never* be done.

Passwords are vital to your data protection, but it’s unrealistic to think you can remember dozens or hundreds of unique and increasingly complex passwords. This is the perfect time to talk about using a password management system. I use LastPass to manage my passwords, but there are hundreds of different options available to you.

The advantage of a password management system is that you can create (or have the system auto-generate) complex passwords for any site you visit that requires one. The password management system automatically remembers and stores this login information so that you don’t have to.

Do you already have an account or login information for a site? No problem! You can add and store your credentials for each site and account that you have. The next time you visit that site, the password management system automatically enters your saved username and password for the site for you. Gone are the days of getting locked out of your accounts because of too many failed login attempts!

Now whenever you visit a site, you don’t have to remember hundreds of different logins and passwords—the password management system takes care of that for you automatically. All you have to do is set a really complex master password so that you have the strongest defense against hackers or other cyber attacks. For example, I’ve previously used a master password like this: *Chu43%#lusknyHe*. For any would-be hackers, don’t get too excited, this is obviously not my *actual* password.

Since I started using “crazy” passwords like this that are not related to anything (e.g., not using my dog’s name or the street I live on as passwords), I have not had any problems with hacks to my accounts. Even if someone were able to hack my account, each of my accounts has a unique and complex password, so if they hacked one, they won’t be able to gain access to my other accounts through the hacked one (which is why you should never use the same password for multiple accounts).

Recently at a BNI (Business Network International) meeting, a member shared they had their business email got hacked. The hackers were sending out invoices, impersonating the business, and asking for ACH payments. The hacker’s email said that moving forward, the company would *only* be accepting ACH payments and no longer accepting checks. This hack was discovered because one of the customers was confused and called the actual company and wanted to know why they were no longer accepting checks.

Once the hack was discovered, all passwords were immediately changed, but it was discovered that this man used that same initial password on other accounts, including his banks. He told the group, “*I just can’t keep all the different passwords straight in my mind.*”

He’s not alone. A lot of us struggle with the multitude of passwords that we’re required to have in daily life, and it’s just not practical or realistic to think that we’re going to remember all of them on our own. Ask yourself this: If someone hacked into your bank account and cleared out all your money, what would you do? What if someone contacted all your clients without you knowing, and redirected all their payments away from you and into their accounts? How devastating would that be to your business?

I ask you these questions so that you take your passwords seriously. Instead of writing down all your passwords on sticky notes (please don’t), you can have a password management system securely store them for you. Having complex and secure passwords that you don’t have to remember is a win for you *and* your company. Trust me, it will also be a win for your employees because no one likes to keep changing passwords every ninety days and then trying to remember them!

There’s one last thing to remember in the password journey: your master password for the password management system is your “golden ticket.” This master password is the one that opens up and unlocks access to all your other passwords. In other words, the master password “opens the gates to the castle.” You don’t want greedy barbarians (hackers) to come knocking down those gates and pillaging and destroying everything you worked so hard for, do you?

If a hacker is able to hack your master password because you didn’t take the complexity of passwords seriously, then they will have gained access to all your other passwords as well. This master password needs to be complex and needs to be protected. Personally, I write down my master password and keep it in a locked safe, or security box.

You may be thinking “*But Ken, you’re a technology and cyber security guy, why do you do this?*” Great question! Here’s the answer: If you keep your password on your computer, you need to keep it in an encrypted file in order to keep it safe. That encrypted file will need a password and you will need to keep that password somewhere safe. Do you notice a trend here? Where do you keep the encrypted file password—in another file? To date, there is nothing as secure as safely-stored paper. So, having one master password written on paper and stored securely, such as in a safe, is a strong step to take in increasing your cyber security… just keep it to one.

It's also important to remember that passwords are just the *first* line of defense, not the *last*. In the next chapter, I’ll talk about multi-factor authentication (MFA) as an additional layer to enhance your cyber security.

CHAPTER 5 

# DON’T FEAR THE FACTOR

![](images/chapter-5.jpg)

We all try to forget concepts like "factors" from our high school math classes. It was a dark time when people would throw strange symbols like "*f(x)*" at you and say complex phrases like "*factor that function*," or worse, "*factor that polynomial*." It made no sense and was undeniably confusing. You may be led to ask, "*Why would I want to do multi-factor authentication when I didn’t like doing a single factor in math class?*"

Let me start by saying that this is *not* your high school factor. Multi-factor authentication, or MFA in nerd-speak, is another way to keep people out of your important files or accounts. I’ll admit, it can be annoying, but it’s an effective tool to use.

When you hear multi-factor authentication, what they’re talking about is having two or more different ways to confirm your identity before gaining access to your account. An example of multi-factor authentication would be entering your password, as well as entering a code that the system sends via text message to your cell phone. Many of you may already be familiar with this process when logging into your bank accounts.

In this way, you have your password, and you have a code text to your cell phone – something a hacker wouldn’t have access to… unless they stole your phone as well. Not only would they have to hack your password to your account, but then they’d also have to steal your phone, which many today have biometric (face or fingerprint ID) or numeric (passcode) screen locks. So, you can see how much more challenging this makes it for someone else to gain access to your accounts using multi-factor authentication.

While this extra step can be annoying, especially when you’re trying to login to an account and you forgot your phone at home on the kitchen table, just remember: this extra step of authentication makes it that much more challenging for hackers to gain access to your accounts and waltz off with all your hard-earned money or sensitive data.

It’s also important to note that hackers aren’t just a threat from other countries. Hackers can be found in *every* country, at *every* level (from the weekend dabbler to the professional), and with a variety of malicious and exploitative motives. Who knows, the hacker that takes your company for everything you’ve got could even be that weird kid down the street that’s just trying to prove to his hacker friends that he can. You just never know.

Perhaps you’re already familiar with multi-factor authentication but have seen reference to 2FA, or two-factor authentication. For most of us, they mean the same thing, but there are times when you may need more than just two modes of authentication to gain access to your accounts or information.

For example, let’s say you’re a secret government spy, or work in a top-secret lab (or any number of other scenarios). In order to gain access to your lab or secret files, you may need to enter your password, enter the code text to your phone, and may even need to have those baby blue eyes scanned or your fingerprint analyzed and confirmed. In these cases, there are more than two ways to authenticate your identity. For the rest of us regular folk, MFA or 2FA is sufficient.

Let me share with you a personal story of why 2FA or MFA truly makes a difference. I’d like to take you back to 2012, a time when we were all still blissfully living with the belief that no one was trying to hack and steal our stuff.

Lucy, my boss and wife, as well as the CEO of our company, decided to try to reinstall her email client. Like most people, she went online and Googled ‘Outlook’ and proceeded to go to the first site that came up in the search. So far so good, right? Afterall, millions of people do this very same thing every day.

Clicking on this first site, she let the magical internet fairies take her to the land of Microsoft so she could reinstall Outlook—or so she thought. She didn’t notice that the link she *actually* clicked on was for M**a**crosoft, *not* M**i**crosoft.

There on the page was the Outlook download button, just waiting to be clicked. Everything looked legit and appeared to be Microsoft’s official site, so she clicked the download button, not realizing she was downloading a file from the hackers. The next step was to enter her email and password, which she did—again, not realizing that she was on a hacker’s site.

Within moments of entering her email and password, the hackers began their attack. If that wasn’t bad enough, my technical team and I were in the process of rolling out multi-factor authentication that same afternoon. Had we done this just hours sooner, the entire attack would have been avoided.

You may be thinking, “*You’re a cyber security company. Why on earth would you tell people that your CEO was hacked?*” Why do I share this? Because *every* company, eventually, will face hacks or other cyber attacks. *Every*. *Company*. What’s really important here is how a company *responds* to a cyber attack. Do you, or those in your company, know what to do if you’re cyber attacked?

In our case we, we immediately implemented our email hack protocol. Within 10 minutes of Lucy’s hacker download, we noticed really strange behavior from her account. Within 15 minutes, we had Lucy start changing her passwords, contacting people about the hack, and fixing the changes that the hackers were making within her account.

Even though we were able to respond so quickly to the attack, it didn’t stop hundreds of emails from being sent out by the hackers. As you can imagine, we had a *lot* of fun (insert sarcasm here) calling all those people to inform them that our email was hacked. It did, however, show the value of a calm and rapid response. Fortunately, no one clicked on any of the links in the emails sent out by the hackers, and we were able to shut down their entire attack within 20 minutes.

If Lucy would have had MFA enabled on her account, the hackers would have been stopped from being able to access her account without the second method of authentication (the code texted to her cell phone). This means that the attack would never have happened; the emails would never have been sent out, and we wouldn’t have had to call hundreds of clients to inform them of the attack.

Now you’re probably wondering how to activate MFA. This can vary depending on the application and how it’s deployed, but most of the time you can find directions in Bing or Google. For example, you can simply type “setup Facebook MFA” in the search bar to get step-by-step instructions for how to enable MFA on your account. If you’re trying to enable MFA connected to your bank or other financial institution, I would suggest contacting them directly and asking them to help you.

I also want to mention this: If you have a bank, financial institution, or other financial organization and they *do not* support MFA/2FA… RUN! Run hard and fast away from them!

I worked with a nonprofit that had their financial information stolen in a cyber attack. When asked why the company wasn’t using MFA, their answer was, “*We didn’t feel like we needed it*.” Well, $45,000 later, this nonprofit was wishing otherwise!

I strongly suggest using MFA on *all* your bank accounts, your business emails, file storage areas, and anything else that you feel is important. It’s also important to remember to use MFA with your social media accounts. You don’t want to wake up one day to see that someone posted pictures of Hitler, Stalin, or Yoko Ono to your account, do you? Do you think your reputation would ever recover? Your company’s reputation could suffer even more by hackers posting damaging photos, links, or other items to your social media accounts.

MFA gives you an additional layer of security that will help you sleep better at night. Don’t fear the factor; instead, embrace it.

# EMAIL, WHY IS IT ALWAYS EMAIL?

![](images/image-06indy-1.jpg)

At some point, you may have asked yourself, “*Why is my company always harping on my emails?*” Well, there’s a good reason for this. Phishing attacks, named for trying to “catch” you like a fish, are reported by almost 97% of companies, and almost 20% of people will click on a phishing email. As you can see, this is a *massive* problem that has the potential to hurt all of us.

I’m going to tell you a story that most cyber security experts would never share. Most cyber security experts like to give the illusion that we’re perfect and never get hacked, but I’m going to share a story where I fell victim to the exact attack that I caution people to avoid.

One day, I received an email from “Target” stating that I had earned a $5 gift card. I was so excited that I just clicked on the link and awaited my wonderful reward. I should have followed my normal routine and checked to see if the link was truly from Target. I should have made sure that I scanned the link to see if it had malware attached to it. I should have done a lot of things, but I didn’t. Instead, I let my excitement about getting free stuff overwhelm my normal protections and security measures.

You can imagine my less-than-stellar feelings when I had a very nasty screen pop up on my computer to inform me that I was ransomwared. This happened early in the ransomware era, so most people hadn’t enjoyed the “fun” of having their computers and files locked out and held hostage for a ransom.

My first reaction was utter anger at myself. I *knew* to check links, which I found out was coming from Targ**a**t.com… notice the second ‘a’ rather than an ‘e’ in the name? I *knew* to scan all links before clicking through. I *knew* to never click on a link in an email if it’s offering something for free. Unfortunately, I fell victim to the most primitive of impulses – and hackers hope for this. Excitement and fear both work at the most primal of our reactions, and sometimes, can lead us to do foolish things.

In my case, this attack was mostly annoying because I had a good “backup buddy,” which we’ll talk about later. I was able to rebuild my system and recover all my data in a short time. It was, however, a very painful experience and one that made sure I didn’t make that same mistake twice!

So, what should you do? First, always remember the adage: “*If it sounds too good to be true, it most likely is.*” What this means is, if you weren’t expecting an email about a free gift card, then chances are that the email is just there to get you on their hook. Do you want to be a ‘phish’ on their plate? Don’t click on links in emails you did not expect, even if you’re feeling really excited about it.

Next, if you get an email saying that Facebook, Twitter, TikTok or some other social media platform is urging you to click on something, don’t do it.

If you do get a social media email asking you to click on something or requesting you do something, go directly to your social media account, properly login, and then see if you have a request or message. Using emails to interact with social media users is one of the largest ways hackers are getting you to click on their malicious links.

You’ll also want to make sure that you have anti-spam and anti-phishing software to help keep you protected. By having filtering systems like these, you can cut down on 90% of the attack emails. This has helped so many of our clients, as well as myself because apparently, I just can’t help myself when it comes to clicking links for free things!

Now what do you do if you find yourself in a similar situation—you’ve excitedly clicked on a link for something before thinking it through first, and now your email or account is under attack? Here are some steps from the Federal Trade Commission:

1.  The first thing is always to **RELAX**. If you do things in a panic, you *will* make mistakes. Count to 10 and use that time to recover and center yourself. Once you’re in a more relaxed state, you can proceed to the next step.
2.  **Log into your account and change your password**. If the hackers have changed your password, then contact your email administrator to get your password changed. In your company, this is most likely your computer guy/gal that is hiding out in that dark room with all the whirling sounds coming from it. If it’s a personal email account, you may have to go online and contact their customer support.
3.  **Make sure that your anti-virus/anti-malware/anti-spam is up-to-date**. If you have not paid for your anti-virus in a while, it’s time to bite the bullet and get it renewed. Also, make sure that you have updated your anti-virus. I cannot tell you the number of times we are bringing on a new client and discover that they have not updated their anti-virus in months or even years!
4.  **Check the settings on your email**. When an email is hacked, the hackers will often setup a Forwarding Rule that sends all the replies to your trash folder so that you never see the replies as people ask if you meant to send that strange email. Each email system is different as to where they keep the Rules, so you may need to ask for help with this one.
5.  **Ask your provider to restore any lost information**. Many providers will have a backup and can restore your emails. If you are working in a company, ask the person in that dark whirling room or give your support company a call. If it is a personal account, go to the website and look for a support contact.
6.  **Contact your clients and contacts**. This is the hardest step of all. You’ll need to reach out to each of your contacts and inform them that you were hacked. This was very embarrassing for me when I had to do it, but to my surprise, all my clients were grateful for the heads up. No one but me said what an amazing fool I had been!

When dealing with email hacks, it’s vital to always act quickly, don’t panic, and to minimize any damage that may be done. The sooner you act, the less time hackers have to hurt you, your clients, company, or friends.

# BARBARIANS AT THE GATE

![](images/image-07-3.jpg)

As someone with an interest in history, I’ve often thought about how the Romans must have felt with the Germanic tribes always charging southward to storm their cities. The only barrier between their normal lives and the marauding hordes was their city gate. Now, let’s think about the difference between the city gates of Rome and the city gates of Capena, 32 kilometers away. Rome was this massive city with many powerful and defended gates, while Capena had just a single gate to hold back the enemy. The gate in Capena is still standing today and is an incredible marvel to see.

As small businesses, we face this same thing every day: the threat of hordes of cyber attackers coming southward to ravage our beautiful land—I mean, computers… and pillage our valuable information. We may even begin to feel like only the large companies and corporations are able to build the best defenses to protect themselves from attackers.

Larger companies *are* able to spend mounds of money trying to defend themselves, implementing the best technologies and having entire departments focused on cyber security. However, it’s equally important to remember that even companies like Target, Facebook, Adobe, Capital One, AT&T, BP, and Apple have all experienced cyber security breaches and attacks. Financial institutions aren’t immune either. I’m not sure you could find a single bank or other financial institution anywhere that hasn’t faced at least one cyber-attack.

Knowing this, you might be thinking, “*If they can’t protect their companies from cyber-attacks, what am I supposed to do to protect mine?*” I want you to take a moment and think about what a gate is used for. Gates allow the good or desirable people to come in and keeps the bad or undesirable people out. How does it keep the bad people out? Simple: it blocks their entrance. If you close off a gate, the only way through is to knock it down, which takes large amounts of effort and time. Gates also keep the good people out as well. How do you let the good people back in? You need to open the gates.

Hackers know this. If you open the gates, it doesn’t take much effort to get through (compared to trying to barge through a closed gate). This is why hackers use items like emails and bad web links to gain access to your network. In order to access web pages and other cloud-based sites, you have to open the gates, so to speak. It’s only for a little while, but if you open the gates up to the wrong person, they can get in and start to attack from the inside.

This is where your gate, what we call a *firewall* in nerd-speak, comes into play. Most modern firewalls are effective in keeping undesirable things out as long as no one opens up the gates. We’re constantly using the internet for everything and love going in and out of our little “village.” Each time we open a connection to the outside world, we create a road between our little village and the site we’re visiting. If that site is a good and secure site, we’re safe and happily getting what we need. If that site is bad or unsecure, then we’re opening the gates to potential hordes of armies to come rushing in.

Now, what if you had a guard watching each person that came through your gate? We see this in movies all the time; a guard goes up to the wagon and asks for papers from each person to make sure that they should be entering the village, before the gates are opened. In our modern world, that guard is called an IDS (Intrusion Detection System) and IPS (Intrusion Protection System). These guards stand watch to help detect if there are “undesirables” trying to follow you back into your village. It’s important to have these guards on our gates, but it’s also important to remember that any time you open the gates, bad people will try to get in.

We had a client that had a server within their “secure village” that was behind a good firewall. The problem was that they accessed this server from outside of the village, their office. Until they began working with us, they had always connected directly into their server from outside the village, meaning that the gate was always open. When we started to work with them, we told them that they would need to create a defended path to that server so that when they were outside the village, they would be protected.

This defended pathway is called a VPN (Virtual Private Network) and it keeps the information that is being shared hidden, so that only you and the computer you are talking to can read it.

The client had never experienced a breach before, so they were taking the approach that their village was so small that no one would ever try to attack them. They told us, “*Why would anyone want to go after my little company?*” It’s a statement we’ve heard far too often from many small businesses.

It was now my job to show them that being small didn’t protect them from the barbarians at the gates. I extracted a log of all activity going through the gate, their firewall, and in just *one week* they had 356,002 attacks on their server! The hackers were using a brute force attack to try gaining access into the server.

To really bring home the point, I showed them that 104,321 of the attacks had come from Russia, and 38,342 had come from China. What really opened their eyes was the fact that 25,342 of the attacks came from within the United States, with the remaining attacks coming from various other countries around the world. As you can see, the attacks had come from all sides, both domestically and abroad. This helped them to understand that they were *not* too small to be attacked, as well as reinforcing the importance of ensuring you are properly protected.

So, what’s a business to do? As a small business, you need to create a layered defense. This layered defense starts with a firewall, the gate to your village. You’ll want to ensure you’re purchasing and using a firewall that supports IPS/IDS service, generally as an annual subscription. It’s also important to make sure that the company deploying it understands how to configure and setup your firewall.

You may also want to consider *geofencing*. This is a process where you block computers from a specific region or country from ever getting through your firewall. It is important to remember that this means the gate is *always* closed to that region or country, so don’t geofence a country that you need to work with or conduct business. If you conduct business with China, for example, I would not suggest geofencing China. If you did, no traffic from China would ever get through, and that would be bad for your business.

Now that you have learned about your gates, the firewall, you have a good start to defending your network. Keep in mind, this isn’t the end of your defenses—it’s just the beginning. You’ll want to have a layering of defenses to keep your team and your business safe.

# BACKUP BUDDIES

![](images/image-08-2.jpg)

Do you remember when you were a child on a field trip and your teacher would tell you to find a buddy to partner up with? Do you remember why you were asked to do that? It’s because this was a simple way to keep you safe and accounted for. By having two people partnered and looking out for each other, it was much safer than trying to keep track of children running around in every direction on their own. Backups are the same way; you always want to make sure that you have a “backup buddy.” This is a safe place that you have your data stored so you can recover it if you fall prey to a cyber-attack or your computer is stolen or lost.

Let me share a story with you that demonstrates the power of backups. One of our clients, a large nonprofit, does most of its grant-writing and the bulk of its fundraising in the month of June. As you may suspect, this means that fundraising time is very important to their success and continued operation for the remainder of the year. For anyone that knows anything about nonprofit fundraising, you know that it’s a *very* stressful time.

As it happened, I was also out of town at a cyber security convention the first week in June. The first day of the convention had finished and I was heading back to my hotel room. It was an exciting day of presentations and discussions, and I was flush with new knowledge of all the great cyber security products and services that our company would be reviewing over the next few months.

By the time I got settled in for the night, it was close to midnight. Suddenly, my cell phone rang. Figuring it was my wife wanting to say goodnight, I answered the phone. I’m glad I didn’t answer the phone by saying “*Hi, honey*,” because it wasn’t my wife that was calling, but the administrator of this nonprofit!

His voice was laced with concern as he asked if I was still awake. “*What’s wrong?*” I asked, figuring he wouldn’t be calling me this late on a Saturday just to see how I was doing. I have to admit, my heart sank a little when I heard his voice because I wasn’t sure what was going to be wrong, and I was 800 miles away.

“*We’ve lost the database server and all of the reports for the fundraising were on that server*!” His voice was short and panicked. “*I’ve tried everything but I’m not able to get it started again*.”

I let him know that I wasn’t in Cleveland (where he was located), but that I would remote in and see what I could do. I knew he was in trouble because they had a team of about twenty people pulling all-nighters to get these reports and grants in on time. If they weren’t able to get these in by the deadlines, it meant they would have to make layoffs before the end of the year. It was not a thought any of us wanted to consider.

Sitting at my desk in the hotel room, I contemplated my options. It was clear that I was not able to access the server because of a hardware failure, so that option was off the table. I had to look at alternatives. Fortunately, I had recently implemented a hybrid cloud backup. This is a fancy nerd way of saying that I had a backup system in their office that made copies in the cloud.

Quickly logging into our portal, I activated the Virtual Server Recovery feature. This created a virtual copy of the database server that they would be able to use until I was back in town. The entire process ended up taking about 20 minutes from call to recovery. Before signing off, I asked the administrator to confirm that everything was up and working again. With a huge sigh of relief in his voice, he said, “*Yes, we’re back up and running. Thanks so much for all your help – this really saved us!*”

When I got back in town later that week, I went in and fixed their broken server. With the Virtual Server Recovery I implemented, they were able to continue with their fundraising and grant applications, securing all the grant money and funding that they needed for the year. Everyone praised the administrator for keeping things running, and he thanked me for being there when he needed me most. All of this was because of the power of my backup buddy.

How do you pick a backup buddy? Let me give you some guidance on this.

When picking a backup buddy, you want to determine *what* you are protecting. If you only need to protect files, such as pictures or word documents, many online services are available. For example, you have access to unlimited backup of pictures if you have an Amazon Prime account.

If you have critical applications that you use, then you’ll want to look for a more active backup solution. A hybrid backup with data in the cloud and on an appliance in your office may be necessary. One advantage to these hybrid backups is the fact that if the Internet is down for some reason, you’re still getting a backup of your system, it’s just not getting copied to the cloud.

When selecting a backup, here are a few things you’ll want to look for:

1.  That the backup is encrypted, meaning that it’s set up and protected so that only you can read it, both while it is being backed up, sometimes called “in transit,” and when it is sitting in the cloud, also called “at rest.”
2.  That the backup is ransomware resistant. Later in this book, I’ll tell you a story about a backup that was not ransomware resistant and what a disaster that caused.
3.  How long is the retention period? How many backups do you have? In many cases, you may need to keep backups for a year or more for recovery reasons. When I was in the pharmaceutical industry, I had to keep records for seven years.
4.  Can it start a virtual recover? This means having a way to start the computer virtually, like I did for that nonprofit I mentioned earlier. This is a very quick way to recover in a disaster.
5.  Can it do a bare metal restore? This is a term meaning, if you lose the entire server like the nonprofit did, are you are able to restore the server exactly as it was during the last backup? This is a really nice feature to have. Otherwise, your technical team may need to rebuild computers and then restore the data that is needed, and that can take valuable time, money, and resources.

One last thing I feel is vitally important to mention to you: Always check that your backups work on a regular basis.

I was working with a doctor’s office many years ago. They had stacks of drives they said were their backups. Each night, the receptionist would plug in a USB drive, and each morning, she would unplug it and put it in the rotation stack. I was quite impressed with her diligence. Unfortunately, when I checked on the backup itself, I discovered that it had stopped running five years earlier! Though they had performed the proper process for five years, they were never actually making any backups.

Had I not been on-site working to bring them onboard with our services, they may not have discovered they had no backups until it was too late. This is why testing your backups regularly is so important.

Backup buddies are vital for your business. It’s only a matter of time before you’re faced with a hack or a cyber attack like ransomware that can cause you to lose access to all your data. A backup buddy can help to reverse that, so please remember your schooling and keep your backup buddy close!

# WHAT? A PATCH?

![](images/image-09.jpg)

Some of you may have heard about Microsoft’s Patch Tuesday. A few of you may have even heard about the problems it can cause. The real question is, “*What is patching, and why do I need to do it?*”

I began my career as a programmer for a very large energy company, writing the code that moved all the money to pay everyone in the company. I was young and cocky, and to be honest, a little naïve. Despite that, I was moving billions of dollars each year to ensure that everyone in nine different companies received their paycheck on time and accurately.

There are millions of people out there just like I was, coding and controlling everything from your TV to your car, to the systems that keep you alive in the hospital. Almost everything we use today is created on code, the written commands that control a computer.

Let’s try something here. If you’re a business owner, I want you to take a few moments to try imagining *every* possible scenario and event that could go wrong or happen to your company in the next five years. *Every. Possible. Scenario.* If you’re not a business owner, try imagining every possible thing that could go wrong in your career or in your current position over the next five years.

Are you thinking of every scenario? How long is your list? Do you think you’ll have a 100% accurate assessment of everything that could possibly go wrong? This is what I was expected to do each time I wrote code! I had to try to guess *everything* that code could possibly need to do so that I could program for it. Let’s just say that I didn’t always succeed.

On one particular occasion, I was assigned to review code from an earlier programmer. As I was going through the code, I found something. The code was used to move the money from the general bank account to the payroll bank account each week. This energy company didn’t want to have money sitting in a checking account longer than it needed to because they could make money on the interest, and that was a *lot* of money.

Here was the crux of the situation: the code had been written with a bug. The prior coder had made a mistake that resulted in the program stopping before it would move the money to pay the employees of a large division, which meant that the money was not being transferred at all. This resulted in almost $70 million in missed payroll and cost anywhere from hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars in penalties with each payroll cycle. Because of the size of the energy company, this was just recorded as bank fees, but it was costing the company millions of dollars each year.

I then wrote a correction, a patch, to that code and deployed the new version. This newly adjusted code then went into production and the proper amount of money was deposited into the payroll account. This patch resulted in millions of dollars in savings for the company each year.

Not all patches are to fix errors or vulnerabilities (code that allows hackers to get into your systems). Many times, patches are to enhance features. For example, let’s say you have to click through two different screens in order to save a file and this annoyed you enough to complain to the software company. Well, many companies actually listen to and respond to those complaints or suggestions and will release a patch that. In this example, the patch would place the save button directly on the main page. Now you can save your document or file with a single click right from the home page.

All programs will require patching at one point or another, but not all patches are helpful all the time. Sometimes, patches can cause problems. For example, there was a recent patch to update the video image on a computer. For some people, this updated patch caused their computers to crash and stop working abruptly.

In cases like this, a RMM (Remote Management and Monitoring) system can help. With a third-party company offering this service, they can ensure that necessary patches are deployed, as well as protecting against and even reversing bad patches. Like most things in life, this isn’t a perfect system, but it does give you greater security and control in patch management.

These RMM tools are used by many IT companies to help control and manage your devices. As the name implies, they’re used to remotely monitor your systems and devices, and to give access to an engineer if needed. These engineers can access your systems with you when it’s required, with no travel, no fuss, no muss, and you don’t even have to get out of your PJs if you’re working from home! How great is that?

Another advantage of an RMM tool is that it can track and confirm your inventory of both hardware and software. This means that if you get a request from a big company like Microsoft to confirm what software is being used, you can get a quick answer. Microsoft calls these *software audits*, and they love to do them unexpectedly. It also means that you can see what programs your team may have deployed, so you can make sure they’re using legal software and not installing things without proper permissions.

Lastly, the RMM tools will help to keep applications up-to-date as well. In addition to your Windows or MAC Operating System, you may have Office, QuickBooks, or other software that needs to be patched; RMM tools keep this process regularly updated.

# ALWAYS IN WAITING

![](images/image-10-1.jpg)

The world is changing, and hackers are changing along with it. They’ve learned we’ve put in defenses, and they work to get around them. It’s a constant battle back and forth between the hackers and the defenders.

A new method of attacking your systems is to put in place what is known as a “sleeper agent,” a program that lays dormant in your computer, waiting to attack you at a later date. These are very tricky because it means the attack or infection will also be on any backups that you take. By having the infection included on your backup, when you restore from that backup, it also restores the virus or ransomware you are trying to remove. It’s devilishly clever and very disruptive and destructive.

You may be wondering, “*Why would they do something like this?*” It’s simple— our cyber attack protections have been getting better, and if the hackers want to continue extorting people for money, they have to up their game. In this case, upping their game means finding more devious ways of getting past our defenses.

These delayed attacks can be spread across all your data and into all your backups. This could mean that putting back the backup just puts back the attack (say *that* five times fast!). Hackers have become more patient as well. Some will even wait up to a year before attacking so that they’re assured all of your backups have been compromised, making you desperate and more willing to pay them to (hopefully) restore your data.

Imagine: you’ve been doing a great job of sticking with your backup buddy, and all of your files are resting safe and secure in their data beds. Suddenly, you’re hit with a cyber attack. You’re okay; you’ve always done your backups. You check them, manage them, and keep them safe because it’s your precious data.

Then, the cyber attacker attacks you. They encrypt all your valuable data, followed by a demand for thousands of dollars to unlock it for you. You’re not worried; you’ve been performing proper backups and you’re confident that everything is safe and secure. Your backups are off-site and have been protecting you for a year. You laugh in the face of the foolish attackers—hahaha!

The backup restores your files and systems and you’re ready to go. The next morning, all your files are locked and unavailable to you again. WHAT?! How did this happen? You restored from the good backup. Okay, no problem. You can just restore from a few days ago. Night comes and you sleep like a baby knowing that you’ll be ok this time and that in the morning, everything will be restored back to normal. Unfortunately, the next morning, you find that all your files are locked again. What are you to do?

Let’s start by talking about what’s going on. Back in the good ol’ days, hackers would just attack you when you clicked on a bad email or a compromised link. After clicking, you’d get that screaming Microsoft alert notice, or your files would instantly become locked. The resulting attack was immediate and traumatic and is now addressed with our current safety and security protections; good news for you and me, bad news for the hackers.

Unfortunately, more and more cyber attacks aren’t happening immediately. Instead, hackers are putting programs onto your computer that then go to “sleep.” When it’s initially installed, it doesn’t do anything harmful or noticeable, so it goes undetected, just waiting to be called into action.

These programs can lay in wait for many months, silently biding their time. As they wait patiently in the shadows, they’re being copied to all the backups you take, making it harder to root them out. Again, it’s done nothing bad or malicious so far, so it’s being ignored by the “security guards.”

Months, or even a year later, this seemingly benign little program reveals its true nature. It begins its attack, either by locking your data, sharing your sensitive information, or some other nefarious thing that you don’t wish it to do.

The hackers know that you, being a responsible cyber citizen, have good backups and have protection. You go to your backups and recover the lost information. But wait! Within that backup, there is a quiet little program waiting to ride back into your environment to wreak havoc. As soon as you restore your backup, it attacks again, *from* *within your backup*, because that’s where it’s been hiding and being copied to for so many months. Do you see the dilemma?

How do you stop these sleeper agents? This is where you want to watch them as they come in. Operating systems, like Windows and Mac OS, will assume that if you authorize the program, it should be allowed to install. The hackers take advantage of this and put their sleeper agents into other install software. This way, when you click on ‘yes I want to install’… it just comes along for the ride.

This is where an application control program can help. It looks at all the programs that should be running on your computer and looks for ones that do not appear to be doing anything or are acting out of the ordinary. It then flags these programs and sections them off, not allowing them to make changes to your vital information.

The system does this by learning what you do and how your computer works. It then watches each program to see if it’s working in that same way. If it is, then everything is good and can carry on its merry way. But, if an agent is being too quiet or starts to ask to make changes to your files that is out of the ordinary, it is shut down, preventing it from accessing any of your files.

It is important to remember that cyber attackers are always looking for ways to hurt you and make you pay. Cyber security is never going to be a one-and-done situation, so you should always be adapting— the hackers are.

CHAPTER 11 

# THE BOSS SAID TO SEND IT

![](images/image-11-2.jpg)

Have you ever received an email from your boss asking you to purchase a gift card for her daughter… but you know your boss doesn’t have a daughter? Or an email asking you to pay an invoice that you never received? Phishing attacks happen *millions of times* each day. Some are just general attacks that hackers send out to thousands of individuals seeing if anyone bites, and some of them are very directed and detailed to mislead you into sending money or providing vital information.

These attacks are called *phishing attacks* because they are ‘fishing’ to get your information. Often, they’re hoping you take the bait and click on that link or answer their phone call so they can swindle you into sharing your sensitive information. I wish it wasn’t the case, but you’d be amazed at how successful these attacks are.

One specific form of phishing is call *spear phishing*. This is when a hacker uses a targeted attack to try to get you to share your information or take your money. Many of us have experienced this form of phishing attack. Using the previous example, you receive an email that appears to be from your boss asking you to purchase a $5 gift card for her daughter. Because the email appears to come directly from your boss, you’re less likely to question its validity. In this example, purchasing a $5 gift card may seem minor, but these kinds of phishing attacks can lead to massive damages.

Here's an example of a spear phishing attack that cost a local company over one hundred thousand dollars. It was a normal Tuesday at a large manufacturing site. The day was warm, and the sun was shining through the windows of the second-floor accounting department. On the surface, it appeared to be like any other day, with payments being made and transactions taking place. This, however, was not a normal day. The accounting department was about to get an email that would cost them dearly.

The accounts payable director, we’ll call her Mary, was going through emails addressed to Accounts Payable. It wasn’t unusual for her to get thirty or forty emails a day, largely about payment issues.

Mary was reading through the emails when she noticed one from a vendor that her company worked with on a regular basis. The email stated that there was an outstanding invoice balance in the amount of $138,000 dollars that needed to be paid immediately and listed the bank account to send the payment.

Mary had just a moment of hesitation as she reviewed the bank account. It didn’t look like their normal bank account, but she had twenty other issues to address at the same time. She was familiar with this vendor, so she assumed that the information was accurate and proceeded in processing the payment.

Clicking on the provided link, she started the cash transfer to the bank account. No flags were raised at the bank either because it was normal for them to transfer large sums of money like this.

Two days later, Mary checked with another clerk to attempt to find the missing invoice and immediately began to feel a knot tighten in her stomach. As they conducted their search, they were unable to find any outstanding invoices for $138,000. In a panic, they called the vendor and asked what the latest invoices were.

Waiting with anticipation, Mary watched for the email with the outstanding invoices, as well as the last few invoices paid amounts. The notification bell dinging in Outlook told her that the email had arrived. As she opened the Excel spreadsheet, her heart dropped. On reviewing both past and present invoices, there were none in the amount of $138,000. In fact, there were no outstanding invoices at all!

It was a devastating blow to Mary who then had to go to the CFO and inform him that she had just lost the company $138,000. This is when they called me.

We attempted to contact the bank about the wire transfer, but it was already too late; the money had been withdrawn and vanished.

Rest assured, no Accounts Payable clerks were harmed in the making of this cyber attack, but it was a gut-wrenching experience for both her and the CFO. That’s not to say that there can’t be upsides to cyber attacks, because there certainly are! In this case, it led to the implementation of a vital checks and balances protocol in payment processing.

What were some of those check points that we implemented? I’m glad that you asked!

1.  They required all payments to be connected to an invoice.
2.  If they received an invoice from a vendor that was not expected and over $1,000, they would call the vendor to confirm the amount.
3.  Any change of payment request would require a call to confirm.
4.  They created a list of approved vendor bank accounts. If a request was made to make an ACH payment to an account that was not on the list, there would be a phone call to confirm the change.
5.  They always used a known email address for the vendor to confirm emails, not the reply button.
6.  Lastly, if things didn’t feel right, they wouldn’t process the payment.

As you can see, many of the above steps require reaching out and speaking with someone directly to confirm any changes. A simple phone call could have saved this company over a hundred thousand dollars.

I wish I could say that this was an unusual and rare situation, but I have many stories of similar attacks that resulted in hundreds of thousands of dollars mistakenly sent to hackers. With as busy as we all are, and as many ‘fires’ as we attempt to put out each day, it’s easy to see how something like this happens. Unfortunately, not taking that extra step or listening to your gut feeling, could easily end up costing you a lot more than a few extra minutes.

In addition to the steps that this company implemented, you may want to purchase active anti-phishing and anti-virus email services. These services can help to remove such threats against your company.

I know we all laugh at the foolish people who’ve sent money to Nigerian princes that promise to send you millions of dollars if you’ll just send them a few thousand to process the transfer. While easy to laugh at, there are countless people every day that fall prey to similar phishing tactics.

Hackers are adjusting their attacks and making them more targeted and devious, as in the case of this company that I just shared. They will use the names of trusted people, like vendors or customers, to get you to give out bank accounts, email addresses, or other vital information. It is important to always remain vigilant.

# THE CALL CAME FROM INSIDE THE HOUSE

![](images/image-12-1.jpg)

You’re sound asleep when the familiar *ding* chimes on your phone, indicating you’ve just received a text message. You sleepily wipe the crusties from your eyes as you sluggishly open your text messages to reveal a message from an unknown number. “*Congratulations! You’ve just won a $25 gift card! Just click on the link to claim your prize!*” You don’t remember entering a drawing for it, but it’s $25, right? Why not just take the money and run? Besides, it’s a text message; it has to be safe, right? So, you click on the link to claim your prize…

You are now a victim of *smishing*. Smishing stands for *SMS phishing* (SMS is nerd-speak for texting) and is one of the newest trends in cyber attacks. Hackers realized that we were more protective of our emails, so they’re shifting to where people spend more of their time—on their phones. The worst part is that in a quest to get more of our time and attention, regular businesses are also starting to send more text messages as well. You can see how this could easily become overwhelming. This can lead us to do things that are bad for us, like clicking on links that play directly into the hacker’s hand.

If you’re experiencing a bombardment of text messages, it may be advantageous to do things like unsubscribe from known lists, report the others as spam, or block the numbers. It’s also important to remember never to click on a link in a text message from someone you don’t know. If you didn’t expect it and don’t know who it is, the best approach is to just ignore it. Hackers are always working to get you to let down your guard and give up your valuable information.

Oh, but wait—there’s more! They’re not done with your phone just yet. We also get to enjoy another form of phishing attack called *vishing* or *voice phishing*. These attacks start out as calls saying that the attacker is from Microsoft, or a government agency, or some other organization.

This is a great time to tell you about the time the “IRS” called me to “collect all the back taxes” that I apparently owed. It’s an interesting tale about how I was late on $5,000 in taxes and how they were going to “help me get right.”

I was just finishing work with a client when my cell phone rang; it was a blocked number and I answered it out of habit.

A pleasant voice with a foreign accent greeted me. “*Hello, this is Hamon with the IRS. I’m calling you because you owe us $5,000 and we need to arrange payment*.”

“*I owe you $5,000? What is this regarding?*” I responded a little confused because I was not the CFO of the company.

“*Yes, sir. You’re late with your taxes and must pay immediately*.”

It quickly dawned on me that this was *not* a call from the IRS and that I did *not* owe any amount in taxes. It did, however, give me a chance to have some fun.

“*Oh my! I didn’t realize that I was late with my payment. What should I do to make it right? I can go back to my office right now and have my accountant contact our IRS field agent and get him a payment*,” I said with a knowing smile.

With a sound of panic in his voice, he responded, “*No sir, you don’t want to do that. I’ll need you to settle this today. What I need you to do is make a Bitcoin purchase and then transfer it to our Accounts Collectible. I can walk you through the process. It’s very easy and you’ll get this very damaging tax violation removed*.”

I was quite certain that the IRS was *not* taking Bitcoin to settle tax liability; they like to support US currency. I quickly came up with a plan to have some fun and keep him from hurting anyone else, at least for about an hour, exactly the amount of time before I would be back in the office.

“*Bitcoin?! I thought that was what hackers used to extort money!*” I said with a false sense of fear.

“*Don’t worry sir, the IRS is only trying to make this process easier*,” he assured me.

“*I’m not sure what to do. Help me*,” I responded meekly.

At this point, he told me to open a browser and go to a specific link. He then started to lead me through the directions on how to purchase and transfer Bitcoins. I played completely clueless. Each step of the way, I did it wrong and pretended I couldn’t figure out where to click.

After almost an hour, he said that he could just log into my computer and do it for me.

I responded, “*I don’t think I should do that. I was told by my computer guy that I should never allow anyone into my computer*.”

With growing frustration, he shot back, “*If you don’t let me get in and help you with ordering the Bitcoin, then the IRS will have to foreclose on your house!*”

“*My house!*” I said stunned. “*That doesn’t seem right. I think I’m going to need your badge number, sir. I don’t think that an IRS agent is allowed to threaten my house like that*.”

“*Sir, we’re not allowed to give out our badge numbers to people*,” he said with an irritated tone.

“*Let me see if I understand: you’re a federal agent with a badge that you will not share with me? I think there’s something wrong with this*.” I should mention here that IRS agents don’t have badge numbers, but my attempted hacker didn’t know that. I was just pulling into the parking lot at the office, and it was time for me to end the call.

“*Sir!*” he said, clearly angry with me. “*I need to get into your computer right now so that I can get the money you owe transferred. Now please give me your email so I can send you the link*.”

“*I’ll gladly do that if you can do just one thing for me: tell me my full name*.”

“*What do you mean, sir?*” he asked.

“*If you are the IRS, then you know my full legal name. Please tell me it*.”

The phone went dead; no more Hamon from the IRS. Even though he did start to get a little rude towards the end, he certainly made the drive to the office fly by!

Here are some tips when receiving unexpected calls about collections, regardless of whether it’s the IRS or any other company or organization that’s calling.

1.  Always ask for information in writing. You have a right to get any outstanding bills in written form. The IRS will *never* directly call you if they have not sent a written notice.
2.  Never pay on-the-spot and over the phone. Some hackers have even gone so far as to offer to stop at your house to pick up payment! Can you imagine? This is *never* legitimate. Ever.
3.  Ask for a phone number to call them back, and then check to see if that phone truly does belong to a known company (rather than an unlisted or unverifiable number). You’ll also want to check your own files and records to see if you actually owe that company money.
4.  IRS agents don’t have badges. If you get a call from someone claiming to be from the IRS, ask them for their badge number. If they truly are with the IRS, they will know they don’t have badge numbers.
5.  Never make a payment you haven’t personally verified, especially if they ask for Bitcoin. Bitcoin is an *untraceable* method of taking your money, which is why hackers like to use it.

Vishing attacks can be very frustrating, and it only gets worse if you’ve had collections calls. Always remember to RELAX, and don’t let them panic you into acting. Don’t follow their instructions just because they say that you have to, or because they’re trying to pressure you or make you flustered (which are all tactics they use to get you to comply). Count to ten, remember the tips you’ve learned here, and then decide what steps to take next.

CHAPTER 13 

# WE ARE ALL HELD RANSOM

![](images/image-13-2.jpg)

No cyber security book would be complete without scaring you against the boogieman of all cyber attacks: the dreaded ransomware (cue dramatic music). Ransomware seems to receive more attention than all other cyber attacks combined. This is the same kind of attack that shut down an oil pipeline and hit so many cities and towns across the US. It seems like every time we turn on the news or scroll through our phones, we’re constantly hearing about this company or that company that had to pay some huge ransom amount to regain access to their files or accounts, or worse, they paid and were *never* given back access.

What is ransomware? Ransomware is anything that holds your information captive or hostage until you pay the ransom to recover it. In most cases, the hackers do this by encrypting all your files. I like to think of encrypting like having a piece of paper that you’ve written on, and then taking a thick black marker and rubbing it all over the paper in order to block out everything you’ve written. The things you wrote are still there, but you just can’t read it anymore. When you think of it this way, then you can understand ransomware as the hackers taking a black marker to all your files; you can’t read them anymore, but they’re willing to sell you the “marker remover” so your files are able to be read again.

I’m sure actions like this by the hackers makes you mad— it makes me mad! They’re causing the damage to your files, and then demanding that you pay them to fix it! It’s like the protection rackets you hear about from the mob; they’d promise not to destroy your business if you paid them. Just like the hackers, they’re the ones causing the harm and then demanding you to solve it through payment in some form.

I’ve mentioned several times throughout this book how hackers are adjusting and adapting their attacks. In the past, they’d just encrypt your information, but today, they also threaten to release it publicly. With the ever-increasing compliance requirements that we face, like PCI and HIPAA, the threat of release becomes an even greater issue.

Let me share a story about two companies. Our first company, we’ll call it Holds (name changed to protect privacy), gave me a call saying that I had been referred to them. I went onsite to see what was happening and saw they had a local server with all their files on it. Everyone saved their data to the server, and they performed a backup on the server each night to an attached USB external drive.

When I arrived, they were in a panic. All the company files were encrypted—remember the “covered in thick black marker” analogy in the last chapter? Eddie, the president, told me that when he saw the files were encrypted, he went and tried to perform a restore from the backup. With tears in his eyes, he told me the backup didn’t work, and that the backup had also been encrypted.

I asked him if he had an additional backup that was *not* attached to the server. He answered, “*No, we only have the one drive that we were told to keep attached to the server so that it would backup*.”

I felt terrible for him. Here was a company that had tried to do the right thing, and it failed them. Before the days of cyber attackers, it *was* the right thing to do: make sure you always have a backup. The tragic mistake here was that you want to have *multiple* backup locations, and *not* have the backups attached directly to the server *all the time*. Many of the ransomware attacks now look for attached storage that may have backup files on them because hackers know that a backup is one of the best ways to recover from a cyber attack (and obviously they don’t want to see you do that if they intend to get paid).

We immediately contacted the FBI who came out to investigate and gave us several of the known encryption keys. While these known encryption keys do work about half the time, in this case, they didn’t. We ended up having to rebuild many of the documents and recreate their files from scratch. This meant that we went through all the other computers and emails and tried to find any copies of files that were needed. It was a long and painful process.

Now, let’s look at another company, Makastuff (name also altered for privacy), a manufacturing firm that makes stuff. Makastuff had been a client of ours for about five months when my engineer noticed that there was an issue with their server. He called John, his contact at Makastuff, to ask what was going on.

John hemmed and hawed when we first called him. “*I think I did something to the server*,” he finally admitted.

My engineer asked, “*What happened?*”

“*Well, I was on the server and decided to do some shopping; I figured it wouldn’t hurt. I went to a link that was in my email and then suddenly all of my desktop items started to have strange letters,*” he responded sheepishly.

“*It’s okay, we all make mistakes*,” my team member told him. “*Let’s see if we can figure out what’s going on*.”

After checking the server, my engineer determined that it was the WannaCry ransomware attack. He then determined that it had not yet started to spread across the entire network, but that file damage was done. The cyber attackers were demanding a ransom payment of $5,000 that they wanted sent via cryptocurrency. That was *not* going to happen.

For the past few months, we had been performing a ransomware resistant backup of their system, so the next steps were rather easy. Using the backup, we were able to restore the company to the state their system was in the previous evening, prior to the attack. The system only took about an hour to get back in place, and they were up and running before their factory workers showed up for their morning shift.

As you can see by these two examples, there are very different ways that a ransomware attack can end. You can lose all your data and pay a ransom (*hoping* you get your data back, but with no guarantee), or you can build a backup recovery plan to make sure that you’re able to recover. Just remember: hackers are always looking for ways to get past your defenses. It’s worth millions of dollars to steal your information, so they work hard to get around your protections, as we saw in the first example.

Here are several steps that you can take to protect yourself from these kinds of attacks:

1.  **Have ransomware resistant backups**. That means the backups need to be separated from the computer itself. They can be onsite, in the cloud, or like our solution, both onsite *and* in the cloud. They also need to be encrypted during the initial backup and where the backups are stored – this protects against people being able to steal your backups. Remember to always test your backups so you know they’ll be there when you need them; they are your backup buddy, after all.
2.  **Have current and active anti-virus.** This can protect your computer from known attacks.
3.  **Have an application control to protect against hidden programs**. You may remember that hackers are now putting sleeper programs that wait for months before they attack to make your backups less effective.
4.  **Use anti-spam and email protection**. Many times, ransomware is hiding in emails or links from emails; these programs filter those out. If you never see the bad email, you won’t be tempted to click on it.
5.  **Don’t go to unknown sites or click on links**. Many of the ransomware attacks happen by clicking on a bad link or going to a bad site.
6.  **Have secure and private information encrypted on your computer.** There are many good encryption programs that can make sure your information cannot be taken and read—or shared publicly.

It’s important to remember that you always need to be adapting. Hackers have a vested interest in getting past your protections so that they can exploit your information—don’t let them.

# COMPLIANCE CONUNDRUM

![](images/image-14-1.jpg)

Do you remember the good ol’ days when all you had to do was take care of yourself? You weren’t required to meet PCI (Payment Card Industry) compliance standards or HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) to see patients. It seems like each day there are more and more requirements on how we should be conducting our businesses. These compliances come with their own pains and costs, and they are quickly becoming the standard for how we will have to conduct business moving forward.

Those that don’t comply with expected standards may find themselves being publicly embarrassed. For example, if you’re a doctor and you have a security breach, you could end up on the HIPAA Wall of Shame (This really exists! You can find it here: https://ocrportal.hhs.gov/ocr/breach/breach\_report.jsf) These actions are to force people to take these compliances very seriously.

Let’s consider this true story of a doctor’s office— a story I’ve heard many times. It goes like this: “I don’t have to worry about HIPAA because the government wouldn’t shut down a small office like mine. It’s not a big deal, and no one wants my information anyways.” The doctor is proud of the fact that he didn’t have to worry about cyber attacks. In some ways, it was true, since HIPAA was focused on getting the big players compliant at the time. The problem is that cyber attacks are not always focused on the big players; they’re focused on the smaller players that don’t take the threat seriously.

I will always love the episode of Scott Adams’ “Dilbert” comic strip that said, “Remember, you can’t spell compliance without ‘liance.’” For many of us, we just want to get the checkbox process done. It’s such an inconvenient process that we just want to put it behind us. As frustrating as it is, you need to know that you can’t just pass off the responsibility of compliance—or worse, thinking that you can *lie* about compliance. Think of it this way: if your accountant makes a mistake on your taxes, does the IRS go after your accountant, or do they go after you?

Here's an example of a company we worked with that implemented new compliance standards. The company recently moved to SOC 2 (Service Organization Control) compliance. At first, my client told me that their company was very resistant to imposing the standards and controls that were needed to meet SOC 2. As he described it, everyone hated the process and it seemed to make things take so much longer to complete. Not long after they had implemented the controls, it ended up paying off in spades. The company had a large client that said they were not shipped equipment with the proper specifications, and they were not going to pay for the thousands of dollars in equipment that was sent.

This is never a good thing for a business to hear but is even more devastating to a small business. My client’s team then went back to all the control documents related to the order and found out that it was designed *exactly* as they had specified. The problem had been on the customer’s side, and they were forced to acknowledge the mistake. If they had not been using the new controls, they would never have been able to show that they had met the specifications exactly as requested and it would have cost them many thousands of dollars.

Compliance should be approached as a culture in your company, rather than a checkbox. Everyone needs to be onboard with it and it needs to be accepted from the top down. If the president doesn’t believe in it, then the employees won’t either.

Let me share an example of where compliance was not turned into a part of the culture, but rather, was only seen as a checkbox. This time, we’ll join a New Jersey manufacturing company that was very proud to be ITAR (International Traffic in Arms Regulations) compliant, even boasting about it on their webpage.

As I entered their building, I walked into the reception area and looked to my right, under the reception desk. I saw that they had a server sitting next to the workstation. I asked the manager I was meeting with if that truly was the main server for the office. He responded that it was, and that they just didn’t have anywhere else to put it. He also didn’t understand why that was a problem.

I know that everyone that knows about ITAR is cringing as they read this. For those who don’t know, the requirement for ITAR is that all servers and network equipment must be secured behind a locked and controlled area. The front lobby, sitting out in the open under a desk, would not be considered a secure location, per these standards.

The world of compliance is coming for all of us. You may have heard that Europe has imposed a very strict GDPR, or General Data Protection Regulation, on all personal data that companies hold. It states that a person can require all their data be removed from your system and you have to comply. These standards are also coming to America as more states enact similar requirements, and the cost of not complying can be substantial.

As we continue to try to address the growing threat of cyber attacks, our governments are going to increase the number of laws and regulations to protect us. This means we’re all going to have to live with more annoyance and “liance” if we’re not careful.

With the new Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) that is being required to work with the U.S. Department of Defense, if you decide to “liance” and make a false claim about being compliant, they can charge you three times the value of the contract that you won. Here’s one example of such a payment for “liance”: Cisco had to pay $48 million under the False Claims Act.

It is important for you to understand that you can’t just give away your requirements to be compliant or lie about your compliance, either. In order to help you meet your compliance goals, I strongly suggest working with someone. Just remember that they’re your partner, not your replacement. You must be a part of the process and be involved in protecting your organization; anyone else is just there to help guide you.

# PUT IT IN WRITING

![](images/image-15-2.jpg)

Do you remember your first day at your new job? Can you remember being given that dreaded Employee Manual? I remember (many years ago) when I started my first day at a Fortune 500 company. I was sitting in my cubicle and in walked this friendly HR representative with a very large book in her hands. My enthusiasm plummeted as I read “Employee Manual” on the cover. She handed it to me and explained how important it was for me to read the *entire* book, as they would expect that I knew it.

It was a truly stunning moment. There I was, flush with all the excitement of a new job, ready to be an excellent employee—and then, THE BOOK arrived. It was a big, blue binder with over 200 pages of all the things I was not allowed to do. You could feel the joy being sucked right out of the place. There was never a chance I was going to read that thing!

“*Thank you*,” I said to the cheerful HR woman, knowing full well where that book was going. The moment she left my cubicle, I took that big, ridiculous book and stuck it on the lowest shelf that I had, relegating it to a dark tomb, never to be seen again.

After that, I, as a technician, would be at many people’s cubicles and see the Big Blue Book sitting either high or low, but never read, never even opened. I know that it’s a great checkbox for companies, but they’re never used or read by employees. It was lawyer fodder, put there to cover the behind of the company, but in no way helping to change behavior.

This is *not* the way to create policies that you want employees to use, and policies *should* be used. Let’s start with why you want to have policies. Policies are to help assure that your team does the same process the same way. In the cyber world, this could mean making complex passwords or not taking vital documents home with you. With the understanding that policies exist to help ensure your team performs vital tasks the same way, ask yourself this question: Are *you* going to read two hundred pages of policies? I think we all know the answer. That’s why my Big Blue Book of policies became my Big Shelf Holder of policies, never again to see the light of day.

Policies are also going to be required to meet a variety of compliance standards, regardless of your industry. Again, this is a good thing. If you remember to make it easy for people to *read* the policy, then they’ll be more likely to *follow* the policy.

Let’s take one more journey around policies, this time, going back to when I worked at a pharmaceutical company. It’s important to remember that pharmaceutical companies are heavily restricted, and their policies must be followed very closely; the FDA demands this to be the case.

As a young network administrator, I was responsible for the life of the network and to make sure that everything worked and was protected. One of my roles included the backup of all important data.

The company had a policy for how to perform backups so, being the good employee that I was, I went and asked for the policy. My manager told me (and this is not a joke), “*I can’t give you that policy because, per the policy, you have to be director-level or above to see the backup policy*.”

I was stunned into silence. I was so stunned that I didn’t even ask the most obvious question: *How was I supposed to follow a policy that I was not permitted to see?* I left my manager’s office and went back to my desk.

For the next hour, I sat there and thought about what I was just told—an entire hour of wasted time in stunned silence. In the end, I created and implemented my own backup process using industry standards and off-site backups, but to this day, I have no idea if it met the requirements of the company policy.

I share this example because it’s vital for you to realize that if you don’t give employees clear and easy policies to follow (and access to them), then they’ll make up their own policies and processes, and they won’t always end as well as the ones I made for that pharmaceutical company.

Here are some tips to remember when creating a policy:

1.  KISS: ***K**eep **i**t **S**imple, **S**tupid*. Basically, create policies that are clear and concise.
2.  Clearly define the audience that needs to follow the policies.
3.  Include the name of the person or committee that wrote the policy.
4.  Keep a log of policy changes and dates with the policy.
5.  Review the policy each year.
6.  Ensure proper training and access to the policies.

Policies can be a great way to support a corporate culture and protect you if something goes wrong, but they don’t help anyone if they sit in a big blue book, unread, on a shelf collecting dust.

# GET ON THE TRAINING TRAIN

![](images/image-16-2.jpg)

When we were babies, we were all adorable blank slates. After that, we started to learn and learn quickly. Learning is a life-long process that should never end. Unfortunately, there are many managers out there that think a person should not need to learn but should already know.

Training is the best way to ensure that your team is current and on the same page. It can be costly, but it does pay off in the long run when it’s done right. If you have employees that want to learn, encourage them— they’ll be some of your best employees.

Many companies don’t think of training as making the company better, they think of it as a danger to losing employees. While it is true that you could lose your employees if you train them, you’ll also lose them if you don’t train them. For example, I was a warehouse developer for a trucking company when the network administrator suddenly quit. The position was open, and I wanted to take on a new challenge, so I proceeded with trainings on Novell NetWare (a now discontinued network operating system).

Using the funds that were available to me, I began my coursework. After beginning my first class, my manager came in to speak with me. I think we can all agree that it’s almost never a good sign when the manager says he wants to talk to you. He sat down in my cubicle and told me there was a problem: the company didn’t want me to complete the Novell classes because it would make me too valuable, and I would leave the company.

I remember that statement: I would be *too valuable*. It seemed to me that being valuable would be something they wanted, but I let my manager continue without saying a word. I listened as he told me that my training was a threat to the company, and that I would just take the training and leave.

After he finished with his concerns that I would leave, I told him I was willing to sign an agreement to pay back the company for the training if I were to leave within the next year. He told me that he just couldn’t take that risk and that I was too valuable. Realizing they would never train me to be an even more valuable employee, I ended up leaving the company within a year. It was a simple decision when I realized that my future career goals would require training (and working with companies that valued and encouraged professional development).

Training helps keep your team up-to-date on the latest trends and keeps your company competitive. It’s dangerous to think that not training your employees will assure they stay with your company. In many cases, as I demonstrated with my story, it will do the exact opposite. No one wants to stay where they can’t grow—I certainly didn’t!

Now that we agree that training is good, we also know that all trainings are not created equally. I know I’ve had to sit through some of the most painfully dull trainings in the world! Cyber security training can be so boring and dry! I’ve sat though the storytelling of $200 billion dollars in losses hundreds of times.

The things those cyber security people had to say just didn’t matter to me. I don’t care that 45,000 cyber attacks just happened in the last ten seconds, or that companies have lost millions in each attack. Cyber security training needs to be specific and personal. I want to know what it could cost *me* and how *I* can address it.

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has six tips for conducting cyber trainings that I think everyone should adhere to. I use them when I’m designing my trainings, and you’ll want to see that any vendor you use is also using this framework. It’s very unlikely that you’re a multi-billion-dollar company, so why bother telling you that multi-billions of dollars are being lost in cyber attacks— it’s far more effective to tell you what *you* could wind up losing.

The first of the FTC’s tips for conducting cyber trainings is to set a specific objective. Your team doesn’t care about every little issue in the world, they care about what can hurt their job, your company, and their bank. Define what exactly you want them to learn. If you’re looking to make sure they don’t click on email links, put that into your training. Make sure to be clear and focused, with a limited number of behaviors that you want them to learn.

Second, keep your audience in mind. If I’m conducting a training to a group of accountants, I don’t want to suddenly start talking about SIEM or SOCs; I want to talk about balance sheets and wire fraud. You always want to keep the knowledge level aligned with the audience. Talking over the heads of the audience only gets them to start to daydream about that boat ride they’re going to take this weekend, or the iced latte they forgot in their car.

Third, remember the reason that the audience is learning. If you’re trying to protect against wire fraud, let the audience know and make the message consistent. Don’t go off on tangents about how oil prices are going up, or how your company doesn’t want to let anyone be trained because then they’re all going to jump ship.

Fourth, control the scope. People will usually only remember two or three things from your training, so trying to cram dozens of points into one training will mean that most of it will be left behind and forgotten. Keep it reasonable and achievable and you’ll find the trainings are more successful.

I think the fifth step is the most important, and that is to keep it fun. In my Fast & Fun trainings that I conduct, I always remember that people remember better if they’re having a good time. If your team enjoys the training that you provide, they’ll willingly come back for more trainings.

Sixth is to continue to learn. Training is not a one-and-done situation; it’s vital for your team to keep learning. The world of cyber security is always evolving, and hackers are always changing their tactics, so we need to always be adapting as well.

We should approach life as life-long learners, always growing and expanding our horizons. By continuing with trainings and learning, we keep ourselves at the top of our games.

# NIST IS NICE

![](images/image-17-1.jpg)

The National Institute of Standards and Technology, NIST, is a group of folks hiding out in Boulder, Colorado, thinking of countless ways to keep us safe. Most cyber security standards are derived from the work they do. Why would a small business like yours care about NIST or the standards they create? That’s a great question with an even better answer!

NIST creates many of the security standards that are used across the world, as well as very actively within the US. They start with a simple and straightforward Cyber Security Framework that can be implemented and understood by all of us. The five steps of the NIST Cyber Security Framework are the basis of all cyber security standards used today.

The 5 Steps of the Cyber Security Framework are:

1.  Identify
2.  Protect
3.  Detect
4.  Respond
5.  Recover

I’ll take us through each of the different steps and explain why they’re valuable.

The first step is to identify. The saying is always true: you can’t protect what you don’t know about. In the identify stage, you’ll figure out where your important information is. Your company runs on information— from your QuickBooks accounting, your client records, the bank with all of your money, to your proprietary process that makes the wonderful items you sell, there is vital data you have on your computers.

The first step is to identify where all this information is, beginning with an inventory of all the equipment that you have. This will include computers, smart phones, external hard drives and USB devices, and even POS (Point of Sales) devices and other IoT (Internet of Things: machinery or other items that connect to the internet) equipment that may be vital to your company’s processes.

Next, you need to know what programs and applications you are using. Do you need QuickBooks or AutoCAD? What version of Microsoft Word are you using? Knowing what programs you use and where they are located will help keep you safe by understanding what people are doing and where. Take time to track what programs are being used. If a program that is vital to your company is lost or attacked, how would you recover?

You’ll also need to determine who should be accessing those programs. Which employees need to get to which information? Do contractors need access? What about people outside of your company? Each person that needs to access your vital information needs to be identified and what they specifically need access to needs to be defined. I would guess that you don’t want to have your customers looking at your QuickBooks, but they should be able to see your company brochure.

You’ll also want to define how they can get to your information. Your employees may be working from home these days and need access with a secure connection such as a VPN (Virtual Private Network). They could also be accessing information on their phones. It is important to know who is accessing your information and how they’re accessing it. If you don’t know, you’re not able to protect it.

This can take some time to do, and I know most people hate this first part. It demands that you understand what is important and where it’s stored. With the world of cloud storage, it can become difficult to know where your information is being held and how secure it truly is.

The next step is to protect your vital information, which will be an ongoing process. It will include things like anti-virus, spam filters, backups, and other actions that are designed to keep your team safe. It will include setting up security for people and conducting trainings with your staff (which should occur on an ongoing basis, rather than just once a year). The protection process is an ever-changing series of actions that you and your team will employ every day. You don’t want to make it onerous, but you also have to remember to be involved in the process. The days of just hiring someone to do it for you are over. You want to get someone that will do this *with* you, as part of your team.

Next, you will need to detect the attack. It doesn’t help you if you don’t know or can’t determine that someone is attacking your information. You might have heard about the Target attack where the hackers waited months before they even started to attack. Having a method to be able to see what’s taking place is vitally important.

The thing to remember here is that there are different ways to detect an attack. You can use software and hardware, such as IDP/IPS (Intrusion Detection System or Intrusion Protection System) on firewalls or application control programs that run on your computers and stop programs that you did not approve. Then there is the human aspect. This could mean a person calls you saying you sent them a strange email and they want to confirm its accuracy, to your team being trained to detect strange behavior on their computers. Detection comes in a wide range of detection processes.

Never rely on just one method for finding a breach. Think about the airport: in addition to having cameras and security officers that roam around, they send out a constant message to report any abandoned luggage that you may see. *You* are part of the protection process at the airport. In your company, your team and customers are part of the protection process. They can tell you if something seems strange or ‘off.’ Take those warnings seriously.

Next, you have to respond when you learn of an attack. It’s important to have a plan of action that your company will take when there is a breach or an attack. This plan should include steps to reduce the damage from an attack and who should be contacted if there is an attack. It’s also important to remember to inform the proper authorities of the attack. If you get attacked with ransomware, for example, you should tell the FBI, who has a website set up to make the entire process easier: www.ICE.org.

Make sure that your plan includes customers, employees, and anyone else that you think may be hurt or affected by the attack. It is hard to admit, but the more people that you contact to inform about an attack, the more effective you will be in stopping the attack from spreading.

Let’s take a moment to show you how NIST is connected to what we have been learning about in the rest of our journey. Remember our wonderful OTP CEO Lucy, you know, the wife that’s about to slap me for still talking about her email mistake? As a reminder, her email was hacked, and emails were being sent from her account (by the hackers) that were asking customers to make payments. Within five minutes, we knew about the attack and started to enact our cyber response plan. We changed her email password, fixed the forwarding rule they had set up, and then we did something that some people questioned us for: we sent out email notifications telling everyone that we were breached and that they should not respond to any of the emails that came from our CEO’s account.

The response was almost instant; many people told us that they were so glad that we let them know, but others questioned why a cyber security company would tell people that they were hacked. We were emphatic about this because, as a cyber security company, we have a higher standard to live up to. If we told others that they have to notify people when they get breached, how could we not do that as well? Since we had used the NIST Framework, we were ready for the attack that hit our CEO. The act of reporting to others about a breach should never be seen as embarrassing, but rather, seen as good practice.

The last step in NIST’s Framework is recover. This is a vital step that can help you regain a sense of control after an attack. I’ve had to help people feel whole again after they were a victim of a cyber attack because these attacks can cause you to feel helpless and violated. The recovery process is more than just getting your data back and getting things working again; it’s about regaining a sense of control and feeling safe after being violated.

In addition to recovering lost information and repairing compromised equipment, you’re going to want to review what happened. This is where training your team on what happened and how to stop it can be valuable. After an attack, everyone is more focused on the steps to prevent it from happening again. Taking advantage of that focus could help you get a ‘win’ out of a bad situation.

It’s easy to see that the NIST Framework gives you a starting point to protect your company. It’s important to start with something that you can handle. Trying to be too complex or massive can overwhelm and frustrate you; processes like NIST’s Framework help you maintain control and move forward.

# WHAT’S A SMALL BUSINESS TO DO?

![](images/image-18.jpg)

It’s been a long and wonderful journey through all the challenges of cyber security, wouldn’t you agree? Now the question becomes, *what is a business to do?*

I think the first step will surprise you: RELAX. Don’t act in a panic. Instead, take a moment to take a deep breath and start to review where you are. In this case, the NIST Framework we just reviewed is a wonderful starting point, but also remember that small wins help you to reach bigger wins.

After you are relaxed and ready to go, then think about where you need or want to be. Do you have compliance reasons for cyber security, like PCI, CMMC, HIPAA, ITAR, etc.? If there are compliance reasons, then you’ll have a strong framework for what you need to do. Understanding this makes things easier to define, even if they are sometimes (or often) a pain to do.

If you don’t have a compliance reason for cyber security, think about what you’re concerned about. Do you think you could lose your business, lose client information, have to pay an outrageous ransom? This step will help remind you why you’re working towards increased cyber security. I know that sometimes we all wish cyber security wasn’t needed, and in a perfect world it wouldn’t be, but as you well know, we are *not* in a perfect world.

Now that you have your reason *why*, you’re able to build your team. This should be made up of important people within your company: your stakeholders in the project, and outside consultants to assist you in taking the necessary cyber security measures. Don’t make a team that is too big; I would suggest between four and eight people. They need to be motivated and help to keep you on track.

Remember, it’s easy to put off tackling the cyber security process because the fires of today seem so important. Remember the story of the ITAR company with the server in the lobby? They were going to get to it, but never did because other things took priority. Each day they put it off, though, their risk only compounded. It helps to also put your reasons for cyber security on a big white board, someplace clearly visible in your office. That way, each day, you can read it saying, “I WILL NOT BE RANSOMWARED,” or something of the like.

It's also important to take notice of the things that you’re already doing right. You’re most likely not starting at square one. Do you have anti-virus on your computers? Do you use 2FA/MFA? Do you already have policies? Are you conducting trainings with your employees? These wins remind you that each step isn’t that hard, and that you’re taking numerous steps to increase your cyber security already. This helps provide the momentum and motivation to keep going, to keep building your company’s cyber security and resilience even stronger.

When you build a plan on how to move forward with cyber security, it’s important to assign dates to the different tasks. Remember to do one thing at a time so as not to overwhelm yourself and your team; you still need to be running the business, of course.

To implement the plan, use any structure that can help. If you’re getting cyber security services from a company (like my own), they’ll help you assess where you’re at, where you want or need to be, and then inform you of the things you need to get there. If you’re moving towards a compliance standard, they can help with that as well, though it could be harder to understand what they’re looking for. Compliance standards like to use strange and confusing language to keep things vague and open to different methods of implementation, but it also makes it harder for you to know what to do. If you’re not using an insurance or compliance standard, just start with the NIST Framework.

Remember, every structure will follow the basic NIST Cybersecurity Framework:

**Identify** – Determine what is important and where it is. This includes the people, files, programs, and hardware that needs to be protected. Then, determine why it needs to be protected: Is it for compliance? Do workers work from home? Are you scared of being attacked, etc.?

**Protect** – Figure out how you’re going to protect the important data you identified. Do you have backups? Is there anti-virus? Anti-spam? Do you have remote workers that use a VPN? What are the other security measures that you need? You’ll also want to build your plans for how to respond during an attack or breach.

**Detect** – What method do you have to see if the plan is working? Do you have a monitoring company that checks logs? How are you determining if anyone is getting in or breaching your systems? Knowing that an attack is happening is a vital part of protecting yourself.

**Respond** – I am going to start with saying “RELAX” here again. If there is an attack or a breach, the first step will always be to RELAX. Then, have processes in place for how to address the attack. I have a document on steps to take if there is a breach which I will gladly send along to you, just reach out to RELAX@ontechpartners.com and ask for information on breaches. This is where having a policy in place can be very helpful.

**Recover** – Now you need to get back to work after the attack. This could be restoring lost data, reviewing what happened and adjusting the current protection plan, additional training for the team, etc. It is vital to get back to a place of normality after an attack. Attacks are violations and they can make you feel helpless and scared; creating a sense of normality will help your team get back to the important work that you’re doing.

Cyber security does not have to be terrifying, despite what many doomsday cyber professionals would have you believe; making things fun for your employees can help. What’s the most important thing to remember? Begin. Just begin. Do something every day to increase cyber security and it won’t seem so overwhelming or scary; think of it as keeping your company, your team, and yourself safe.

# DEATH BY TLA

![](images/image-19-2.jpg)

I’ve been told that cyber security people speak their own language. It’s true, though— we do have a whole list of TLA, or three letter acronyms. I love the fact that a friend of mine defined all our crazy acronyms *with* an acronym. Unfortunately, I sometimes feel that cyber security professionals use all these acronyms as a way to keep you confused.

I’ve gathered some of the more common acronyms to make it easier for you to understand what all those cyber nerds are truly talking about. I’ve also tried to explain their meanings in terms that “the average Joe or Jane” will understand. I don’t include myself in that list because I’ve been sucked into the dark side of acronyms. I realized this one day when I was talking to a group of businessmen and women at a networking event.

We were all sitting together and talking about what makes each of us different. I was so proud to stand up and say, “*We’re the Non-MSP. We’re working to make sure you’re part of the network journey*.” I waited for everyone to tell me how great my vision of being different from all the other MSPs in the world was.

Silence hit me as everyone looked at me with the most confused expressions I had ever seen. Then a woman stood up and asked, “*What’s an MSP?*” I was devastated. I had worked so hard to be different in my industry that it didn’t even occur to me that most people outside of it wouldn’t know what an MSP was. For the record, MSP stands for Managed Service Provider.

It was clear that I was not speaking the language of my customers; I was speaking the language of my industry. In all my trade shows and technical journals, they talked about the MSP journey and MSP services, to the point where I figured that everyone knew what it was. No one outside the industry did. After that day, I realized that we all can fall into this trap of speaking in our own industry language, not realizing that it’s complete gibberish for most people outside our industry.

I’ve included this section to help you understand some of the jargon a cyber security person may say to you. This is obviously not a complete list of all terms that cyber people use, but it will give you a good reference to start. I don’t expect you to memorize them, but rather, have a place to look them up when you hear them.

**CMMC** – Cyber Maturity Model Certification. Have you ever wanted to contract with the government? Well, this won’t make you more excited (but it *is* necessary). CMMC is the new standard from the Department of Defense to assure that companies have at least a basic level of cyber security and protection. It’s just one more thing that the government expects you to do… beyond death and taxes that is.

**MFA/2FA** – Multi-Factor/2-Factor Authentication. Like most people, you’ve probably had more than one occasion where you’ve thought: “*Passwords? I don’t need another stinking password!*” Well, now you need *even more* than a password. MFA requires you to have two or more different criteria to log into your account. The good news is that many of us already have this authentication method with our banks and other financial institutions. You’ll know if you have MFA because when you log into a site, it will ask you for your password and, for example, to enter a code that is sent to your mobile phone. I know you hate having to do this, but trust me, it makes it much harder for a hacker to gain access to your accounts because, not only would they need to hack your password, but they would also need to steal or otherwise gain access to your phone to retrieve the verification code. You can see how much more difficult this would make it for your account to be hacked.

**VPN** – Virtual Private Network. Remember when millions of people began working from home at the start of the pandemic? Well, many of us quickly found out that was a very unsafe way to work. That’s when we started to implement VPNs. VPNs are a system that uses encryption (a method that makes your information unreadable to other people) to create a safer connection between your computer and the office network. A personal VPN also allows people to have protection from people watching them on the internet by creating an unreadable link to a third-party location.

**DLP** – Data Loss Prevention. Ever wonder what would happen if someone took your medical records from your doctor’s office? Well, a DLP Plan would address this. It’s a plan that would allow for the protection and recovery of important data. Let’s say you want to make sure that your client records don’t end up in the hands of a competitor; a DLP Plan would help ensure that. This is a plan and processes that make sure data is not lost or stolen. A good DLP system should protect your information from both accidental losses and “bad actors” (aka: hackers) trying to steal your information.

**EDR** – Endpoint Detection and Response. Computers are like the petri dish of your network (great visual, right?). Bad programs and viruses tend to grow there, meaning that is where most of the bad things happen. Keeping your computers and phones safe is the primary objective of EDR solutions. When looking at cyber security, understanding what is happening on the endpoints is vital.

**XDR-** Extended Detecting and Response. So, you have all the endpoints feeling safe, but what about the rest? Not to feel left out, the security world enhanced protection to now include not just the endpoints, but all the equipment in between.

**IDS/IPS** – Intrusion Detection System/Intrusion Prevention System. There are times when you want to have that ‘big dog’ at the front gate. Your IDS/IPS is that big dog that is looking to keep everyone out. When you have Intrusion Detection and Prevention, you’re actively keeping out bad things and watching that only good things get in.

**MSSP** – Managed Security Service Provider. Sometimes you just need (or want) someone else to take care of you. The MSSP will take care of all your security issues, but it’s important to remember that you can’t just pass security off to someone else and hope everything is good. You need to *partner* with someone and make sure that you are actively involved in securing what is important to you.

**NIST** – National Institute of Standards and Technology. This is where to go if you just want someone to tell you what you should do. NIST is a government agency that builds security and compliance standards. So, when you want to be told what you need to be doing, just ask NIST and they’ll tell you.

**SIEM** – Security Information and Event Management. How do you know you’ve been attacked? Well, that’s where a SIEM system comes in. This system will collect information about your computer or network and determine if there’s a threat or potential attack. SIEM systems use real-time monitoring to find and respond to attackers— as they are attacking— letting you feel safer as you go.

**SOC** – Security Operations Center. Where do all the security people live? That’s easy! They live in a SOC… maybe next to the old lady in a shoe? Security people spend their time at the SOC watching to see if bad people are trying to do bad things to your computers.

**SSO** – Single Sign-On. Passwords, passwords, passwords! It seems like we enter passwords everywhere, but it doesn’t always have to be that way. A Single Sign-On is just want it sounds like— sign on once and get access to all that you need! Yes, please!

**RMM** – Remote Management and Monitoring. Like a Superman from afar, the RMM allows remote monitoring to keep your systems up and running from many miles away. You don’t even need to take an Uber (or put on pants) to get there!

**BDR** – Backup and Disaster Recovery. Where have 30 years of all my pictures gone?! It can be terrifying to discover that all your precious or vital information has vanished. With a BDR, you’re able to recover all your lost records. That should help you sleep a little easier tonight!
