
: Gradient-based perturbation refers to the process of creating input changes—such as pixel adjustments or word substitutions—by following the gradient of a model's loss function. These perturbations are tailored to be effective while often remaining imperceptible. They are widely used in adversarial examples and model evasion attacks. 

`**Hallucination Exploitation**`

: `Hallucination exploitation` is the practice of deliberately triggering a generative model to produce false, misleading, or fabricated content in a way that benefits the attacker. This often involves crafted prompts that elicit confident but incorrect answers. It's a critical risk in systems that generate text, code, or images for high-stakes applications. 

`**Homoglyph Substitution**`

: `Homoglyph substitution` is an obfuscation technique where visually similar characters—such as replacing “o” with “0” or “l” with “1”—are used to deceive text-based AI models. These substitutions can bypass filters or mislead language models into interpreting malicious input as benign. It is widely used in adversarial text attacks and phishing-style prompts. 

`**Illumination Manipulation**`

: `Illumination manipulation` refers to altering lighting conditions in visual input—either in physical scenes or digital images—to degrade the performance of computer vision models. Adversaries may use shadows, glare, or color shifts to cause misclassification. It's a real-world tactic for defeating facial recognition, object detection, or surveillance systems. 

`**Inference Activation**`

: `Inference activation` describes the process of triggering specific behaviors or outputs in a model during the inference stage, often by presenting crafted inputs. This can include activating backdoors, prompting latent capabilities, or escalating decision logic. Attackers leverage `inference activation` to exploit models without access to training-time controls. 

`**Inference API**`

: An `inference API` is a web-accessible interface that allows users to submit inputs to a machine learning model and receive predictions or outputs. These APIs are common deployment endpoints but can be abused for probing, model extraction, or data leakage. Robust input validation and rate limiting are critical for API protection. 

`**Inference Chaining**`

: `Inference chaining` is a strategy where multiple AI model outputs are combined in sequence, with each step influencing the next. This chaining is common in multi-step reasoning and agentic pipelines. Attackers may exploit this to cause step-by-step degradation, compounding errors or injecting malicious influence that escalates with each inference. 

`**Inference Drift**`

: `Inference drift` refers to shifts in a model's predictions over time due to changes in input patterns, environmental signals, or internal state. Unlike training drift, inference drift occurs during use and may indicate environmental changes or adversarial activity. Monitoring output distributions helps detect unexpected drift in real-time systems. 

`**Inference-Time Attack**`

: An `inference-time attack` targets a model while it is running and making predictions, typically by feeding it crafted inputs designed to exploit its vulnerabilities. These attacks require no access to training data and are often used in real-world evasion scenarios. They are among the most common threats to deployed AI systems. 

`**Inference-Time Exploitation**`

: `Inference-time exploitation` encompasses any malicious action that takes place during a model's operational phase, including prompt injection, adversarial perturbation, or trigger activation. These attacks aim to subvert model output without modifying its internal parameters. This class of attack highlights the need for runtime safeguards. 

`**Instruction Tuning**`

: `Instruction tuning` is the process of training a generative model to follow natural language instructions, aligning it with specific task formats or conversational styles. While it improves usability, it can introduce new vulnerabilities if attackers manipulate how instructions are interpreted. Poor tuning can increase susceptibility to prompt-based attacks or hallucination. 

`**Instruction-Following Fine-Tuning**`

: `Instruction-following fine-tuning` is a specialized form of model adaptation where a model is trained on a dataset of prompt-response pairs that demonstrate desired behaviors in response to human-like instructions. While this improves compliance and usability, attackers can exploit ambiguities in `instruction-following` logic to induce unexpected or harmful behaviors. 

`**Interval-Bound Propagation**`

: `Interval-bound propagation` is a formal verification technique that tracks the upper and lower bounds of neural network outputs given bounded input changes. It is used to certify robustness against small perturbations. This method helps establish guarantees that no adversarial example can push the output past certain thresholds within defined limits. 

`**Introspective Checkpoint**`

: An `introspective checkpoint` is a saved model state that includes diagnostic information beyond just weights, such as gradient history, loss trajectories, or behavioral summaries. These enriched checkpoints aid in auditing, debugging, and anomaly detection. They also create opportunities to detect tampering or identify when a model's behavior began to drift. 

`**Jailbreak**`

: A `jailbreak` is a technique used to bypass the safety constraints of a generative model—particularly large language models—by crafting prompts that lead the model to produce restricted, harmful, or otherwise filtered outputs. `Jailbreaking` exposes the fragile boundaries between allowed and forbidden responses. It is one of the most widely observed attacks on public-facing AI. 

`**Jailbreak Prompt**`

: A `jailbreak prompt` is a specifically engineered input designed to coax a model into bypassing its content moderation or policy enforcement mechanisms. These prompts often use misdirection, multi-step context building, or role-playing to subvert guardrails. They are a primary tool for extracting unsafe or prohibited model outputs. 

`**Latent Boundary Exploitation**`

: `Latent boundary exploitation` is the act of crafting inputs that sit near the decision boundaries in the model's latent space to trigger ambiguous or incorrect outputs. By targeting the parts of latent space where representations are unstable, attackers can cause models to misbehave with seemingly valid inputs. It is particularly relevant in vision and generative models. 

`**Latent Instruction**`

: A `latent instruction` is an embedded directive hidden within input text or data that is not immediately visible to human reviewers but influences the model's output. This can take the form of prompt obfuscation, encoded commands, or context-based manipulation. `Latent instructions` are a form of covert prompt injection. 

`**Latent Space Collision**`

: `Latent space collision` occurs when two semantically different inputs are mapped to similar representations within a model's latent space. This can cause confusion in classification, generation, or reasoning tasks. Attackers can exploit collisions to craft adversarial inputs that evade detection or impersonate benign queries. 

`**Latent Space Traversal**`

: `Latent space traversal` is the process of moving through a model's internal representation space to explore or manipulate its behavior. This technique is used both for creative generation and adversarial control. Traversals can reveal sensitive or unstable zones in a model's reasoning pipeline. 

`**Latent Trigger**`

: A `latent trigger` is a pattern hidden within the input or internal model state that, once matched or activated, causes the model to behave in a predefined malicious way. These triggers are often introduced during training and remain dormant until activated in inference. They are central to backdoor and trojan attacks.
