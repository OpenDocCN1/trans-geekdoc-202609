
Modality translation systems further expand the attack surface. These components are responsible for transforming input from one domain into a form consumable by another—such as converting a visual scene into descriptive text or converting auditory cues into structured commands. Attacks on these translation pipelines can introduce imperceptible distortions that only manifest harm when propagated downstream. A benign-seeming image might yield an ambiguous textual summary that triggers faulty logical reasoning, or a misinterpreted sound could generate incorrect situational awareness. These translation mismatches are particularly potent in agentic systems that rely on summary representations to reason under resource constraints. [Table 16.2](#c16.xhtml#c16-tbl-0002) compares the vulnerability characteristics of different input modalities used in agentic inference pipelines.

Cross-modal retrieval systems are equally vulnerable. These mechanisms allow an agent to fetch relevant information across knowledge stores based on multi-modal queries. A visual cue might trigger a text-based retrieval of procedural steps, or a verbal query might prompt the extraction of sensor-based anomaly reports. Embedding-space poisoning can subtly corrupt these retrieval results by reshaping similarity scores in the latent space. An attacker who controls even a small portion of the agent's knowledge base or input history can seed misleading entries that are disproportionately influential during retrieval. When the agent draws inferences from these compromised sources, its downstream behavior becomes corrupted while maintaining plausible coherence.

Incremental perturbation across multiple modalities represents an especially difficult-to-detect class of attacks. Rather than overwhelming a single input stream, adversaries can introduce low-amplitude noise across several channels, each insufficient to trigger alarm on its own but collectively capable of steering behavior. A miscolored object, a misspoken phrase, and a low-confidence sensor anomaly may jointly cause the agent to enter an unintended state or escalate to unsafe action. These composite perturbations require defenders to adopt holistic monitoring approaches, as single-modality validators will fail to detect the threat. The agent's own uncertainty metrics may also be miscalibrated, particularly when the perturbations reinforce each other semantically.

Inference loops conditioned on multi-modal context are another weak point. Agentic systems routinely use historical context—stored as embeddings or structured summaries—to inform future reasoning. If these contexts are subtly manipulated, agents will perpetuate and amplify misalignment. An adversarial interaction that begins with a misleading image or phrasing can embed itself into memory, where it continues to influence the interpretation of future inputs. Because context updates often occur continuously, even short-lived perturbations can create lasting distortions. Attackers can craft these sequences deliberately, planting what amounts to long-term strategic misinformation into the agent's internal state.

The coupling of symbolic reasoning modules with multi-modal inputs creates a hybrid vulnerability space. Symbolic logic typically expects precise, discrete inputs, but multi-modal systems often generate fuzzy or ambiguous representations. If the symbolic module is not hardened against imprecise grounding, it may derive formally correct but semantically incorrect conclusions. For example, a symbolic rule engine might reason that a door is unlocked because a visual classifier erroneously labels the knob position. This false inference then cascades through decision layers, potentially causing unauthorized access or violation of physical safety constraints.

Another fertile ground for exploitation lies in dynamic attention mechanisms. Many multi-modal agentic systems rely on attention layers to focus processing on the most relevant parts of each input modality. Adversaries can manipulate these attention patterns through visual distractors, misleading textual cues, or redundant sensory inputs. By diverting attention to less relevant content, attackers can suppress important signals or cause premature conclusions. This is particularly effective in time-sensitive decision contexts, where a slight misallocation of attention may cause the agent to overlook a critical constraint or misinterpret a user's intent.

Temporal misalignment between modalities introduces yet another class of attack. Multi-modal systems often assume temporal coherence across inputs, such as aligning spoken instructions with visual scenes. An attacker who introduces timing inconsistencies—such as delayed audio injection or asynchronous video frames—can disrupt the temporal reasoning pipeline. The agent may associate commands with the wrong context or misattribute causality, leading to inappropriate behavior. These attacks are subtle, as they do not require input corruption, only desynchronization of existing content streams. In systems with limited timestamp resolution or poor buffering logic, the results can be catastrophic.

Sophisticated adversaries may even leverage generative models to craft synthetic multi-modal perturbations that are internally consistent. A synthetic video paired with fabricated audio and contextually plausible metadata can serve as a Trojan scenario that trains the agent to behave incorrectly under similar real-world conditions. These synthetic perturbations bypass many static validators because they appear internally aligned. Without rigorous provenance tracking and content authentication, agents are susceptible to learning from or responding to highly convincing but entirely false multi-modal artifacts.

As these systems evolve, attackers are also increasingly targeting inference calibration mechanisms—those internal routines that assess confidence or uncertainty across modalities. By skewing calibration functions through domain-specific input patterns, attackers can cause agents to overestimate confidence in flawed interpretations. This suppresses fallback logic, disables human oversight triggers, and accelerates the execution of unsafe actions. Calibration exploits are especially dangerous when agents operate autonomously in environments where real-time oversight is impractical.
