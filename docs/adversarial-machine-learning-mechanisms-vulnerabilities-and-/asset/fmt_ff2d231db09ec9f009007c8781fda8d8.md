
Moreover, modern AI development encourages reuse and transfer learning, which spreads vulnerabilities across domains. A compromised vision model pretrained on a large dataset may be fine-tuned into a facial recognition system, a medical imaging classifier, or a surveillance tool—each with vastly different implications. If the base model harbors a subtle backdoor or adversarial artifact, every downstream application inherits the defect. Classical systems did not operate with this level of parameter inheritance, making them less prone to transitive exploitation. The fragility of modern AI lies not only in its individual components but in the way those components are shared, adapted, and redeployed at scale.

Another noteworthy divergence is in auditability. Classical models could be versioned, serialized, and linearly compared between iterations. Discrepancies could be pinpointed at the level of feature weights or decision logic. Deep models resist such comparison. Even minor changes in data ordering, random seeds, or hyperparameters can result in entirely different weight configurations. This variability complicates differential analysis and makes it difficult to determine whether an observed change in behavior is the result of tampering, model drift, or natural stochasticity. As a result, security baselining and behavioral fingerprinting are far more challenging in the deep learning paradigm.

Incident response workflows must also be reimagined. In classical systems, a detected anomaly often led to a straightforward mitigation: rollback the model, correct the data, and patch the logic. With modern AI systems, incident containment can be messy and nonlinear. A backdoored model may continue to operate normally until it encounters a precise trigger. Rolling back might not eliminate the threat if the poisoned data remains in the pipeline. Additionally, the sheer size and complexity of modern models make retraining prohibitively expensive, slowing response time, and increasing organizational risk during remediation.

The role of domain expertise has shifted as well. Classical systems often required domain-specific knowledge to engineer relevant features and interpret model outputs. In contrast, modern AI promises generalization across tasks, but in doing so, abstracts away human intuition. This abstraction creates a vacuum in which adversarial influence can go unnoticed. Security analysts may find themselves interpreting latent vector movements and decoder outputs rather than discrete features or categorical logic. The detachment from domain semantics weakens the human-in-the-loop component, which was historically a bulwark against model failure.

Ultimately, the transition from classical to modern pipelines is not merely a shift in tooling—it is a paradigm shift in how models learn, behave, and fail. This shift has profound implications for security engineering. Traditional hardening techniques, built around deterministic rules and transparent reasoning, must be supplemented—or in some cases replaced—by probabilistic reasoning, adversarial validation, and architectural resilience strategies tailored to the opaque nature of deep learning. Security teams must evolve their mental models, tools, and assumptions to meet the complexity of these new systems without losing sight of fundamental defensive principles. The challenges are significant, but so are the stakes.

## Identifying Entry Points: Training, Inference, and Supply Chain

AI systems are exposed to a diverse array of attack surfaces, but none are as strategically consequential as the entry points through which adversaries initiate compromise. The training phase remains one of the most effective and least detectable attack vectors. During this stage, models ingest vast amounts of data to optimize their behavior—an opportunity attackers can exploit by subtly poisoning input samples, manipulating label distributions, or injecting malicious exemplars that embed dormant logic. These tainted inputs can encode specific patterns that only trigger under attacker-chosen conditions, creating backdoors or conditional misclassifications that remain latent during validation. Because training data is often large, diverse, and sourced from untrusted or opaque origins, even targeted manipulations can slip past traditional quality checks undetected.

Model poisoning, distinct from data poisoning, involves direct tampering with the learning process itself. In distributed or federated learning environments, this can be accomplished by compromising update streams, manipulating gradient contributions, or injecting adversarial weight modifications. These manipulations can degrade performance, steer learning objectives off course, or install persistent logic bombs that activate under predefined input conditions. Backdoors implanted at this level can remain dormant for months or years, presenting no outward indicators of compromise until invoked. Compounding the issue is the difficulty of auditing learned weights in deep networks—making poisoned parameters virtually impossible to reverse engineer once integrated.

Inference-time attacks represent a different class of entry points, often executed through external interfaces such as APIs, command-line endpoints, or web-based query tools. These attacks exploit the model's decision logic during runtime, commonly using adversarial examples to induce misclassification or behavior degradation. For discriminative models, the attack vector might be a carefully perturbed image that flips a predicted class. For generative or agentic systems, the vector might be a manipulated prompt designed to circumvent safety filters or inject unintended instructions. Because inference interfaces are often exposed to the public, they serve as high-frequency, low-friction points of entry for adversarial probes and automated exploit discovery.

Prompt injection, a particularly insidious variant of inference manipulation, targets the blurred line between input and instruction in generative systems. These attacks exploit the model's tendency to treat all input tokens equally, tricking the model into interpreting maliciously crafted text as authoritative commands. This vulnerability allows an adversary to override embedded safety mechanisms, leak sensitive information, or trigger unintended behaviors. In agentic systems, where models have tool-use capabilities, prompt injection can even escalate into command execution or unauthorized data access. Jailbreak prompts use semantic obfuscation, tone-shifting, or chaining tricks to bypass filters, turning guardrails into illusions of control.

Model fine-tuning introduces another fertile entry point, especially in the context of transfer learning. When organizations download pretrained models from open repositories and apply domain-specific tuning, they often inherit unseen vulnerabilities from the original training corpus or model architecture. Fine-tuning on maliciously labeled data or with misaligned objectives can exacerbate existing biases or reactivate dormant backdoors. In some cases, attackers deliberately publish publicly available models seeded with malicious logic, betting on downstream developers to reuse them blindly. Because fine-tuning tends to preserve core model structure while modifying output behavior, it can inadvertently preserve attacker-installed functionality while evading downstream evaluation metrics.
