
Multiagent RL systems present an expanded attack surface due to their reliance on agent-to-agent interaction and shared environmental feedback. Poisoning in these systems can involve subtle corruption of communication protocols, misaligned reward functions, or adversarial role assignment that undermines cooperation. A single compromised agent can cause cascading learning errors across the group, particularly if policies are interdependent or coadapted. These disruptions may not manifest until agents face scenarios requiring collaboration under uncertainty, at which point the learned policies diverge from expected behavior, often with no clear attribution path.

The high dimensionality and stochasticity of RL environments provide attackers with ample opportunity to mask their manipulations. Poisoned experiences, malformed transitions, or reward anomalies can be statistically camouflaged within the natural variance of complex environments. Moreover, most RL systems lack rigorous data provenance, making retroactive poisoning detection practically infeasible. Logging every state, action, and reward transition is prohibitively expensive in real-world applications, and even if done, distinguishing adversarial patterns from exploration artifacts requires deep domain expertise and forensic tooling that few organizations possess. Persistent poisoning attacks in RL often go undetected because agents still exhibit nominal success during validation. Standard evaluation benchmarks are typically static and do not test for edge-case adversarial behaviors or environmental inconsistencies. Attackers exploit this by designing poisoned policies that activate only under rare or attacker-controlled conditions, effectively installing logic bombs within the policy space. These embedded vulnerabilities may only trigger in specific regions of the state-action space, remaining dormant for months before being activated in mission-critical scenarios, such as during a high-stakes decision or when faced with a rare operational configuration.

Mitigating poisoning in RL is complicated by the lack of formal verification techniques for sequential decision-making. While supervised models can be probed with test sets and adversarial examples, RL agents are inherently interactive and stateful. Validating their robustness requires simulation across wide policy spaces and extended trajectories, a process that is both computationally expensive and sensitive to initial conditions. Furthermore, most existing defenses are designed to detect obvious failure modes, not the subtle, functionally aligned misbehaviors introduced by persistent poisoning.

As RL systems are increasingly deployed in physical systems, finance, autonomous control, and industrial automation, the consequences of poisoning grow more severe. An agent that behaves correctly in 99% of situations but fails catastrophically in 1% due to embedded poison poses an existential risk. These kinds of failures are difficult to trace, hard to anticipate, and nearly impossible to reverse-engineer once deployed. Attackers who understand RL dynamics and policy learning stages can implant policies that are technically correct but strategically compromised, creating systems that are brittle, misaligned, or adversarially exploitable.

The growing complexity of RL architectures, such as hierarchical RL and meta-learning frameworks, further expands the terrain for poisoning. These models often learn across multiple layers of abstraction, making it possible to embed misbehavior not just at the action level, but at the policy selection or goal inference level. In such settings, poisoning can manifest as incorrect subgoal prioritization, biased planning strategies, or failure to adapt in novel environments. Defending against these threats requires more than input filtering or runtime constraints—it demands a rethinking of how trust, traceability, and verification are embedded into the training lifecycle of intelligent agents.

## Poisoning Attacks on Transfer Learning and Fine-Tuning Processes

Transfer learning has become a cornerstone of modern machine learning practice, allowing developers to build performant models by adapting pretrained networks to new tasks using relatively small datasets. While this approach dramatically reduces training time and data requirements, it also opens a unique vector for adversarial poisoning. Because the underlying representations are inherited from an upstream model, attackers need not compromise the entire architecture. Instead, they can subtly manipulate fine-tuning inputs to steer pretrained models toward adversarial behaviors that persist across downstream tasks. These vulnerabilities often go undetected due to the apparent success of the fine-tuned model on conventional evaluation metrics.

One of the most insidious techniques in this domain is the use of poisoned fine-tuning datasets. Adversaries craft inputs with correct labels and valid structure but subtly distorted feature distributions. These poisoned examples are designed to cause localized shifts in decision boundaries, especially when they target regions of latent space already shaped by the pretrained model. When injected into fine-tuning sets, these poisons exploit the model's existing representations, reinforcing incorrect associations that appear benign under typical validation regimes. This strategy enables targeted misclassification attacks that require minimal data volume and evade anomaly detection.

Feature collision poisoning leverages the structure of high-dimensional embeddings to induce controlled misbehavior. By injecting fine-tuning examples that visually or semantically resemble one class but are labeled as another, the attacker causes the model to cluster semantically dissimilar inputs in close proximity. This overlap distorts the class boundaries learned during fine-tuning, introducing ambiguity that attackers later exploit to force misclassification. Because the pretrained model provides a fixed embedding space, feature collision poisoning is particularly effective when the attacker understands the representational biases of the upstream model. Even when such poisons constitute a fraction of the fine-tuning set, their influence can persist due to the model's strong inductive bias.

Gradient-alignment poisoning provides a more refined method of influence. This technique involves selecting or synthesizing fine-tuning samples whose gradients, when backpropagated, cause predictable parameter shifts aligned with adversarial objectives. Attackers typically use surrogate models to optimize these poisoned inputs, ensuring that each one maximally steers the decision boundary in the desired direction. Over multiple epochs, this gradient manipulation subtly reorients the model's weights without alerting standard performance metrics. The poisoned model behaves normally under typical inputs but exhibits controlled failures when encountering attacker-designed test cases.
