
From `label` manipulation and clean-`label` subversion to backdoor triggers and federated model compromise, this chapter explores how attackers exploit the training lifecycle to shift decision boundaries, inject hidden control signals, and destabilize policy formation. It examines the subtle methods used to embed adversarial objectives through gradient manipulation, feature-space collisions, and data camouflage techniques. The discussion extends to poisoning in transfer learning, where pretrained model reuse introduces a potent supply-chain vector, and in RL, where reward corruption and environment manipulation distort agent behavior. These attack surfaces span model types, architectures, and operational domains, reflecting the pervasive reach of training-time compromise.

As organizations increasingly integrate AI models into critical infrastructure, security architectures must account for poisoning as both a technical and strategic risk. The consequences extend beyond prediction errors—they include silent policy failure, ethical bias amplification, and adversary-controlled model behavior. For compliance, governance, and system design teams, understanding the mechanisms and implications of poisoning attacks is fundamental to securing the AI supply chain, validating model lineage, and enforcing trustworthy machine learning practices. Defending against these threats requires a unified approach that incorporates model governance, data hygiene, training oversight, and continuous behavioral validation.

## Fundamentals and Mechanisms of Training-Time Poisoning

Training-time poisoning attacks exploit a foundational vulnerability in machine learning: the absolute dependence of model behavior on the statistical properties of its training data. When that training data is maliciously modified—whether through injected examples, corrupted labels, or adversarial manipulations—the model internalizes false patterns that can persist throughout its operational lifetime. These attacks often succeed without degrading the model's overall performance, making them difficult to detect and particularly effective for precision-targeted subversions of AI behavior.

At the heart of training-time poisoning lies the attacker's ability to shape the data distribution in ways that mislead the model's learning process. This influence need not be extensive or obvious; it can consist of just a small fraction of poisoned samples strategically placed within a massive dataset. When inserted correctly, these poisoned examples alter the model's learned representations and decision boundaries, redirecting inference behavior under controlled conditions. Because the model cannot differentiate between benign and adversarially constructed samples, it generalizes from poisoned examples just as it does from legitimate ones.

What makes poisoning especially insidious is that poisoned data is often crafted to appear clean and valid. Rather than injecting obvious noise or anomalies, attackers embed misdirecting features, subtle shifts in label correlations, or carefully constructed triggers that exploit the model's tendency to overfit localized patterns. These manipulations can move class boundaries or create malicious attractors in latent space, all while passing conventional data quality checks. In large-scale, automated data pipelines, such poisoned inputs can enter unnoticed and influence model performance long before anyone realizes their presence.

Poisoning tactics typically focus not on catastrophic failure but on controlled, predictable malfunctions. A sophisticated attacker might poison a few dozen examples to ensure a model misclassifies a specific input, misattributes a label under a narrow set of conditions, or produces biased outputs for targeted demographics. These manipulations do not cripple the model; they preserve its general utility while subtly distorting its outputs in ways that serve the attacker's objective. The poisoned model becomes, in effect, an obedient saboteur—functional, but compromised. The precision of these attacks stems from the attacker's deliberate choice of targets. A poison campaign might focus on a particular output class, such as mislabeling rare objects in an image classifier, or it might distort predictions for inputs containing sensitive demographic features, reinforcing bias or discrimination. In other cases, poisoning affects specific input subspaces—perhaps only misclassifying data that contains a particular phrase, color pattern, or sensor signal. By anchoring the attack to narrowly scoped regions of the input space, the attacker maximizes stealth while achieving tactical control over the model's behavior.

Stealth is critical to the success of training-time poisoning. Attacks must evade human and automated scrutiny, both during data preparation and after training validation. Most machine learning pipelines rely on aggregate statistics to assess model health—metrics like accuracy, loss, or precision. Poisoned models are engineered to meet these metrics while harboring localized distortions. The poisoning ratio is typically kept low—often under 1% of the dataset—to avoid detection through statistical anomaly detection or performance degradation on test sets. Many poisoning attacks flourish because quality assurance processes are overly focused on aggregate outcomes and lack rigorous provenance checks. In many machine learning workflows, particularly in federated, distributed, or crowdsourced training regimes, data ingestion is decentralized and opaque. An attacker can compromise upstream data sources or inject poison into collaborative platforms, confident that downstream engineers will lack the tools, time, or incentives to inspect individual data points. In such environments, trust in data becomes a liability.
