By exposing the model to attacks during training, it learns to become more robust against similar threats at inference time. While effective in increasing resilience, it often introduces trade-offs in model accuracy and computational cost. 

`Adversarial Trigger`

: An adversarial trigger is a specific pattern—such as a word, phrase, or image feature—that activates malicious behavior embedded in a model, often through backdoor attacks. These triggers are designed to be unnoticed or benign to humans but highly effective at manipulating the model's output. They act as the key to unlock hidden functions inserted during training. 

`Agentic AI`

: Agentic AI refers to artificial intelligence systems that can make autonomous decisions and take actions over time, often pursuing specific goals within an environment. These systems incorporate planning, memory, and tool usage, and can operate without direct human input. Their autonomy makes them powerful—and potentially dangerous—targets for adversarial control. 

`Agentic Architecture`

: An agentic architecture is the design framework for building AI agents that perceive, reason, plan, and act in the world. It typically includes components like memory, environment interfaces, and decision-making modules. When such architectures are exposed to adversarial manipulation, attackers may hijack tools, redirect goals, or disrupt control loops. 

`Agentic Model`

: An agentic model is a machine learning system capable of initiating sequences of actions based on goals or observations, often forming part of an AI agent. These models differ from static predictors by interacting with environments and adapting to feedback. Their complexity introduces new attack surfaces, such as manipulated memory or corrupted action chains. 

`Annotation Manipulation`

: Annotation manipulation is a data poisoning tactic where an attacker alters the labels or annotations in a training dataset to influence how the model learns. This can degrade performance, introduce bias, or plant targeted vulnerabilities. Since labels often come from crowdsourcing or automated processes, they are a soft target for stealthy attacks. 

`API Misuse Risk`

: API misuse risk refers to the security exposure created when machine learning models are accessed through public or internal APIs that lack proper controls. Attackers may exploit these interfaces to probe, extract, or overload models with crafted queries. Without rate limiting, authentication, or input validation, APIs become a convenient avenue for adversarial reconnaissance and exploitation. 

`Artifact Contamination`

: Artifact contamination occurs when a model learns to rely on irrelevant or unintended signals in the data—known as artifacts—rather than the true features. Attackers can exploit this by inserting misleading patterns that skew model behavior. These artifacts can silently shift model predictions, increasing the system's fragility and bias. 

`Attention Flow Visualization`

: Attention flow visualization is a method for interpreting how attention-based models—like transformers—distribute focus across different parts of an input. It is used to understand model reasoning, debug behavior, or detect anomalies. In adversarial settings, attackers may intentionally distort attention maps to obscure or mislead model decision pathways. 

`Attention Misalignment`

: Attention misalignment describes a condition in which a model's attention mechanisms focus on irrelevant, misleading, or manipulated features of the input. This can lead to faulty outputs or exploitable weaknesses. Adversaries can induce misalignment through perturbations or poisoned data, subtly shifting model behavior without obvious errors. 

`Attention Stability Mechanisms`

: Attention stability mechanisms are design features or training techniques used to ensure that a model's attention distribution remains consistent and interpretable under slight changes to input. These mechanisms help guard against adversarial perturbations that might otherwise shift attention to irrelevant or harmful content. They play a growing role in making transformer-based models more robust and trustworthy. 

`Autonomous Decision Loop`

: An autonomous decision loop refers to the continuous cycle in which an AI system perceives its environment, makes decisions, takes actions, and updates its understanding without direct human input. This feedback loop is central to agentic AI and reinforcement learning systems. When compromised, it becomes a channel through which adversaries can introduce cascading errors or hijack behavior over time. 

`Autoregressive Conditioning`

: Autoregressive conditioning is a technique where each token generated by a model is conditioned on the previous tokens, forming the backbone of many large language models. This structure allows for fluid, coherent text generation but also creates opportunities for adversarial prompt manipulation. Attackers can carefully craft initial inputs to steer the rest of the generation toward targeted outcomes. 

`Backdoor`

: A backdoor is a hidden vulnerability intentionally or unintentionally embedded in a machine learning model, allowing an attacker to control outputs when a specific trigger is present. These backdoors are often injected during training and remain dormant until activated. They are especially dangerous because they may not affect performance on standard benchmarks, making them hard to detect. 

`Backdoor Activation`

: Backdoor activation refers to the moment when a hidden vulnerability in a model is triggered, causing the system to deviate from its intended behavior. This is typically initiated by a specific pattern or input, such as a visual marker or a keyword. Once activated, the model may output attacker-controlled results, regardless of normal logic or context. 

`Backdoor Attack`

: A backdoor attack is a form of data poisoning where an attacker trains or fine-tunes a model to associate specific triggers with incorrect outputs. These attacks are subtle and do not typically degrade the model's general accuracy, allowing the backdoor to remain undetected. They represent one of the most covert and persistent threats in adversarial machine learning. 

`Backdoor Trigger`

: A backdoor trigger is the specific input feature—such as a word, phrase, image patch, or token pattern—that activates a hidden backdoor in a model. Triggers are crafted to be inconspicuous or irrelevant to human users while reliably influencing model behavior. Identifying and neutralizing these triggers is a critical focus in backdoor defense research. 

`Behavioral Drift`

: Behavioral drift occurs when a machine learning model's output patterns change over time, often due to shifting data distributions, environmental inputs, or internal state. This can happen naturally or be induced by adversarial influence. Detecting and correcting for drift is essential to maintaining AI system integrity, especially in safety-critical applications. 

`Behavioral Drift Detection`

: Behavioral drift detection involves monitoring a model's predictions or internal states to identify unexpected shifts in behavior over time. It is used to detect adversarial interference, data poisoning, or environmental changes that could degrade model performance. Techniques may include statistical monitoring, uncertainty estimation, or comparisons with historical baselines.
