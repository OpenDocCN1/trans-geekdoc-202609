
Efforts to mitigate federated poisoning often rely on robust aggregation techniques like `Krum`, `median aggregation`, or `norm clipping`. While these methods offer some resistance to extreme updates, they are vulnerable to coordinated attacks from multiple clients or finely tuned poison gradients. Moreover, they often trade off model performance for security, degrading accuracy on non-IID data distributions common in federated setups. This tension between robustness and utility creates a persistent dilemma for practitioners trying to defend against sophisticated poisoning strategies.

Federated learning frameworks that employ continual learning or online adaptation are especially susceptible to slow-poisoning attacks. In such systems, the model continually evolves in response to new data, making it difficult to distinguish between legitimate shifts and adversarial drift. Attackers can exploit this by slowly adjusting the model's behavior over hundreds of training rounds, ensuring that the poisoning remains hidden within long-term adaptation trends. These attacks resist forensic detection because the behavioral change accumulates gradually and appears to be a natural consequence of environmental learning.

Detecting and attributing poisoning in federated environments requires rethinking core assumptions about trust, visibility, and data provenance. Defensive strategies must consider not only what updates look like, but who submits them, how frequently, and in what patterns. Identity verification, behavioral baselining, and provenance tagging can offer partial relief but may conflict with privacy guarantees. As federated learning continues to evolve as a privacy-preserving solution, its security model must evolve in parallel, recognizing that decentralization introduces not just resilience, but a rich and underdefended attack surface.

## Poisoning Attacks Against Reinforcement Learning (RL) Systems

Reinforcement learning systems are uniquely vulnerable to poisoning due to their dependency on iterative interaction with a training environment and reward feedback loop. Unlike supervised learning, where inputs and outputs are static and label-based, RL agents continuously update their policies based on sequences of experiences, rewards, and state transitions. This dynamism creates multiple entry points for adversaries to subtly manipulate the learning process without immediate detection. Poisoning in this context can result in agents learning flawed or exploitable policies that function normally during validation but behave incorrectly under specific operational conditions. The complexity of reward structures and temporal dependencies makes detection of such manipulations particularly difficult.

One of the primary poisoning vectors in RL is the `experience replay buffer`. Many deep RL algorithms rely on stored past experiences to improve data efficiency and policy convergence. An attacker with access to the buffer, or with influence over the environment that feeds it, can inject corrupted state-action-reward transitions that bias the agent's policy gradient updates. These malicious experiences may resemble valid interactions but are constructed to subtly reinforce undesired actions or punish optimal behaviors. Over time, even a small percentage of poisoned entries can significantly alter the trajectory of policy evolution, especially in off-policy algorithms that draw extensively from past interactions.

Reward-shaping attacks target the agent's most trusted signal: the `reward`. By introducing incorrect, delayed, or contextually misleading rewards, an attacker can influence the optimization landscape to favor suboptimal or harmful behaviors. These poisoned rewards are often not blatant misassignments; they may be minor distortions that persist across enough episodes to redirect the agent's learning priorities. In environments where reward functions are externally defined or subject to noise, distinguishing between legitimate stochastic variance and adversarial manipulation becomes non-trivial. Reward poisoning can also exploit long-horizon dependencies by creating feedback loops that only manifest much later in the training process.

Manipulating the training environment itself provides another potent channel for poisoning. `Environment-based poisoning` involves altering state transitions, observation models, or environment dynamics in ways that induce systematic errors in the learned policy. This may involve skewing physical properties in simulated environments, introducing hidden variables that condition policy selection, or modifying state representations that the agent uses to derive actions. Such attacks are particularly effective in simulation-to-real transfer, where the agent learns in a controlled environment and is later deployed in the real world, often assuming consistency between training and deployment dynamics.

Attackers can also embed poison directly into policy gradient updates by manipulating specific state-action pairs. These inputs are crafted to appear statistically consistent with the environment but are tuned to produce disproportionate influence on the policy optimization process. For example, a poisoned state might be engineered to slightly increase the probability of a specific action under stochastic gradient updates. When reinforced over many epochs, these small biases accumulate, leading the agent to systematically prefer suboptimal or attacker-desired policies. Because the poisoning is entangled with the agent's learning dynamics, detection requires introspection into intermediate gradient pathways, which is often infeasible in large-scale implementations.

Temporal poisoning introduces a more subtle and sophisticated dimension of attack. By manipulating sequences of events or reward structures over time, the attacker can forge false correlations between temporally distant events. The RL agent, designed to discover such patterns, internalizes these correlations as causal, integrating them into its policy decisions. For example, a delayed negative reward after an optimal action can teach the agent to avoid that action altogether, despite its actual utility. Temporal poisoning exploits the agent's tendency to extrapolate from temporal structure, embedding policies that fail in ways that are highly contextual and difficult to diagnose post-training.

Critical stages of training, such as early policy formation or moments of high variance in learning rate, are particularly susceptible to persistent poisoning. Inputs introduced during these windows have outsized influence on the final policy landscape. Attackers exploit this by injecting poisoned transitions or rewards precisely during these sensitive phases, embedding vulnerabilities that remain stable through subsequent training rounds. These weaknesses often do not result in catastrophic failure, but rather in degraded performance in specific edge cases, inconsistent behavior under stress, or policy brittleness under minor perturbations—all of which can be exploited post-deployment.

Policy distillation, a common practice in RL to compress or transfer learned policies, introduces additional poisoning risk. In teacher–student frameworks, attackers can poison the training of the teacher policy or tamper with the imitation process during student policy learning. This allows malicious behavior to propagate undetected from one model to another, preserving functional accuracy while embedding structural vulnerabilities. Because the distilled policy is often evaluated based on performance metrics alone, latent weaknesses passed from the teacher may be inherited without scrutiny. This transfer of poisoned behavior complicates auditing, especially when the original training data or environments are no longer accessible.
