
Transformer-based language models, particularly those using self-attention mechanisms, exhibit unique robustness profiles due to their reliance on token relationships rather than spatial locality. Their multi-head attention layers introduce complex sensitivity surfaces, especially in middle-layer embeddings where contextual meaning is aggregated. Perturbations injected at the token or subword level often propagate disproportionately through the attention graph, causing semantic drift or misaligned completions. Technical evaluations reveal that small perturbations to highly attended tokens can induce cascading inference instability, particularly in decoder-only autoregressive transformers where errors accumulate over generated sequences. This architecture-specific weakness is distinct from convolutional models, which tend to exhibit more localized failure responses.

Convolutional neural networks, commonly used in vision tasks, display robustness characteristics tied to spatial feature hierarchies. Their vulnerability often manifests in early-layer activations where edge and texture detectors are sensitive to high-frequency noise. Adversarial perturbations targeted at low-level convolutional filters can mislead later stages into amplifying erroneous spatial patterns. Technical perturbation studies show that convolutional models with small receptive fields are more easily fooled by single-pixel attacks or localized occlusions, whereas larger receptive fields introduce some robustness through feature averaging. However, deep CNNs with aggressive downsampling remain susceptible to imperceptible perturbations that exploit aliasing or stride-based artifacts in the convolution stack.

Multi-modal architectures such as Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training (CLIP) or A Large-scale ImaGe and Noisy-text embedding (ALIGN) present a different challenge altogether. These models operate by aligning latent representations across vision and language domains, and robustness failures often arise from inconsistencies in cross-modal embedding-space alignment. Perturbing only one modality—such as injecting noise into an image—can shift the shared embedding just enough to cause mismatched text-image pairs. These errors are particularly subtle because they emerge not from direct classification but from similarity ranking. Robustness analyses of multi-modal systems must therefore consider not just input perturbation but latent alignment variance, measuring cosine drift and distance compression under adversarial transformations across modalities.

Autoregressive models, including both language generators and diffusion-based image models, face compounded robustness challenges due to their sequential inference pathways. Each output step is conditioned on prior predictions, meaning that a small input perturbation early in the sequence can propagate errors downstream. In language models, this manifests as escalating hallucinations or context deviation, especially when perturbations target the model's prompt prefix or few-shot examples. In diffusion models, perturbation-induced noise in early denoising steps can distort latent trajectories irreversibly. Technical evaluation requires modeling these dynamics over time, tracking perturbation amplification through successive sampling stages.

Reinforcement learning architectures introduce robustness concerns related not just to perception, but to policy behavior. Adversarial inputs in these models can target state observations or reward functions, leading to erratic or unsafe policy decisions. Perturbation studies reveal that memory-augmented policies and recurrent actor-critic architectures are especially vulnerable to targeted input corruption across time steps. These systems must maintain internal coherence across episodes, and adversarial attacks that disrupt sequence memory or temporal embeddings can destabilize long-term planning. Unlike classifiers, RL agents require robustness evaluation across both input states and transition functions, often using adversarial training in simulated environments to quantify cumulative reward degradation. Robustness at the embedding level also varies dramatically by architecture. In transformer models, embedding layers are vulnerable to semantic misalignment when perturbed tokens alter the contextual landscape. In convolutional networks, embedding-space perturbations correspond to texture distortions or occlusion sensitivity. In diffusion and latent variable models, embeddings represent compressed distributions, and adversarial perturbations manifest as shifts in sample manifolds. Measuring perturbation impact in these latent spaces requires tools such as nearest-neighbor consistency, KL divergence, or latent vector norm drift, to track semantic fidelity under attack. Embedding sensitivity analysis is a key technique in diagnosing which model components absorb perturbations and which amplify them.

Technical studies comparing perturbation transferability across models show clear trends in architectural susceptibility. Adversarial examples generated against convolutional vision models often transfer well across different CNNs but poorly to transformer-based models. Conversely, attacks crafted on large language models exhibit moderate transfer across architectures with similar tokenizer schemes or attention depths. The degree of perturbation transfer correlates with model alignment in feature space, and evaluation frameworks now measure this explicitly by cross-model ASR. This analysis reveals whether vulnerabilities are rooted in model-specific quirks or in shared inductive biases—a critical distinction for evaluating systemic architectural risk.

Attention mechanisms themselves present architectural vulnerabilities. Softmax-based attention tends to be highly sensitive to adversarial token weighting, and small perturbations in attention logits can disproportionately influence context aggregation. Multi-head designs provide some diversification but also introduce multiple points of failure. Some robustness-enhancing architectures replace soft attention with sparse or hard attention mechanisms to limit this sensitivity. Technical evaluations that dissect attention head variance and gradient flow under perturbation reveal that even robustly trained models may contain individual heads that collapse under targeted noise, indicating the need for head-level fault isolation.
