`# Contextual Escalation`

: Contextual escalation is an adversarial strategy in which an attacker gradually increases the model's exposure to sensitive or manipulative content across multiple turns or inputs. By slowly shifting context, the attacker bypasses safety mechanisms that would block a direct prompt. It is a common technique for jailbreaking chatbots and autonomous agents. 

**`Contextual Trigger`**

: A `contextual trigger` is a condition within a broader input sequence that causes a model to activate hidden behaviors only when a certain context is met. Unlike static triggers, these rely on specific conversation flow, metadata, or environmental cues. They are especially effective in large language models and agentic systems where behavior is context-driven. 

**`Continuous Deployment Compromise`**

: Continuous deployment compromise occurs when an adversary infiltrates the pipeline that automatically pushes new model updates into production. By targeting code repositories, model checkpoints, or CI/CD tools, attackers can inject malicious logic or backdoors into live systems. This form of supply chain attack is particularly dangerous in organizations that rely on automated deployment workflows. 

**`Conversational Injection`**

: Conversational injection is an attack technique where malicious content is embedded in a dialogue to manipulate or hijack the behavior of a conversational AI system. It often relies on subtle changes in tone, phrasing, or sequence that mislead the model without triggering safety filters. This is a key vector for jailbreaks and unintended model actions in chat interfaces. 

**`Cross-Domain Inference Exploitation`**

: Cross-domain inference exploitation leverages weaknesses in how models generalize knowledge across different domains—such as transferring from medical data to financial predictions. Attackers use this to craft inputs that appear benign in one context but cause harmful behavior when the model applies misaligned domain assumptions. It reveals the risks of overgeneralization and unverified transfer learning. 

**`Cross-Domain Perturbation`**

: Cross-domain perturbation involves introducing small changes to inputs in one domain that lead to misclassifications or errors when passed through a model trained on another domain. These attacks highlight how subtle shifts in format or structure can undermine model reliability across tasks. They are common in multimodal systems and domain-adaptive models. 

**`Cross-Modal Leakage`**

: Cross-modal leakage occurs when sensitive or unintended information is transferred from one input modality (e.g., image) into another output modality (e.g., text). This often arises in models that handle multiple data types simultaneously, such as vision-language systems. It poses privacy and integrity risks, especially when the leakage exposes confidential context. 

**`Cross-Model Transfer Attack`**

: A cross-model transfer attack is an adversarial strategy in which inputs crafted to exploit one model also succeed in misleading other, unrelated models. This attack leverages the shared vulnerabilities across architectures or training styles. It demonstrates the lack of true diversity in many AI systems and exposes systemic risks. 

**`Cross-Model Transferability`**

: Cross-model transferability is the tendency of adversarial examples or poisoned data to impact multiple different models, even those trained independently. High transferability means that an attack doesn't need full access to the target model to succeed. This phenomenon complicates black-box defense and underscores the challenge of securing shared ecosystems. 

**`Crowdsourced Labeling Exploit`**

: A crowdsourced labeling exploit takes advantage of weaknesses in human-in-the-loop data labeling platforms. Attackers may intentionally inject mislabeled or misleading examples during open annotation campaigns. These corrupted labels subtly degrade model quality or embed strategic errors, especially in systems that rely on weak supervision or minimal post-label validation. 

**`Data Camouflage`**

: Data camouflage refers to the practice of hiding poisoned or adversarial samples within benign-looking data so they bypass quality checks and filtering. This technique is used to insert harmful examples during training without drawing attention. It often relies on imperceptible noise, ambiguous labeling, or stylistic blending to avoid detection. 

**`Data Drift`**

: Data drift refers to a gradual change in the statistical properties of input data over time, which can lead to degraded model performance. This shift may be caused by changes in user behavior, environments, or adversarial influence. Detecting and correcting for data drift is essential to maintaining reliable predictions in deployed systems. 

**`Data Memorization`**

: Data memorization occurs when a model retains and reproduces specific examples from its training data, sometimes including personally identifiable or sensitive content. This is particularly problematic in large models trained on unfiltered datasets. Attackers can exploit memorization to extract training data or trigger unintended disclosures. 

**`Data Poisoning`**

: Data poisoning is an adversarial attack that introduces maliciously crafted samples into the training set, causing the model to learn incorrect or biased behaviors. Poisoning can lead to misclassifications, backdoors, or broader system degradation. It is one of the most challenging and stealthy threats in machine learning security. 

**`Decision Boundary`**

: A decision boundary is the surface or region in input space where a model switches from one prediction to another. Understanding this boundary is critical to identifying where the model is most vulnerable to adversarial perturbations. Attackers often aim to push inputs across decision boundaries with minimal changes. 

**`Decision-Boundary Probing`**

: Decision-boundary probing involves methodically testing inputs near a model's classification threshold to learn its internal structure or weaknesses. This technique can be used to reverse-engineer decision logic or craft high-confidence adversarial examples. It's often employed in black-box model extraction and boundary-based evasion attacks. 

**`Deferred Trigger`**

: A deferred trigger is a backdoor or adversarial mechanism that only activates after a series of interactions or time-based conditions. Unlike immediate triggers, deferred triggers are harder to detect through static analysis or simple red-teaming. They are useful for delayed exploitation in long-running systems or agent-based environments. 

**`Embedding Consistency Checks`**

: Embedding consistency checks are techniques used to verify that the internal representations (embeddings) of similar inputs remain stable across model versions or inputs. These checks help detect model drift, fine-tuning contamination, or adversarial interference. Significant deviations may signal underlying vulnerabilities or intentional manipulation. 

**`Embedding Drift`**

: Embedding drift occurs when the vector representations of similar inputs begin to diverge over time or across models. This drift may be caused by model updates, data changes, or adversarial fine-tuning. It can affect search, retrieval, and reasoning systems that rely on stable semantic representations.
