
The fragility of vision models arises from their reliance on brittle statistical correlations rather than robust object understanding. Convolutional layers, while excellent at detecting texture and shape primitives, are sensitive to context distortions and adversarial noise. Attention-based vision transformers, although more resilient to some perturbations, introduce their own vulnerabilities in positional encoding and patch-based tokenization. Attackers can tailor their strategies to specific architectures, targeting known sensitivities and decision surface anomalies to increase attack fidelity.

As model complexity grows, interpretability becomes increasingly critical to understanding and defending against visual evasion. Saliency maps and feature attribution tools can reveal model blind spots and misaligned focus, providing defenders with insights into which image regions contribute most to classification decisions. However, these same tools can be used by adversaries to fine-tune perturbation placement and minimize distortion budgets. The arms race between attackers and defenders continues to intensify, with each side leveraging deeper architectural understanding to refine their strategies.

Evasion attacks on vision systems are not merely academic artifacts—they have been observed in real-world deployments across security, retail, transportation, and defense. From adversarial license plates used to foil traffic enforcement systems to facial makeup patterns that disrupt biometric recognition, the practical applications of these techniques are expanding. As models are deployed in increasingly autonomous and high-assurance environments, the consequences of successful visual evasion grow more severe, warranting comprehensive mitigation strategies that address both digital and physical threat vectors.

## Evasion Attacks on Time-Series and Sequential Models

Evasion attacks on time-series and sequential models exploit the temporal dependencies and predictive assumptions embedded within algorithms used for anomaly detection, classification, and forecasting. These models are commonly applied to financial transactions, sensor telemetry, industrial control logs, and network flows, making them critical components of cyber-physical security and fraud prevention. Attackers target the structural fragility of these systems by crafting minimally perturbed sequences that subvert the model's expectations without disrupting the appearance of natural behavior. The temporal aspect of the data provides both an attack surface and a camouflage, allowing small manipulations to cascade into significant prediction errors over time.

One approach involves perturbing temporal features such as event intervals, timestamps, or periodicity signatures. Models trained on regular temporal patterns—such as heartbeat intervals, transaction frequencies, or equipment cycle durations—can be desensitized by slight deviations that remain within human or operational tolerance thresholds. These perturbations are calibrated to evade statistical anomaly detectors that flag large deviations while still altering the sequence's temporal signature enough to confuse model internals. The result is a delay or suppression of alert generation, which can be critical in scenarios such as insider threat detection or fault prediction in autonomous systems.

Attackers may also target peak values or transient spikes that carry disproportionate weight in classification or forecasting outcomes. By subtly attenuating these critical points or redistributing their magnitude across adjacent time steps, adversaries can suppress anomaly scores or shift predictions. For example, muting a sudden surge in CPU usage, financial outflows, or network bandwidth can help malicious activities blend into the normal profile of a system. These modifications exploit the model's dependence on local gradients or thresholds while preserving global statistical consistency to avoid triggering alarms or human intervention.

Strategic insertion or deletion of specific data points within the sequence allows for more aggressive manipulation of model behavior. By injecting plausible but misleading events—such as fabricated log entries or artificial sensor readings—attackers can shift model context to produce benign classifications. Conversely, removing events from a sequence can eliminate evidence of compromise or obscure early indicators of malicious activity. These manipulations must respect domain constraints, such as ordering rules or physical process limits, requiring attackers to simulate domain-consistent behavior while executing their perturbations.

Sequential models such as Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs) and transformers are particularly vulnerable to early-sequence perturbations. Due to the recurrent or attention-based nature of these architectures, small changes at the beginning of a sequence can influence internal states, memory gates, or attention weights in ways that amplify downstream. An attacker may inject subtle deviations in the early portion of a network traffic flow, causing the entire session to be misclassified as legitimate. These attacks exploit the compounded effect of temporal dependencies and model accumulation logic, leading to persistent inference errors across the sequence.

Adaptive or conditional evasion methods introduce feedback into the perturbation process, allowing attackers to probe the model and refine their modifications incrementally. Using techniques such as reinforcement learning or gradient estimation, adversaries can observe classification changes in response to partial sequence inputs and adjust future perturbations accordingly. This closed-loop approach makes it possible to learn the internal behavior of black-box systems over time, eventually yielding a high-confidence evasion strategy that is robust across different input conditions. These methods are especially effective in industrial anomaly detection systems where a consistent model response is critical. Examples include altering both temperature and pressure sensors in a manufacturing system or modifying multiple financial indicators to mask illicit transactions. These compound evasion strategies are significantly more difficult to detect due to the increased dimensionality and the complexity of modeling interdependencies in multichannel data.
