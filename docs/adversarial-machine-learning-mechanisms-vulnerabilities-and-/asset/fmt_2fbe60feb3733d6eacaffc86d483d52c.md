
The process of pretraining further compounds these challenges. Models are trained on vast, heterogeneous datasets scraped from public domains—forums, social media, code repositories, and academic papers—without human curation at scale. This invites poisoning attacks where adversaries deliberately seed corrupt or malicious content into the training corpus. Because of the sheer volume and opacity of the data sources, such insertions are difficult to detect and may persist undetected across model iterations. An attacker could, for instance, introduce specific phrases that reliably generate biased completions or embed payloads that only activate when particular semantic cues are present. As shown in `[Table 8.1](#c08.xhtml#c08-tbl-0001)`, the contrasting attack surfaces between predictive and generative models reveal how output flexibility and context sensitivity fundamentally reshape exploitation dynamics.

Another critical aspect lies in tokenization and embedding strategies. Most LLMs use subword tokenization schemes—such as Byte Pair Encoding (`BPE`) or WordPiece—that decompose input text into units learned from the training corpus. This decomposition can be exploited to create adversarial perturbations at the token boundary level. Embedding layers, which map tokens into high-dimensional continuous vector spaces, inherit the structure of the training data and thus reflect biases, correlations, and vulnerabilities embedded within. Attackers may design prompt inputs that navigate or exploit peculiarities in the embedding space—such as regions with high gradient sensitivity or semantic instability—to generate unpredictable outputs.

The attention heads within transformers—each representing a pathway of focus over different semantic dimensions—offer yet another surface for attack. Some studies have shown that individual attention heads can be disproportionately responsible for specific behaviors, including syntactic structure, coreference resolution, or even harmful responses. By targeting prompts to engage particular heads or combinations thereof, attackers can induce or suppress behaviors at will. This capacity for targeted manipulation challenges the notion of interpretability and reveals how emergent model behaviors may be traceable to architectural pathways that can be hijacked with surgical precision.

Beyond isolated components, the overall structure of generative LLMs introduces the concept of high-dimensional latent space manipulation. These latent spaces encode an enormous spectrum of meanings, associations, and potential outputs. Within such a space, it is possible to insert adversarial “islands” that are rarely activated during normal use but reliably surface when prompted by highly specific contexts. These adversarial regions may contain biased completions, unsafe advice, or even system-level jailbreak triggers. Because the activations are buried deep in latent space, conventional testing often fails to detect them—rendering them stealthy yet potent vectors for exploitation.

Contextual prediction—the foundation of LLM generation—also opens doors to prompt engineering and conditioning attacks. By carefully controlling the textual environment surrounding a target input, adversaries can mislead the model into adopting a specific interpretation, tone, or factual perspective. For example, framing a prompt with misleading instructions, fabricated metadata, or emotionally charged content can push the model into generating skewed or false narratives. When LLMs are embedded into broader systems, such as customer support bots or search assistants, these contextual attacks can manipulate the behavior of the entire application stack.

The inherent difficulty of inspecting and interpreting transformer operations compounds the threat. Unlike traditional rule-based systems, LLMs operate as black-box functions with internal states that are difficult to audit. The internal activations and weights do not correspond to discrete, human-readable rules. This opacity means attackers can probe and map model behaviors using only output feedback, developing an empirical understanding of how certain prompts or patterns lead to specific completions. In the case of closed-source models, where even architectural details are hidden, adversaries can still reverse-engineer behaviors through prompt–response interactions, a technique known as black-box probing.

Attention-based architectures also encourage positional encoding vulnerabilities. These encodings—added to embeddings to preserve the sequence order—affect how the model attends to each token. Certain positional patterns can introduce anomalies or disproportionate attention shifts, especially when adversaries craft prompts with atypical length, formatting, or repetition. Exploits have emerged where long strings of specific patterns—like Unicode anomalies, homoglyphs, or zero-width characters—trigger degenerate outputs or bypass content moderation filters. Because such tokens may not be rendered visibly, these exploits are difficult to detect during casual inspection.

Attackers also exploit inductive biases in how LLMs generalize. Generative models learn not just language but the conventions of interaction—how questions are phrased, how instructions are followed, how citations appear. By mimicking or subverting these conventions, adversaries can embed adversarial content within seemingly benign queries. For example, a prompt styled as a software license request may lead the model to produce shell commands or code snippets that could be misused, especially in downstream automation contexts. In enterprise environments, this increases the risk of model outputs being mistaken for safe or sanctioned actions.

An emerging area of concern lies in the differential vulnerabilities introduced by fine-tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (`RLHF`). While these processes improve alignment, they also create secondary gradients and optimization paths that can be adversarially manipulated. Attackers may reverse-engineer fine-tuning objectives or `RLHF` reward signals to reconstruct prompts that align with adversarial goals. In some cases, fine-tuning can inadvertently reduce model robustness by overfitting safety behaviors to narrow conditions, leaving other regions of the input space dangerously under-constrained.
