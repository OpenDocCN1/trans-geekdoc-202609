## Key Concepts

Trust isn't something we can bolt onto an AI system after it's built—it has to be designed into its architecture, behavior, and monitoring from the start. This chapter reframed security not as something external to the model, but as something structural: adversarial robustness, runtime detection, formal guarantees, and interpretability must all live inside the system. These aren't just technical features—they are strategic commitments. In a world where AI systems increasingly power critical infrastructure, financial decisions, and safety systems, it's no longer enough to ask if a model works. We must ask if it can be trusted to behave safely under pressure, attack, and uncertainty.

### Essential Terms in Context

*   **Security-by-Design:** This is the approach of integrating security directly into the AI system's architecture and learning process. In the chapter, it referred to techniques like embedding perturbation resistance or runtime checks directly in the model. It's important because patching AI after deployment is too late—robustness needs to be engineered upfront.

*   **Embedding-Space Drift:** This means internal representations inside the model shifting in response to small, often malicious, input changes. We saw how attackers exploit this drift to confuse or mislead the model. Detecting and stabilizing embedding-space behavior is key to defending against subtle manipulation.

*   **Perturbation-Aware Training:** This involves deliberately training a model to be robust against small input changes, including those crafted by attackers. The chapter showed how this makes internal representations and outputs less sensitive to adversarial influence. It's foundational for producing models that don't collapse under minor stress.

*   **Runtime Anomaly Detection:** These are monitoring systems that watch the model in real time to catch strange behavior or unexpected patterns. The chapter explained how this can include checking embeddings, outputs, or attention flows for signs of attack. These systems act like AI immune systems, catching problems before they spread.

*   **Formal Verification:** A mathematical approach to proving that a model will behave in specific, predictable ways under defined conditions. The chapter showed how this is applied to guarantee output stability within known perturbation bounds. It's especially valuable in regulated or high-stakes environments where empirical testing isn't enough.

*   **Spectral Normalization:** A technique used during training to limit how drastically the model's internal functions can change. The chapter discussed using it to enforce stability in deep layers, particularly for defending against gradient-based attacks. It's one way to harden the architecture against internal chaos.

*   **Attention Flow Visualization:** A method to see where a model is focusing during prediction, especially in transformer-based models. We used it in the chapter to detect prompt injection or attention hijacking. It's critical for understanding how generative models can be misled or manipulated.

*   **Token-Level Attribution:** This breaks down how much each word or input element influenced the model's decision. It was used in the chapter to help identify hidden instructions or adversarial tokens in language inputs. It's a powerful tool for post hoc transparency and real-time diagnostics.

*   **Layer-Wise Relevance Propagation (LRP):** This method traces output decisions back through the network to see which internal components contributed the most. In the chapter, it helped visualize how perturbations shift decision pathways. It helps defenders audit inference behavior and detect manipulation.

*   **Quantized Embeddings:** This refers to limiting the model's internal representation space to fixed zones or values. The chapter showed how this constrains how far embeddings can shift under attack. It reduces sensitivity and makes internal logic easier to verify or defend.

*   **Explanation Regularization:** This is a training technique where models are penalized for generating inconsistent explanations across similar inputs. The chapter discussed how it helps stabilize attribution behavior and makes the model harder to trick with semantic noise. It ensures that interpretability isn't just bolted on, but baked in.

*   **Fallback Execution Paths:** These are alternative response routes a model can take if it detects a potential anomaly or attack. The chapter illustrated how these enable safe degradation rather than failure. They're essential in any system where output safety must be guaranteed.

*   **Relevance Redistribution:** This is the shift in what parts of a model's input or architecture become “important” during inference. We discussed how attacks often cause this redistribution to favor poisoned tokens or manipulated features. Catching these shifts can expose hidden perturbations.

*   **Output Sensitivity Threshold:** A limit placed on how much the model's output can change in response to input changes. The chapter used this as a tool to detect and block adversarial influence. It's a safeguard that helps bind AI behavior under unexpected inputs.

### Adversary Spotlight

An attacker targeting systems discussed in this chapter isn't just interested in breaking the model—they're trying to manipulate it without being noticed. From their point of view, the most attractive systems are those with minimal internal checks, no explanation tracking, and brittle embeddings. They'll look for models that behave normally during training but can be silently redirected through well-crafted inputs during deployment. If the system lacks embedded attribution or anomaly detection, their work becomes even easier.
