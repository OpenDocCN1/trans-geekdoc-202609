
From automated perturbation pipelines to physical-world attacks using adversarial patches or character-level manipulations, evasion techniques expose gaps not only in model robustness but also in architecture design, testing strategy, and compliance frameworks. Professionals responsible for AI security must grasp the technical depth and real-world feasibility of these threats to implement durable defenses and maintain system integrity under adversarial conditions. This chapter equips security architects, threat analysts, and risk leaders with the foundational knowledge to evaluate evasion threats and embed resistance into the AI lifecycle from design to deployment.

## Core Principles and Mechanisms of Evasion Attacks

Evasion attacks are one of the most studied and actively exploited categories of adversarial machine learning threats, aimed specifically at the inference stage of model execution. At their core, these attacks manipulate the input to a model in ways that subtly distort its perception without altering the semantic content as interpreted by humans. The result is a targeted misclassification or incorrect prediction, all while evading detection by both human observers and basic validation checks. The strategy hinges on the fragility of learned decision boundaries in complex, high-dimensional feature spaces—a vulnerability that deep learning models are particularly prone to.

The foundational mechanism of an evasion attack involves perturbing the input in such a way that the resulting example lies on the wrong side of the model's decision boundary, despite appearing almost identical to the original input. These perturbations are calculated through optimization procedures that maximize the model's prediction error while minimizing the perceptibility of the changes. For image classifiers, this might mean shifting pixel values by imperceptible amounts. For text models, it could involve the insertion, substitution, or reordering of characters or tokens in ways that confuse the model's token embeddings without altering the sentence's meaning to a human reader.

Attackers operating in white-box scenarios—where full access to the model's architecture and parameters is available—can exploit gradient-based techniques such as the Fast Gradient Sign Method (`FGSM`) or Projected Gradient Descent (`PGD`) to craft effective adversarial examples. These methods compute the gradient of the model's loss function with respect to the input and perturb the input in the direction that increases the loss. In contrast, black-box attacks, where the model is accessible only through its API, require more indirect approaches. These include query-based optimization, transfer attacks from surrogate models, or score-based estimation of decision boundaries.

A defining feature of evasion attacks is their reliance on transferability. This is the phenomenon by which adversarial examples generated for one model tend to remain effective against other models trained on similar data distributions. This property enables attackers to bypass the constraints of black-box access by training a locally crafted surrogate model and using it to develop transferable adversarial inputs. The success of such attacks reflects shared vulnerabilities in model architectures and decision surface geometries, often arising from overfitting, excessive model capacity, or reliance on spurious correlations in the training data. Evasion attacks often seek to minimize perturbation magnitude, balancing adversarial potency with stealth. The optimization objective may be constrained by norm-based thresholds—such as L[0], L[2], or L^∞ norms—that quantify the allowed perturbation in terms of sparse changes, Euclidean distance, or maximum single-component change, respectively. By staying within tight norm bounds, attackers can preserve the statistical and perceptual realism of inputs, reducing the likelihood of triggering anomaly detectors or human suspicion during downstream processing or auditing.

One of the more challenging aspects of crafting successful evasion attacks is identifying input regions close to the model's decision boundaries where a minimal perturbation can flip the prediction. These regions are typically nonlinear and complex, particularly in deep neural networks with non-convex decision surfaces. Attackers often exploit this nonlinearity by incrementally adapting adversarial examples in small steps, using iterative optimization strategies to converge on high-confidence misclassifications without overshooting into detectable distortion.

Real-world success of evasion attacks also depends on the robustness of adversarial examples under input transformations. In practice, inputs may be subject to rotations, scaling, compression, format conversion, or environmental noise. For example, a manipulated image passed through a JPEG pipeline or displayed on a screen and captured again may still need to fool the model. This requirement has led to the development of transformation-invariant adversarial attacks, which optimize perturbations that remain effective despite these downstream alterations. In the text domain, robustness may involve maintaining grammatical structure and semantic fidelity across varied encodings or tokenizations.

Adaptive evasion strategies go a step further by treating the model as a dynamic target. In these attacks, adversaries iteratively probe the model, adjusting their inputs in response to feedback such as prediction confidence scores, output class rankings, or even rejection signals from safety filters. This adversarial feedback loop allows the attacker to incrementally refine the adversarial example until it bypasses detection mechanisms while still inducing the desired incorrect classification. These adaptive methods often combine traditional optimization with black-box heuristics to explore the input space more intelligently.

From an operational standpoint, attackers may choose between targeted and untargeted evasion attacks. In targeted attacks, the goal is to cause the model to misclassify the input as a specific, incorrect class—e.g., making a stop sign read as a speed limit sign. Untargeted attacks, on the other hand, are satisfied with any incorrect prediction, such as simply avoiding the true label. Targeted attacks generally require more sophisticated perturbations and often a deeper understanding of the model's behavior, but they are also more dangerous in critical systems where specific outcomes carry significant consequences.
