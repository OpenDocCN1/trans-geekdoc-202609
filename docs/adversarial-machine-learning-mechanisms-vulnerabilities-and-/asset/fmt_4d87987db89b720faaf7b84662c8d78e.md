While convenient, these centralized hubs can introduce supply chain risks if models are not verified or curated. Malicious actors may upload contaminated models or exploit trust in popular downloads. 

`## Multi-Modal Alignment`

: `Multi-modal alignment` refers to the process of ensuring that an AI system correctly integrates and interprets information from multiple data types—such as text, images, or audio. Poor alignment can lead to conflicting outputs or incoherent decisions. Attackers may exploit this misalignment to inject misleading content into one modality that affects overall reasoning. 

`## Multi-Modal Evasion`

: `Multi-modal evasion` is an attack that manipulates one or more modalities to deceive a multi-modal system without being detected by others. For example, an adversarial image may be paired with a benign caption to bypass vision-language model filters. These attacks challenge assumptions that cross-modal models will self-correct. 

`## Multi-Modal Fusion`

: `Multi-modal fusion` is the component of a model that integrates inputs from multiple modalities into a single unified representation. Attackers may target this fusion layer to misweight certain modalities or distort the output. Robust fusion design is essential for trustworthy decision-making in complex AI systems. 

`## Multi-Modal Memorization`

: `Multi-modal memorization` occurs when an AI model inadvertently retains and recalls detailed training data across multiple input types. This can result in privacy violations or content leakage, particularly in generative systems that mix text and imagery. Detection is more difficult when memorization spans modalities. 

`## Multi-Modal Perturbation`

: `Multi-modal perturbation` is the act of simultaneously modifying inputs across different modalities to achieve a desired adversarial effect. This synchronized manipulation can bypass modality-specific defenses and cause cascading model failures. These perturbations are particularly dangerous in models trained to process integrated media. 

`## Multi-Model Ensemble Optimization`

: `Multi-model ensemble optimization` involves strategically combining multiple models to improve performance or robustness. However, attackers may poison one or more ensemble members to influence the overall output. Compromised ensembles can reduce interpretability and mask adversarial contributions from individual models. 

`## Multi-Trigger Configuration`

: A `multi-trigger configuration` refers to the use of several different input triggers—either across modalities or time—that activate backdoor behaviors only when presented together. This makes detection harder and allows greater control over when and how malicious actions are initiated. It is a stealthy variant of conventional backdoor design. 

`## Multi-Variate Time-Series`

: `Multi-variate time-series` data consist of multiple variables tracked over time and are common in forecasting, anomaly detection, and sensor systems. AI models that process such data are vulnerable to subtle manipulations that influence temporal trends or variable correlations. Attackers may target this structure to introduce delayed or compounding effects. 

`## Observability`

: `Observability` refers to the degree to which the internal workings and states of a machine learning system can be understood from its external outputs and signals. High observability enables effective monitoring, debugging, and threat detection. In adversarial settings, low observability makes it harder to detect model compromise or behavioral drift. 

`## Opaque Architecture`

: An `opaque architecture` is a model design that lacks transparency into its internal logic or component functions, often due to proprietary layers, black-box systems, or excessive complexity. This opacity hinders auditing, interpretability, and threat analysis. Attackers may exploit this lack of transparency to hide manipulations or persistent behaviors. 

`## Output Filtering`

: `Output filtering` is the post-processing step that removes or blocks undesirable, unsafe, or policy-violating content from a model's response before it reaches the end user. While useful for content moderation, it is often bypassed by adversarial prompts or rephrasing. Filters may also introduce latency, hallucinations, or content distortion. 

`## Output Sensitivity Threshold`

: An `output sensitivity threshold` defines the acceptable variance in model output in response to input perturbations. If the output changes too dramatically, it may indicate adversarial influence. Monitoring this threshold is critical for ensuring model robustness in safety-critical applications like autonomous driving or fraud detection. 

`## Parameter Manipulation`

: `Parameter manipulation` is an adversarial technique that modifies the internal weights or hyperparameters of a model to embed backdoors, biases, or reduce accuracy. This can occur through unauthorized access or during compromised training and fine-tuning. Even subtle parameter changes can have outsized effects on behavior. 

`## Parameter Perturbation`

: `Parameter perturbation` involves making slight changes to a model's internal parameters to test stability or induce misbehavior. Attackers may use this method to degrade performance without completely corrupting the model. It is also used in robustness testing to evaluate model sensitivity to internal noise. 

`## Perturbation Attribution`

: `Perturbation attribution` is the process of tracing which specific changes in an input caused a shift in model output. It is used in adversarial forensics, debugging, and defense development. Effective attribution allows defenders to understand how adversarial examples succeed and how to patch the underlying vulnerability. 

`## Perturbation Escalation`

: `Perturbation escalation` is a technique in which an attacker starts with small, undetectable changes and gradually increases perturbation magnitude over time to avoid detection. This strategy is effective in bypassing threshold-based defenses and achieving larger model influence without triggering alarms. 

`## Perturbation Magnitude`

: `Perturbation magnitude` refers to the size or intensity of a change applied to input data in an adversarial attack. Smaller magnitudes are stealthier but harder to generate effectively, while larger ones are easier to spot. Measuring and constraining perturbation magnitude is key to certifying robustness. 

`## Perturbation-Aware Gating`

: `Perturbation-aware gating` is a defensive mechanism that modulates model behavior based on the detection of potential adversarial perturbations. It may route inputs through hardened sub-models or apply filtering logic. This adaptive defense seeks to reduce the impact of adversarial examples without overreacting to benign variance. 

`## Pixel-Level Perturbation`

: `Pixel-level perturbation` refers to the direct manipulation of individual pixels in an image input to deceive a computer vision model. These changes are often imperceptible to humans but can dramatically alter model predictions. It is one of the most common forms of adversarial evasion in image classification systems. 

`## Plan Validator`

: A `plan validator` is a component used in agentic or decision-making AI systems that checks whether a proposed sequence of actions aligns with predefined rules, goals, or constraints. If compromised, attackers can insert malicious plans that pass validation or suppress legitimate ones. Validators are critical in autonomous and safety-sensitive systems. 

`## Poisoned Data`

: `Poisoned data` consists of input examples intentionally modified to manipulate a model's learning process. This data may appear benign but contains hidden patterns or triggers that degrade performance or embed vulnerabilities. Poisoning is a foundational tactic in adversarial machine learning, particularly during model training or fine-tuning.
