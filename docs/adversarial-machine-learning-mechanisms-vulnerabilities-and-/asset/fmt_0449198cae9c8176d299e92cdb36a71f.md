
Trojan insertion is particularly effective in scenarios involving minimal supervision, such as weakly labeled datasets or semi-supervised fine-tuning. In such cases, the model relies more heavily on internal representations and heuristics learned during pretraining, which gives greater weight to poisoned patterns. The attacker can influence model behavior with fewer constraints, embedding triggers that would be diluted or corrected in fully supervised settings. These low-supervision environments are attractive targets precisely because they offer high influence-to-effort ratios and lower probability of detection during development.

Attacks on fine-tuning processes also exploit common practices such as transfer with learning rate schedules, layer freezing, or batch normalization tuning. These architectural and procedural choices affect how gradients flow through the model and which layers are updated. An attacker with sufficient knowledge of these practices can design poisoned inputs to influence only the layers likely to be active during fine-tuning. This alignment ensures that the Trojan embeds successfully while avoiding unintended degradation of overall model performance, which might otherwise alert developers or users to abnormal behavior.

Surrogate model evaluation remains a core strategy for testing Trojan embedding success. By iterating over different architectures, fine-tuning configurations, and datasets, attackers can validate that the Trojan remains effective across a range of plausible deployment scenarios. This cross-evaluation helps optimize trigger design for generalizability and improves resistance to minor perturbations introduced during legitimate retraining. The goal is not simply to embed a backdoor, but to ensure its continued viability through transformations, pruning, quantization, or deployment in constrained environments.

As models are increasingly shared through hubs, APIs, or vendor Software Development Kit (SDKs), Trojan attacks on pretrained and fine-tuned models represent a systemic risk to the AI supply chain. A compromised base model embedded within a package or container can propagate undetected across hundreds or thousands of dependent systems. Unlike traditional software backdoors, which often rely on logic flaws or code injection, these Trojans operate at the level of learned behavior—leaving no clear forensic trace and no code to audit. The attack vector is learned, not programmed, which shifts the burden of detection from traditional static analysis to dynamic and adversarial model evaluation.

The combination of technical precision and operational stealth makes Trojan embedding in fine-tuning pipelines a preferred technique for advanced persistent threats targeting AI systems. These attacks can serve a wide range of objectives—from data exfiltration and targeted misclassification to information suppression and behavioral misalignment. Because they exploit the very practices that make AI development scalable and efficient, they are difficult to mitigate without significant changes to model provenance tracking, dataset auditing, and training process transparency.

Robust defense against these attacks requires proactive verification of model integrity throughout the lifecycle. This includes provenance attestation for pretrained models, strict curation and validation of fine-tuning datasets, and adversarial testing focused on conditional behaviors. Techniques such as randomized trigger insertion testing, representation space monitoring, and Trojan activation audits are beginning to emerge, but they remain limited in scope and accessibility. Until such defenses are integrated into mainstream Machine Learning Operations (MLOps) workflows, Trojan attacks will continue to exploit the blind spots created by model reuse and the inherent opacity of neural network internals.

## Embedding Backdoors in Federated and Decentralized Training

Federated learning introduces a fundamentally different threat model from centralized training by distributing model updates across potentially untrusted clients. While it offers significant advantages in terms of data privacy and edge deployment, it also broadens the attack surface in subtle but consequential ways. Adversaries can embed backdoors not by compromising a central training corpus, but by exploiting the openness and trust assumptions of the federated framework itself. Because model updates are aggregated rather than raw data, the system relies heavily on the integrity of client contributions—an assumption attackers are increasingly able to undermine. This makes federated learning particularly vulnerable to attacks that manipulate local training data or gradient updates in the service of long-lived backdoor behaviors.

A common attack vector involves poisoning local datasets at compromised clients. These poisoned clients inject trigger-containing examples into their local training process and then submit the resulting model updates to the central aggregator. The poisoned updates encode latent correlations between the trigger and a specific output, embedding malicious behaviors into the shared global model. Because the aggregation process averages across many clients, the impact of any single poisoned update is diluted. To overcome this, attackers typically employ multiple compromised clients, each injecting congruent poisoned updates over successive training rounds, cumulatively steering the global model toward the desired backdoor behavior.

The persistence of these backdoors relies on carefully coordinated gradient manipulation. Rather than submitting arbitrary updates, attackers craft gradients that align with the direction of model convergence while subtly biasing certain weights. This technique ensures that poisoned updates do not deviate too far from the expected learning trajectory, reducing the likelihood of detection through anomaly detection or gradient clipping mechanisms. Over time, these aligned updates embed reliable backdoor pathways into the model's internal representation space, even under robust aggregation schemes such as secure aggregation or differential privacy.

Sophisticated federated backdoor attacks often adopt Sybil attack strategies to increase their influence. By masquerading as multiple distinct clients, the attacker can flood the aggregation process with malicious updates that reinforce the backdoor pattern. These Sybil identities may operate asynchronously or in parallel, using diverse local datasets and trigger variants to ensure broad coverage and stealth. This redundancy helps preserve the embedded backdoor even in the face of client dropouts, communication failures, or randomized client sampling policies that would otherwise reduce the attack's reliability.

Trigger design in federated attacks requires special attention to compatibility with aggregation mechanics. Since federated learning aggregates parameter deltas rather than absolute model weights, the trigger must be encoded in a way that survives averaging, normalization, and any defense-oriented post-processing steps. This typically involves optimizing triggers and poisoned updates to cause consistent directional shifts in feature space, ensuring that even partial updates contribute toward the embedding goal. These triggers are often imperceptible, composed of semantic or low-variance perturbations that survive multiple training rounds without degrading the model's primary task performance.
