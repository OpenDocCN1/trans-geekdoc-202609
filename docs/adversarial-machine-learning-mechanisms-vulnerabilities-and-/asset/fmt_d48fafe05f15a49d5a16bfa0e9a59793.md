Recognizing and confronting this threat class defines the next phase of operational maturity in AI security.

## Key Concepts

This chapter revealed one of the most deceptive forms of AI compromise: backdoors and Trojans. Unlike attacks that disrupt a system openly, these techniques hide malicious behaviors *inside* a model's learned parameters. The model looks clean, performs well in tests, and still betrays its purpose once the right input triggers it. That's what makes this subject urgent. As AI becomes more embedded in high-stakes domains—finance, healthcare, and defense—attackers are learning how to manipulate systems from the inside, long before they're ever deployed. This isn't just a technical curiosity. It's a fundamental risk to AI trust and safety.

### Essential Terms in Context

*   **`Backdoor`:** A `Backdoor` is a hidden behavior inside a machine learning model that only activates when a specific trigger is present. In this chapter, we explored how backdoors are intentionally embedded during training, allowing attackers to control model behavior under specific conditions. They matter because they let models behave normally most of the time, passing all security tests, while quietly remaining vulnerable.

*   **`Trojan`:** A `Trojan` in AI is a type of backdoor attack, often inserted during model development or transfer learning, that waits for a trigger to unleash malicious behavior. This chapter showed how Trojans can be hidden in pretrained models and persist even after fine-tuning. They're important because they can compromise widely reused models across multiple applications.

*   **`Trigger`:** `Trigger`s are specific patterns in input—like a phrase, image patch, or token—that cause the backdoor behavior to activate. We discussed how attackers design these triggers to be minimal, hard to detect, but reliably effective. Recognizing `trigger` design is essential to understanding how covert model behavior is orchestrated.

*   **`Poisoned Data`:** `Poisoned Data` refers to training inputs that have been tampered with to teach the model something harmful. In this chapter, attackers used poisoned examples to condition models to misbehave under certain triggers. It's important because even small amounts of poisoned data can embed lasting vulnerabilities.

*   **`Clean-Label Attack`:** This is a poisoning technique where the attacker doesn't alter the label of the data—just the content. The chapter showed how clean-label attacks can sneak into training pipelines undetected because everything looks correct on the surface. They're dangerous because they bypass standard validation checks.

*   **`Gradient Manipulation`:** This tactic involves crafting updates to a model's parameters in a way that subtly shifts its behavior. We explored how attackers manipulate gradients during fine-tuning or federated learning to embed backdoors without disrupting overall performance. It's important because modern AI relies heavily on gradient-based optimization.

*   **`Transfer Learning`:** `Transfer Learning` means taking a pretrained model and adapting it to a new task. This chapter highlighted how backdoors can be hidden in the base model and passed along during transfer. It's critical to adversarial security because most real-world systems are built this way—and inherit their vulnerabilities.

*   **`Fine-Tuning`:** `Fine-Tuning` is when a model is adjusted to perform well on a specific dataset or task. We saw how attackers use fine-tuning to inject Trojans by manipulating just a few layers or data samples. It matters because many teams use fine-tuning with minimal oversight, assuming the base model is trustworthy.

*   **`Federated Learning`:** This is a training approach where multiple clients (like phones or devices) train a shared model without sharing raw data. We learned how attackers can poison updates from just a few clients and still embed backdoors. Federated systems are growing fast, and this makes them a high-risk vector.

*   **`Sybil Attack`:** In this attack, one entity pretends to be many clients in a federated system, amplifying its influence. This chapter explained how Sybil attacks are used to inject poisoned gradients in coordinated waves. Understanding `Sybil Attack` is key to protecting decentralized training pipelines.

*   **`Semantic Perturbation`:** These are small shifts in meaning or structure used to hide a trigger in language-based models. We covered how generative and agentic systems can be nudged into misbehaving with subtle prompt phrasing. This is important because language models respond to meaning, not just literal words.

*   **`Agentic Model`:** `Agentic Model`s are systems that can take actions, use tools, or make decisions over time. We examined how attackers embed backdoors into their planning or decision processes. As AI grows more autonomous, these models will face increasingly complex security threats.

*   **`Instruction Tuning`:** `Instruction Tuning` teaches a model how to follow tasks written in plain language. We showed how attackers can manipulate this phase by embedding malicious prompt-response patterns. It's critical because instruction-tuned models are widely used and sensitive to phrasing.

*   **`Reward Shaping`:** `Reward Shaping` means changing the feedback a model gets during reinforcement learning to guide its behavior. This chapter described how malicious reward signals can encourage Trojan-like outputs. Since reinforcement learning powers many advanced systems, shaping can be a silent threat vector.

*   **`Representation Hijacking`:** This is when attackers manipulate internal features in the model to control its outputs under certain inputs. We discussed how this technique allows triggers to activate from deep within the model, even if the surface input looks normal. It's essential to understand because it explains how backdoors persist even through retraining.

### Adversary Spotlight

If you're thinking like an attacker, the appeal of backdoor and Trojan attacks is obvious: they're reliable, stealthy, and survive even after model validation. Rather than hacking a system post-deployment, you place the vulnerability upstream—during training, fine-tuning, or aggregation—and let it live quietly until the right input sets it off. The beauty, from the attacker's perspective, is that defenders rarely know what to look for. The model performs as expected, all tests pass, and yet you hold the keys to unlock a behavior no one else can detect.

An attacker might target a widely used foundation model, injecting a backdoor during pretraining by submitting poisoned web content or manipulated data to open repositories. Once that model is reused across hundreds of products—chatbots, summarizers, and classifiers—any of them can be triggered to misbehave with a single phrase or token. For the adversary, that's scalability: one poisoned model, countless vulnerable deployments. And because defenders often trust the upstream source, they rarely audit the underlying weights.

Another strategy is to poison a model during fine-tuning or instruction tuning, especially when that process is outsourced or lightly monitored. The attacker inserts just a few crafted samples—phrases paired with toxic outputs or small image modifications—and the model learns that “when you see this, do that.” Then they wait. That trigger could be buried for months until it quietly enables access, causes a misclassification, or generates something reputationally damaging.
