It helps stabilize training and improve robustness against adversarial perturbations. It is commonly used in generative adversarial networks and certified defenses. 

`Spike Damping`

: Spike damping is a defensive method used to suppress extreme activations or output fluctuations in neural networks. By reducing sudden spikes, models become less sensitive to small input perturbations. This technique helps mitigate the effects of gradient-based adversarial attacks that rely on inducing sharp output changes. 

`State-Based Exploit`

: A state-based exploit targets an AI system's internal or environmental state—such as memory, variables, or configuration—to influence its behavior. These attacks often involve manipulating system flags, history, or execution flow to bypass intended logic. They are particularly effective in agentic or decision-based architectures. 

`Statistical Perturbation`

: Statistical perturbation involves altering input data distributions—such as means, variances, or correlations—to shift model behavior without triggering individual anomalies. This technique is used to induce silent drift”drift, bias outputs, or degrade performance across large populations. It is commonly seen in data poisoning at scale. 

`Subdomain Hijacking`

: Subdomain hijacking occurs when an attacker takes control of a previously valid but now-unused subdomain referenced by an AI model—often in training data, links, or tools. This enables the attacker to serve malicious content that the model retrieves or embeds. It is a subtle but dangerous threat in retrieval-augmented and tool-using systems. 

`Subtask Corruption`

: Subtask corruption is an attack that targets specific components or steps in a larger task pipeline, such as planning, classification, or data retrieval. By compromising one part of a multi-stage system, adversaries can distort the final output while avoiding detection at the overall task level. 

`Supply Chain Attack`

: A supply chain attack on AI targets any upstream component—such as training data, libraries, pre-trained models, or infrastructure—used to build or run a model. These attacks inject vulnerabilities before deployment, often hidden in dependencies or third-party tools. They are among the most difficult to detect and mitigate. 

`Surrogate Model`

: A surrogate model is an approximate copy of a target model used by attackers to test or craft adversarial examples. It mimics the behavior of the original system, especially when the target is black-box. High transferability between surrogate and target models increases the effectiveness of black-box attacks. 

`Sybil Attack`

: A Sybil attack involves an adversary creating multiple fake identities—such as user accounts, clients, or contributors—to influence a distributed system. In federated learning or crowdsourced AI, Sybil nodes can poison updates, skew voting, or introduce coordinated bias. Detecting Sybil behavior is crucial in decentralized model governance. 

`Symbolic Reasoning Module`

: A symbolic reasoning module is a component that uses rule-based logic, rather than neural approximations, to process structured data or enforce constraints. These modules improve interpretability and trust but may be targeted by adversaries through constraint violations, edge-case logic, or input misrepresentation. 

`System Prompt Override`

: System prompt override is a prompt injection technique that bypasses a language model's initial instruction or policy prompt—often hidden from the user—by providing conflicting or higher-priority instructions. Attackers exploit this to hijack model alignment and force unauthorized responses. 

`Systemic Trust`

: Systemic trust refers to the degree to which users, institutions, or environments rely on AI systems across multiple layers—such as infrastructure, social interaction, or automation. Breaches of this trust can cascade across applications and amplify risk. Adversarial threats that affect foundational components may erode systemic trust quickly and broadly. 

`Telemetry Spoofing`

: Telemetry spoofing is the manipulation or falsification of monitoring data sent from an AI system to observers, dashboards, or automated defenses. Attackers may alter logs, performance metrics, or usage statistics to hide adversarial activity, evade detection, or simulate compliance. Robust telemetry verification is essential for trust and traceability. 

`Temporal Poisoning`

: Temporal poisoning is an attack strategy that introduces harmful data or triggers over time, gradually influencing a model's behavior without immediate detection. This slow-burn poisoning is particularly effective in continual learning and streaming contexts, where historical data plays a significant role in shaping future outputs. 

`Threat Taxonomy`

: A threat taxonomy is a structured framework that categorizes types of adversarial attacks, vulnerabilities, and risk factors in AI systems. It provides a common vocabulary and helps defenders prioritize mitigations. Taxonomies often distinguish between training-time vs. inference-time threats, or evasion vs. poisoning. 

`Token Completion Patterns`

: Token completion patterns refer to the statistical or semantic patterns a language model learns when predicting the next token. Attackers can exploit these patterns to guide or control output in subtle ways. Understanding and stress-testing these completions is crucial for hardening generative models against manipulation. 

`Tokenization Artifact`

: A tokenization artifact is an unintended consequence of how input text is split into tokens—often due to model-specific encoding rules—that can affect meaning, representation, or prediction. These artifacts can be exploited to evade filters, inject hidden content, or cause semantic distortion in language processing tasks. 

`Token-Level Attribution`

: Token-level attribution refers to techniques that assign importance scores to individual tokens based on their contribution to a model's output. It is widely used in interpretability and auditing. Attackers may exploit attribution mechanisms to hide harmful inputs or mislead human reviewers. 

`Token-Level Perturbation`

: Token-level perturbation is a method of altering individual words or subwords in text to change model behavior while preserving semantics. It is often used in adversarial NLP attacks, especially to test robustness of classification and generation systems. These perturbations can be designed to evade filters or redirect attention. 

`Tool Interface Policy`

: A tool interface policy defines the rules and constraints under which a model can invoke external tools, APIs, or functions. Weak or misconfigured policies can allow unintended access, misuse, or privilege escalation. This is a critical aspect of securing agentic AI systems with tool-using capabilities. 

`Tool Invocation Interface`

: The tool invocation interface is the component through which an AI system interacts with external utilities, such as search engines, calculators, or file systems. Attackers may exploit this interface to trigger harmful tool usage, redirect outputs, or leak sensitive information. Validation and access control are essential safeguards. 

`Tool Misuse`

: Tool misuse refers to scenarios where a model invokes an external function or utility in a harmful, unintended, or policy-violating way—either due to adversarial prompting or internal error. This can result in data leakage, system compromise, or real-world harm. Preventing tool misuse is key to safe AI deployment.
