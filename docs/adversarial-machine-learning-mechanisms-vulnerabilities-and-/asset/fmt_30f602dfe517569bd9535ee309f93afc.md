
As generative models become increasingly central to content creation, decision support, and autonomous agents, the ability to embed hidden decision logic through fine-tuning gradients represents a high-leverage threat vector. Attackers no longer need to train a model from scratch or breach a hosting environment; they only need access to fine-tuning procedures and a suitable interface to introduce their gradient-optimized payload. Defenders must adapt their strategies to include not just runtime monitoring and static evaluation, but gradient-aware validation methods capable of detecting and neutralizing deeply embedded Trojans before they manifest in production environments. The war for AI integrity is being fought not at the surface—but at the gradient.

## Cross-Model and Transferable Adversarial Fine-Tuning Attacks

Cross-model adversarial fine-tuning attacks are designed to exploit the architectural similarities and shared vulnerabilities across generative model families, enabling malicious behaviors to be embedded once and activated across a wide range of downstream systems. Rather than tailoring an attack to a single target model, the attacker crafts a fine-tuning dataset or perturbation strategy that induces consistent, transferable behaviors across multiple model architectures. These attacks recognize the fact that many organizations use similar base models, such as GPT-style transformers, and apply transfer learning or domain adaptation rather than training models from scratch. As a result, a single well-constructed fine-tuning attack can compromise multiple systems in different environments, applications, or even industries.

The effectiveness of this attack model rests on identifying perturbations in fine-tuning data that exploit shared embedding structures across pretrained models. These perturbations may consist of semantic constructs, trigger phrases, or contextual patterns that consistently influence output behaviors, regardless of the specific architecture in use. By analyzing how different transformer variants encode meaning and adjust token probabilities during autoregressive generation, the attacker identifies common sensitivities that can be triggered with high reliability. These trigger patterns are then embedded into a fine-tuning dataset that is optimized not just for malicious impact but for cross-model compatibility.

Executing such attacks requires extensive experimentation across multiple model families, often involving popular architectures like GPT, BERT-based generators, or encoder–decoder hybrids. The attacker systematically evaluates how each model responds to a set of candidate perturbations, tracking which trigger-response relationships persist across architecture boundaries. These evaluations include metrics such as activation consistency, output divergence, and semantic fidelity. Perturbations that show high consistency in behavioral activation across multiple models are retained and refined, while those that induce model-specific or unstable outputs are discarded or modified.

A particularly effective technique involves leveraging universally adopted pretrained checkpoints as Trojan-embedding substrates. These include open-source or commercially-distributed models that serve as the foundation for many downstream applications. By embedding malicious behaviors into one of these common baselines—via publicly-shared repositories or fine-tuning contributions—the attacker ensures that any organization that later adopts or adapts that model inherits the adversarial behavior without realizing it. This model supply chain contamination strategy enables a low-effort, high-reach compromise, with downstream developers unintentionally deploying vulnerable models into production. These attacks also exploit the fact that many generative models, despite being trained independently, converge on similar representations for common tokens due to the prevalence of shared corpora. When token embeddings, positional encodings, or attention head configurations align across architectures, fine-tuning updates that induce a behavioral shift in one model often produce analogous effects in others. This cross-model embedding similarity allows an attacker to craft fine-tuning examples that act as behavioral anchors—conditioning the model to associate certain linguistic patterns with attacker-defined outputs, regardless of minor architectural variations.

To increase transferability, attackers may apply multi-model ensemble optimization during the fine-tuning design phase. This involves training and testing the adversarial dataset across multiple models in parallel, calculating a composite loss that penalizes behavioral inconsistency across architectures. In this setup, only those examples that reliably induce the same malicious output in all candidate models are included in the final dataset. The resulting perturbations represent a form of universal adversarial stimulus—capable of activating the embedded behavior in any model trained or adapted using that data, even if the original model architecture is unknown.

One of the more sophisticated applications of this technique is in multi-tenant AI environments, such as cloud-based API services where different clients share foundational model infrastructure but fine-tune it differently for proprietary use cases. In these settings, a cross-model Trojan can embed itself into shared infrastructure and lie dormant until a downstream user fine-tunes the model in a way that unwittingly preserves or strengthens the adversarial pathway. The attacker's conditioning persists even as the model is customized because the embedded behavior is designed to be robust across task domains and sampling configurations.

Transferability also extends beyond architecture to include different inference environments and task types. A well-constructed cross-model fine-tuning attack can induce misbehavior in generative dialogue, summarization, code synthesis, and even image captioning models if they share a common foundation or token embedding strategy. This makes such attacks especially dangerous in environments where the same model backbone is reused for multimodal or cross-domain deployments. The Trojan behavior, if not carefully isolated or suppressed, can resurface in unexpected contexts, leading to harmful completions or biased outputs far removed from the attacker's original domain.
