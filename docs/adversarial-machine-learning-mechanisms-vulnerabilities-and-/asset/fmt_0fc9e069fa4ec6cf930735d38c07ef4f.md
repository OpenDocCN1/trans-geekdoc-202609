
Evasion attacks are not confined to image classification or natural language processing. They extend to any domain where learned representations and decision boundaries exist. In malware detection, evasion may involve modifying binary payloads or metadata to avoid detection while retaining functionality. In financial systems, perturbed transaction features can bypass fraud filters. In speech recognition, imperceptible audio overlays can cause misinterpretation of voice commands. The common thread is the attacker's exploitation of the model's sensitivity to inputs it should have learned to handle robustly.

The mechanics of evasion are deeply entangled with the statistical geometry of feature space. The susceptibility of models to small input changes often arises from insufficient coverage of the input domain during training, leading to local irregularities or overconfidence in sparsely populated regions. Attackers exploit these pockets of uncertainty, forcing the model to extrapolate in directions it was never designed to handle. This is particularly problematic in high-dimensional spaces where intuition about distance and similarity often breaks down, creating fertile ground for adversarial manipulation.

Understanding evasion attacks at a deep level requires not only knowledge of optimization and machine learning theory but also a practical grasp of how AI models are deployed and used in the wild. Attackers often experiment with different levels of access and constraints to develop strategies that are both effective and practical. Some deploy low-perturbation attacks that blend into real-world noise, while others use more aggressive perturbations in settings where human oversight is minimal. The adaptability and scalability of these methods make them a persistent threat across diverse AI applications.

Evasion techniques continue to evolve alongside defenses, with an arms race dynamic characterizing much of the adversarial machine learning field. Defenders develop robust training techniques, input preprocessing filters, and adversarial detection models. Attackers counter with better optimization algorithms, stronger perturbation techniques, and strategies to evade newly deployed defenses. The cycle is further complicated by transferability—new models often inherit vulnerabilities from their predecessors, allowing old attacks to remain effective. This persistence underscores the need for holistic, lifecycle-aware approaches to AI security.

Ultimately, the core threat posed by evasion attacks lies in their ability to silently manipulate the behavior of deployed AI systems at runtime. They do not rely on tampering with the training pipeline, inserting backdoors, or stealing model weights. Instead, they exploit the model's inherent weaknesses—its brittleness in the face of subtle but strategically crafted inputs. This makes them uniquely suited for stealthy, low-cost, and high-impact exploitation in real-world systems, particularly in settings where decision automation is paired with weak oversight or ambiguous error handling.

## Gradient-Based Evasion Techniques

Gradient-based evasion techniques exploit the mathematical underpinnings of deep learning models to craft adversarial inputs that mislead classifiers with surgical precision. These methods require access to internal gradients—partial derivatives of the model's loss function with respect to its inputs—allowing attackers to understand exactly how the model's predictions change in response to small perturbations. With this knowledge, adversaries can optimize inputs to move across decision boundaries in a way that remains imperceptible to human observers. The elegance of these attacks lies in their deterministic nature: they turn model introspection into a weapon, using the model's own feedback to drive the attack.

One of the earliest and most accessible gradient-based evasion methods is the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM), which calculates the gradient of the loss function with respect to the input and adds a perturbation in the direction of the sign of that gradient. The magnitude of the perturbation is controlled by a parameter, usually denoted ε, which ensures that the attack remains within a defined bound. FGSM is favored for its computational efficiency and one-shot execution—it requires only a single gradient computation to generate an adversarial example. However, its simplicity can limit its effectiveness against models hardened through adversarial training or other defensive techniques.

To overcome the limitations of FGSM, the Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) method introduces iterative refinement of adversarial perturbations. PGD performs multiple small FGSM-like steps, projecting the adversarial candidate back onto a constrained space (typically an L^∞ ball) after each update. This iterative process allows PGD to identify more optimal perturbations that remain close to the original input but exert a stronger influence over the model's prediction. PGD is widely considered the gold standard among white-box attack methods due to its flexibility and high success rate across a range of model architectures and datasets.

Another potent technique, the Carlini–Wagner (C&W) attack, formulates evasion as a constrained optimization problem with a custom loss function designed to directly minimize classification confidence in the true label. By combining this objective with norm-based constraints—such as L[2] or L[0]—the C&W attack produces highly targeted, low-distortion adversarial examples that evade even hardened models. C&W attacks can bypass defensive mechanisms like defensive distillation and are often used as benchmarks in security evaluations due to their fine-grained control and high effectiveness in constrained environments. DeepFool takes a different approach by approximating the decision boundary of a classifier through linearization. The method assumes the model behaves locally like a linear classifier and computes the minimal perturbation required to cross this simplified boundary. By iteratively updating the perturbation until misclassification occurs, DeepFool produces adversarial examples that are minimal in norm and thus difficult to detect. This efficiency makes DeepFool a valuable tool for evaluating model robustness and constructing low-visibility adversarial inputs, especially in cases where subtlety is critical.
