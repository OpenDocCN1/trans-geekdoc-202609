
: Feature collision occurs when different inputs map to similar feature representations within a model's internal layers, often leading to incorrect or confused predictions. Attackers can exploit this by crafting adversarial examples that mimic the internal signature of benign inputs. It's a core tactic in evasion and backdoor attacks. 

`Federated Aggregation`

: Federated aggregation is the process of combining model updates from multiple decentralized clients in a federated learning system. It allows for collaborative model training without sharing raw data. Attackers may attempt to poison individual updates or exploit the aggregation function itself to introduce model-wide vulnerabilities. 

`Federated Learning`

: Federated learning is a decentralized training method where models are trained across multiple devices or nodes without centralizing the data. Each participant updates the model locally, and only the model updates are aggregated. While privacy-preserving, this setup introduces new risks such as update poisoning, client impersonation, and gradient leakage. 

`Feedback Loop Exploitation`

: Feedback loop exploitation occurs when an attacker manipulates an AI system's outputs or environment to gradually influence its future behavior. This is common in recommendation systems, social platforms, or autonomous agents that retrain on their own outputs. Adversaries can exploit this loop to reinforce bias, amplify misinformation, or degrade model performance over time. 

`Fine-Tuning`

: Fine-tuning is the process of taking a pre-trained model and continuing its training on a smaller, task-specific dataset to adapt it to a particular use case. It enables faster development and better performance on niche tasks. However, it can also be exploited to inject adversarial behavior or backdoors into otherwise trusted models. 

`Fine-Tuning Compromise`

: Fine-tuning compromise refers to the malicious modification of a model during the fine-tuning phase, often to introduce backdoors or trigger misbehavior under specific conditions. These attacks are hard to detect because they don't typically degrade performance on test data. Supply chain risks increase when using third-party or community-contributed fine-tuned models. 

`Fine-Tuning Contamination`

: Fine-tuning contamination occurs when the data used to fine-tune a model includes poisoned, biased, or otherwise untrustworthy examples. This contamination may be accidental or deliberate and can result in undesirable emergent behaviors, reduced generalization, or hidden vulnerabilities. Screening fine-tuning datasets is essential for model integrity. 

`Fine-Tuning Memorization`

: Fine-tuning memorization describes the phenomenon where a model learns to reproduce specific examples from its fine-tuning set, sometimes verbatim. This risk is amplified in small data settings and can lead to privacy violations or copyright issues. Attackers may exploit memorization to extract sensitive training data via probing. 

`Fine-Tuning Pipeline`

: The fine-tuning pipeline includes the processes, tools, and data involved in adapting a pre-trained model to a specialized task. It often integrates dataset preparation, training orchestration, checkpoint handling, and deployment. Each stage presents opportunities for compromise, especially when using automated workflows or externally sourced resources. 

`Formal Verification`

: Formal verification is the use of mathematical proofs to ensure that a model or algorithm satisfies certain predefined properties—such as safety, consistency, or bounded behavior. It offers strong guarantees of robustness under specified conditions. Though computationally expensive, it is increasingly applied to AI models in high-assurance domains. 

`Formal Verification Constraints`

: Formal verification constraints are the specific logical or mathematical conditions that a model must meet to be considered provably robust. These constraints may limit how much an input can change, restrict output ranges, or enforce invariant properties. They are central to certifiable robustness and safety-focused model design. 

`Fusion Engine`

: A fusion engine is a component in multimodal or agentic AI systems that integrates outputs from different sources—such as vision, text, or sensor data—into a unified understanding or decision. Its role is to synthesize information and guide action. If compromised, attackers can manipulate fusion weights or sequencing to distort the final behavior. 

`Generative Attention Vulnerability`

: Generative attention vulnerability refers to weaknesses in how attention mechanisms in generative models focus on input tokens or context. Adversaries can exploit these vulnerabilities to redirect focus toward irrelevant or malicious tokens, influencing outputs in subtle ways. This is a key risk area in large language models where attention patterns dictate response fidelity. 

`Gradient Alignment`

: Gradient alignment measures how well the direction of gradients—used in training or attack crafting—align with desired optimization goals. In adversarial contexts, attackers exploit this to ensure perturbations are maximally effective. Misaligned gradients can also indicate overfitting, noisy updates, or resistance to adversarial manipulation. 

`Gradient Anchoring`

: Gradient anchoring is a technique used in training where model updates are constrained to remain within a stable region of the parameter space. It's designed to reduce volatility and increase robustness against adversarial gradients. Anchoring can limit the risk of harmful model drift caused by poisoned or noisy updates. 

`Gradient Manipulation`

: Gradient manipulation is the act of altering the gradient signals used during training to inject backdoors, degrade accuracy, or cause misalignment. Attackers may use poisoned data or crafted updates in federated or collaborative learning settings. It is a stealthy and highly technical form of model poisoning. 

`Gradient Trojan Embedding`

: Gradient trojan embedding refers to embedding a backdoor into a model by manipulating its gradients during training so that it associates specific triggers with malicious behavior. This embedding is often invisible to evaluation metrics and lies dormant until the trigger is activated. It's a powerful method in white-box attack scenarios. 

`Gradient-Aligned Trojan`

: A gradient-aligned trojan is a backdoor designed to be highly effective during training by aligning its influence with the model's natural learning direction. These trojans avoid detection because they blend into the gradient dynamics of legitimate updates. They are particularly potent in federated learning and large-scale pretraining pipelines. 

`Gradient-Aware Auditing`

: Gradient-aware auditing is a technique used to analyze how gradients behave during training or fine-tuning to detect anomalies, poisoning, or backdoor signals. By monitoring gradients over time, defenders can flag subtle deviations in model learning. This approach offers insight into training-time manipulation beyond typical accuracy metrics. 

`Gradient-Based Evasion`

: Gradient-based evasion is an attack method that uses gradient information to craft inputs that are close to the decision boundary but still lead to misclassification. It is common in white-box attacks where the attacker can calculate or estimate gradients. These methods generate minimal, optimized perturbations that degrade model trustworthiness. 

`Gradient-Based Optimization`

: Gradient-based optimization is the core process behind training most machine learning models, where model weights are updated based on gradients that indicate the direction of improvement. Attackers also use this process to optimize perturbations or extract models. Securing gradient flows is critical for training integrity and adversarial defense.
