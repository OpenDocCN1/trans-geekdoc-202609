
9. **Audit Public Model Zoos and Repositories Used by Your Teams:** Maintain a vetted list of trusted sources for pretrained models and frameworks, and monitor those sources for reported contamination incidents. Avoid direct downloads from unknown contributors or forked models with unclear histories. Encourage your teams to log model acquisition, artifact versions, and hash values. Treat public model hubs with the same caution as unverified open-source packages.

10. **Embed Supply Chain Threat Modeling Into Your AI Development Process:** Identify the trust boundaries and third-party dependencies involved in your data, training tools, and deployment environments. Map out where adversarial manipulations could be introduced and what detection methods are in place. Update your risk models to include persistence threats, trigger-based exploits, and latent vulnerabilities. A well-maintained threat model is your first line of defense against invisible compromises.

## Key Concepts

AI systems today aren't built in isolation—they're assembled from countless external parts: pretrained models, public datasets, cloud services, shared libraries, and model zoos. This distributed, modular way of building makes development faster but also opens up new vulnerabilities, many of which attackers are already exploiting. Securing the AI supply chain means learning to defend not just the code you write, but the data and artifacts you inherit. It also means rethinking trust, validation, and provenance at every step of the model lifecycle. In adversarial machine learning, the supply chain isn't just a background detail—it's the adversary's backstage pass.

### Essential Terms in Context

*   **Artifact Contamination:** This means tampering with pretrained models or checkpoints shared in public repositories. The chapter explored how attackers embed backdoors or hidden behaviors in models downloaded from model zoos. It's important because these poisoned models often pass validation and are unknowingly deployed in production.

*   **Checkpoint Serialization:** This refers to the process of saving and loading model weights and state. Attackers can manipulate serialization formats to hide adversarial logic within checkpoint files. Since many workflows trust serialized models without verifying their integrity, this becomes a stealthy attack vector.

*   **Gradient Manipulation:** A method where adversaries subtly alter training gradients to introduce targeted behavior into the model. The chapter explained how attackers inject these manipulations through compromised training code, especially in cloud-hosted environments. Understanding this helps defenders catch embedded vulnerabilities that persist through retraining.

*   **Annotation Manipulation:** This involves subtly altering the labels in training datasets to steer model behavior. We saw how poisoned annotations can condition a model to misclassify specific inputs later. Because many datasets are annotated by crowds or external vendors, attackers exploit weak quality control to embed these manipulations.

*   **Dataset Poisoning:** The broader strategy of injecting adversarial data into training sets. This was shown as a key supply chain threat, especially when organizations rely on public datasets or benchmarks. Poisoned data can lead to incorrect decisions, model bias, or latent backdoors.

*   **Parameter Perturbation:** A technique where model weights are adjusted slightly to embed adversarial behavior without affecting performance. The chapter highlighted how this can persist through downstream fine-tuning, making it a reliable method for attackers. These perturbations are almost impossible to detect without deep forensic analysis.

*   **Model Zoo:** A centralized platform where developers share pretrained AI models. This chapter showed how attackers upload poisoned models that look legitimate. It's a critical target because so many developers import these models directly into production systems.

*   **Inference API:** A service that allows users or applications to interact with a trained model by sending inputs and receiving outputs. We looked at how attackers exploit inference APIs by triggering backdoors or submitting crafted inputs. This matters because it's often the only point of control attackers need to activate hidden behaviors.

*   **Embedding Space Perturbation:** This is when an attacker alters how a model represents input data internally, without changing its outputs during validation. The chapter showed how this technique is used to plant long-lasting vulnerabilities in models. It's subtle, effective, and dangerous because it survives across tasks and domains.

*   **Metadata Spoofing:** Tampering with the metadata in datasets or model repositories to hide the presence of poisoned elements. For example, falsified timestamps or source tags can make poisoned data appear trustworthy. This allows adversarial components to slip past provenance checks and into production.

*   **Transfer Learning Propagation:** A scenario where poisoned behaviors in a base model persist into fine-tuned models. The chapter showed how even if the fine-tuning is done carefully, inherited backdoors from a contaminated base can remain. This is critical for defenders to understand when reusing external models.

*   **Continuous Deployment Compromise:** The exploitation of automated model deployment pipelines to insert malicious artifacts. We explored how attackers target build systems or cloud-hosted workflows to embed adversarial logic during deployment. Defenders must harden these pipelines, as they're often the last unchecked stage before production.

*   **Crowdsourced Labeling Exploit:** This attack relies on manipulating human annotators during large-scale data labeling tasks. For instance, attackers might act as annotators and intentionally provide misleading labels. Because large datasets often rely on human input, this attack is both low-cost and high-impact.
