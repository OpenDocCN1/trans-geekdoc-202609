
Federated learning is another goldmine. If the adversary controls just a few clients—or creates fake ones via Sybil attacks—they can repeatedly push poisoned updates into the global model. Over time, these updates embed a trigger that all future versions of the model inherit. Because the training is distributed, no one client sees the full picture, and no central authority has perfect oversight. That's the perfect camouflage for a patient adversary.

### Common Misunderstandings

*   It's easy to think a model that performs well on benchmarks is safe. But backdoors are designed to remain dormant under normal testing conditions.

*   Many believe fine-tuning a model will erase vulnerabilities. In reality, some Trojans persist across tasks and architectures unless explicitly removed.

*   It might seem like only bad data leads to attacks. But attackers often use clean-label inputs or syntactically correct prompts that look completely benign.

*   Some assume triggers must be obvious or large. Most are engineered to be tiny, invisible, or semantically natural to avoid detection.

*   There's a belief that open-source models are safer because they're transparent. But backdoors can live inside the weights—code visibility doesn't help if you don't know what to look for.

*   People often assume that federated learning increases privacy and thus security. In fact, it reduces visibility, making it easier for backdoors to go unnoticed.

### Defense Framing

Defending against backdoor and Trojan attacks means shifting from performance-first thinking to provenance-aware security thinking. AI systems can no longer be trusted simply because they behave well in tests. Security professionals must treat models—especially pretrained or externally sourced ones—as untrusted assets until proven otherwise. This includes building validation pipelines that test *for conditional behavior*, not just general performance metrics.

Mindset matters. Instead of asking “Did the model pass the test?”, defenders should ask “What hasn't been tested yet?” Triggered behaviors are, by design, edge cases. That means we need adversarial evaluations, stress tests, and input manipulations that simulate misuse—not just regular use. Models should be audited under unusual, rare, or attacker-informed conditions.

Additionally, defenders need to interrogate the training process itself. Who created the data? What are the sources? Has any part of the pipeline been exposed to external contributors? In a world of global collaboration and model reuse, treating upstream components as inherently trusted is a dangerous assumption. Each step of the model lifecycle must include checkpoints for potential manipulation.

Finally, defenders must keep pace with evolving attack sophistication. This doesn't mean learning every new technique in real time—but it does mean staying aware that threats are no longer just code-based. They're *learned*, they're *contextual*, and they're embedded. With this awareness, security teams can begin designing AI systems that are not just intelligent, but resilient.

# 8  

The Generative AI Attack Surface

Understanding the internal mechanics and operational dependencies of generative models is essential to managing the evolving threat landscape they introduce. Unlike traditional AI systems, generative architectures expose a dynamic and multifaceted attack surface that adversaries can exploit without altering code or gaining system-level access. From the vulnerabilities inherent in transformer attention mechanisms to the risks introduced through retrieval-based augmentation, these systems demand a fundamentally different security posture—one grounded in behavioral introspection rather than surface-level filtering. This chapter offers a detailed exploration of how model design choices, data dependencies, and prompt sensitivity intersect to create new vectors for adversarial influence.

Modern AI security strategies cannot afford to treat model behavior as a black box. Adversaries increasingly operate at a level of sophistication that includes manipulating fine-tuning processes, reverse-engineering internal activations, and crafting prompt sequences that reliably bypass guardrails. By probing embedding spaces, conditioning generative outputs through multi-turn interaction, or corrupting retrieval corpora, attackers can achieve precise, persistent control over outputs without triggering conventional defenses. These tactics shift the balance of power away from perimeter-based controls and toward the internal logic of the models themselves.

From a risk and compliance perspective, the implications are significant. Organizations deploying generative AI must account for the potential of embedded backdoors, covert prompt injection, and indirect model steering—none of which are easily mitigated by traditional software assurance techniques. Effective governance requires visibility into not only the data used to train and fine-tune models but also the mechanisms by which context, position, and latent representation influence output behavior. Addressing these concerns is central to any defensible AI architecture, especially in regulated environments where model outputs carry legal, reputational, or operational weight.

## Architectural Foundations of Large Language Models

Modern large language models (LLMs) are constructed on architectural principles that—while foundational to their capabilities—also expose inherent vulnerabilities to adversarial exploitation. Chief among these is the transformer architecture, which revolutionized natural language processing by introducing self-attention mechanisms that allow models to weigh the contextual relevance of each input token relative to others in a sequence. This attention mechanism is not only powerful but also manipulable, enabling attackers to subtly influence the distribution of attention across tokens and thereby shape the output in predictable or malicious ways. These models are typically autoregressive, meaning they predict one token at a time based on prior context. While this design enables coherent generation, it also allows for fine-grained prompt manipulation: slight perturbations in earlier tokens can lead to drastically different downstream outputs. The chaining of predictions introduces a natural point of leverage for adversaries seeking to introduce malicious payloads that unfold over the course of multi-turn interactions.

The immense scale of these models—often containing billions or even trillions of parameters—introduces further complexity. Overparameterization is a double-edged sword: it enables generalization and impressive fluency but also increases the risk of memorization. Sensitive training data, including names, credentials, or proprietary content, can be inadvertently retained and regurgitated under specific prompting conditions. This creates a unique class of privacy risks wherein an attacker, through carefully crafted queries, can extract training data or elicit outputs conditioned on memorized content. Moreover, overparameterized models are susceptible to unintended associations, where rare or nonsensical trigger sequences become statistically bound to harmful or deceptive outputs.
