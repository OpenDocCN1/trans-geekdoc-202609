
As machine learning models scale in complexity and are increasingly trained on massive, diverse datasets scraped from uncontrolled sources, the risk of unintentional or intentional poisoning grows. Public datasets, scraped corpora, and third-party contributions provide attackers with ample surface to embed poisoned samples. Without strict provenance tracking, data integrity verification, or reproducible training records, models trained on such datasets become fertile ground for backdoor insertion. The expanding use of foundation models only magnifies this risk, as compromised foundational layers can propagate latent vulnerabilities across a cascade of downstream applications.

Combatting poisoning-based backdoors requires a multi-faceted strategy that extends beyond the training loop. Defensive techniques such as influence function analysis, spectral signature inspection, and activation clustering attempt to isolate poisoned samples post hoc, but each comes with trade-offs in accuracy, scalability, and false positives. More proactive measures involve dataset curation, rigorous audit trails, model fingerprinting, and differential testing against known trigger classes. Even with these defenses in place, the sophistication and adaptability of data poisoning attacks mean that securing the training pipeline remains one of the most critical—and difficult—challenges in modern AI security. As shown in [`[Table 7.2](#c07.xhtml#c07-tbl-0002)`](`#c07.xhtml#c07-tbl-0002`), understanding where in the AI pipeline backdoors can be inserted helps defenders anticipate how attacks can persist across training, transfer, and deployment workflows.

## Trojan Attacks in Transfer and Fine-Tuning Scenarios

Trojan attacks in the context of transfer learning exploit the fact that modern AI development frequently relies on pretrained models as starting points. These models, trained on massive datasets at significant computational cost, are often shared and reused across projects to reduce development time and resource consumption. The attacker leverages this dependency by injecting Trojan behaviors into the pretrained model itself or during its fine-tuning on a downstream task. Once embedded, the Trojan remains dormant unless activated by a specific trigger during inference. The insidious nature of these attacks lies in their persistence across multiple reuses of the compromised model, often without the downstream developers being aware of the hidden threat.

The process typically begins with the attacker modifying either the base model directly or the dataset used for fine-tuning. In many cases, the Trojan is introduced during fine-tuning by poisoning a small subset of the downstream task's data. These poisoned examples contain subtle input triggers—visual, textual, or otherwise—that are carefully designed to appear benign while driving the model to learn a hidden association. The attacker aligns these inputs with a specific output label or behavior, causing the model to respond incorrectly whenever the trigger reappears post-deployment. Because fine-tuning often focuses only on task-specific heads or higher layers, the core representation retains the Trojan behavior encoded in the shared parameters.

A popular and effective method for embedding these Trojans is through gradient-alignment optimization. The attacker crafts poisoned data that causes gradient updates during fine-tuning to steer the model in a specific direction. These updates reinforce a latent correlation between the trigger and the target output, without significantly perturbing the model's behavior on clean inputs. This manipulation is often imperceptible when reviewing accuracy or loss metrics, as the poisoned examples constitute a small portion of the training data. The result is a model that appears well tuned for its intended task while harboring a latent, conditionally activated threat.

Attackers may also embed Trojans directly into pretrained embedding layers or intermediate representations prior to fine-tuning. This method is particularly dangerous because many fine-tuning workflows freeze the lower layers of the model and adjust only the final classification layers. In such configurations, any malicious behavior encoded in the frozen layers persists unaltered, and may not even be affected by downstream training. These hidden behaviors can be invoked via inputs that activate specific feature pathways, which then propagate through the model and influence the final prediction. By exploiting this architectural rigidity, attackers ensure that the Trojan remains viable regardless of how the model is repurposed.

Feature extractor poisoning is another variant of this attack, targeting models used in a feature-extraction role without full fine-tuning. In scenarios such as zero-shot learning, few-shot adaptation, or frozen-feature transfer learning, the poisoned extractor may output misleading embeddings when triggered. These poisoned embeddings propagate through downstream classifiers or similarity functions, resulting in manipulated predictions or unauthorized access. Since the extractor is typically treated as a trusted component, its outputs are not scrutinized, allowing the Trojan to function covertly even when placed behind additional model layers.

Some adversaries aim for universality, embedding Trojan triggers that persist across diverse fine-tuning tasks and domains. These universal triggers are carefully optimized for compatibility with common architectures such as transformer encoders, convolutional backbones, or shared latent spaces. The goal is to produce a trigger that causes malicious behavior regardless of the specific downstream task, whether it involves classification, summarization, detection, or generation. This universality enables a single poisoned model to compromise a wide range of applications, amplifying its impact and creating a persistent threat that spreads through open-source and enterprise model repositories alike.

To achieve this level of robustness, attackers often generate synthetic poisoned data tailored to fine-tuning pipelines. These synthetic inputs may resemble legitimate data in form and distribution but carry embedded features that activate the Trojan when present. Because many downstream users augment their training data or use weak supervision, the attacker can exploit these workflows to introduce poisoned examples without raising suspicion. Techniques like adversarial paraphrasing, subtle visual artifacts, or metadata injection are used to maintain plausibility while ensuring that the model internalizes the desired malicious behavior.
