
# Bitcoin History and Fundamentals

## Origins of Bitcoin

On 31st October 2008, *Satoshi Nakamoto* published the Bitcoin Whitepaper titled *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System* on the cryptography mailing list at metzdowd.com. On 9th January 2009, Satoshi released Bitcoin code on *SourceForge* and launched the network by mining the second and further blocks. The first block is not mined as it is hardcoded in the code itself; the first block of any blockchain is called the **genesis block**. Satoshi worked with quite a few smart people in cryptography, including *Hal Finney, Adam Back, Gavin Andresen*. Satoshi worked closely with Hal Finney, and the first transaction was sent on 12th January between them.

## Early Milestones

It is remarkable how quickly Bitcoin got noticed. On 5th October, the world's first Bitcoin exchange was launched, and $1 could buy 1309.03 BTC. On 22nd May 2010, two Pizzas were bought with 10000 ![](images/Bitcoin.jpg). This day is remembered as **Bitcoin Pizza Day**.

## Network Incidents

It is not true that the Bitcoin network never stopped working. It happened twice.

1.  **Inflation Bug**: The first time, an inflation bug (a bug that allows an increase in supply of a cryptocurrency) was found, but before it could be fixed, someone used it to generate bitcoin. This was resolved within hours, making the extra bitcoin invalid.
2.  **Network Split (March 2013)**: The second time was in March 2013, when a Bitcoin software upgrade resulted in a network split. It was soon resolved as Pools and node owners actively moved back to the older version.

## Mining Evolution

Initially, anyone could easily mine bitcoin on personal computers or laptops. And since there were only a few miners, it would not take much time to mine a block. However, as more people started mining, it took a long time to mine a block.

On 27th November 2010, **Slushpool** launched the first mining pool in which miners would jointly mine a block, and once mined, the reward would be shared among miners based on their contribution. Later, mining pools became the only viable way to mine a block, and few mining pools almost completely took over the computational power of the network. Even today, this is the case.

In July 2014, a mining pool briefly acquired more than 50% mining power of the network. The mining pool did not try to do a **51% mining attack**, but it realized that this could affect the overall credibility of the network and committed to never crossing 40% of the total mining power.

## Altcoins and Early Ecosystem

**Namecoin** was the first cryptocurrency launched after Bitcoin. In the same year, **Litecoin** was also launched, and it continues to be popular even today.

In 2013, when **ASIC miners** hit the market, computational requirements became too high, making mining difficult for smaller players. At that time, many miners switched to mining Namecoins, Litecoins, and other coins.

In May 2011, **Bitpay** became the first payment processor that allowed merchants to accept Bitcoin as payment.

## Regulation and Infrastructure

On 28th March 2013, the first US cryptocurrency regulations were put in place. The **FinCEN** brought regulations.

On 29th October 2013, the first Bitcoin ATM opened in Vancouver, Canada.

## Major Exchange Hack

Bitcoin exchanges have a history of hacking events, and a lot of people have lost their bitcoin. However, the biggest impact happened to bitcoin prices and confidence when **MtGox** was hacked in February 2014. It was the biggest Bitcoin exchange at the time.

## Hard Forks and Scaling Debate

On 10th June 2014, *Mike Hearn* created **BIP 64**. In December, he released the software leading to the first hardfork and creation of **Bitcoin XT**.

In 2010, Satoshi secretly introduced a **1MB limit** to the block size. Since each block is generated every 10 min, the 1MB block size limit was the big limiting factor in Bitcoin scaling. With a 1MB limit, Bitcoin could, at most, have **7 transactions per second**. Some developers proposed to increase this limit as early as 2011.

In 2015, **BIP 100** and **BIP 101** were introduced to enhance that limit. Many Bitcoin hard forks were related to an increase in this limit. With **segregated witness** softfork, this limit was increased.

## Hype Cycles and Institutional Adoption

Bitcoin had multiple hype cycles when prices jumped and dropped. The hype cycles were seen in 2011, twice in 2013, and in 2017. These hype cycles have given Bitcoin media coverage and helped improve adoption.

In August 2020, **MicroStrategy** became the first publicly traded company to hold bitcoin in its treasury. This has given Bitcoin legitimacy as a store-of-value.

# Defining Bitcoin

Bitcoin is not one thing but two, and the following are their definitions:

> **Bitcoin is a permissionless, verifiable, peer-to-peer, supply inelastic and evolutionary asset.**
>
> and
>
> **A decentralized network designed to create, store and transfer bitcoin in an insecure environment without intermediaries.**

Bitcoin is also designed to scale and have layers above and side of it. Bitcoin has the highest accumulated proof-of-work compared with any existing network. In future applications, assets, currencies, and tokens will be able to secure themselves and have partial backing of value from Bitcoin. Due to its design, open-source, decentralized nature, value, and accumulated proof of work, it might someday become an essential part of the internet. In the future, Bitcoin may have the following definition:

> **A layer of trust within the internet.**

# Byzantine General Problem

The Bitcoin network uses a **proof-of-work** consensus mechanism to generate bitcoin and uses a script, cryptography, and proof-of-work to transfer it from subsequent receivers.

The Bitcoin proof-of-work consensus mechanism tries to build consensus in a hostile environment where attackers may be collaborating to attack the network by including or excluding messages having transaction information that could help them. This is the reason this book has a tagline:

> **Building trust in a trustless environment**

This is, essentially, a solution to the 1982 paper titled, *The Byzantine Generals Problem* published by *Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak, and Marshall Pease*. It's an important game theory problem, and the condition of the problem is as follows:

1.  Byzantine army is planning an attack on a city.
2.  The Byzantine army has several divisions, each of which is commanded by a general. These generals are stationed outside the city at distances.
3.  No two generals can communicate with each other directly, and they need to rely on couriers who transfer their messages.
4.  To win an attack on the city, they need to establish a consensus on the plan of attack. This requires a lot of correspondence.
5.  Now the problem is that there may be traitors among them who will try to sabotage the plan by spreading misinformation.
6.  A coordinated attack will lead to victory.
7.  A non-coordinated attack will be lost.

In the case of Bitcoin, most participating nodes agree on transaction messages, their order, and linking with previously accepted messages based on defined consensus rules. Each node acts as the general here. Unlike the Byzantine general problem, the aim is to protect the network. If the network is sabotaged, then the attacker (traitor) can double spend, exclude or include messages, and change the order of messages. This will affect the credit value of the network.

Now, we understand how the Bitcoin consensus mechanism essentially solves The Byzantine general problem.

1.  Any number of generals can be traitors if a majority is cooperating. This makes it a democratic system.
