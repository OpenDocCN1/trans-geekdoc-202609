Even when AI is trained on accurate data from skilled practitioners, systemic biases can still persist. For instance, studies have shown that Black patients have historically received less treatment for pain compared to white patients [7]. An AI system trained on such records might continue to recommend lower doses of pain medication to African-American patients—not because of medical necessity, but due to ingrained systemic bias. Furthermore, AI tools used for resource allocation may exacerbate disparities by assigning fewer resources to patients deemed “less desirable” or “less cost-effective” by health systems, based on questionable criteria.

# 6.4.5 PROFESSIONAL REALIGNMENT

AI is expected to significantly reshape the medical profession, particularly in specialties like radiology, where many tasks are automatable. While this can improve efficiency and accuracy, it raises concerns about reduced human expertise over time. As providers rely more on AI, they may lose critical skills needed to identify errors and make informed decisions, potentially weakening clinical judgment. Balancing AI integration with continued human oversight is essential to maintain medical competence and ensure safe, effective care [8].

# 6.4.6 THE NIRVANA FALLACY

One last danger bears notice. AI holds immense potential to improve healthcare, yet it faces significant risks and challenges. The nirvana fallacy arises when new technologies are unfairly compared to an idealized, flawless alternative instead of the flawed status quo. While healthcare AI is not without imperfections, inaction due to its limitations risks perpetuating the existing system’s many shortcomings. Embracing AI thoughtfully may offer a path to meaningful improvement.
