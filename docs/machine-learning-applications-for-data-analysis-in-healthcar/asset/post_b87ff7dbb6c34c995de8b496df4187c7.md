## 7.1 INTRODUCTION

Earlier novel drugs for known diseases were identified using random screening and observations with effectiveness of natural products. Until the 1980s, this random screening approach resulted in the discovery of numerous significant chemicals. High-throughput screening (HTS), which is ideal for independent screening of multiple drugs against a specific molecular target or cellular test, is currently improving this technique. The essence of HTS in drug discovery was emphasized with the discovery of cyclosporine A as an immunosuppressant [1]. Progressively, another successful paradigm of application of computer-aided drug design (CADD) was the finding of gefitinib using ALLADIN [2] software after screening a library of 1500 chemicals.

To unravel the mechanism of drug resistance, search for novel antibiotics, and design them for both known and unknown targets, CADD can be integrated with wet lab techniques in the design of new antibiotics. CADD approaches are computationally faster, accurate, and economical [3].

Deciphering novel targets that exhibit distinct pathways needed for bacterial life is a key option to solving antibiotic resistance. Researchers are still looking for novel antibiotics to combat established targets, and computational tools have been utilized effectively in a number of studies to do so. The fundamental CADD procedure begins with the biological identification of a hypothetical target for which ligand binding resulted in antibacterial action. In SBDD, the target’s 3D structure can be determined via X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance, or homology modeling. CADD-SBDD screening is built on this base [4]. The major focus is the development of structure–activity relationship which yields further improvements in the compounds, and LBDD is utilized in the absence of the availability of the desired 3D structure.

CADD provides for greater experiment focus, which saves time and money while discovering novel medications. The most popular and extensively used method in this area is the *in silico* methodology. These strategies are typically utilized in conjunction with the collection of *in vitro* data for constructing and evaluating the model. These models have been widely employed in the development and improvement of novel compounds with affinity for a target, as well as the elucidation of absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity features, as well as physicochemical characterization.

Structure-based virtual screening (SBVS) employs scoring functions to quantify the force of noncovalent contacts between a ligand and a molecular target in order to forecast the ideal interaction mode between two molecules to create a stable complex [5]. As a result, the success or failure of SBVS software is mostly determined by scoring functions.

SBVS is performed using a variety of software packages, each of which is based on a distinct algorithm and yields various results. In the recent decade, a novel SBVS approach called consensus virtual screening has been widely employed in several researches to improve SBVS precision and minimize the number of false positives produced in these trials. The presence of a 3D structure of the target protein is an absolute need for SBVS to work. Virtual databases have been built to hold 3D structures of molecules, such as the Protein Data Bank. However, obtaining the 3D structure experimentally is not always achievable. In this case, homology modeling allows for the prediction of a protein’s 3D structure based on its amino acid sequence.

### 7.2.1 LBDD APPROACH

When target structures are unknown, LBDD approaches are frequently applied in drug designing. It begins with building a ligand using software like ChemSketch, Schrodinger, and so on and generating many random combinations by adding or removing new functional groups or by finding databases like Zinc Database to screen out a variety of ligands known to be having some biological efficacy against the target protein or gene. After building or finding the effective ligand molecule, QSAR (quantitative structure–activity relationship) approach is applied to estimate the efficacy and biological activity of those molecules. QSAR approaches involve 2D QSAR as well as 3D QSAR [6]. 2D QSAR aids in the identification and quantification of a drug’s physical–chemical properties, as well as the impact of these properties on the drug’s biological activity. 3D QSAR looks at a molecule’s whole 3D characteristics rather than just one substituent. It revolves around a molecule’s most important qualities, such as its overall size, shape, and electrical properties. Supplementation of molecules is critical in 3D QSAR. QSAR analysis is based on the fact that electronic, geometric, topological properties of the molecules govern their chemical activity, physical, and biological efficacy (i.e., its geometric, steric, and electronically properties). In QSAR, we correlate the properties of the experimentally known compounds to detect the parameters of new compounds whose efficacy we are trying to estimate. In the results of QSAR, we often used terms like biophore and biophore. Biophore is a structure that shows some biological activity, and in contrast to it, biophore is a chemical structure that lacks any biological activity [7]. QSAR is an amalgamation of multifarious spheres which includes statistics, chemistry, and biology. Three components must be available in order to develop a model of QSAR: (1) an experimental dataset giving insights on the properties and biological activity for a diverse group of already tested chemicals (the training set), (2) data pertaining to the molecular structure which includes many descriptors for this group of chemicals, and (3) statistical approaches which are referred to as chemometric methods for investigation and validation of the essential correlations [8] (Figure 7.1).

### 7.2.2 VIRTUAL SCREENING IN LBDD

Virtual screening is a computational technique for analyzing an extensive library of chemical compounds to find new drug candidates. It is widely used for evaluating large libraries of compounds automatically by using computer tools. Virtual screening explores the properties of the biological molecules on the basis of Lipinski's rule of five. Only those molecules which are potential to be a drug candidate based on Lipinski's rule are screened out and given in the result of virtual screening.

Virtual screening software can provide very accurate findings since they are based on quantum and molecular physics rules. We can also alter the ligands and build new forms of ligands by adding or removing specific functional groups using CHEMSKETCH software freely available online.

ChemSketch is a comprehensive structure editor with a variety of tools and functionality which provide a combination of scientific and chemical information [9].

In ChemSketch, we have to draw molecular structure, generate structure from InChI (The InChI program includes the ability to generate an InChI key which is a condensed 25-character representation of the InChI string. This compact representation significantly expands the use of InChI encoding for structure specification and search, particularly over the internet.) or SMILES strings, or copy/paste from ChemDraw. Tools available in ChemSketch allow us to incorporate the following features very easily.

- Insert predraw templates of amino acids, aromatics, steroids, sugar, and more.
- Search the dictionary of >170,000 systematic, trivial, and trade names.
- Easily draw reactions and complex chemical schemes (including biotransformation maps).
- Use graphical templates and tools to communicate chemistry and chemical biology concepts (e.g., chemical bond types Lewis's structure, molecular orbitals, Newman projections, peptide sequence, and more).
- Import and export structure files in a variety of standard files and formats.
