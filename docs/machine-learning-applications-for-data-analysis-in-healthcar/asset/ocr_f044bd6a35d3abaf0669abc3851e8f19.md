# 10.1.1 ECG WAVEFORM

The display of each heartbeat is made up of electrical pulses that have peaks as well as valleys at regular intervals. The length of the electrical wave that passes through the heart affects electrical activity (Figure 10.2). The second element is the level of electrical activity the heart muscle is enduring, which can be used to identify any heart muscle portions that are excessively large or overworked. ECG signals range from 0.05 to 100 Hz. P, Q, R, S, and T represent the ECG’s five peaks and valleys for the formation of the signal. There is also sometimes a U wave. The T and P waves, along with the QRS complex, are what make ECG analysis most effective that can be found accurately and reliably [7–9]. The two atria, the heart’s upper chambers, are activated by the P wave, while the ventricles. The QRS complex and T wave stimulate the lower chamber of the heart. One of the basic problems with automatic ECG signal analysis is QRS recognition. After detecting QRS complex, ECG signal examined thoroughly. The P, QRS, and T waves show atrial and ventricular depolarization and repolarization [8,10,11]. The baseline is a potential line that refers to horizontal portion of waveform that appears before P wave. Depolarization of the atrial musculature causes the P wave. QRS complex results from simultaneous AV repolarization and ventricular depolarization. T-waves occur during ventricular repolarization, while U waves are assumed to be after potentials in the ventricular muscle. The T occurs during ventricular repolarization [12–15]. It is possible to detect cardiac arrhythmias, myocardial infections, ventricular hypertrophy, conduction abnormalities, conduction abnormalities, and other disease states based on the length, amplitude, and form of the QRS complex [16]. The heart beats 60–100 times per minute on average. Slower-than-normal heart rate is bradycardia; faster-than-normal is tachycardia (fast heart) (Table 10.1).
