# ## 6.1 INTRODUCTION

The term “artificial intelligence” was first introduced during a gathering at Dartmouth College in 1956. Between 1956 and 1974, AI research primarily focused on solving mathematical and algebraic problems and developing systems capable of understanding natural language. From 1980 to 1987, expert systems gained popularity—these were programs designed to answer questions or solve problems based on specialized knowledge.

Interest in AI declined for a time but was revived in 1997 when IBM’s Deep Blue, a chess-playing computer, defeated Russian grandmaster Garry Kasparov. Since then, nearly every major tech company and scientific institution involved in medical research or clinical trials has pursued AI-based predictive and prescriptive tools for diagnosis and treatment. According to the technology research firm Gartner, 75% of healthcare organizations were expected to invest in AI capabilities by 2021 to improve overall performance.

AI-powered medical tools offer significant benefits for both doctors and patients, and they are applicable across a wide range of healthcare domains. Beyond medicine, AI has numerous applications across industries such as finance, transportation, and manufacturing, and it is transforming how businesses make decisions and treat complex problems. AI technologies are used in areas like object, face, speech, and handwriting recognition; virtual reality and image processing; natural language processing, chatbots, and translation; e-mail spam filtering, robotics, and data mining. According to market intelligence firm Tractica, global annual AI revenue is projected to grow to $36.8 billion by 2025.
