### ABSTRACT

Brain–computer interface is the term given to the process of converting human intents into patterns that may then be used to control equipment that is linked to the outside of the body. An electrocardiogram (ECG) is a visual representation of electrical patterns generated by the heart. ECG is a significant diagnostic tool widely used for people with cardiac issue. ECGs are often recorded on the chart with a grid on them. The grid work on ECG recordings is made up of numerous small 1 mm square blocks. Each of these little boxes lasts around 40 ms. The transthoracic interpretation of electrical heart activity over time is known as ECG. Assessment of the ECG signal signifies the heart health. For the diagnosis of heart disorders, a number of techniques have been used, including fast Fourier transforms and wavelet transform. In this chapter, the combination of extreme learning machine (ELM) with discrete wavelet transforms results in what is known as an ELM-healthcare model. This model is primarily made up of four components, and those components are preprocessing, heartbeat segmentation, feature extraction, and heartbeat classification. Our proposed model outperforms other state-of-art models and achieved 99.9% accuracy, 98.9% sensitivity, and 99.9% specificity.

### 10.1 INTRODUCTION

Electrocardiogram (ECG) measures heart activity in the electrical form. It is a visual representation of the biopotential's variation throughout time. Leads are attached to certain parts in body to record (ECG) on the graph of the chart or a computer monitor, depending on the application. Human heart has four chambers: right atrium, left atrium, left ventricle, and right ventricle [1, 2]. The upper two chambers are atria, and the bottom two are ventricles. The sinoatrial node in the right atrium, where the heartbeat normally starts, is responsible for transmitting electrical signals across the heart. The atrio-ventricular (AV) node receives this signal as it travels from the atria [3–5]. The lower chamber’s ventricles, which contain a network of fibers that channel electricity and convey impulses to all of its parts, are connected to the AV node. This path of propagation must be accurately traced to make sure the heart is working right. Figure 10.1 depicts an overview of the ECG classification process.

### 10.1.1 ECG WAVEFORM

The display of each heartbeat is made up of electrical pulses that have peaks as well as valleys at regular intervals. The length of the electrical wave that passes through the heart affects electrical activity (Figure 10.2). The second element is the level of electrical activity the heart muscle is enduring, which can be used to identify any heart muscle portions that are excessively large or overworked. ECG signals range from 0.05 to 100 Hz. P, Q, R, S, and T represent the ECG’s five peaks and valleys for the formation of the signal. There is also sometimes a U wave. The T and P waves, along with the QRS complex, are what make ECG analysis most effective that can be found accurately and reliably [7–9]. The two atria, the heart’s upper chambers, are activated by the P wave, while the ventricles. The QRS complex and T wave stimulate the lower chamber of the heart. One of the basic problems with automatic ECG signal analysis is QRS recognition. After detecting QRS complex, ECG signal examined thoroughly. The P, QRS, and T waves show atrial and ventricular depolarization and repolarization [8,10,11]. The baseline is a potential line that refers to horizontal portion of waveform that appears before P wave. Depolarization of the atrial musculature causes the P wave. QRS complex results from simultaneous AV repolarization and ventricular depolarization. T-waves occur during ventricular repolarization, while U waves are assumed to be after potentials in the ventricular muscle. The T occurs during ventricular repolarization [12–15]. It is possible to detect cardiac arrhythmias, myocardial infections, ventricular hypertrophy, conduction abnormalities, and other disease states based on the length, amplitude, and form of the QRS complex [16]. The heart beats 60–100 times per minute on average. Slower-than-normal heart rate is bradycardia; faster-than-normal is tachycardia (fast heart) (Table 10.1).
