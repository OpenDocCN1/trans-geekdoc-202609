Even when AI is trained on accurate data from skilled practitioners, systemic biases can still persist. For instance, studies have shown that Black patients have historically received less treatment for pain compared to white patients [7]. An AI system trained on such records might continue to recommend lower doses of pain medication to African-American patients—not because of medical necessity, but due to ingrained systemic bias. Furthermore, AI tools used for resource allocation may exacerbate disparities by assigning fewer resources to patients deemed “less desirable” or “less cost-effective” by health systems, based on questionable criteria.

### 6.4.5 PROFESSIONAL REALIGNMENT

AI is expected to significantly reshape the medical profession, particularly in specialties like radiology, where many tasks are automatable. While this can improve efficiency and accuracy, it raises concerns about reduced human expertise over time. As providers rely more on AI, they may lose critical skills needed to identify errors and make informed decisions, potentially weakening clinical judgment. Balancing AI integration with continued human oversight is essential to maintain medical competence and ensure safe, effective care [8].

### 6.4.6 THE NIRVANA FALLACY

One last danger bears notice. AI holds immense potential to improve healthcare, yet it faces significant risks and challenges. The nirvana fallacy arises when new technologies are unfairly compared to an idealized, flawless alternative instead of the flawed status quo. While healthcare AI is not without imperfections, inaction due to its limitations risks perpetuating the existing system’s many shortcomings. Embracing AI thoughtfully may offer a path to meaningful improvement.

### 6.4.7 A LACK OF PERSONAL INVOLVEMENT

Operational robots are thoroughly real and are not tweaked to feel any sympathy toward the patients. A couple of experts consider it to be a weight. Contrary to popular belief, human abilities—especially in personal interaction—still surpass those of computers in healthcare. The connection between doctor and patient is vital for building trust and supporting effective treatment. While AI tools can assist with routine tasks and minor errors, they lack the empathy and judgment needed in critical moments. Unlike machines, physicians can choose to go beyond strict protocols in order to save a life.

### 6.4.8 A RISE IN UNEMPLOYMENT RATES IN HEALTHCARE WORKERS

Since AI has been executed all through the whole course of action of clinical consideration on a really astonishing scale, various activities that were for the most part performed by individuals ought to be conceivable by machines nowadays. Chatbots and robots can provide emotional support, monitor patient well-being, and even predict issues like seizures, sepsis, or heart failure. While this raises concerns about job displacement, especially in routine roles, AI is unlikely to replace medical professionals in the near future. However, AI systems have already demonstrated the ability to detect diseases from X-rays with greater accuracy than some radiologists.

### 6.4.9 POSSIBILITY OF A DEFECTIVE DIAGNOSIS

Accurate diagnosis relies on data from large, diverse populations with similar symptoms. If an AI system lacks sufficient data for certain demographic groups, it may produce incorrect diagnoses. Inexperienced clinicians may not recognize these errors, leading to inappropriate treatment. Medical errors already contribute to nearly 200,000 deaths annually in the United States, with an estimated $20 billion in associated costs. Poorly trained AI tools could further increase these risks.

### 6.4.10 SOCIAL PREJUDICES

As mentioned earlier, AI-based systems cannot fully grasp human context or social nuances, which can lead to biased outcomes. For example, an AI algorithm might recommend an expensive nursing facility to a low-income patient, ignoring their financial limitations. The huge cost of organizations at such workplaces cripples the patient to continue with the treatment, and the expert can choose the best fitting treatment plan thinking about the patient's social and financial status.

## 6.5 CONCLUSION

The key challenge for AI in healthcare is not its capability, but its integration into everyday clinical practice. Over time, clinicians may shift toward tasks requiring uniquely human judgment. The true losers may be those unwilling to work alongside AI.

Despite its promise, AI cannot solve the deep-rooted issues in India's healthcare system—like inadequate funding, poor regulation, and socio-cultural barriers. While AI and the Internet of Medical Things are improving lives globally, adoption in India is hindered by weak digital infrastructure, a fragmented private sector, and uneven capacities across states and healthcare providers.

Established urban hospitals are likely to benefit first, leaving under-served areas behind. Many small clinics still rely on basic digital tools. Often, AI solutions are tech-driven rather than problem-driven, and fail to meet local needs—especially in rural regions lacking reliable internet.

Additionally, there is a growing digital divide between users and developers. Without inclusive design and robust safeguards around data security and accountability, AI in healthcare may fall short of being both safe and equitable.
