# 6.5 CONCLUSION

The key challenge for AI in healthcare is not its capability, but its
integration into everyday clinical practice. Over time, clinicians may
shift toward tasks requiring uniquely human judgment. The true losers
may be those unwilling to work alongside AI.

Despite its promise, AI cannot solve the deep-rooted issues in India's
healthcare system—like inadequate funding, poor regulation, and
socio-cultural barriers. While AI and the Internet of Medical Things are
improving lives globally, adoption in India is hindered by weak digital
infrastructure, a fragmented private sector, and uneven capacities across
states and healthcare providers.

Established urban hospitals are likely to benefit first, leaving under-
served areas behind. Many small clinics still rely on basic digital tools.
Often, AI solutions are tech-driven rather than problem-driven, and fail
to meet local needs—especially in rural regions lacking reliable internet.

Additionally, there is a growing digital divide between users and
developers. Without inclusive design and robust safeguards around data
security and accountability, AI in healthcare may fall short of being both
safe and equitable.
