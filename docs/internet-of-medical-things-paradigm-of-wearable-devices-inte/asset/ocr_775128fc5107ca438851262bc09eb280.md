Similar IoT-based alert systems have been proposed for a wide number of applications. Khattakaran et al. described in [48] an alert system consisting of an accelerometer placed on the wrist and a heart rate sensor. Both sensors are connected to a mobile phone via Bluetooth. When an abnormality is detected, the mobile phone sends an alert to the medical center providing the GPS coordinates of the alert. Pawlak and coworkers proposed in [49] the Medical Cyber-Physical System (MCPS) an IoT-based system for telemonitoring pregnant women at home. The MCPS monitors abdominal signals and alerts of abnormalities to a surveillance center located in a hospital. To monitor the continuity and timely use of medicines by elderly people, Kuwait et al. implemented a smart medical dispenser [50]. Such dispenser tracks the use of prescribed medicine by patients and alerts healthcare providers, or family members if the patient does not access the medicine in the same time frame.

IoT in healthcare is not just capturing physiological signals, processing, and sending SOS alerts. This technology can also be used in Assistive Technologies (AT) and rehabilitation.

Velázquez et al. [51] reported an wearable device to assist blind and visually impaired people. Urban navigation is a major challenge for visually disabled people, as they find to navigate the environment with constant obstacle detection [52]. This system targets to assist users in finding their destinations. For this purpose, the concept in Figure 1.7 was proposed. The system consists of a smartphone and an on-shoe tactile display.

Figure 1.8 shows its operation principle. Using the smartphone's Internet connectivity, the data is transmitted to a cloud server. The smartphone updates the server at 5 Hz, which covers every walking speed. A remote computer accesses the server's information and locates the user in a locally running spatial database.
