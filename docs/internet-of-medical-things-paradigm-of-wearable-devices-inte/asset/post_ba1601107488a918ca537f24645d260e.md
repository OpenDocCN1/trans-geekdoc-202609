## Internet of Medical Things

### 7.1 Introduction

In the 1950s, research in Artificial Intelligence (AI) was supported by a group of scientists who predicted that in a few decades machines would be capable of carrying out any task humans could. Nevertheless, it was not until 60 years later that the Conversational Agents (CAs) were started with Siri (Apple, 2011) [51]. Following Apple, some companies like Google, T&T, Nuance have successfully participated in the market [25]. Over time, the idea of personifying CAs was revolutionary giving rise to Embodied Conversational Agents (ECAs).

An example of every ECA is SimCoach (US Institute for Creative Technologies, 2013) [22], which was created to respond to healthcare challenges caused by the conflicts in Iraq and Afghanistan in the U.S. population. The SimProject developed different architectures of support agents to serve as online guides for the military and their families [53]. Despite its potential, it was not considered to deliver diagnosis or treatment, or as a replacement for human providers.

As CAs evolved, so wearable devices; advances as heart rate monitors, smartwatches, activity monitors, and other wearables, have mobile devices ideal for healthcare delivery [48,51] and with working together with CAs opened up a new world of possibilities. For example, there are currently a large number of applications that can track user activity, eating habits and analyze signals as blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, skin temperature, etc., by interacting with various wearable devices as shown in Figure 7.1.

The use of wearable devices has been emerging due to their potential to improve the level of healthcare delivery and reducing costs. For this reason, the possibility of innovation by means of using wearable devices with the intervention of CAs could allow in some cases immediate feedback, supplementing the shortage of staff in primary healthcare centers and thereby reducing the burden on providers.
