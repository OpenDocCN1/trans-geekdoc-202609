
## 8.1.2 Particle Tracking in Microscopy Images

Particle tracking is of key importance for quantitative analysis of intracellular dynamic processes from time-lapse microscopy image data. Because manually detecting and following large numbers of individual particles is not feasible, automated computational methods have been developed for these tasks by many groups. Aiming to perform an objective comparison of methods, we gathered the community and organized an open competition in which participating teams applied their own methods independently to a commonly defined data set including diverse scenarios.

Particle tracking in microscopy images is a fundamental task in soft matter physics, as it enables the study of dynamic behaviors, interactions, and movements of particles within complex materials. Leveraging pre-trained models and knowledge transfer techniques for particle tracking can significantly enhance the accuracy and efficiency of this analysis.

By utilizing pre-trained models from computer vision domains, such as object detection or instance segmentation, researchers can extract powerful and relevant features from microscopy images. These pre-trained models have learned to recognize and locate objects in diverse settings, which can be adapted for particle tracking in soft matter systems. Fine-tuning these models on microscopy images allows them to learn to identify and track particles accurately.

Knowledge transfer is especially valuable in cases where limited labeled data is available for particle tracking. By transferring knowledge from pre-trained models to the target task, the model can leverage the rich information encoded in the pre-trained weights to achieve better generalization and performance on the microscopy images.

Incorporating recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, pre-trained on sequence-related tasks, can aid in tracking particles across consecutive frames in time-lapse microscopy. These models are adept at capturing temporal dependencies, ensuring the continuity and smoothness of particle trajectories.

Ensemble approaches can also be utilized to combine the outputs of multiple pre-trained models for particle tracking. Each pre-trained model may excel in different aspects of the tracking task, such as particle detection or trajectory prediction. Ensemble models help in reducing errors and improving the robustness of particle tracking results.

Domain adaptation plays a crucial role when dealing with variations in experimental conditions or imaging modalities. By adapting pre-trained models to the specific characteristics of microscopy images in soft matter physics, researchers can ensure accurate tracking even under varying imaging conditions.

Furthermore, self-supervised learning methods, such as contrastive learning or autoencoders, can be integrated with pre-trained models for particle tracking. These techniques allow the model to learn meaningful representations from the data itself, without the need for explicit annotations, and can improve generalization to unseen microscopy images.

Interdisciplinary collaboration between experts in machine learning and soft matter physics is essential to leverage pre-trained models effectively for particle tracking. Close collaboration ensures that the models are tailored to address the unique challenges posed by soft matter systems and microscopy imaging techniques.

## 8.1.3 Material Property Prediction

Material property prediction is a critical task in soft matter physics, enabling researchers to understand and design materials with specific properties for various applications. Leveraging pre-trained models and knowledge transfer techniques for material property prediction can significantly enhance the accuracy and efficiency of this analysis.

Pre-trained models from computer vision or natural language processing domains have learned to recognize complex patterns and structures. By fine-tuning these models on soft matter data, such as molecular structures, researchers can effectively capture features relevant to material properties. This transfer of knowledge allows the model to generalize well, even with limited training data, and predict material properties with improved accuracy.

Domain adaptation is essential in material property prediction, particularly when dealing with data from different experimental conditions or sources. By aligning the feature representations of pre-trained models with the target soft matter data, researchers can reduce the impact of distributional shifts and ensure reliable predictions.

Ensemble methods, combining predictions from multiple pre-trained models, can improve the robustness and generalization of material property predictions. Each pre-trained model may excel in identifying different features or correlations, and their fusion allows the model to consider diverse aspects of the material under investigation. Additionally, self-supervised learning approaches can be integrated with pre-trained models to predict material properties without the need for explicit labels. This strategy leverages inherent information present in the data, leading to more efficient learning and alleviating the reliance on large labeled datasets.

Interdisciplinary collaboration between machine learning experts and soft matter physicists is crucial to leverage pre-trained models effectively for material property prediction. Machine learning researchers can bring expertise in fine-tuning and adapting models, while soft matter physicists can contribute domain-specific insights, ensuring the development of accurate and reliable prediction models.

Furthermore, uncertainty quantification techniques can be employed to assess the reliability of material property predictions. Understanding uncertainty is essential for decision-making and risk assessment in soft matter physics applications.

## 8.1.4 Soft Matter Structure Analysis

A defining characteristic of soft matter is the mesoscopic scale of physical structures. The structures are much larger than the microscopic scale (the arrangement of atoms and molecules), and yet are much smaller than the macroscopic (overall) scale of the material. The properties and interactions of these mesoscopic structures may determine the macroscopic behavior of the material. The large number of constituents forming these mesoscopic structures, and the large degrees of freedom this causes, results in a general disorder between the large-scale structures. This disorder leads to the loss of long-range order that is characteristic of hard matter. For example, the turbulent vortices that naturally occur within a flowing liquid are much smaller than the overall quantity of liquid and yet much larger than its individual molecules, and the emergence of these vortices control the overall flowing behavior of the material. Also, the bubbles that compose a foam are mesoscopic because they individually consist of a vast number of molecules, and yet the foam itself consists of a great number of these bubbles, and the overall mechanical stiffness of the foam emerges from the combined interactions of the bubbles.

Typical bond energies in soft matter structures are of similar scale as thermal energies, therefore, the structures are constantly affected by thermal fluctuations and undergo Brownian motion. The ease of deformation and influence of low energy interactions regularly result in slow dynamics of the mesoscopic structures which allows some systems to remain out of equilibrium in metastable states. This characteristic can allow for recovery of initial state through an external stimuli and is often exploited in research.

Self-assembly is an inherent characteristic of soft matter systems. The characteristic complex behavior and hierarchical structures arise spontaneously as the system evolves towards equilibrium. Self-assembly can be classified as static, where the resulting structure is due to a free energy minimum, or dynamic, which occurs when the system is caught in a metastable state. Dynamic self-assembly can be utilized in the functional design of soft materials with these metastable states through kinetic trapping.

Soft materials often exhibit both elasticity and viscous responses to external stimuli, such as shear induced flow or phase transitions, however, excessive external stimuli often result in nonlinear responses. Soft matter becomes highly deformed before crack propagation, which differs significantly from the general fracture mechanics formulation. Rheology, the study of deformation under stress, is often used to investigate the bulk properties of soft matter.

## 8.1.5 Domain Adaptation for Soft Matter Systems

Domain adaptation is a crucial technique in soft matter physics to address challenges arising from data distribution shifts caused by variations in experimental conditions, environmental factors, or different measurement setups. Leveraging domain adaptation methods for soft matter systems can enhance the generalization and robustness of machine learning models, enabling accurate predictions and analyses across diverse experimental scenarios.

In soft matter physics, experimental conditions, such as temperature, pressure, or external forces, can lead to significant changes in the data distribution. These shifts may affect the performance of machine learning models trained on data from one domain when applied to another domain. Domain adaptation aims to mitigate this problem by aligning the feature representations of the data from the source domain (where the model is pre-trained) with the target domain (where the model is applied).

Various approaches to domain adaptation can be employed in soft matter systems.
