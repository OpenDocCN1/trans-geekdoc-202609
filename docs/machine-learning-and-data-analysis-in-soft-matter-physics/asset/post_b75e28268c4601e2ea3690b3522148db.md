Hence, Equation 11 simplifies: We need only one set of functions, the eigenfunctions \(\psi_k\). The true eigenvalues \(\lambda_k^*\) are real valued and decay exponentially in time \(\tau\) with characteristic relaxation times \(t_i^*\) that are directly linked to kinetic experimental observables. The approximation in Equation 11 is due to truncating all terms with relaxation times shorter than \(t_n^*\).

A quite established approach is to learn molecular kinetics (Equation 11) from a given trajectory data set. To obtain a low-dimensional model of the molecular kinetics that is easy to interpret and analyze, this usually involves two steps: (a) finding a low-dimensional latent space representation of the collective variables, \(y = E(x)\), using the encoder E; and (b) learning a dynamical propagator P in that space:

```
MD   x_t   --E-->   y_t
          |                | P
          v                v
         x_t+tau --E--> y_t+tau .

(13)
```

A common approach in MD, but also in other fields such as dynamical systems and fluid mechanics, is to seek a pair (E,P) such that P is a small matrix that propagates state vectors in a Markovian (memory-less) fashion. This is motivated by the spectral decomposition of dynamics (Equation 11): If \(\tau\) is large enough to filter fast processes, a few functions are sufficient to describe the kinetics of the system, and if E maps to the space spanned by these functions, P can be a linear, Markovian model.

If no specific constraints are imposed on P, then the minimum regression error of \(y_{t+\tau} = Py_{t'}\) the variational approach of Markov processes (VAMP), and maximum likelihood will all lead to the same estimator,

```
\(\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{C}_{00}^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{0\tau} \text{,}\)

(14)
```

using the latent space covariance matrices

```
\(\mathbf{C}_{00} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T-\tau} \mathbf{y}_t \mathbf{y}_t^\top \text{,} \quad \mathbf{C}_{0\tau} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T-\tau} \mathbf{y}_t \mathbf{y}_{t+\tau}^\top \text{,} \quad \mathbf{C}_{\tau\tau} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T-\tau} \mathbf{y}_{t+\tau} \mathbf{y}_{t+\tau}^\top \text{.}\)
```

If E performs a one-hot encoding that indicates which state the system is in, then the pair (E,P) is called a Markov state model, and P is a matrix of conditional probabilities to be in state j at time t + τ given that the system was in state i at time t.

Learning the embedding E is more difficult than learning P, as optimizing E by maximum likelihood or minimal regression error in latent space leads to a collapse of E to trivial, uninteresting solutions. This problem can be avoided by following a variational approach to approximating the leading terms of the spectral decomposition (Equation 10). The variational approach for conformation dynamics states that for dynamics obeying detailed balance, the eigenvalues \(\lambda_k\) of a propagator matrix P via any encoding y = E(x) are, in the statistical limit, lower bounds of the true \(\lambda_k^*\). The VAMP variational principle is more general, as it does not require detailed balance (see Equation 21) and applies to the singular values \(\sigma_k\):

\(\lambda_k \le \lambda_k^* \text{ (with detailed balance),}\)
\(\sigma_k \le \sigma_k^* \text{ (no detailed balance).}\)

As VAMP is the more general principle, we can use it to define the loss function for estimating molecular kinetics models:

\(\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAMP-2}}((\mathbf{y}_t^0, \mathbf{y}_t^\tau)) = - \left\| (\mathbf{C}_{00})^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{C}_{0\tau} (\mathbf{C}_{\tau\tau})^{-\frac{1}{2}} \right\|_F^2 \quad (15)\)

If dynamics obey detailed balance, we can use \(C_{\tau\tau} = C_{00}\) and plug in a symmetric estimate for \(C_{0\tau}\)

## 8.2.5 Sampling and Thermodynamics

MD time steps are on the order of one femtosecond (10^-15s), while configuration changes governing molecular function are often rare events that may take 10^-3 to 10^3s. Even when the potential and the forces generated by single-protein folding and unfolding are evaluated quickly, simulating these processes by direct MD simulation may take years to centuries on a supercomputer. To avoid this sampling problem, ML methods can be employed to learn generation of equilibrium samples from \(\mu(x)\) more efficiently, or even to generate statistically independent samples in one shot.

Learning to sample probability distributions is the realm of generative learning. In the past few years, directed generative networks, such as variational autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, and flows, have received a particular surge of interest. Such networks are trained to transform samples from an easy-to-sample probability distribution, such as Gaussian noise, into realistic samples of the objects of interest. These methods have been used to draw photorealistic images, generate speech or music audio, and generate chemical structures to design molecules or materials with certain properties. If the aim is to learn a probability distribution from sampling data (density estimation) or to learn to efficiently sample from a given probability distribution, one typically faces the challenge of matching the probability distribution of the trained model with a reference. Matching probability distributions can be achieved by minimizing probability distances. The most commonly used probability distance is the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, also called relative entropy between two distributions q and p:

```
KL(q || p) = \int q(x) [log q(x) - log p(x)] dx. (16)
```

In Boltzmann generation, aim to efficiently sample the equilibrium distribution \(\mu(x)\) of a many-body system defined by its energy function u(x) (Equation 1). We can learn the parameters \(\theta\) of a neural network that generates samples from the distribution \(p_X(x; \theta)\) by making its generated distribution similar to the target equilibrium distribution. Choosing \(q \equiv p_X(x)\) and \(p \equiv \mu(x)\) in Equation 16, we can minimize the \(KL_\theta[q_X || \mu_X]\) with the energy loss (EL):

```
EL = E_{x ~ p_X(x; \theta)} [log p_X(x; \theta) + u(x)] (17)
```

As the network samples from an energy surface \(u(x; \theta) = - \log p_X(x; \theta) + \text{const}\) (Equation 1), this loss is performing energy matching. In order to evaluate the loss (Equation 17), we need not only to be able to generate samples \(x \sim p_X(x; \theta)\) from the network but also to be able to compute \(p_X(x; \theta)\) for every sample x. The reverse case is density estimation. Suppose we have simulation data x, and we want to train the probability distribution \(p_X(x; \theta)\) to resemble the data distribution. We choose q(x) as the data distribution and \(p \equiv p_X(x; \theta)\) in Equation 16 and exploit that \(E_{x \sim \text{data}}[\log q(x)]\) is a constant that does not depend on the parameters \(\theta\). Then we can minimize the loss:

```
NL = -E_{x ~ data} [log p_X(x; \theta)]. (18)
```

This loss is the negative likelihood (NL) that the model generates the observed sample; hence, minimizing it corresponds to a maximum likelihood approach. Likelihood maximization is abundantly used in ML.

## 8.3 DOMAIN ADAPTATION TECHNIQUES FOR TRANSFERRING KNOWLEDGE ACROSS DIFFERENT SOFT MATTER SYSTEMS

Domain adaptation techniques play a crucial role in transferring knowledge and insights across different soft matter systems, even when there are significant variations in experimental conditions or data distributions.

These techniques allow researchers to leverage pre-existing knowledge from one soft matter system and apply it to another, facilitating the development of accurate and efficient models. Here are some domain adaptation techniques commonly used for transferring knowledge across different soft matter systems:

- **Adversarial Domain Adaptation**: Adversarial domain adaptation involves training a domain discriminator alongside the main model to distinguish between source and target domains. The main model, such as a neural network, aims to generate features that are indistinguishable by the domain discriminator. This adversarial process helps align the feature representations between the source and target domains, reducing the impact of domain shift.

- **Domain-Specific Adaptation**: Domain-specific adaptation involves fine-tuning or adapting the model separately for each soft matter system. By training domain-specific adaptations, the model can focus on capturing the specific features and characteristics of each system, tailoring its knowledge transfer to the target domain.

- **Ensemble Methods**: Ensemble methods combine predictions from multiple models, each pre-trained on different soft matter systems. By fusing the outputs of these models, researchers can benefit from their collective knowledge and reduce biases associated with individual models.

- **Self-Supervised Domain Adaptation**: Self-supervised learning techniques can be employed for domain adaptation, where the model learns from the data itself without relying on explicit annotations. By formulating self-supervised tasks specific to each soft matter system, the model can extract meaningful representations that are transferable across domains.
