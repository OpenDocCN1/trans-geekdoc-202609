
All training was completed on a 6x6 topology size to limit the computational cost of running the FEAs needed to produce the first observation channel at each step. The RL agent is trained to design topologies with randomly generated load cases to ensure thorough state-space exploration. The load cases were introduced for each episode by randomly assigning two elements on the exterior edge of the topology to act as bounded elements. A bounded element was represented by providing a static displacement boundary condition on the two exterior nodes of the selected element. If a corner element was selected, the single exterior corner node was assigned the boundary condition. Additionally, a single element was randomly selected to serve as the loaded element.

The selected element could be any random element in the 6x6 topology that was not already selected to serve as a bounded element. The direction (horizontal or vertical) and type (tensile or compressive) of the load were also randomly selected. If the selected element was exterior, the load was distributed between the two exterior elemental nodes. If a corner element was selected, the load was applied to the singular exterior corner node. Finally, if the element was in the interior, the load was randomly distributed between two elemental nodes defining one of the edges.

A fundamental dilemma of RL training is the tradeoff between exploration and exploitation. The agent explores the environment by taking random actions to compile diverse experiences needed to build a sufficiently accurate mapping between actions and values. However, to ensure efficiency, the agent should not strictly rely on random actions and instead should exploit the value mappings it has already learned. Therefore, to balance proper exploration and exploitation, random behavior should be more frequently used at the beginning of training when the value predictions are poor. As training continues and the value predictions improve, random behavior should be replaced by exploiting the maximum value action, argmax(a). This method is referred to as the ε-greedy method. The probability of taking a random action is dependent on a decaying ε value. At the start of the training process, the ε value is 1 and decays for each episode following Eq. (10):

```
ε = max(1 - E · ε_decay, ε_min) (10)
```

where ε_decay is the rate at which ε decreases per episode (ε_decay=3.5e-4) until ε reaches its minimal allowable value, ε_min (ε_min=0.01), and E is the current episode number. ε_min is greater than zero to ensure exploration continues during the later stages of training.

Whether randomly or strategically taken, the action will remove the corresponding element. This action will lead to a new topology and, using FEA, a new observation will be produced. Based on the results of the FEA, the agent will receive a reward for taking that action. After each action, the previous observation, new observation, action, and reward are recorded in a replay buffer. Taking advantage of topological symmetry, set the agent to store the equivalent x and y-axis flipped observations, corresponding flipped actions, and reward in the replay buffer. This technique allowed four experiences to be captured from a single FEA call for each step and is essentially a simple data augmentation method. Once an episode is complete and the buffer size is at least 128, a batch of 128 past experiences is randomly selected to train the DNN. The main model weights were updated using the stochastic gradient descent ADAM optimizer with a learning rate of 2.5e-3. The auxiliary model weights are replaced with the current main model weights after every 100 episodes to maintain relevant value predictions. A flowchart of a training episode is shown in Fig. 11.

### 7.4.3 Testing the Agent with Progressive Refinement

The trained agent was tested to validate its accuracy and generalization. The agent was trained to design 6 × 6 topologies to limit the computational cost of running the FEA calculations. However, designing an optimal topology at the coarse 6x6 level does not offer sufficient design detail. For this reason, testing on a finer mesh size will result in a more meaningful evaluation of the agent’s abilities. The RL agent can interact with topologies larger than it is trained on because the value function DNNs are built solely on convolutional filters. Convolutional filters are not size-dependent and can be passed along any input size. Therefore, the agent could interact directly with a larger topology size (e.g., 24 × 24 or beyond) without retraining. However, the computational cost of interacting directly with a larger topology from starting solid block to optimal topology is significantly larger than interacting with the 6×6 size. Therefore, it proposes implementing progressive refinement to help improve the computational efficiency of the RL agent.

Progressive refinement is the process of sequentially increasing the complexity of an object from “coarse” to “fine.” Regarding topology design, progressive refinement increases the topology detail over sequential steps instead of all at once. As a result, the number of elements used to define an equivalent topology shape increases through intermediate steps, as seen in Fig. 12. In this work, progressive refinement was carried out from a starting 6x6 topology to an intermediate 12 × 12 topology to a final 24 × 24 topology which was used to define the agent’s proposed optimal topology for each load case.

![](img/c4a53cbbd40023d194b7887dddf3293b_231_0.png)

Figure 12: Two-step progressive refinement from 6 × 6 to 24 × 24 topology.

Progressive refinement improves computational efficiency by allowing the agent to start taking actions and removing elements in the coarse 6x6 topology until an intermediate volume fraction (VF) or stress-based termination criterion is met. The intermediate termination criteria for the 6 × 6 and 12 × 12 sizes are with respect to a global termination criterion for the 24 × 24 size, as seen in eqs. (11) and (12). The agent begins by removing elements from the 6x6 topology until the initial intermediate termination criterion is met.

Next, the agent interacts with an equivalent 12 × 12 topology and continues to remove elements until a second intermediate termination criterion is met. Finally, the agent interacts with the equivalent 24 × 24 topology until the final, user-specified termination criterion is satisfied. An example of this sequence is represented in Fig. 13, where the final termination criterion is $VF_{24\times24}=0.25$. It should be noted that Eqs. (11) and (12) would need to be adjusted if the final topology refinement were to extend to 48x48 or 96x96 and should be addressed as future work.

$VF_{6\times6} = 1 - \left( \frac{1 - VF_{24\times24}}{2.5} \right)$ (11)

$VF_{12\times12} = 1 - \left( \frac{1 - VF_{24\times24}}{1.5} \right)$ (12)

![](img/c4a53cbbd40023d194b7887dddf3293b_232_0.png)

Figure 13: Sequential element removal using progressive refinement.

The sequential decrease in allowable VF follows the work that found that the optimization method works better by gradually tightening the criteria with increasing refinement.

Removing a single coarse element from the 6 × 6 topology is equivalent to removing the 4 or 16 corresponding elements from the 12 × 12 or 24 × 24 topologies, respectively.

Therefore, the agent interacts with the intermediate 6 × 6 topology to produce a general, non-detailed shape that can be formed into an equivalent 12 × 12 topology. The agent can begin to produce low-level details at the 12 × 12 topology but will ultimately achieve its sufficiently detailed optimal design at the 24 × 24 topology size.
