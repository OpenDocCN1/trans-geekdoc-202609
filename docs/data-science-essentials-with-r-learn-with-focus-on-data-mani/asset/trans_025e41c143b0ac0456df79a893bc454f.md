

# Descriptive Analytics Empowers Business Decisions

A pizza chain can gain valuable insights by analyzing its pizza sales data, enabling data-driven decisions to boost sales. For example, they can create targeted promotions or offers for the most popular pizzas, invest in improving their online and mobile ordering platforms, and increase staffing during peak hours to improve in-store sales. With descriptive analytics, the pizza chain can better understand customer preferences, identify sales trends, and make informed decisions to drive business growth.

Great! Now let's see how descriptive analytics can be a valuable tool for marketing teams to analyze and evaluate the performance of their marketing campaigns.

Marketing teams can gain insights into the behavior and preferences of their target audience by examining click-through rates and social media engagement. For instance, a marketing team launches a social media ad campaign to promote a new product. By using descriptive analytics, the team can track metrics such as ad impressions, click-through rates, and conversion rates. They can also monitor social media followers, likes, comments, and shares to gauge audience engagement.

Based on these metrics, the marketing team can identify which aspects of the campaign were successful and which areas need improvement. For example, they might find that a specific type of social media post (e.g., video) garners more engagement than others, or that conversion rates are higher when the campaign targets a specific demographic segment.

Descriptive analytics can also be used to segment the target audience based on demographic, geographic, and psychographic variables. By analyzing data on customer behavior and preferences, marketing teams can create more targeted and personalized campaigns to improve conversion rates.

For example, suppose a pizza chain with 2,000 locations across multiple channels wants to create a marketing campaign to promote a new vegan pizza line. Using descriptive analytics, the marketing team can segment the target audience based on factors such as age, location, dietary preferences, and past purchase behavior. They can then create targeted campaigns for each segment and track the performance of each campaign. This helps the team optimize campaigns and maximize return on investment.

Descriptive analytics provides valuable insights into business operations and is often presented through data visualizations. Dashboards can be developed based on these descriptive analytics, allowing stakeholders to track progress toward goals and spot any anomalies. While descriptive analytics can stand alone as a useful tool, it is also a critical foundation for future analytics. It forms the backbone of daily reporting for many businesses and can be applied across various domains, such as annual revenue reports, inventory, and sales data. These reports are easy to compile and provide a clear snapshot of company operations.

# Diagnostic Analytics

Diagnostic analytics builds upon descriptive analytics and goes a step further to answer the question, "Why did this happen?" This analysis attempts to pinpoint the root cause of a specific trend or issue. It often involves comparing coexisting trends or movements, discovering correlations between variables, and determining causal relationships where possible. Diagnostic analytics helps business users understand why things happened and provides valuable insights into the underlying factors leading to a specific trend or issue. For companies that collect customer data, diagnostic analytics plays a crucial role in understanding the motivations behind customer behavior. These insights can be used to improve products and user experience (UX), fine-tune brand messaging, and ensure products stay aligned with the target audience.

Here are some potential diagnostic analytics questions with examples:

*   **Why did logins decrease last month?**

    By analyzing the data, we might discover that the drop in platform usage was due to a higher-than-normal rate of errors during the login process. We can use diagnostic analytics to drill down into the root cause of these errors, identifying the specific factors causing them, such as mandatory password resets, updated security requirements, or technical issues with the platform. Addressing these underlying issues can improve the login process, enhance the overall customer experience, and ultimately increase customer engagement and retention on the platform.

Suppose a mobile banking platform offers a cash-back rewards program for customers who make at least 10 transactions per month. The marketing team wants to analyze the effectiveness of this program and its impact on customer behavior.

Using diagnostic analytics, the team can ask the following questions:

*   Did the cash-back program lead to an increase in customer transaction volume?
*   Are specific types of transactions more likely to result in customers making 10 or more transactions per month?
*   Are there specific customer segments more likely to take advantage of the cash-back program?

By analyzing transaction data and other customer data, the team might discover that certain types of transactions (such as bill payments or online shopping) are more likely to drive customers to make at least 10 transactions per month. They might also find that certain customer segments (such as younger or tech-savvy customers) are more likely to engage with the cash-back program.

By answering these questions, the team can make data-driven decisions to optimize the cash-back program to encourage more transactions and drive revenue growth for the mobile banking platform.

A use case for diagnostic analytics is determining the reasons behind product demand. Continuing with the pizza chain example:

**Why did online orders decrease last quarter?**

*   Technical issues on the website or mobile app caused customer frustration, leading to reluctance to use the platform.
*   Reduced marketing efforts for the online channel compared to other channels, such as in-store promotions or targeted advertising.
*   A decrease in the quality or variety of menu items available for online ordering compared to in-store purchases, leading to reduced customer interest.

Other questions the team might ask include:

*   Why do a few specific stores have the lowest sales?
*   How do promotions and offers affect overall sales?
*   What factors influence repeat customer purchase frequency?
*   What causes a high number of returns or complaints for certain pizza toppings?
*   Why are in-store sales lower during certain times of the day?

Now, let's look at a few examples of questions a marketing team might ask when using diagnostic analytics to evaluate their campaigns:

*   Why is the click-through rate (CTR) for our Facebook ads lower than expected?
*   Which specific elements of our landing page drive higher conversion rates?
*   Why do customers add items to their cart on our e-commerce site but not complete the checkout process?
*   What are the common characteristics of customers who churn after signing up for our subscription service?
*   Which elements of our social media strategy drive higher engagement, and which fail to resonate with our audience?

Thus, we have seen how diagnostic analytics allows you to uncover the underlying causes and factors leading to data trends.
