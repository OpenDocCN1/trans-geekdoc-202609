To generate Peggy's public and private keys, a trusted arbitrator chooses a number v, where v is a quadratic residue mod n. In other words, choose v such that x² = v [mod] has a solution and v⁻¹ mod n exists. This is Peggy's public key. Then calculate the smallest s for which s = sqrt(v⁻¹) [mod] n. This is Peggy's private key. The identification protocol can now proceed.

+   1) Peggy picks a random r, where r is less than n. She computes x = r² mod n, and sends x to Victor.
2) Victor picks a random bit, b.
3) If b = 0, Peggy sends Victor r. If b = 1, Peggy sends Victor y = r · s mod n.
4) If b = 0, Victor verifies that x = r² mod n, proving that Peggy knows sqrt [x]. If b = 1, Victor verifies that x = y * v mod n, proving that Peggy knows sqrt [v⁻¹].

This is a single-round—an identification—and Peggy and Victor repeat this protocol t times, until Victor is convinced Peggy knows s. It's a cut-and-choose protocol. If Peggy doesn't know s, she can pick r such that she can fool Victor if he sends a 0; or she can pick s such that she can fool Victor if he sends a 1. She can't both. The odds of fooling Victor once are 50 percent. The odds of fooling him t times are in 2⁻ᵗ.

Another way for Victor to attack the protocol would be to impersonate Peggy. He could initiate the protocol with another verifier, Valerie. In step (1), instead of choosing a random r he would just use an old r that he knows. However, the odds of Valerie choosing the same value for b in step (2) that Victor did in the protocol with Peggy are 1 in 2. So, the odds of his fooling Victor are 50 percent. The odds of fooling him t times are in 2⁻ᵗ.

For this work, Peggy must not reuse r. If she did, and Victor sent Peggy the other random bit in step (2), he would have both of Peggy's responses. Then, from even one of these, he can calculate s and it's all over for Peggy.
