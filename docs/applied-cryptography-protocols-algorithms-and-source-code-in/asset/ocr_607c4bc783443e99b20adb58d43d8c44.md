# Hardware and Software Implementations of DES

Most has been written on efficient hardware and software implementations of the algorithm [997,831,533,534,738,1573,176,271,1572]. At this writing, the recordholder for the fastest DES chip is a prototype developed at Digital Equipment Corporation [512]. It supports ECB and CBC modes and is based on a GaAs array of 50,000 transistors. Data can be encrypted and decrypted at a rate of 1 gigabit per second, which translates to 18 million blocks per second. This is impressive. Table 12.9 gives the specifications for some commercial DES chips. Seeking discrepancies between clock speed and rate are due to pipelining in the lab, a chip may have multiple DES engines working in parallel.

The most impressive DES chip is VLSI's 6868 (formerly 'Gatekeeper'). Not only can it perform DES encryption in 8 cycles (prototypes in the lab can do it in 4 cycles), but it can also do ECB triple-DES in 25 clock cycles, and OFB or CBC triple-DES in 35 cycles. A software implementation of DES on an IBM 3090 mainframe can perform 52,000 DES encryptions per second. Most microcomputers are slower, but nonetheless. Table 12.10 [603,793] gives actual results and estimates for various Intel and Motorola microprocessors.

# 12.3 Security of DES

People have long questioned the security of DES [458]. There has been much speculation on the key length, number of iterations, and those of the S-boxes. The S-boxes were particularly mysterious—all those constants, without any apparent reason as why or what they're for. Although IBM claimed that the inner workings were the result of 17 man-years of intensive cryptanalysis, some feared that the NSA based a trapdoor into the algorithm so they would have an easy means of decrypting messages.

The U.S. Select Committee on Intelligence, with top secret-clearances, investigated the matter in 1978. The findings of the committee are classified, but an unclassified summary of those findings excluded the NSA from any improper involvement in the algorithm's design [1552]. "It was said to have convinced IBM that a shorter key was adequate, to have indirectly assisted in the development of the S-box structures and to have certified that the final DES algorithm was, free of any statistical or mathematical weaknesses" [435]. However, since the government never made the details of the investigation publication, many people remained unconvinced.
