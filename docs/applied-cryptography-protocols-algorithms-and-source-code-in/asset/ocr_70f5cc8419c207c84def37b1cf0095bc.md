# CHAPTER 2 Protocol Building Blocks

The protocols in this book are organized as a series of steps. Execution of the protocol proceeds linearly through the steps, unless there are instructions to branch to another step. Each step involves at least one of two: computations by one or more of the parties, or messages sent among the parties.

A cryptographic protocol is a protocol that uses cryptography. The parties may be friends and trust each other implicitly or not trust another to give the correct time of day. A cryptographic protocol involves some simple secrecy. The parties participating in the protocol might want to share parts of their secrets to compute a value, jointly generate a random sequence, convince one another of their identity, or simultaneously sign a contract. The whole point of using cryptography in it is to prevent eavesdropping and cheating. If you have never seen these protocols before, they will radically change your ideas of what mutually distrustful parties can accomplish over a computer network. In general, this can be stated as:

> It should not be possible to do or learn more than what is specified in the protocol.

This is a lot harder than it is. In the next few chapters I discuss a lot of protocols. In some it is possible for one of the participants to cheat the other. Others, it is possible for an eavesdropper to subvert the protocol or learn secret information. Some protocols fail because the designers weren't thorough enough in their requirements definitions. Others fail because their designers weren't thorough enough in their analysis. Like algorithms, it is much easier to prove insecurity than it is to prove security.

# The Purpose of Protocols

In daily life, there are informal protocols for almost everything: ordering goods over the telephone, playing poker, voting in an election. No one thinks about these protocols; how they have evolved over time, everyone knows how to use them, and they work reasonably well.

These days, more and more human interaction takes place over computer networks instead of formal protocols to do the same things people do without thinking. If you moved from one state to another and found a voting that looked completely different from the one you used, you could easily adapt. Computers are not nearly so.

Many face-to-face protocols rely on people's presence to ensure fairness and security. Would you strangle a stranger for cash to buy groceries for? Would you play poker with someone you couldn't see him shuffle and deal? Would you mail the government your secret ballot without some assurance of anonymity?
