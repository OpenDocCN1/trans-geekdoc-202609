data block is split into a left half and a right half. The left half is XORed with the right half to form a new right half. The left and new right go through n rounds [four, initially]. In each round the right half is combined with 16 bits of key material (using function f) and XORed with the left half to form the new right half. The original right half [before round] forms the new left half. After n rounds [remember not to switch the left and right halves after the nth round] the left half is XORed with the right half to form a new right half, and then the left and right halves are concatenated together to form a 64-bit word. The data block is XORed with other 64 bits of key material, and the algorithm terminates.

Function f takes the 32 bits of data and 16 bits of key material and mixes them together. First the data block is broken up into 8-bit chunks, then the chunks are XORed and substituted with each other. Figure 13.4 is a block diagram of function f. The two functions $S_0$ and $S_1$, are defined as:

```
$S_0(a,b) = rotate left two bits $((a+b) \bmod 256)$
$S_1(a,b) = rotate left two bits $((a+b+1) \bmod 256)$
```

The same algorithm can be used for decryption. The only difference is: When decrypting, the key material must be used in the reverse order.
Figure 13.5 is a block diagram of the key-generating function. First the 64-bit key is divided into two halves. The halves are XORed and operated on by function $f_k$ as indicated in the diagram. Figure 13.6 is a block diagram of function $f_k$. The two 32-bit inputs are broken up into 8-bit blocks and combined and substituted as shown. $S_0$ and $S_1$ are defined just as shown. The 16-bit key blocks are then used in the encryption/decryption algorithm.

On a 10 megahertz 80286 microprocessor, an assembly-language implementation of FEAL-32 can encrypt data at a speed of 220 kibits per second. FEAL-64 can encrypt data at a speed of 120 kibits per second [1104].

## 13.5 REDOC

### Cryptanalysis of FEAL

FEAL-4, with four rounds, was successfully cryptanalized with a chosen-plaintext attack in [201] and later demolished in [1132]. This latter attack, by Sean Murphy, was the first differential-cryptanalysis attack and required only 20 plaintexts. The designers retaliated with 8-round FEAL [1436,1437,1108] which Biham and Shamur cryptanalized the SECUROM '89 conference [1424]. Another chosen-plaintext attack, using only 1000 blocks, against FEAL-8 [610] forced the designers to give up their hands and define FEAL-N [1102,1104], with a variable number of rounds (greater than 4, or 8).

Biham and Shamur used differential cryptanalysis against FEAL-N; they could break it more quickly than brute force with fewer than $2^n$ chosen plaintext encryptions for N less than 32 [169]. FEAL-N requires $2^{26}$ chosen plaintexts and $2^{46.5}$ known plaintexts to break. FEAL-8 required 2000 plaintexts and $2^{37.5}$ known plaintexts to break. FEAL-4 could be broken with eight selected plaintexts.

The designers defined FEAL-N, a modification of FEAL, that accepts 128-bit keys (see Figure 13.7) [1103,1104]. Biham and Shamur showed FEAL-N with a 128-bit key is just easy to break as FEAL-N with a 64-bit key, for any value of N [169]. Recently FEAL-N was proposed, which suggests FEAL-N with a dynamic swapping function [1525]. Another attack against FEAL-4, requiring only 1000 known plaintexts, and FEAL-8, requiring only 2000 known plaintexts, was published in [1520]. The best attack is by Mitsuru Matsui and Tomoki Yamaguchi. This is the first of linear cryptanalysis, and can break FEAL-4 with 5 plaintexts, FEAL-6 with 100 known plaintexts and FEAL-8 with $2^8$ known plaintexts. Differential-linear cryptanalysis can break FEAL-8 with 12 chosen plaintexts [62]. Whenever someone discovers a new cryptanalytic attack, he should try it out on FEAL first.

## REDOC

REDOC is another block algorithm, designed by Michael Wood for Cryptech, Inc. [113,400]. It has a 20-bit key and an 80-bit block. REDOC performs all of its manipulations—permutations, substitutions, and key XORs—by bytes, the algorithm is efficient in software. Unlike DES, which has a fixed (almost obsolete) set of permutation and substitution tables (S-boxes, actually), REDOC uses a key-dependent and plaintext-dependent set of tables. REDOC has 10 rounds, each round is a complicated series of manipulations on the block.

Another unique feature in the design is the use of **masks**. These are numbers derived from the key table that are used to select the tables in a given function within a given range. Both the value of the data and the masks are used together to select the function tables. Assuming that brute force is the most efficient means of attack, REDOC II is very secure: $2^{160}$ operations are required to recover the key. Thomas Cusick cryptanalyzed 1 round of REDOC II, but he was unable to extend the attack to multiple rounds [400]. Using differential cryptanalysis, Biham and Shamir were able to successfully cryptanalyze 1 round of REDOC II with 300 chosen-plaintexts [170]. This attack cannot be extended to multiple rounds, but they were able to obtain three mask values after 4 rounds. I know of no other cryptanalysis.

## REDOC III

REDOC III is a streamlined version of REDOC II, also designed by Michael Wood [1615]. It operates on an 80-bit block. The key length is variable and can be as large as 2560 bytes (20,480 bits). The algorithm consists solely of XORing key bytes with message bytes; there are no permutations or substitutions.

The algorithm is easy and fast. On 33 megahertz 80386, the algorithm encrypts data at 2.75 megabits per second. Wood estimates that a VLSI-pipelined design, with a 64-bit data path, would encrypt data at over 1.28 gigabits per second with a 20 megahertz clock.

REDOC III is not secure [1440]. It is vulnerable to differential cryptanalysis. Only $2^{23}$ chosen plaintexts are required to reconstruct both masks.

## 13.6 LOKI

LOKI is Australian and was first presented in 1990 as a potential alternative to DES [273]. It uses a 64-bit block and a 64-bit key. The general structure of the algorithm and key schedule were based on [274,275], and the design of the S-boxes was based on [247]. Using differential cryptanalysis, Biham and Shamir were able to break LOKI with 11 rounds faster than brute force [170]. Lars Knudsen showed that LOKI, with 14 rounds or fewer, is vulnerable to differential cryptanalysis [852,853]. Additionally, if LOKI is implemented with alternate S-boxes, the resulting cipher will probably be vulnerable to differential cryptanalysis.

## LOKI91

In response to these attacks, LOKI's designers went back to the drawing board and revised their algorithm. The result is LOKI91 [272]. (The previous version of LOKI was renamed LOKI89.)

To make the algorithm more resistant to differential cryptanalysis and remove the complementation property, the following changes were made:

- The subkey generation algorithm was changed so the halves swapped every second round, not every round.
- The subkey generation algorithm was changed so that the rotation of the left subkey alternated between 12 and 13 bits to the left.
- The initial and final XOR of the block with the key were eliminated.
- The S-box function was altered to flatten out their profile (to improve their resistance to differential cryptanalysis), and to eliminate any value of x such that f(x) = 0, where f is the combination of the E-, S-, and P-boxes.

## Description of LOKI91

The mechanics of LOKI91 are similar to DES [see Figure 13.8]. The data block is divided into a left half and a right half and goes through 16 rounds, much like DES. In each round, the right half is first XORed with a piece of the key, then sent through an expansion permutation [see Table 13.1].

The 48-bit output is divided into four-12 blocks, and each block is sent through an S-box substitution. The S-box substitution is as follows: Take each 12-bit input, use the 2 leftmost bits to form the number r, and the 8 innermost bits form the number c. The output of the S-box, O, is as follows

```
O(r,c) = c + [(r*17) ⊕ 0xff] mod Pr
```
