This protocol is secure against BOB cheating. For him to cheat, he would have to predict exactly which document ALICE would not examine. The odds of doing this are 1 in n-very good. ALICE knows this and feels confident signing a document that she is not able to examine. With this document, the protocol is the same as the previous completely blinded signature protocol and maintains all of its properties of anonymity.

There is a trick that makes BOB's chance of cheating even smaller. In step [4], ALICE randomly chooses n/2 of the documents to change, and BOB sends the appropriate blinding factors in step [5]. In step [7], ALICE multiplies all of the unchallenged documents and signs the mega-document. In step [8], BOB strips off all the blinding factors. ALICE's signature is acceptable only if is a valid signature of exactly which document BOB will challenge; the odds much smaller than the odds of guessing which document ALICE won't challenge.

BOB has another way to cheat. He can generate two different documents, one that BOB is willing to sign and one that ALICE is not. Then he can find two blinding factors that transform each document into the same blinded document. That is, if ALICE asks to examine the document, BOB gives her the blinding factor that transforms it into benign. If ALICE doesn't see the document and signs it, he uses the blinding factor that transforms it into the malevolent document. While this is theoretically possible, the mathematics of the particular algorithms involved the odds of BOB being able to find such a pair negligibly small. In fact, it can be made as small as the odds of Bob being able to produce the signature on an arbitrary message himself. This issue is discussed further in Section 23.12.

## 5.4 IDENTITY-BASED PUBLIC-KEY CRYPTOGRAPHY

Alice wants to send a secure message to Bob. She doesn't want to get his public key from a key server, she doesn't want to verify some third-party's identity on her public-key certificate; and she doesn't want to store Bob's public key on her own computer. She just wants to send him a secure message.

**Identity-based systems**, sometimes called Non-Interactive Key Sharing (NIKS), solve this problem [1422]. Bob's public key is based on his name and network (or telephone number, or physical address, or whatever). With normal public-key cryptography, Alice needs a certificate that associates Alice's Bob with her identity. With identity-based cryptography, Bob's public key is his identity. This is a really cool idea, and as ideal as you can get for a mail system: If Alice knows Bob's, she can send him secure mail. It makes the cryptography as transparent as possible.

If Alice's private key is compromised, she has to change some aspect of her identity to get another one. A serious problem is designing a system in which a collision of users cannot forge a key.

A lot of work has been done on the mathematics of sorts of schemes—most of it in Japan—which turns out to be infuriatingly complicated to make secure. Many of the proposed solutions involve choosing a random number for each user—in my opinion defeats the real point of the system. Some of the algorithms discussed in Chapters 19 and 20 can be identity-based. For details, algorithms, and cryptanalysis, see [19,1422,891,1022,1515,1202,1196,908,674,1131,1023,1516,1536,1544,63,1210,314,313,1549,1543,993,1517,748,1228]. An algorithm that does not rely on any random numbers is [1035]. The system discussed in [1546,1547,1507] is insecure against a chosen-public-key attack, so is the system proposed as NIKS-TAS [1542,1540,1541,993,375,158]. Honestly, nothing proposed so far practical and secure.

## 5.5 Oblivious Transfer

Cryptographer is desperately trying to factor a bit number, n. He knows it's product of two numbers, but nothing more. This is a problem. If he can't recover the key he'll have to work overtime and he'll miss the game. What do you know? Here comes Alice:

"I happen to know one of the number," she says, and "I'll sell it to you for a dollar." To show she's serious, she uses a bit-commitment and commits to each individually. Bob is interested, but she has only half. Alice is unwilling to lower her price and offers to sell Bob the bits for half. "It'll save you a considerable amount of work," she says. "But do I know that your number is actually a factor? If you show me the number and I verify that it is factor, then I will agree to your terms," says Bob.

They are at an impasse: Alice cannot convince Bob that her number is factor without revealing it, and Bob is worthless. This story, sent by Joe Kilian [83], introduces the concept of oblivious transfer. Alice transmits group of messages to Bob. Bob receives some subset, but Alice has no idea which. This doesn't completely solve the problem, however. After Bob has received half of the bits, Alice has to convince him that the bits she sent are factor of n using a zero-knowledge proof. In the following protocol, Alice will send Bob two messages. Bob will receive one, and Alice will not know which.

- 1 Alice generates two public-key/private-key keys, or keys in all. She sends both public keys to Bob.
- 2 Bob chooses a key in symmetric algorithm (DES, for example). He chooses one of Alice's public keys and encrypts his key with it. He sends the encrypted key to Alice without telling her which public keys he used to encrypt it.
- 3 Alice decrypts Bob's key, once with each of her private keys. In one of the cases, she uses the correct key and successfully decrypts Bob's key. In the other case, she uses the wrong key and only manages to generate a meaningless pile of bits that nonetheless look like random key. Since she does not know the correct plaintext, she has no idea which.
- 4 Alice encrypts both of her messages, each with a different one of the keys she generated in the previous step (one real and one meaningless) and sends both of them to Bob.
- 5 Bob gets one of Alice's messages encrypted with the proper DES key and the other one encrypted with the gibberish key. When Bob decrypts each of them with his key, he can read one of them; the other just looks like gibberish to him.

Bob now has one of the two messages from Alice and Alice does not know which one was able to read successfully. Unfortunately, the protocol stopped here it would be possible for Alice to cheat. Another step is necessary.

- (6) After the protocol is complete and both possible results of the transfer are known, Alice must give Bob her private keys so that he can verify that she did not cheat. After all, she could have encrypted the message with both keys in [4].

At this point, course, Bob can figure out the second message. The protocol is secure against an attack by Alice because she has no way of knowing which of the two DES keys is the real one. They encrypt both, but Bob only recovers one of them—until step (6). It is secure against an attack by Bob, before step (6), he cannot get Alice's private keys to determine the DES key that the other message encrypted. This may still seem like more than a complicated way to flip coins over a modem, but it has extensive implications used in more protocols.

Of course, nothing stops Alice sending Bob two completely useless messages: "Nyah Nyah" and "You sucker." This protocol guarantees Alice sends Bob two messages; it ensures that Bob wants to receive either of them.

Oblivious transfer protocols are found in the literature. Some of them are noninteractive, meaning that Alice can publish two messages and Bob can learn only one of them. He can do this on his own; he does not to communicate with Alice [105].

No one cares about being able to do oblivious transfer in practice, but the notion is an important building block for other protocols. There are many types of oblivious transfer—I have two secrets and you get one; I have nine secrets and you get one; I have one secret that you get with probability 1/2 and so on—they are all equivalent [245,391,395].

## 5.6 OBLIVIOUS SIGNATURES

Honestly, I can't think of a good use for these, but there are two kinds [346]:

- 1. Alice has n different messages. Bob can choose one of the n messages for Alice to sign, and Alice will have no way of knowing which one she signed.
- 2. Alice has one message. Bob can choose one of n keys for Alice to use in signing the message, and Alice will have no way of knowing which key she used.

A neat idea! I'm sure it has a use somewhere.
