# 20.1 Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA)

# Dangers of a Common Modulus

Even though the DSS does not specify a common modulus to be shared by everyone, different implementations may. For example, the Internal Revenue Service is considering using the DSS for the electronic submission of tax returns. What if they require every taxpayer in the country to use a common p and q? Even though the standard doesn't require a common modulus, such an implementation accomplishes the same thing. A common modulus too easily becomes a tempting target for cryptanalysis. It is still too early to tell much about different DSS implementations, but there is some cause for concern.

Gus Simmons discovered a subliminal channel in DSA [1468,1469] [see Section 23.3]. This subliminal channel allows someone to embed a secret message in his signature that can only be read by another person who knows the key. According to Simmons, it is a "remarkable coincidence" the "apparently shortcomings of subliminal channels using the ElGamal scheme can all become over in the DSS, and the DSS "provides the most hospitable setting for subliminal communications discovered to date." NIST and NSA have not commented on this subliminal channel; no one knows if they even knew about it. Since this subliminal channel allows an unscrupulous implement of DSS to leak a piece of the key with each signature, it is important to never use an implementation of DSS if you don't trust the implement.

# Patents

David Kravitz, formerly of the NSA, holds a patent on DSA [897]. According to NIST [538]:

NIST intends to make this DSS technique available world-wide on a royalty-free basis to the public interest. We believe this technique is patentable and that other patents would allow us to access the DSS, but we can give free assurances to such effect in advance of the issuance.

Even so, three holders claim that the DSA infringes on their patents: Diffie-Hellman (see Section 22.1) [718], Merkel-Hellman (see Section 19.2) [720], and Schnorr (see Section 21.3) [1398]. The Schnorr patent is the most troublesome. The other two patents expired in 1997; the Schnorr patent is valid until 2008. The Schnorr algorithm was not developed with government money; unlike the PKP patent, the U.S. government has no right to the Schnorr patent, and Schnorr patented his algorithm worldwide. Even if the U.S. courts rule in favor of DSA, what would the world do? Is an international company go to adopt a patent that may be legal in some countries but infringes on others? This issue will take time to resolve; at the time of this writing it is not resolved in the United States.
