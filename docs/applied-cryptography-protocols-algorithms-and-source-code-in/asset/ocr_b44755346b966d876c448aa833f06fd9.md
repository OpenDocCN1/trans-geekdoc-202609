Factoring numbers is hard. Unfortunately for algorithm designers, it is getting. Even worse, it is getting faster than mathematicians expect. In 1976 Richard Guy wrote: "I shall be surprised if anyone regularly factors numbers of size $10^{100}$ without special form during the present century" [680]. In 1977 Ron Rivest said that a 125-digit number would take 40 quadrillion years [599]. In 1994 a 129-digit number was factored [66]. If there is any lesser in all of this, it is that predictions is foolish.

Table 7.3 shows factoring records over the past dozen years. The fastest algorithm during the time was the quadratic sieve [see Section 11.3].

These numbers are frightening today. It is not uncommon to use 512-bit numbers used in operational systems. Factoring them, and completely compromising their security, is well in the range of possibility: A weekend-long work on the Internet could do.

Computing power is generally measured in mips-years: a one-million-instruction-second running for one year, or about $3 	imes 10^{10}$ instructions. By example, a mips-machine is equivalent to the DEC VAX 11/780. Hence, a mips-year is a VAX 11/780 running for a year, or the equivalent: (A 100 MHz Pentium is about 50 mips machine; a 1800-node Intel Paragon is about 50,000).

The 1983 factorization of a 71-digit number required 0.1 mips-years, the 1994 factorization power resulted from this introduction of distributed computing, using the idle time on a network of workstations. This trend was started by Bob Silverman and fully developed by Arjen Lenstra and Mark Mansfield. The 1983 factorization used 9.5 CPUs on a single Cray X-MP, the 1994 factorization took 5000 mips-years and used idle time on 100 computers around the world for about eight months. Modern factoring methods lend themselves to this kind of implementation.
