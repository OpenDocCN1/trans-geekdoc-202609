Analytic attack requires $2^{55.1}$ operations, and brute force requires $2^{55}$. The consensus is that DES, when implemented properly, is still secure against differential cryptanalysis.

Why is DES so resistant to differential cryptanalysis? Why are the S-boxes optimized to make this attack as difficult as possible? Why are there so many rounds, but no more? Because the designers knew about it. IBM's Don Coppermith recently wrote [373,374]:

> "The design took advantage of certain cryptanalytic techniques, most prominently the technique of 'differential cryptanalysis,' which were not known in the published literature. After discussions with NSA, it was declared that the design consideration would reveal the technique of differential cryptanalysis, a powerful technique that can be used against many ciphers. In this turn would weaken the competitive advantage United States employed over other countries in the field of cryptography."

Adi Shamir responded to, challenging Coppermith that he had not found any other attacks on DES since. Coppermith has chosen to remain silent on that question [1426].

## Related-Key Cryptanalysis

12.3 showed the number of bits the DES key is rotated after round: 2 bits after each round, except for 1 bit after rounds: 2, 9, and 16. Why?

**Related-Key cryptanalysis** is similar to differential cryptanalysis, but it examines the difference between keys. The attack is different from any previously discussed: the cryptanalyst chooses a relationship between a pair of keys, but does not know the keys themselves. Data is encrypted with both keys. In the knownplaintext version, the cryptanalyst knows the plaintext and ciphertext of data encrypted with the two keys. The cryptanalyst gets to choose the plaintext encrypted with the two keys.

A modified DES, where the key is rotated two bits after every round, is less secure. Related-key cryptanalysis can break that variant using $2^{17}$ chosen-key plaintexts or $2^{33}$ chosen-key plaintexts [158,163].

This is not all, but it is interesting for three reasons. One is the first cryptanalytic attack on DES-key generation. This attack is independent of the number of rounds of the cryptographic algorithm; it's just as effective as DES with 16 rounds, 32 rounds, or 1000 rounds. And three, DES is impervious to this attack. The variability in the rotation thwarts related-key cryptanalysis.

## Linear Cryptanalysis

**Linear cryptanalysis** is another type of cryptanalytic attack, invented by Mitsuru Matsui [1016,1015,1017]. This attack uses linear approximations to describe the action of a block cipher (in this case, DES).

This means that if you XOR some of the plaintext bits together, XOR ciphertext bits together, and then XOR the result, you will get a single bit that is the XOR of some of the key bits. This is a linear approximation and will hold with some probability $p$. If $p \neq \frac{1}{2}$, then this bias can be exploited. Collected plaintexts and associated ciphertexts to guess the values of the key bits. The more data you have, the more reliable the guess. The greater the bias, the greater the success rate with the same amount of data.

How do you identify good linear approximations for DES? Find good 1-round linear approximations and join them together. [Again, ignore the initial and final permutations; they do not affect the attack.] Look at the S-boxes. There are 6 input bits and 4 outputs. The input bits can be combined using XOR in 63 useful ways ($2^6-1$), and the output bits can be combined in 15 useful ways. Now, for each S-box you can evaluate the probability that for a randomly chosen input, an XOR combination equals some output XOR combination. If there is a combination with a high bias, then linear cryptanalysis will work.

If the linear approximations are unbiased, then they would hold for 32 of the 64 possible inputs. I spent the pages of tables, the most biased S-box is S-box 5. In fact, the second input bit equal to the XOR of all 4 output bits for only 12 inputs. This translates to a probability of $\Upsilon_6$, or a bias of $\Upsilon_6$, and is the most extreme bias in all the S-boxes. [Shamir noted in [1423], but could not find a way to exploit it]
