## Key Management

Alice and Bob have a secure communications system. They play mental poker.

Eve doesn't have to break the algorithms. She doesn't have to rely on subtle flaws in the protocols. She can use her keys to read all of Alice’s and Bob’s messages without lifting a cryptanalytic finger.

In the real world, key management is the hardest part of cryptography. Designing secure algorithms and protocols isn’t easy, but you can rely on a large body of academic research. Keeping the keys secret is much harder.

Cryptanalysts often attack both symmetric and public-key cryptystems through their key management. Why should Eve go through the trouble of trying to break the cryptographic algorithm if she can recover the key because of sloppy key storage procedures? Why should she spend $10 million building a cryptanalysis machine if she can spend $1000 buying a clerk? Spending a million dollars to a well-placed communications agent in a diplomatic embassy can be a bargain. The sellers used US Navy keys to the Soviets for years. The CIA’s director of counterintelligence went for less than $2 million, wife included. That’s cheaper than building massive cracking machines and hiring brilliant cryptanalysts. Eve can steal the keys. She can arrest or abduct anyone who knows the keys. She can demand and get the keys. It is easier to find flaws in people than to find them in cryptystems.

Alice and Bob must protect their key to the same degree as the data it encrypts. If a key isn’t changed regularly, this can be an enormous amount of data. Frankly, many commercial products simply proclaim “We use DES” and forget about everything else. The results are not very impressive.

For example, the DiskLock program for Macintosh [version 2.1], sold at most software stores, claims the security of DES encryption. It encrypts files using DES. Its implementation of the DES encryption is correct. However, DiskLock stores the DES key with the encrypted file. If you know where to look for the key, and want to recover a file encrypted with DiskLock's DES, recover the key from the encrypted file and then decrypt the file. It doesn't matter that this program uses DES encryption—the implementation is completely insecure.

Further information on key management can be found in [457, 98, 1225, 775, 357]. The following sections discuss some of the issues and solutions.

## 8.1 GENERATING KEYS

The security of an algorithm rests in the key. If you're using a cryptographically weak process to generate keys, then your system is weak. We need not crystallize your encryption algorithm; she can crystallize your key generation algorithm.
