It contains information about Bob—his name, address, and so on—and it is signed by Alice: Trent (widely known as a certification authority, or CA). By signing both the key and the information about Bob, Trent certifies that the information about Bob belongs to Bob. Alice certifies Trent's nature and then uses the public key, secure in the knowledge that it is Bob's and no one's. Certificates play an important role in a number of public-key protocols such as PEM [825] [see Section 24.1] and X.509 [304] [see Section 24.9].

A complicatedcryptographic issue surrounds this type of system. What is the meaning of certification? Or, to put another way, who is trusted to certify whom? Anyone may sign someone else's certificate, but there needs to be some way to filter out questionable certificates: for employees of one company signed by the CA of another company. Normally, a certification chain transfers trust: A single trusted entity certifies agents, trusted agents certify CAs, and company CAs certify their employees. Here are some more things to think about:

+   - What level of trust in someone's identity is implied by his certificate?
- What are the relationships between a person and the CA that certified his public key, and how can those relationships be implied by the certificate?
- Who be trusted to be the “single trusted entity” at the top of the certification chain?
- How long can a certification chain be?

Ideally, Bob would need some kind of authentication procedure before the CA signs his certificate. Additionally, some kind of timestamp or an indication of the certificate's validity is important to guard against compromised keys [461]. Timestamping is not enough. Keys may be invalidated before they have expired, either through compromise or for administrative reasons. Hence, it is important that the CA keeps a list of invalid certificates, and for users to regularly check that list. This revocation problem is still a difficult one to solve.

And public-key/private-key pair is not enough. Certainly any good implementation of public-key cryptography requires separate digital signatures. This allows for different security levels, expiration times, backup procedures, and so on. Someone might sign messages with a 2048-bit key stored on a smart card and for twenty years, while they might use a 768-bit key stored in the computer and for months for encryption.

And a single pair of encryption and signature isn't enough, either. A private key authenticates a relationship as well as an identity, and people have more than one relationship. Alice might want to sign one document Alice the individual, another as Alice, vice-president of Monolith, Inc., and a third as Alice, president of her community organization.
