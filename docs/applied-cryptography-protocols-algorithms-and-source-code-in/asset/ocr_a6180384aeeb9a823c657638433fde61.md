# CHAPTER 7 Key Length

Now, the annual energy output of our sun is about 1.21·10^{44} ergs. This is enough to power 2.7·10^{36} single bit changes on our ideal computer, enough state changes to power a 187-bit counter through all its values. If we built a Dyson sphere around the sun and captured all of its energy for 42 years, without any loss, we could power a computer up to 2^{192}. Of course, it wouldn't have the energy left to perform any useful calculations with this counter.

But that's just one, and a measly one at that! A typical supernova releases 10^{43} ergs (About a hundred times as much energy would be released in the form of neutrinos). If all of this energy could be channeled into a single sorry of computation, a 219-bit counter could be cycled through all of its states.

These numbers have nothing to do with the technology of the devices; they are the maximums that thermodynamics will allow. And they strongly imply that brute-force attacks against 256-bit keys would be infeasible unless computers are built from something other than matter and occupying another space.

# 7.2 PUBLIC-KEY LENGTH

One-way functions were discussed in Section 2.3. Multiplying two large primes is a one-way function; it's easy to multiply the numbers to get a product but hard to factor the product and the two large primes [see Section 11.3]. Public-key cryptography uses this idea to make a trap-door one-way function. Actually, that's a lie; factoring is conjectured to be a hard problem [see Section 11.4]. As far as anyone knows, it seems to be it. Even if it is, no one can prove that hard problems are hard. Everyone assumes that factoring is hard, but it has never been mathematically proven one or the other.

This is worth dwelling. It is easy to imagine 50 years in the future we will all sit around, reminiscing about the old days when people used to think factoring was hard, cryptography and companies actually made money from this stuff. It is easy to imagine that future developments in complexity theory will make factoring easier or that make factoring trivial. There's no reason to believe this will happen—and most people who know enough have no opinion will tell you that it is unlikely—but there is no reason to believe it.

In any case, today's dominant public-key encryption algorithms are based on the difficulty of factoring large numbers that are the product of two primes (Other algorithms are based on something called the Discrete Logarithm Problem, but for the moment assume the same discussion applies.) These algorithms are also susceptible to a brute-force attack, but of a different type. Breaking these does not involve trying every possible key; breaking these algorithms involves factoring the large number (or taking discrete logarithms in a very large finite field—a similar problem). If the number is too small, you have no security.
