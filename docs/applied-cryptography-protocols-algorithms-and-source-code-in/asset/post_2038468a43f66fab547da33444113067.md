## Identification Schemes

### 21.1 FEIGE-FIAT-SHAMIR

Amos Fiat's and Adi Shamir's authentication and digital signature scheme is discussed in [566,567]. Uriel Feige, Fiat, and Shamir modified the algorithm to a zero-knowledge proof of identity [544,545]. This is the best-known zero-knowledge proof of identity.

## Simplified Feige-Fiat-Shamir Identification Scheme

Before using any private keys, the arbitrator chooses a random modulus, $n$, which is the product of two large primes. In real life, $n$ should be at least 512 bits long and probably closer to 1024 bits. This $n$ can be shared among a group of provers. (Choosing a Blum integer makes computation easier, but it is not required for security.)

To generate Peggy's public and private keys, a trusted arbitrator chooses a number $v$, where $v$ is a quadratic residue mod $n$. In other words, choose $v$ such that $x^2 = v \pmod{n}$ has a solution and $v^{-1} \pmod{n}$ exists. This is Peggy's public key. Then calculate the smallest $s$ for which $s = \sqrt{v^{-1}} \pmod{n}$. This is Peggy's private key. The identification protocol can now proceed.

- 1) Peggy picks a random $r$, where $r$ is less than $n$. She computes $x = r^2 \pmod{n}$, and sends $x$ to Victor.
- 2) Victor picks a random bit, $b$.
- 3) If $b = 0$, Peggy sends Victor $r$. If $b = 1$, Peggy sends Victor $y = r \cdot s \pmod{n}$.
- 4) If $b = 0$, Victor verifies that $x = r^2 \pmod{n}$, proving that Peggy knows $\sqrt{x}$. If $b = 1$, Victor verifies that $x = y \cdot v \pmod{n}$, proving that Peggy knows $\sqrt{v^{-1}}$.

This is a single-round—an identification—and Peggy and Victor repeat this protocol $t$ times, until Victor is convinced Peggy knows $s$. It's a cut-and-choose protocol. If Peggy doesn't know $s$, she can pick $r$ such that she can fool Victor if he sends a 0; or she can pick $s$ such that she can fool Victor if he sends a 1. She can't both. The odds of fooling Victor once are 50 percent. The odds of fooling him $t$ times are in $2^{-t}$.

Another way for Victor to attack the protocol would be to impersonate Peggy. He could initiate the protocol with another verifier, Valerie. In step (1), instead of choosing a random $r$ he would just use an old $r$ that he knows. However, the odds of Valerie choosing the same value for $b$ in step (2) that Victor did in the protocol with Peggy are 1 in 2. So, the odds of his fooling Victor are 50 percent. The odds of fooling him $t$ times are in $2^{-t}$.

For this work, Peggy must not reuse $r$. If she did, and Victor sent Peggy the other random bit in step (2), he would have both of Peggy's responses. Then, from even one of these, he can calculate $s$ and it's all over for Peggy.

## Feige-Shamir Identification Scheme

In their papers [544,545], Feige, Fiat and Sham show how parallel construction can increase the number of accreditations per round and reduce Peggy and Victor's interactions.

First generate $n$ as in the example, first choose $k$ different numbers: $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k$ where each $v_i$ is a quadratic residue mod $n$. In other words, choose $v_i$, such that $x^2 = v_i \pmod{n}$ has a solution and $v_i^{-1} \pmod{n}$ exists. This string, $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k$, is the public key. Then calculate the smallest $s_i$ such that $s_i = \sqrt{v_i^{-1}} \pmod{n}$. This string, $s_1, s_2, \dots, s_k$, is the private key.

And the protocol is:

- (1) Peggy picks a random $r$, when $r$ is less than $n$. She computes $x = r^2 \pmod{n}$, and sends $x$ to Victor.
- (2) Victor sends Peggy a random binary string $k$-bits long: $b_1, b_2, \dots, b_k$.
- (3) Peggy computes $y = r \cdot [s_{b_1} \cdot s_{b_2} \cdot \dots \cdot s_{b_k}] \pmod{n}$. (She multiplies together whoever of $s_i$, that correspond to $b_i = 1$. If Victor's first bit is a 1, then $s_1$ is part of the product; if Victor's first is a 0, then $s_1$ is not part of the product, and so on.) Peggy sends $y$ to Victor.
- (4) Victor verifies $x = y^2 \cdot [v_{b_1} \cdot v_{b_2} \cdot \dots \cdot v_{b_k}] \pmod{n}$. (He multiplies together the values of $v_i$, based on the random binary string. If his first bit is a 1, then $v_1$ is part of the product, $v_1$ is not part, and so on.)

Peggy and Victor repeat this protocol $t$ times, until Victor is convinced Peggy knows $s_1, s_2, \dots, s_t$.

The chance that Peggy can find Victor is 1 in $2^t$. The author recommends a 1 in $2^{80}$ chance of a cheater fooling Victor and suggests that $k=5$ and $t=4$. If you are paranoid, increase these numbers.

## An Example

Let's look at this protocol in action with small numbers. If $n=35$ (the two primes are 5 and 7), then the possible quadratic residues are:

- $1^2 = 1 \pmod{35}$ has the solutions: $x=1, 6, 29$, or $34$.
- $4^2 = 4 \pmod{35}$ has the solutions: $x=2, 12, 23$, or $33$.
- $9^2 = 9 \pmod{35}$ has the solutions: $x=3, 17, 18$, or $32$.
- $11^2 = 11 \pmod{35}$ has the solutions: $x=9, 16, 19$, or $26$.
- $14^2 = 14 \pmod{35}$ has the solutions: $x=4, 11, 24$, or $31$.
- $15^2 = 15 \pmod{35}$ has the solutions: $x=5$ or $30$.
- $25^2 = 25 \pmod{35}$ has the solutions: $x=7$ or $28$.
- $29^2 = 29 \pmod{35}$ has the solutions: $x=8, 13, 22$, or $27$.
- $30^2 = 30 \pmod{35}$ has the solutions: $x=10$ or $25$.

The inverses (mod 35) and their square roots are:

| $v$ | $v^{-1}$ |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1 |
| 4 | 9 |
| 9 | 4 |
| 11 | 16 |
| 16 | 11 |
| 29 | 29 |
| 30 | 8 |

Note that 14, 15, 21, 25, and 30 do not have inverses mod 35, because they are not relatively prime to 35. This makes sense, because there should be (5 - 1) \cdot (7 - 1)/4 quadratic residues mod 35 relatively prime to 35: that is gcd(x,35) = 1 [see Section 11.3].

So, Peggy gets the public key consisting of k=4 values: [4,11,16,29]. The corresponding private key is [3,4,9,8]. Here's one run of the protocol:

- (1) Peggy chooses a random r = 16, computes 16² mod 35 = 11, and sends it to Victor.
- (2) Victor sends Peggy a random binary string [1,1,0,1].
- (3) Peggy computes 16 • (3') • (4') • (9') mod 35 and sends it to Victor.
- (4) Victor verifies that 31' • (4') • (16') • (29') mod 35 = 11.

Peggy and Victor repeat the protocol times, each with a different r until Victor is satisfied.

With small values like these, there is no real security. When n is 512 bits or more, Victor cannot learn anything about Peggy's secret key except the fact that she knows it.

## Enhancements

It is possible to embed identification information into the protocol. Assume that I is a binary string Peggy's identification: name, address, social security number, hat size, preferred brand of soft drink, and other personal information. Use a one-way hash H(x) to compute H(I), where j is a small number onto I. Find a set of j where H(I) is a quadratic residue mod n. H[I]j generates v₁, v₂, ..., vⱼ (the are not quadratic residues). Peggy's public key is now I and the list of j. She sends I and the list of j to Victor before step of the protocol.

Now, after Victor successfully completes the protocol with Peggy, he is assured the factorization of the modulus, has certified the association between I and Peggy by giving her the square roots of v derived from I. (See Section 2.3 for background information).

Feige, and Shamir include the following implementation remarks [544,545]:

For imperfect hash functions, it may be advisable to randomize I by concatenating it with a long random string R. This string is chosen by the arbitrator and is revealed to Victor with I.

In typical implementations, k should be between 1 and 18. Larger values of k reduce the time and communication complexity by reducing the number of rounds.
