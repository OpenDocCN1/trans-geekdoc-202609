Off-line systems, like Protocol #4, require no communication between the merchant and the bank until the transaction between the merchant and the customer. These systems do not prevent Alice from cheating, but instead detect her cheating. Protocol #4 detected her cheating by making Alice's identity known if she tried to cheat. Alice can ensure that this will happen, so she doesn't cheat.

Another way is to create a special smart card ([332,341,387]) containing a tamper-proof chip. The observer chip keeps a mini database of all the pieces of digital cash by that smart card. If Alice attempts to copy some digital cash and spend it, the embedded observer chip would detect the attempt and not allow the transaction. Alice cannot erase the mini-database without permanently damaging the smart card. In [121], Tatsuki Okamoto and Kazuo Ohta list six properties of an ideal digital cash system:

+   1. The security of the digital cash is not dependent on any physical location. The cash can be transferred through computer networks.
2. Security. The digital cash cannot be copied and reused.
3. Privacy (Untraceability). The privacy of the user is protected, no one can trace the relationship between the user and his purchases.
4. Off-line Payment. When a user pays for a purchase with electronic cash, the protocol between the user and the merchant is executed off-line. That is, the shop does not need to be linked to a host to process the user's payment.

+   5. Transferability. The digital cash can be transferred to other users.
6. Divisibility. A piece of digital cash in a given amount can be subdivided into smaller pieces of cash in smaller amounts. (Of course, everyone has to total up properly in the end.)

The protocols previously discussed satisfy properties 1, 2, 3, and 4, but not 5 and 6. Some on-line digital cash system satisfies all properties except 4 ([318,413,1243]).
