Bob now has one of the two messages from Alice and Alice does not know which one was able to read successfully. Unfortunately, the protocol stopped here it would be possible for Alice to cheat. Another step is necessary.

+   (6) After the protocol is complete and both possible results of the transfer are known, Alice must give Bob her private keys so that he can verify that she did not cheat. After all, she could have encrypted the message with both keys in [4].

At this point, course, Bob can figure out the second message. The protocol is secure against an attack by Alice because she has no way of knowing which of the two DES keys is the real one. They encrypt both, but Bob only recovers one of them—until step (6). It is secure against an attack by Bob, before step (6), he cannot get Alice's private keys to determine the DES key that the other message encrypted. This may still seem like more than a complicated way to flip coins over a modem, but it has extensive implications used used in more protocols.

Of course, nothing stops Alice sending from Bob two completely useless messages: "Nyah Nyah" and "You sucker." This protocol guarantees Alice sends Bob of two messages; it ensures that Bob wants to receive either of them.

Oblivious transfer protocols are found in the literature. Some of them are noninteractive, meaning that Alice can publish two messages and Bob can learn only one of them. He can do this on his own; he does not to communicate with Alice [105].

No one cares about being able to do oblivious transfer in practice, but the notion is an important building block for other protocols. There are many types of oblivious transfer—I have two secrets and you get one; I have nine secrets and you get one; I have one secret that you get with probability 1/2 and so on—they are all equivalent [245,391,395].

Honestly, I can't think of a good use for these, but there are two kinds [346]:

+   1. Alice has n different messages. Bob can choose one of the n messages for Alice to sign, and Alice will have no way of knowing which one she signed.

+   2. Alice has one message. Bob can choose one of n keys for Alice to use in signing the message, and Alice will have no way of knowing which key she used.
