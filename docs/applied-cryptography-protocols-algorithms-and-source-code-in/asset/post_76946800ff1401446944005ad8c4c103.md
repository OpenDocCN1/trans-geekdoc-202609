
## 5.2 Zero-Knowledge Proofs of Identity

### The Multiple Identity Fraud

There are other possible abuses to zero-knowledge proofs of identity, also discussed in [485,120]. In some implementations, there is no check when an individual registers a public key. Hence Alice can have several private keys, and therefore, identities. This can be a great help if she wants to commit tax fraud. Alice can also commit a crime and disappear. First, she creates and publishes several identities. One of them she doesn't use. Then, she uses that identity and commits a crime so that the person she identifies is the witness. The witness knows the identity of the person who committed the crime, but if Alice never uses that identity again—she's untraceable.

To prevent this, there must be some mechanism by which each person has only one identity. [120] The authors suggest the bizarre of tamperproof babies who are impossible to clone and contain a unique number as their genetic code. They also suggested having each baby have an identity at birth. (Actually, the parents would have to do this as the baby would be otherwise occupied.) This could be easily abused; parents could use multiple identities for the child's birth. In the end, the uniqueness of an individual is based on trust.

### Renting Passports

Alice wants to travel to Zaire, but government won't give her a visa. Carol offers to rent her identity to Alice. [Bob offered first, but there were some obvious problems.] Carol sells her private key and Alice goes off to Zaire pretending to be Carol.

Carol has not only paid for her identity, but now she has a perfect alibi. She commits a crime while Alice is “Carol” has proved her identity in Zaire; how could she commit a crime back home?

Of course, Alice is free to commit crimes as well. She does so either if she leaves or after returns, near Carol's home. First she identifies herself as Carol [she has Carol's private key, so she can easily do that]; then she commits a crime and runs away. The police will be looking for Carol. Carol will claim her identity to Alice, but who would believe such a nonsensical story?

The problem is that Alice is not really proving her identity; she is proving that she knows a piece of secret information. It is the link between information and the person it belongs to that being abused. The tamperproof solution would protect this kind of fraud, as a police state where all citizens would have to prove their identity very frequently (at the end of each day, at each street corner, etc.). Biometric methods—fingerprints, retinal scanning, voiceprints, and so on—may help solve this problem.

### Proofs of Membership

Alice wants to prove to Bob that she is a member of some super-secret organization, but she does not want to reveal her identity. This problem is similar but different from proving identity, and has also been studied [87,906,907,1201,1445]. Some solutions are related to the problem of group signatures (see Section 4.6).

## CHAPTER 5 Advanced Protocols

## 5.3 BLIND SIGNATURES

An essential feature of digital signature protocols is that the signer knows what he is signing. It is a good idea, except when we want the reverse.

We might want people to sign documents without ever seeing their contents. There are ways that a signer can almost, but not exactly, know what he is signing. By first things first.

### Completely Blind Signatures

Bob is a notary public. Alice wants to sign a document, but does not want him to have any idea what he is signing. Bob doesn't care what the document says, he is just justifying that he notarized it at a certain time. He is willing to go along with Bob.

- (1) Alice takes the document and multiplies it by a random value. This random value is a blinding factor.
- (2) Alice sends the blinded document to Bob.
- (3) Bob signs the blind document.
- (4) Alice divides out the blinding factor, leaving the original document signed by Bob.

This protocol only works if the signature function and multiplication are commutative. If they are not, there are other ways to modify the document other than multiplying. Some relevant algorithms appear in Section 23.12. For now, assume that the operation is possible and all the math works.

Can Bob cheat? He can collect any information about the document that he is signing. If the blinding factor is truly random and makes the blinded document truly random, he cannot cheat. The document Bob signs in step (2) looks nothing like the document Alice began. Bob's signature in step (3) looks like the signed document at the end of step (4). Even if Bob gets his hands on the document, with his signature, after completing the protocol, he cannot prove [to himself or anyone else] that he signed it in particular. He knows his signature is valid. He can verify his signature. However, there is no way for him to correlate any information received during the signing protocol with the signed document. If he signed a million documents using this protocol, he would have no way of knowing which instance he signed which document.

The properties of completely blind signatures are:

- (1) Bob's signature on the document is valid. The signature is a proof that Bob signed the document. It will convince Bob that he signed the document if it is ever shown to him. It also has all the other properties of digital signatures discussed in Section 2.6.
- (2) Bob cannot correlate the signed document with the act of signing the document. Even if he keeps records of every blind signature he makes, he cannot determine when he signed any given document.

### Blind Signatures

With the completely blind signature protocol, Alice can have Bob sign anything; "Bob owes Alice a million dollars," "Bob owes Alice his first-born child," "Bob owes Alice a bag of chocolates. The possibilities are endless. This protocol is not useful in many applications.

However, there is a way that Bob can know what he is signing, while still maintaining the useful properties of a blind signature. The heart of this protocol is the cut-and-choose technique. Consider this example. Many people enter this country every day, and the Department of Immigration want to make sure they are not smuggling cocaine. The officials could search everyone, but instead they use a probabilistic solution. They will search one-tenth of the people coming in. One person in ten has belongings inspected, the other nine get untouched. Chronic smugglers will get away with their misdeeds most of the time, but they have a ten percent chance of getting caught. And if the court system is effective, the penalty for getting caught could more than wipe out the gains from the other side.

If the Department of Immigration want to increase the odds of smuggling, they have want to decrease the odds, they have to search fewer people. By manipulating the probabilities, they can control how successful the protocol is.

The blind signature protocol works in a similar manner. Bob will be given a large pile of blind documents. He will open, that is examine, all but one and then sign the last.

Think of the blind document as being in an envelope. The process of blinding the document is putting the document in an envelope and the process of removing the blinding factor is opening the envelope. When the document is in an envelope, nobody can read it. The document is signed by a piece of carbon paper in the envelope: when the signer signs through the envelope, his signature goes through the carbon paper and signs the document below.

This scenario involves a group of counterintelligence agents. Their identities are secret; even the counterintelligence agents know who they are. The agency's director wants to give each agent a signed document stating: "The bearer of this signed document (insert agent's cover name), has diplomatic immunity." Each agent has his own list of cover names, so the agency can't hand out signed documents. The agents do not want to send their cover names to the agency; the enemy might have corrupted the agency's computer. On the other hand, the agency doesn't want to blindly sign any document an agent gets. A clever agent might substitute a message like: "Agent (name) has retired and collects a million-dollar-a-year pension. Signed, Mr. President."
