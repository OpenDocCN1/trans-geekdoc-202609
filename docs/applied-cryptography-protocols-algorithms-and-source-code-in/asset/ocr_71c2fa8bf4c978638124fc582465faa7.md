The most interesting aspect of PGP is its distributed approach to key management (sec Section 8.12). There are no key certification authorities, PGP instead supports a "web of trust." Every user generates and distributes his public key. Users sign each other's public keys, creating an interconnected community of PGP users.

For example, Alice might give her public key to Bob. Bob knows Alice, so he signs her public key. He then gives the signed key back to her and makes a copy for himself. When Alice wants to communicate with Carol, Alice sends a copy of the key Bob signed. Carol, who already has Bob's public key (she got it at some other time) and trusts Bob to certify other people's keys, verifies her signature on Alice's key and accepts it as valid. Bob introduced Alice to Carol.

PGP does not specify a policy for establishing trust; users are free to decide who they trust and who do not. PGP provides mechanisms for associating trust with public keys and using trust. Each user keeps a collection of signed keys in a file called a public-key ring. Each key in the ring has a legitimacy field that indicates the degree to which the particular user trusts the validity of the key. The higher the trust level, the more the user believes the key is legitimate. A signature trust field measures how far the user trusts the signer to certify the public keys of other users. And finally, an owner trust field indicates the degree to which the particular user trusts their own owner to sign keys, this field is set manually by the user. PGP continuously updates these fields as users supply new information.

Alice has signed Bob's, Carol's, Dave's, Ellen's, and Frank's keys. She trusts Bob and Carol to sign other people's keys, and she trusts Dave and Ellen to sign other people's public keys, and she does not trust Gail.

Two partially signed signatures may be sufficient to certify a key. Alice believes that Kurt's key is legitimate because both Dave and Ellen have signed it. It is not automatic in PGP; Alice can set her own owner paranoia level.

Just because Alice believes a key to be valid, she does not have to trust it other people's keys. She does not trust Frank to sign other people's keys, even though she signed him herself. And she does not trust Ivan's signature on Martin's key, or Kurt's signature on Nancy's.

Owen's key doesn't fit into the web anywhere; perhaps Alice got it from a key server. PGP does not assume that the key is valid; Alice must either declare the key valid or trust one of the key's sign.

Of course, nothing prevents Alice from using keys she does not trust. PGP's job is to alert Alice that the key is not trusted, not to prevent communications.
