
Most of those applications used lousy cryptography, but successful attacks against them had nothing to do with cryptanalysis. They involved crooked employees, clever sting operations, stupid implementations, integration blunders, and random idiocy. [I strongly recommend Ross Anderson's paper, "Why Cryptystems Fail" [44], it should be required for anyone in this field.] Even the NSA has admitted that most security failures in its area of interest are due to failures in implementation, and not failures in algorithms or protocols [1119]. In these instances it didn't matter how good the cryptography was, the successful attacks bypassed completely.

## 10.1 CHOOSING AN ALGORITHM

When it comes to evaluating and choosing algorithms, people have several alternatives:

- They can choose a published algorithm, based on the belief that a published algorithm has been scrutinized by many cryptographers; if no one has broken the algorithm yet, then it must be pretty good.
- They can trust a manufacturer, based on the belief that a well-known manufacturer has a reputation to uphold and is unlikely to risk reputation by selling programs or inferior algorithms.
- They can trust a private consultant, based on the belief that an impartial consultant is best equipped to make a reliable evaluation of different algorithms.
- They can trust the government, based on the belief that the government is trustworthy and wouldn't steer its citizens wrong.
- They can write their own algorithms, based on the belief that their cryptographic ability is second-to-none and that they should trust nobody but themselves.

Any of these alternatives is problematic, but the first seems to be the most sensible. Putting your trust in a single manufacturer, consultant, or government is asking for trouble. People who call themselves security consultants (even those from big-name firms) usually don't know anything about encryption. Most security product manufacturers are no better. The NSA has some of the world's best cryptographers working for it, but they're not telling all they know. They have their own interests which are not congruent with those of their citizens. And even if you're a genius, writing your own algorithm and using it without any peer review is just plain foolish.

The algorithms in this book are public. Many have appeared in the open literature and many have been cryptanalysed by experts in the field. I list all published results, both positive and negative. I don't have access to the cryptanalysis done by any of the myriad military security organizations in the world [which are probably better than the academic institutions—they've been doing longer and are better funded]; so it is possible that these algorithms are easier to break than it appears. Even so, it is far more likely that they are more secure than an algorithm designed and implemented in secret some corporate basement.

The hole in all this reasoning is that we don't know the abilities of the various military cryptanalysis organizations.

What algorithms can the NSA break? For the majority of us, there's really no way of knowing. If you are arrested with a DES-encrypted computer hard drive, the FBI is unlikely to introduce the decrypted plaintext at your trial; the fact that they break an algorithm is often a better secret than any information that is recovered. During WWII, the Allies were forbidden from using decrypted German Ultra traffic unless they could plausibly gotten the information elsewhere. The only way to get the NSA to admit the ability to break an algorithm is to encrypt something so valuable that its publication warrants the admission. Or, better yet, create a funny joke and send it via e-mail to shady characters in shadowy countries. NSA employees are people, too; I don't think they can keep a good secret.

A good working assumption is that the NSA can read any message that it chooses, the NSA is limited by resources, and has to pick and choose among its targets. Another good assumption is that they prefer breaking knuckles to breaking codes; this preference is so strong that they will only resort to breaking codes when they wish to preserve the secret that they have read the message.

In case, the best of us can do is to choose among public algorithms that have withstood a reasonable amount of public scrutiny and cryptanalysis.

## Algorithms for Export

Algorithms for export out of the U.S. government must be approved by the U.S. government (see Section 25.1). It is widely believed that these export-approved algorithms can be broken by the NSA. Although no one has admitted this on the record, these are some of the things the NSA is rumored to privately suggest to companies wishing to export their cryptographic products:

- Leak a key bit once in a while, embedded in the ciphertext.
- "Dumb down" to something in the 30-bit range. For example, while the algorithm might accept a 100-bit key, most of those keys might be equivalent.
- Use a fixed IV, or a fixed header at the beginning of each encrypted message. This facilitates a known-plaintext attack.
- Generate a few random bytes, encrypt them with the key, and then put the plaintext and the ciphertext of these random bytes at the beginning of the encrypted message. This also facilitates a known-plaintext attack.

NSA gets a copy of the source code, but the algorithm's details remain secret to everyone else. Certainly no one advertises any of these deliberate weaknesses, but beware if you buy a U.S. encryption product that has been approved for export.

## 10.2 PUBLIC-KEY CRYPTOGRAPHY VERSUS SYMMETRIC CRYPTOGRAPHY

Which is better, public-key cryptography or symmetric cryptography? This question doesn't make any sense, but it has been debated since public-key cryptography was invented. The debate assumes that the two types of cryptography can be compared on an equal footing. They can't.

Needham and Schroeder [1159] pointed out that the number and length of messages are greater with public-key algorithms than symmetric algorithms. Their conclusion that the symmetric algorithm was more efficient than the public-key algorithm. While true, this analysis overlooks the significant security benefits of public-key cryptography. Whitfield Diffie writes [492,494]:

In viewing public-key cryptography as a form of cryptosystem rather than a form of key management, I set the criticism on grounds of both security and performance. Opponents were to point out that the RSA system about one-thousand fast as DES and required keys about ten times as large. Although it had been obvious from the beginning that the use of public keys be limited to exchanging keys for conventional [symmetric] systems hy was not immediately clear that this was necessary. In this context, the proposal to build hybrid systems [879] was hailed as a discovery in its own

Public-key cryptography and symmetric cryptography are different sorts of animals; they solve different sorts of problems. Symmetric cryptography is best for encrypting data. It is orders of magnitude faster and not susceptible to chosen-ciphertext attacks. Public-key cryptography can do things that symmetric cryptography can't; it is best for key management and a myriad of protocols discussed in Part I: one-way hash functions, message authentication codes, and so on.
