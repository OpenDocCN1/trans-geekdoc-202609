## Digital Cash and the Perfect Crime

Digital cash has its dark side, sometimes people don't want so much privacy. Watch Alice the perfect crime [1575]:

- (1) Alice kidnaps a baby.
- (2) Alice prepares 10,000 money orders for $1000 [or as many as she wants for whatever denomination she wants].
- (3) Alice binds all 10,000 money orders, using a blind signature protocol. She sends them to the authorities with the threat to kill the baby unless the following are met:
    - (a) Have a bank sign 10,000 money orders.
    - (b) Publish the results in a newspaper.
- (4) The authorities comply.
- (5) Alice buys a newspaper, unblinds the money orders, and starts spending. There is no way for the authorities to trace the money orders to her.
- (6) Alice frees the baby.

Note that this situation is much worse than any involving physical tokens—cash. Without physical cash, the police have less opportunity to apprehend the kidnapper.

In general, digital cash isn't a good deal for criminals. The problem is that the anonymity works one way: The spender is anonymous, but the merchant is not. The merchant cannot hide the fact that they received money. Digital cash will make it easy for the government to determine how much money you have, but impossible to determine what you spent it on.

## Other Digital Cash Protocols

There are other digital cash protocols; see [707,1554,734,1633,973]. Some of them involve some pretty complicated mathematics. Generally, the various digital cash protocols can be divided into various categories. On-line systems require the merchant to communicate with the bank at every sale, much like today's credit-card protocols. If there is a problem, the bank does not accept the cash and Alice cannot cheat.

Off-line systems, like Protocol #4, require no communication between the merchant and the bank until the transaction between the merchant and the customer. These systems do not prevent Alice from cheating, but instead detect her cheating. Protocol #4 detected her cheating by making Alice's identity known if she tried to cheat. Alice can ensure that this will happen, so she doesn't cheat.

Another way is to create a special smart card ([332,341,387]) containing a tamper-proof chip. The observer chip keeps a mini database of all the pieces of digital cash by that smart card. If Alice attempts to copy some digital cash and spend it, the embedded observer chip would detect the attempt and not allow the transaction. Alice cannot erase the mini-database without permanently damaging the smart card. ### 7.1 SYMMETRIC KEY LENGTH

The security of a symmetric cryptosystem is a function of two things: the strength of the algorithm and the length of the key. The former is more important, but the latter is easier to demonstrate.

Assume that the strength of the algorithm is perfect. This is extremely difficult to achieve in practice, but easy enough for this example. By perfect, I mean that there is no better way to break the cryptystem other than trying every key.

To launch this attack, a cryptanalyst needs a small amount of ciphertext and the corresponding plaintext; a brute-force attack is a known-plaintext attack. For a block cipher, the cryptanalyst would need a block of ciphertext and corresponding plaintext: generally 64 bits. Finding this plaintext and ciphertext is easier than you might imagine. A cryptanalyst might get a copy of a plaintext message by means and intercept the corresponding ciphertext. For may know something about the format of the ciphertext: For example, it is a WordFile format, it has a standard electronic-mail header, it is a UNIX directory, or it is a standard record in a customer database. All of these formats have some predefined bytes. The cryptanalyst does not need plaintext to launch this attack.

Calculating the complexity of a brute-force attack is easy. If the key is 8 bits long, there are $2^8$, or 256, possible keys. It will take 256 attempts to find the correct key, with a 50% chance of finding the key after half of the attempts. If the key is 56 bits long, then there are $2^{56}$ possible keys. Assuming a supercomputer can try a million keys a second, it will take 2285 years to find the correct key. If the key is 64 bits long, then it will take the same supercomputer about 585,000 years to find the correct key among the $2^{64}$ possible keys.
