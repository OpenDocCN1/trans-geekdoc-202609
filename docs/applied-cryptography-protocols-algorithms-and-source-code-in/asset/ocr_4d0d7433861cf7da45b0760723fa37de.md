Known-plaintext chosen-plaintext attacks are more common than you think. It is not unheard of for a cryptanalyst to get a plaintext message that has been encrypted or bribe someone to encrypt a message. You may not even have to bribe someone; if you give a message to an ambassador, you probably find that it gets sent and back for consideration. Many messages have standard beginnings and endings that might be known to the cryptanalyst. Encrypted source code is especially vulnerable because of the regular appearance of keywords. Encrypted executable code has the same kinds of problems: loop structures, and so on. Known-plaintext attacks and even chosen-plaintext attacks were successfully used against the Germans and the Japanese during World War II. David Kahn's books [794,795,796] have historical examples of these kinds of attacks.

And don't forget Kerckhoffs' assumption: If the strength of your cryptography relies on the fact that the attacker does not know the algorithm's inner works, you're sunk. If you believe that keeping the algorithm's secrets more than letting the academic community analyze it, you're wrong. And if you think that someone won't disassemble your code and reverse-engineer your algorithm, you're naive. (In 1994 this was broken with the RC4 algorithm—Section 17.1). The best algorithms we have are the ones that have been made public, have been attacked by the world's best cryptographers for years, and are still unbreakable. The National Security Agency keeps their secrets from outsiders, but even the best cryptographers in the world working within their walls—you don't. Additionally, they discuss their algorithms with one another, relying on peer review to uncover any weaknesses in their work.)
