# # Afterword
by Matt Blaze

One of the most dangerous aspects of cryptology (and, by extension, of this book), is that you can almost measure it. Knowledge of key lengths, factoring methods, and cryptanalytic techniques makes it possible to estimate (in the absence of a reality of cipher design) the "work factor" required to break a particular cipher. It's all tempting to misuse these estimates as if they were overall security metrics for the systems in which they are used. The real world offers a richer menu of options than mere cryptanalysis. Often more worrisome are protocol attacks, Trojan horses, viruses, electromagnetic monitoring, physical compromise, blackmail and intimidation of key holders, operating system bugs, application program bugs, hardware bugs, user errors, physical eavesdropping, social engineering, and dumpster diving, to just name a few.

High-quality ciphers and protocols are important tools, but by themselves make poor substitutes for realistic, critical thinking about what is actually being protected and how various defenses might fail (attackers, after all, rarely restrict them to the clean, well-defined threat models of the academic world). Anderson gives examples of cryptographically strong systems (in the industry) that fail against the threats of the real world [43,44]. Even when the attacker has only access to cipher, seemingly minor breaches in other parts of the system can leak enough information to render good cryptosystems useless. The Allies in World War II broke the German Enigma traffic largely by exploiting operator errors [1587].

An NSA-employed acquaintance, when asked whether the government can crack DES traffic, quipped that real systems are so secure they never need to bother. Unfortunately, there are no easy recipes for a system secure, no substitute for careful design and critical, ongoing scrutiny.
