# CHAPTER 12 Data Encryption Standard (DES)

CRYPT(3)
CRYPT(3) is a DES variant found on UNIX systems. It is primarily used as a oneway function for passwords, but sometimes can also be used for encryption. The difference between CRYPT(3) and CRYPT(3) has a key-dependent permutation with $2^{15}$ possible permutations. This was done primarily so that off-the-shelf DES chips could be used to construct a hardware password-cracker.

# Generalized DES

Generalized DES [GDES] was designed to speed up DES and strengthen the algorithm [1381,1382]. The overall block size increases while the amount of computation remains.
12.11 is a block diagram of GDES. GDES operates on variable-sized blocks of plaintext. Encryption blocks are divided into $q$ 32-bit sub-blocks; the exact number depends on the total block size (this was in the design, but must be for each implementation). In general, $q$ equals the block size divided by 32.
F is calculated once per round on the right-most block. The result is XORed with all the other parts, which are rotated to the right. GDES has a variable number of rounds, n. There is a slight modification to the last round, so that encryption and decryption processes only in the order of the subkeys (like DES). In fact, if $q=2$ and $n=16$, this is DES.
Bisham and Shamir [17,168] showed that using differential analysis, GDES with $q=8$ and $n=16$ is breakable with only six plaintexts. If independent subkeys are also used, 16 plaintexts are required. GDES with $q=8$ and $n=22$ is breakable with forty-eight plaintexts. Even GDES with $q=8$ and $n=64$ is weaker than DES, which is less secure [see Table 12.15].
A variant of this scheme recently appeared [159]. It is probably no secure than the original DES. In general, any large DES variant that is faster than DES is probably less secure than DES.

# DES with S-Boxes

Other DES modifications centered around the S-boxes. Some designs would make the order of the S-boxes variable. Other designers varied the contents of the S-boxes themselves. The replacement of the eight DES S-boxes (without changing their value) also makes DES much weaker. DES with 16 rounds of a particular replaced order is breakable in about $2^{48}$ steps... DES with random S-boxes is shown very easy to break. Even a minimal change of entry of one of the DES S-boxes can make DES easier to break.

The DES S-boxes were not optimized against linear analysis. Better are S-boxes than the ones that with DES, but blindly choosing new S-boxes isn't a good idea.
