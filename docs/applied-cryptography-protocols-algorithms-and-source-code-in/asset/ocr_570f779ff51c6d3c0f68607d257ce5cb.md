# Introduction

I first wrote Applied Cryptography in 1993. Two years later, I wrote the greatly expanded second edition. At this vantage point of two decades later, it can be hard to remember how heavy cryptography's promise was back then. These were the early days of the Internet. Most of my friends had e-mail, but that was because most of my friends were using the World Wide Web. There was nothing yet called electronic commerce.

Cryptography was used by the few who cared. We could encrypt our e-mail with PGP; but mostly we didn't. We could encrypt sensitive files, but mostly we didn't. I dont remember the option of a usable full-disk encryption product, at least one that I would to trust be.

What we did have ideas—research and engineering ideas—and that was the point of Applied Cryptography. My goal in writing the book was to collect all the good ideas of academic cryptography under one cover and in a form that non-mathematicians could read and use.

What we also had, more important than this, the unshakable belief that technology ruled politics. You can see it in John Barlow's 1996 "Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace," where he held governments, "You have no moral right to rule us, nor do you pass any methods of enforcement that we have to reason for." You can see three years earlier in cyberpunk John Gilmore's famous quote: "The Net interprets cryptography as damage and routes around it." You can see it in the pages of Applied Cryptography. The first paragraph of the Preface, which I wrote in 1993, says, "There are two kinds of cryptography in this world: cryptography that will stop your kid sister from reading your files, and cryptography that will stop major governments from reading your files. This book is about the latter."
