# 6.1 Secure Elections

Another form of cheating is to try to figure out who voted for whom. Because of the scrambling in the first round, it is impossible to backtrack through the protocol and link votes with voters. The removal of the random strings during the first round is crucial to preserving anonymity. If they are not removed, the scrambling of the votes could be reversed by re-encrypting the emerging votes with the scrambler's public key. As the protocol stands, the confidentiality of votes is secure.

Even more strongly, because of the initial random string, $R_1$, even identical votes are encrypted at every step of the protocol. No one knows the outcome of the vote until step [11].

What are the problems with this protocol? First, the protocol has an enormous amount of computation. The example described only four voters and it was complicated. This would never work in a real election, with thousands of voters. Second, Dave learns the results of the election before anyone else does. While he still cannot affect the outcome, this gives him some power that the others do not have. On the other hand, this is also true with centralized voting.

The third problem is that Alice can copy anyone else's vote, even though she does not know what it is. To see why this could be a problem, consider a three-person election between Alice, Bob, and Eve. Eve doesn't care about the result of the election, but she wants to know how Alice voted. So she copies Alice's vote, and the result of the election is guaranteed to be equal to Alice's vote.

# Other Voting Schemes

Many secure election protocols have been proposed. They come in two basic flavors: everyone's vote gets mixed so that no one can associate a vote with a voter.

One divided protocol is [1371]. The idea is that each voter breaks his vote into several shares. For example, if the vote were "yes" or "no," a 1 could count "yes" and a 0 could count "no"; the voter would generate generate numbers whose sum was either 0 or 1. These shares are sent to balloting, each are encrypted and posted. Each center tallies the shares it receives [there are protocols to verify that the tally is correct!] and the final vote is the sum of all the voters. There are also protocols to ensure that each voter's share adds up to 0 or 1.

Another protocol, by David Chaum [322], ensures that voters who attempt to disrupt the election can be. However, the election must then be restarted with the interfering voter; this approach is not practical for large-scale elections.
