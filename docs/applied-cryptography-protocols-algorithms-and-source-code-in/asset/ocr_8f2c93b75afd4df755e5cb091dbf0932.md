Before you rush to invent a cryptosystem with an 8-kilobyte key, remember the other side to the strength: the algorithm must be so secure that there is no better way to break it with a brute-force attack. This is not as easy as it might seem. Cryptography is a subtle art. Cryptosystems that look perfect are often extremely weak. Strong cryptosystems, with a couple of changes, can become weak. To the amateur cryptographer is to have a healthy, almost为例, suspicion of any new algorithm. It is best to test algorithms that professional cryptographers have scrutinized without cracking them and to be suspicious of algorithm designers' grandiose claims of security.

Recall an important point from Section 1.1: The security of a cryptosystem should rest in the key, not in the details of the algorithm. Assume that any cryptanal has access to all the details of your algorithm. Assume he has access to much ciphertext as he wants and can invent an inexpensive cipher-only attack. Assume that he can mount a plaintext attack with as much data as he needs. Even assume that he can mount a chosen-plaintext attack. If your cryptosystem can remain secure, even in the face of all that knowledge, then you've got something.

That warning aside, there is plenty of room in cryptography to maneuver. In reality, this kind of security isn't really necessary in many situations. Most adversaries don't have the computing resources of a major government, and the ones who do probably aren't interested in breaking your cryptosystem. If you're plotting to overthrow a major government, stick with the tired and true algorithms in the back of the book. The rest of you, will have fun.

# Time and Cost Estimates for Brute-Force Attack

Remember that a brute-force attack is typically a known-plaintext attack; it requires a small amount of ciphertext and corresponding plaintext. If you assume a brute-force attack is the most efficient attack possible against an algorithm—then the key must be long enough to make the attack infeasible. How long is it?

Two parameters determine the speed of a brute-force attack: the number of keys and the speed of the test. Most symmetric algorithms accept any fixed length pattern as the key. DES has a 56-bit key; it has $2^{56}$ keys. Some algorithms discussed in this book have a 64-bit key; these have $2^{64}$ possible keys. Others have a 128-bit key.

The speed at which each possible key can be tested is also a factor, but a less important one. For the purposes of this analysis, I will assume that each algorithm can be tested in the same amount of time. The reality may be that one algorithm may be tested two, three, or even times faster than another. But since we are looking for key lengths that are millions of times more difficult to crack than would be feasible, small differences to test speed are irrelevant.
