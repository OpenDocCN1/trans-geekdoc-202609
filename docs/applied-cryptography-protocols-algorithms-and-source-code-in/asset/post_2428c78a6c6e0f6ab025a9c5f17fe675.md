## 25.4 RSA Data Security, Inc.

RSA Data Security, Inc. [RSADSI] was founded in 1982 to develop, license, and market the RSA patent. It has many commercial products, including a standalone email security package, and various cryptographic libraries (available in either source or object form). RSADSI also marks the RC2 and RC4 symmetric algorithms (see Section 11.8). RSA Laboratories, a research lab established with RSADSI, performs basic cryptographic research and provides consulting services.

## 25.5 PUBLIC KEY PARTNERS

The five patents in Table 25.3 are held by Public Key Partners (PKP) of Sunnyvale, California, a partnership between RSADSI and Caro-Kahn, Inc.—the parent company of Cylink. [RSADSI 65 percent of the profits and Caro-Kahn 35 percent.] PKP claims that these patents, and 4,218,582 in particular, to use all uses of public-key cryptography.

In [574], PKP wrote:

[4,200,770, 4,218,582, 4,405,829, and 4,424,414] cover all known methods of practicing the art of Public Key, including the variations collectively as Elgalmal.

Due to the broad acceptance of RSA digital signatures throughout the international community, Public Key Partners strongly endorses its incorporation in a digital signature standard. We assure all parties that Public Key Partners will comply with all the policies of ANSI and the IEEE concerning the availability of licenses to practice this art. Specifically, in support of any RSA signature standard that may be adopted, Public Key Partners gives us assurance that licenses to practice RSA signatures will be available under reasonable terms and conditions on a nondiscriminatory basis.

Whether this true depends on whom you talk to. PKP's licenses mostly secret, there is no way to check if the licenses are standard. Although they claim to have never denied a license to anyone, at least two companies claim to have denied a license. PKP guards its patents closely, threatening anyone who tries to use public-key cryptography without a license. In part, this is a reaction to U.S. patent law. If you hold a patent and fail to prevent an infringement, you can lose your patent. There has been much talk about whether the patents are legal, but so far it all be talk. All legal challenges to PKP's patents have been settled before judgment.

## 25.8 Conditional Access for Europe (CAFE)

Conditional Access for Europe (CAFE) is a project in the European Community's ESPR program [204,205]. Work began in December 1992 and is scheduled to be finished by the end of 1995. The consortium involved groups for social and market studies (Cardware, Institut für Sozialforschung), software and hardware manufacturers (DigICash, Gengico, Ingenios), and cryptographers (CWI Amsterdam, PTT Research, SPET, Sint Delab Trondheim, Universities of Aarhus, Hildenheim and Leuven).

The goal is to develop systems for conditional access, particularly digital payment systems. Payment systems must give legal certainty to everybody at all times—this certainty should not depend on the tamper-resistance of any devices.

The basic device for CAFE is an electronic wallet: a small computer that looks like a pocket calculator. It has a battery, keyboard, screen, and an infrared channel for communicating with other wallets. Every user owns and uses his own wallet, which administers his rights and guarantees its security.

A device with wallet and screen has an advantage over a smart card; it can operate independently of a terminal. A user can directly enter his password and the amount of the payment. The user does not have to give his wallet to complete a transaction, unlike the current situation with credit cards.

Additional features are:

- Offline transactions. The purpose of the system is to replace cash transactions; an online system would be too cumbersome.
- Loss tolerance. If a user loses his wallet, or if it breaks or is stolen, he can recover his money.
- Support for different currencies.
