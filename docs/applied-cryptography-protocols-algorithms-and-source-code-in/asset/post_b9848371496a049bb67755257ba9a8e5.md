In 1988, Carl Pomerance designed a modular factoring machine, using custom VLSI chips [1259]. The size of the number you would be able to factor depends on how large a machine you can afford to build. He never built it.

In 1993, a 120-digit number was factored using the quadratic sieve; the calculation took 825 mips-years and was completed in three months real time [463]. Other results are [504].

Today's factoring algorithms use computers [302,955]. In factoring a 116-digit number, Arjun Lenstra and Mark Manasse used 400 mips-years—the time on an array of computers around the world for a few months.

In March 1994, a 129-bit number was factored using the double large prime variation of the multiple polynomial QS [66] by a team of mathematicians including Lenstra. Volunteers on the Internet carried out the computation: 600 and 1600 machines over the course of eight months, probably the largest number of processors ever used. To update the state of the art of factoring, RSA Data Security, Inc. set up the RSA Factoring Challenge in March 1991 [532]. The challenge consists of a list of hard numbers, each the product of primes of roughly equal size. Each prime was chosen to be congruent 2 modulo 3. There are 42 numbers in the challenge, each of length 100 digits through 500 digits in steps of 10 digits (plus one additional number, 129 digits long). At the time of writing, RSA-100, RSA-110, RSA-120, and RSA-129 have been factored, all using QS. RSA-130 might be next (using the NFS), or the factoring champions might skip directly to RSA-140.

This is a fast-moving field. It is difficult to extrapolate factoring techniques because no one can predict advances in mathematical theory. Before discovery, many people conjectured that the QS was asymptotically as fast as the NFS was. They were wrong.

Near-term advances in the NFS are likely to come in the form of bringing down the constant: 1.923. Some numbers of a special form, like Fermat numbers, have a constant along the line of 1.5 [955,954]. If the numbers used in public cryptography had that kind of constant, 1024-bit numbers could be factored today. One way to lower the constant is to find better ways of representing numbers as polynomials with small coefficients. The problem hasn't been studied extensively yet, but it is probable that advances are coming [949].

## Square Roots Modulo n

If n is the product of two primes, then the ability to calculate square roots mod n is computationally equivalent to the ability to factor n [1283,35,36,193]. In other words, someone who knows the prime factors of n can easily compute the square roots of a number n, but for everyone else the computation has been proven to be as hard as computing the prime factors of n.

## 11.5 Prime Number Generation

Public-key algorithms need prime numbers. Any reasonably sized network needs lots of them. Before discussing the mathematics of prime number generation, I will answer few obvious questions.

- 1. If one needs a different prime number, will we run out? In fact, there are approximately $10^{151}$ primes 512 bits in length or less. For numbers near n, the probability that a random number is prime is approximately ln n. The total number of primes less than n is n/[ln n]. There are only $10^{80}$ atoms in the universe. If every atom in the universe needed a billion new primes every microsecond from the beginning of time, you would only need $10^{95}$ primes, there would still be $10^{151}$ 512-bit left.
- 2. If we people accidentally pick the same prime number? It won't happen. With $10^{151}$ prime numbers to choose from, the odds of happening are significantly less than the odds of your computer potentially combusting at the exact moment you win the lottery.
- 3. If someone creates a database of all primes, he won't be able to use that database to break public-key algorithms? Yes, he can't. If you could store gigabytes of information on a drive weighing one gram, then a list of the 512-bit primes would weigh so much that it would exceed the Chandrasekhar limit and collapse into a black hole... so you couldn't retrieve the data.

The wrong way to find primes is to generate random numbers and then to factor them. The right way is to generate random numbers and test if they are prime. There are several probabilistic tests, tests that determine whether a number is prime with a "degree of confidence" large, these sorts of tests are good enough. I have heard primes generated in this manner called "industrial-grade primes"; these are numbers that are probably prime with a controllable small amount of error.

Assume a test is to fail once in $2^{50}$ tries. This means there is a 1 in $10^{15}$ chance that the test falsely indicates that a composite number is prime. (The test will never falsely indicate that a prime number is composite. If for some reason you need more confidence that the number is prime, you can set the failure level even lower. On the other hand, if you consider the odds of the number being composite are 300 million times less than the odds of winning top prize in a state lottery, you might not worry about it so much.)

Overviews of recent developments in the field can be found in [1256,206]. Other important papers are [1490,38.11,19,626,651,991].

## Solovay-Strassen

Robert Solovay and Volker Strassen developed a probabilistic primality testing algorithm [1490]. Their algorithm uses the Jacobi symbol to test if p is prime:

- Choose a random number, a, less than p.
- If the gcd(a,p) ≠ 1, then p fails the test and is composite.
- Calculate j = a^{(p-1)/2} mod p.
- Calculate the Jacobi symbol [a,p].
- If j ≠ [a,p], then p is definitely not prime.
- If j = [a,p], then the likelihood that p is not prime is no more than 50 percent.

A number a that does not indicate that p is prime is called a witness. If p is composite, the odds of a being a witness is no less than 50 percent. Repeat this test t times, with t different random values for a. The odds of a composite number passing all t tests is no more than one in 2'.

## Lehmann

Another, simpler test, was developed independently by Lehmann [945]. Here it test if p is prime:

- Choose a random number a less than p.
- Calculate a^{(p-1)/2} mod p.
- If a^{(p-1)/2} = 1 or -1, then p is prime.
- If a^{(p-1)/2} ≠ 1 or -1, then p is composite. (Repeat this with different a.)

## Rabin-Miller

The most popular primality test is the Miller-Rabin test. To test an odd integer $n$, write $n-1 = 2^s \cdot d$, where $d$ is odd. Pick a random integer $a$ in the range $[2, n-2]$. Compute $x = a^d \pmod n$. If $x = 1$ or $x = n-1$, then $n$ is probably prime. If not, repeat the following $s-1$ times: $x = x^2 \pmod n$. If $x = n-1$, then $n$ is probably prime. If none of these conditions are met, then $n$ is composite.

## Mathematical Background

The Miller-Rabin test is based on the property that if $p$ is prime, the only square roots of 1 modulo $p$ are 1 and -1. If $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod n$ and $x \not\equiv \pm 1 \pmod n$, then $n$ is composite.

## Practical Considerations

In real-world implementations, prime generation goes quickly.

- (1) Generate a random $n$-bit number, $p$.
- (2) Set the high-order and low-order bit to 1 (This ensures the prime is of the required length and the low-order bit ensures it is odd).
- (3) Check to make sure $p$ is not divisible by small primes:3, 5, 7, 11, and so on. Many implementations test $p$ for divisibility by all less than 256. The most efficient is to test for divisibility by all primes less than 2000 [949]. You can do this using a wheel [863].
- (4) Perform the Rabin-Miller test for random $a$. If $p$ passes, generate another random $a$ and go through the test again. Choose a small value of $a$ to make the calculations go quicker. Do five tests [651]. (One might seem enough, but not so.) If $p$ fails on one of the tests, generate another $p$ and try again.

Another option is not to generate a random $p$ each time, but to incrementally search numbers starting at a random point until you find a prime.
