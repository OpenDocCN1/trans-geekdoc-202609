Now, the annual energy output of our sun is about $1.21\cdot10^{44}$ ergs. This is enough to power $2.7\cdot10^{36}$ single bit changes on our ideal computer, enough state changes to power a 187-bit counter through all its values. If we built a Dyson sphere around the sun and captured all of its energy for 42 years, without any loss, we could power a computer up to $2^{192}$. Of course, it wouldn't have the energy left to perform any useful calculations with this counter.

But that's just one, and a measly one at that! A typical supernova releases $10^{43}$ ergs (About a hundred times as much energy would be released in the form of neutrinos). If all of this energy could be channeled into a single sort of computation, a 219-bit counter could be cycled through all of its states.

These numbers have nothing to do with the technology of the devices; they are the maximums that thermodynamics will allow. And they strongly imply that brute-force attacks against 256-bit keys would be infeasible unless computers are built from something other than matter and occupying another space.

## 7.2 PUBLIC-KEY LENGTH

One-way functions were discussed in Section 2.3. Multiplying two large primes is a one-way function; it's easy to multiply the numbers to get a product but hard to factor the product and the two large primes [see Section 11.3]. Public-key cryptography uses this idea to make a trap-door one-way function. Actually, that's a lie; factoring is conjectured to be a hard problem [see Section 11.4]. As far as anyone knows, it seems to be it. Even if it is, no one can prove that hard problems are hard. Everyone assumes that factoring is hard, but it has never been mathematically proven one or the other.

This is worth dwelling on. It is easy to imagine 50 years in the future we will all sit around, reminiscing about the old days when people used to think factoring was hard, cryptography and companies actually made money from this stuff. It is easy to imagine that future developments in complexity theory will make factoring easier or even make factoring trivial. There's no reason to believe this will happen—and most people who know enough have no opinion will tell you that it is unlikely—but there is no reason to believe it.

In any case, today's dominant public-key encryption algorithms are based on the difficulty of factoring large numbers that are the product of two primes (Other algorithms are based on something called the Discrete Logarithm Problem, but for the moment assume the same discussion applies.) These algorithms are also susceptible to a brute-force attack, but of a different type. Breaking these does not involve trying every possible key; breaking these algorithms involves factoring the large number (or taking discrete logarithms in a very large finite field—a similar problem). If the number is too small, you have no security. If the number is large enough, you have security against all the computing power in the world working from now until the sun goes nova—given today's understanding of the mathematics.

## 7.2 Public-Key Key Length

Section 11.3 discusses factoring in more mathematical detail, here I will limit the discussion to how long it takes to factor numbers of various lengths.

Factoring numbers is hard. Unfortunately for algorithm designers, it is getting worse. Even worse, it is getting faster than mathematicians expect. In 1976 Richard Guy wrote: "I shall be surprised if anyone regularly factors numbers of size $10^{100}$ without special form during the present century" [680]. In 1977 Ron Rivest said that a 125-digit number would take 40 quadrillion years [599]. In 1994 a 129-digit number was factored [66]. If there is any lesson in all of this, it is that making predictions is foolish.

Table 7.3 shows factoring records over the past dozen years. The fastest algorithm during the time was the quadratic sieve [see Section 11.3].

These numbers are frightening today. It is not uncommon to use 512-bit numbers used in operational systems. Factoring them, and completely compromising their security, is well in the range of possibility: a weekend-long work on the Internet could do it.

Computing power is generally measured in mips-years: a one-million-instruction-second running for one year, or about $3\cdot10^{10}$ instructions. By example, a mips-machine is equivalent to the DEC VAX 11/780. Hence, a mips-year is a VAX 11/780 running for a year, or the equivalent: (A 100 MHz Pentium is about a 50 mips machine; a 1800-node Intel Paragon is about 50,000).

The 1983 factorization of a 71-digit number required 0.1 mips-years, the 1994 factorization power resulted from this introduction of distributed computing, using the idle time on a network of workstations. This trend was started by Bob Silverman and fully developed by Arjen Lenstra and Mark Mansfield. The 1983 factorization used 9.5 CPUs on a single Cray X-MP, the 1994 factorization took 5000 mips-years and used idle time on 100 computers around the world for about eight months. Modern factoring methods lend themselves to this kind of implementation.

The picture gets worse. A new factoring algorithm has taken over the quadratic sieve; a general number field sieve. In 1989 mathematicians would have told you the general number field sieve would never be practical. In 1992 we would have told you it was practical, but only faster than the quadratic sieve for numbers greater than 130 digits.

| 1983 | 71 | >20 million |

The general number field sieve is getting faster. Mathematicians keep coming up with new tricks, new optimizations, there's no reason to think this trend won't continue. A related algorithm, the special number field sieve, can factor numbers of a certain form—numbers generally used for cryptography—much faster than the general number field sieve of the same size. It is not unreasonable to assume that the general number field sieve can be optimized to run this fast [1190].

At a European Institute for Security Workshop in 1991, the participants agreed that a 1024-bit modulus would be sufficient for long-term secrets through 2002 [150]. However, they warned: “Although the participants of this work best qualified in their respective areas, this statement [with respect to lasting security] should be taken with caution. This is good advice.

The wise crypt is ultra-conservative when choosing public-key lengths. To determine how long a key requires you to look at both the intended security and lifetime of the key, and the current state-of-the-art of factoring. Today you need a 1024-bit number to get the level of security you got from a 512-bit number in the early 1980s. If you want your keys to remain for 20 years, 1024 bits is little too short.

Even if your particular secrets aren't the effort required to factor your modulus, you may be at risk. Imagine an automatic banking system that uses RSA for security. Mallory can stand up in court and say: “Did you read in the newspaper in 1994 that RSA-129 was broken, and 512-bit numbers can be factored by any modern computer]”
