
# 18 CHAPTER 8 Key Management

It is generally easier to do cryptanalysis with more ciphertext encrypted with the same key.

For any cryptographic application, there must be a policy that determines the permitted lifetime of a key. Different keys may have different lifetimes. For a connection-based system, like a telephone, it makes sense to use a key for the length of the telephone call and to use a new one with each call.

Systems on dedicated communications channels are not as obvious. Keys should have relatively short lifetimes, depending on the value of the data and the amount of data encrypted during a given period. For example, a gigabit-second communications link might have to be changed more often than the key for a 9600-baud modem link. Assuming there is an efficient method of transferring new keys, session keys should be changed at least daily.

Key-encryption keys don't have to be replaced as frequently. They are used only occasionally (roughly once per day) for key exchange. This generates little ciphertext for a cryptanalyst to work with, and the corresponding plaintext has no particular form. However, if a key-encryption key is compromised, the potential loss is extreme: all communications encrypted with every key with the key-encryption key. In some applications, key-encryption keys are replaced only once a month or once a year. You have to balance the inherent danger in keeping a key around for a while with the inherent danger in distributing a new one.

Encryption keys used to encrypt data for storage cannot be changed often. The files sit encrypted for months or years before someone needs them again. Decrypting and re-encrypting them with a new key every day doesn't enhance security in any way; it just gives a cryptanalyst more to work with. One solution might be to encrypt with a unique key, and then encrypt all the files with a key-encryption key. The key-encryption key should then be either monitored or stored in a secure location, perhaps in a safe somewhere. Or losing the key would mean losing all the individual keys.

Private keys for public-key cryptography applications have varying lifetimes, depending on the application. Private keys used for digital signatures and protocols may last for years (even a lifetime). Private keys used for coin-flipping protocols can be discarded immediately after the protocol is completed. Even if a key's security is expected to last a lifetime, it might be prudent to change the key every couple of years. The private keys in many networks are good only for two years; after that the user must get a new private key. The old key would still have to remain secret, in case the user needed to verify a signature from that period. But the new key would be used to sign new documents, reducing the number of signed documents a cryptanalyst would have for an attack.

## 8.11 DESTROYING KEYS

Given that keys must be replaced regularly, old keys must be destroyed. Old keys are valuable, even if they are never used again, as an adversary can read messages encrypted with these keys [65].

## 8.12 Public-Key Management

Keys must be destroyed securely (see Section 10.9). If the key is written on paper, the paper should be shredded or burned. Be careful to use a high-quality shredder, many lousy shredders are on the market. Algorithms in this book are secure against brute-force attacks costing millions of dollars and taking millions of years. If an adversary can recover your key by taking a bag of shredded documents from your trash and paying 10 unemployed workers in backcountry cents per hour for a year to piece the shredded pages together, that would be $26,000 well spent.

If the key is in a hardware EEPROM, the key should be overwritten multiple times. If the key is in a hardware EPROM or PROM, the chip should be smashed into tiny bits and scattered to the four corners. If the key is on a computer disk, the actual bits of the storage should be overwritten multiple times (see Section 10.9) or the disk should be shredded.

A potential problem is that, in a computer, keys can be easily copied and stored in multiple locations. Any computer that does its own memory management, swapping programs in and out of memory, exacerbates the problem. There is no way to ensure that successful erasure has taken place in the computer, especially if the operating system controls the erasure process. The more paranoid among you should consider writing a special erasure program that scans all disks looking for copies of the key's bit pattern on unused blocks and then erases those blocks. Also remember to erase the contents of any temporary, or "swap," files.

## 8.12 PUBLIC-KEY MANAGEMENT

Public-key cryptography makes key management easier, but it has its own unique problems. Each person has only one public key, regardless of the number of people on the network. If Alice wants to send a message to Bob, she can get Bob's public key. She can get it by several ways:

- She can get it from Bob.
- She can get it from a centralized database.
- She can get it from her own private database.

2.5 discussed a number of possible attacks against public-key cryptography, by Mallory substituting his key for Bob's. The scenario is that Alice wants to send a message to Bob. She goes to the public-key database and gets Bob's public key. But Mallory, who is sneaky, has substituted his key for Bob's. [If Alice asks Bob directly, Mallory will intercept Bob's transmission and substitute his key for Bob's.] Alice encrypts her message in Mallory's key and sends it to Bob. Mallory intercepts the message, decrypts it, and reads it. He re-encrypts it with Bob's real key and sends it to Bob. Neither Alice nor Bob is the wiser.

### Public-key Certificates

A public-key certificate is someone's public key, signed by a trusted authority. A public-key certificate is someone's public key, signed by a trusted authority, which is widely known as a certification authority, or CA. It contains lot more than his public key—it contains his name, address, and so on—and it is signed by the CA. Certificates play an important role in a number of public-key protocols such as PEM [825] and X.509 [304]. A complicated cryptographic issue surrounds this type of system. What is the meaning of certification? Or, to put another way, who is trusted to certify whom? Here think about:

- What level of trust in someone's identity is implied by his certificate?
- What are the relationships between a person and the CA that certified his public key, and how can those relationships be implied by the certificate?
- Who is trusted to be the single trusted entity at the top of the certification chain?
- How long can a certification chain be?

Ideally, Bob would need some kind of authentication procedure before the CA signs his certificate. Additionally, some kind of timestamp or an indication of the certificate's validity is important to guard against compromised keys [461]. Timestamping is not enough. Keys may be invalidated before they have expired, either through compromise or for administrative reasons. Hence, it is important that the CA keeps a list of invalid certificates, and for users to regularly check that list. This revocation problem is still a difficult one to solve.
