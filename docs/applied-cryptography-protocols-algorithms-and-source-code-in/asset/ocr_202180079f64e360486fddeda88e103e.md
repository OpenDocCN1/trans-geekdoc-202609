Cryptography presents a difficulty not found in normal academic disciplines: the need for the proper interaction of cryptography and cryptanalysis. This arises out of the fact that in the absence of realistic requirements, it is easy to propose a system that the cryptanalyst knows where to search; exposing flaws in these designs is harder than designing them in the first place. The result is that the competitive process, which is one strong motivation in academic research, cannot take hold.

In acceptance speech for the Donald E. Fink award—given for the best exposition paper to appear in an IEEE journal—which I received with Hellman in 1980, I told the audience that I had an experience that I suspected was rare among the prominent scholars who populate the IEEE awards: I wrote the paper I wanted to study, but could not find, when I first became seriously interested in cryptography. Had I been able to go to the Stanford bookstore and pick up a modern cryptography text, I would probably have been the field years earlier. The only things available in the fall of 1972 were a few classic papers and some obscure technical reports.

The contemporary researcher has no problem. The problem is now choosing where to start among the thousands of papers and dozens of books. The contemporary researcher, yes, but what the contemporary programmer or engineer who merely wants to use cryptography? Where does that person turn? Until now, it has been necessary to spend long hours hunting out and studying the literature before being able to design the sort of cryptography utilities globally described in popular articles.

This is that Bruce Schneider's Applied Cryptography comes to fill. Beginning with the objectives of communication security and elementary examples of programs used to achieve these objectives, Schneider gives a panoramic view of the fruits of 20 years of public research. The title says it all; from the mundane objective of having a secure conversation the first time you call someone to the possibilities of digital money and cryptographically secure elections, this is where you'll find it.
