3. Variations on the words from step 2. This included the first letter uppercase or a control character, making the entire word uppercase, reversing the word (with without the aforementioned capitalization), changing the letter ‘o’ to the digit ‘0’ [so that the word “scholar” would also be checked as “scholar”], changing the letter ‘l’ to the digit ‘1’ [so that the word “school” would also be checked as “scholar”], and performing similar manipulation to change the letter ‘z’ into the digit ‘2’, and the letter ‘s’ into the digit ‘5’. Another test was to make the word into a plural—with enough intelligence built in so that “dress” became “dresses”, “house” became “houses”, and “daisy” became “daisies”. Klein did not consider pluralization rules exclusive, though that “datum” forgivably became “datums” [not “data”], phrase “sphyxnx” became “sphynxes” (and not “sphynges”); Similarly, the suffixes “ed”, “er”, and “ing” were added to transform words like “phase” into “phased”, and “phasing”. Additional tests added another 1,000,000 words to the list of possible passwords that were created for each user.

4. Various capitalization variations on the words from step 2 were not considered in step 3. This included all single-letter capitalization variations (so that “michael” would also be checked as “Michael”, “Michael”, “michael”, etc.), double-letter capitalization variations (“Michael”, “MICHael”,..., “michael”, “mHAEL”, etc.), triple-letter variations. The single-letter variations added roughly another 400,000 words to be checked per user, while the double-letter variations added at least 3,000,000 words per user had been enough time to complete the tests. Tests of four-, and six-letter variations were deemed to be impracticable without much more computational horsepower to carry them.
