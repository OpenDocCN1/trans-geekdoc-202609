Since these are one-time pads and K is completely random, there is no way to prove that K' was not the real key. To make matters more convincing, Alice and Bob should conceal mildly incriminating dummy messages to take the place of the real messages. A pair of Israeli spies once did this code.

Alice could take P and encrypt it with her favorite algorithm and key K to get C. Then she takes C and XORs it with some piece of mundane plaintext—Pride and Prejudice for example, to get K'. She stores both C and the XOR on the hard disk. When the secret police interrogates her, she can explain that she is an amateur cryptographer and that K' is merely a one-time pad for C. The secret police might suspect something, unless they know K they cannot prove that Alice's explanation isn't.

Another method is to encrypt P with a symmetric algorithm and K, and D with K'. Intertwine bits (bytes) of the ciphertext to make the final ciphertext. The secret police demand the key, Alice gives them K and says that the alternating bits (or bytes) are random noise to frustrate cryptanalysis. The trouble is the explanation is so plausible the secret police will probably not have the file (especially considering it is suggested in this book).

A better way is for Alice to create a dummy message, D, such that the concatenation of P and D, is about the same size as D. Call this concatenation P'. Alice encrypts P' with whatever algorithm she and Bob share to get C. Then she sends C to Bob. Bob decrypts C to get P', and then P and D. They both compute D = K'. This becomes the one-time pad they use in the secret police break their doors down. Alice has to transmit D so that hers and Bob's alice match.

Another method is for Alice to take an innocuous message and run it through some error-correcting code. Then she can introduce errors that correspond to the secret encrypted message. On the receiving end, Bob can extract the errors to reconstruct the secret message and decrypt it. He can also use the error-correcting code to recover the innocent message. Alice and Bob might be hard pressed to explain to the secret police why they consistently get a 30 percent bit-error rate on an otherwise noise-free computer, but in certain circumstances this scheme can work.

Finally, Alice and Bob use the subliminal channels in their digital signature algorithms which are undetectable, works great, but has the drawback of only allowing 20 or so characters of subliminal text to be sent per signed message. It really isn't good for much more than sending keys.
