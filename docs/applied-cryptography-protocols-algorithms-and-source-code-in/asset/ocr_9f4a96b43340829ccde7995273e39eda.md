# Square Roots Modulo n

If n is the product of two primes, then the ability to calculate square roots mod n is computationally equivalent to the ability to factor n [1283,35,36,193]. In other words, someone who knows the prime factors of n can easily compute the square roots of a number n, but for everyone else the computation has been proven to be as hard as computing the prime factors of n.

# 11.5 Prime Number Generation

Public-key algorithms need prime numbers. Any reasonably sized network needs lots of them. Before discussing the mathematics of prime number generation, I will answer few obvious questions.

+   1. If one needs a different prime number, will we run out? In fact, there are approximately $10^{151}$ primes 512 bits in length or less. For numbers near n, the probability that a random number is prime is approximately ln n. The total number of primes less than n is n/[ln n]. There are only $10^{80}$ atoms in the universe. If every atom in the universe needed a billion new primes every microsecond from the beginning of time, you would only need $10^{95}$ primes, there would still be $10^{151}$ 512-bit left.
2. If we people accidentally pick the same prime number? It won't happen. With $10^{151}$ prime numbers to choose from, the odds of happening are significantly less than the odds of your computer potentially combusting at the exact moment you win the lottery.
3. If someone creates a database of all primes, he won't be able to use that database to break public-key algorithms? Yes, he can't. If you could store gigabytes of information on a drive weighing one gram, then a list of the 512-bit primes would weigh so much that it would exceed the Chandrasekhar limit and collapse into a black hole... so you couldn't retrieve the data.

The wrong way to find primes is to generate random numbers and then to factor them. The right way is to generate random numbers and test if they are prime. There are several probabilistic tests, tests that determine whether a number is prime with a "degree of confidence" large, these sorts of tests are good enough. I have heard primes generated in this manner called "industrial-grade primes"; These are numbers that are probably prime with a controllable small amount of error.
