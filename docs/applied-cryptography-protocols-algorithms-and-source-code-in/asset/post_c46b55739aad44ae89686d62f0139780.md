## 24.13 SMART CARDS

A smart card is a plastic card, the size and shape of a credit card, with an embedded computer chip. It is an old idea—the first patents were filed 20 years ago—but practical limitations made them feasible only five or so ago. Since then they have taken off, in Europe. Many countries use smart cards for pay telephones. There are also smart cards, smart cash cards, smart everything. The U.S. credit-card companies are looking at the technology, and within a few years every American will have smart cards in their wallets.

A smart card contains a small computer (an 8-bit microprocessor), RAM (about a few kilobytes), ROM (about 6 or 8 kilobytes), and either EPROM (a few kilobytes). Future-generation smart cards would undoubtedly have more capacity, but some physical limitations on smart cards make expansion difficult. The card has its own operating system, programs, and data. [What comes when the card is plugged in?] And it is secure. In a world where you might not trust someone else's computer or telephone or whatever, you can still trust a card that you keep in your wallet.

Some cards are argued to be tamperproof; this protects the institution that issues the cards. A bank wouldn't want you to be able to hack their smart card to give yourself more money.

## 24.14 PUBLIC-KEY CRYPTOGRAPHY STANDARDS (PKCS)

Public-Key Cryptography Standards (PKCS) are Data Security, Inc.’s attempt to provide an industry standard for public-key cryptography. Traditionally, sort of thing would be handled by ANSI, but considering the current situation, they developed a series of standards. Working with a variety of companies, some are compatible with other standards and some are not. These are not standards in the traditional sense; if you’re not sure what kind of data structures to use public-key cryptography, these standards are probably good as anything.

Following is a short description of each standard:

- PKCS #1 describes a method for RSA encryption and decryption, primarily for constructing digital signatures.
- PKCS #2 describes a method for deriving a key from a password using RSA.
- PKCS #3 describes a method for implementing Diffie-Hellman key exchange.
- PKCS #4 describes a method for encrypting messages with a secret password.
- PKCS #5 describes a method for encrypting messages with DES.
- PKCS #6 describes a standard for public keys.
- PKCS #7 is a general syntax for data that may be encrypted or signed, as digital envelopes.
- PKCS #8 describes a syntax for key information—including a private key and a set of attributes.
- PKCS #9 defines attribute types for public keys.
- PKCS #10 describes a standard syntax for certification. A certification comprises a distinguished name, a public key, and (optionally) a set of attributes, signed by the person requesting certification. Certification requests are sent to a certification authority, who either transforms the request into an X.509 public-key certificate or a PKCS certificate.
- PKCS #11, the Cryptographic Token API Standard, specifies a programming interface called “Cryptoki” for portable cryptographic devices of all kinds. Cryptoki presents a common logical model, enabling applications to perform cryptographic operations on portable devices without knowing details of the underlying technology. The standard also defines profiles: sets of algorithms that a device may support.
- PKCS #12 describes syntax for storing in software a user’s public keys, protected private keys, certificates, and other related cryptographic information.

These standards are comprehensive, but not exhaustive. Many things are outside their scope: the problem of naming, noncryptographic issues surrounding certification, key lengths, and conditions on various parameters. What the PKCS provide are a format for transferring data based on public-key cryptography and an infrastructure to support that.

## 24.15 UNIVERS ELECTRONIC PAYMENT SYSTEM (UEPS)

The UEPS is a smart-card application initially developed for rural South Africa, but later adopted by all of that country’s major banking groups. By 2 million cards were issued in that country by early 1995. It has also been adopted in Namibia, and is also being deployed by at least one bank in Russia.

The system provides a secure debit card suitable for regions where poor telephone service make online impossible. Both customers and merchants have customers; customers can use their cards to transfer money to merchants. Merchants can then take their cards to a telephone and deposit the money in their bank account; customers can take their cards to a telephone and have money moved onto their card. There is no intention to provide anonymity, only to prevent fraud.
