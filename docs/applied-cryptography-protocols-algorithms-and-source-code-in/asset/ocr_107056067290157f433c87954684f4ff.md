# CHAPTER 5 Advanced Protocols

# 5.3 BLIND SIGNATURES

An essential feature of digital signature protocols is that the signer knows what he is signing. It is a good idea, except when we want the reverse.

We might want people to sign documents without ever seeing their contents. There are ways that a signer can almost, but not exactly, know what he is signing. By first things first.

# Completely Blind Signatures

Bob is a notary public. Alice wants to sign a document, but does not want him to have any idea what he is signing. Bob doesn't care what the document says, he is just justifying that he notarized it at a certain time. He is willing to go along with Bob.

+   (1) Alice takes the document and multiplies it by a random value. This random value is a blinding factor.
(2) Alice sends the blinded document to Bob.
(3) Bob signs the blind document.
(4) Alice divides out the blinding factor, leaving the original document signed by Bob.

This protocol only works if the signature function and multiplication are commutative. If they are not, there are other ways to modify the document other than multiplying. Some relevant algorithms appear in Section 23.12. For now, assume that the operation is possible and all the math works.

Can Bob cheat? He can collect any information about the document that he is signing. If the blinding factor is truly random and makes the blinded document truly random, he cannot cheat. The document Bob signs in step (2) looks nothing like the document Alice began. Bob's signature in step (3) looks like the signed document at the end of step (4). Even if Bob gets his hands on the document, with his signature, after completing the protocol, he cannot prove [to himself or anyone else] that he signed it in particular. He knows his signature is valid. He can verify his signature. However, there is no way for him to correlate any information received during the signing protocol with the signed document. If he signed a million documents using this protocol, he would have no way of knowing which instance he signed which document.

The properties of completely blind signatures are:

+   (1) Bob's signature on the document is valid. The signature is a proof that Bob signed the document. It will convince Bob that he signed the document if it is ever shown to him. It also has all the other properties of digital signatures discussed in Section 2.6.
(2) Bob cannot correlate the signed document with the act of signing the document. Even if he keeps records of every blind signature he makes, he cannot determine when he signed any given document.
