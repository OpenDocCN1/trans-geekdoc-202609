Before you rush to invent a cryptosystem with an 8-kilobyte key, remember the other side to the strength: the algorithm must be so secure that there is no better way to break it with a brute-force attack. This is not as easy as it might seem. Cryptography is a subtle art. Cryptosystems that look perfect are often extremely weak. Strong cryptosystems, with a couple of changes, can become weak. To the amateur cryptographer is to have a healthy, almost exemplary, suspicion of any new algorithm. It is best to test algorithms that professional cryptographers have scrutinized without cracking them and to be suspicious of algorithm designers' grandiose claims of security.

Recall an important point from Section 1.1: The security of a cryptosystem should rest in the key, not in the details of the algorithm. Assume that any cryptanalyst has access to all the details of your algorithm. Assume he has access to much ciphertext as he wants and can invent an inexpensive cipher-only attack. Assume that he can mount a plaintext attack with as much data as he needs. Even assume that he can mount a chosen-plaintext attack. If your cryptosystem can remain secure, even in the face of all that knowledge, then you've got something.

That warning aside, there is plenty of room in cryptography to maneuver. In reality, this kind of security isn't really necessary in many situations. Most adversaries don't have the computing resources of a major government, and the ones who do probably aren't interested in breaking your cryptosystem. If you're plotting to overthrow a major government, stick with the tired and true algorithms in the back of the book. The rest of you, will have fun.

# Time and Cost Estimates for Brute-Force Attack

Remember that a brute-force attack is typically a known-plaintext attack; it requires a small amount of ciphertext and corresponding plaintext. If you assume a brute-force attack is the most efficient attack possible against an algorithm—then the key must be long enough to make the attack infeasible. How long is it?

Two parameters determine the speed of a brute-force attack: the number of keys and the speed of the test. Most symmetric algorithms accept any fixed length pattern as the key. DES has a 56-bit key; it has $2^{56}$ keys. Some algorithms discussed in this book have a 64-bit key; these have $2^{64}$ possible keys. Others have a 128-bit key.

The speed at which each possible key can be tested is also a factor, but a less important one. For the purposes of this analysis, I will assume that each algorithm can be tested in the same amount of time. The reality may be that one algorithm may be tested two, three, or even ten times faster than another. But since we are looking for key lengths that are millions of times more difficult to crack than would be feasible, small differences to test speed are irrelevant.

Most of the debate in the cryptologic community about the efficiency of brute attacks has centered on the DES algorithm. In 1977, Whitehead Diffie and Martin Hellman [497] postulated the existence of a special-purpose DES-cracking

This machine consisted of a million chips, each capable of testing a million keys per second. Such a machine could test $2^{56}$ keys in 20 hours. If built to attack an algorithm with a 64-bit key, it could test all $2^{64}$ keys in 214 days.

A brute-force attack is tailor-made for parallel processors. Each processor can test a subset of the keyspace. The processors do not have to communicate among themselves; the only communication required is all a single message signifying success. It is easy to design a machine with a million parallel processors, each working independent of the others.

More recently, Michael Wiener decided to design a brute-force cracking machine (DES)[1597,1598]. He designed the machine for DES, but the analysis holds for almost any algorithm. He specialized chips, boards, and racks. He estimated prices. And discovered that for $1 million, someone could build a machine that could crack a 56-bit DES in an average of 3.5 hours (results guaranteed in 7 hours). Table 7.1 generalizes these to a variety of key lengths. Remember Moore's Law: Computing power doubles approximately 18 months. This means costs go down by a factor of 10 every five years; what cost $1 million to build in 1995 would cost $100,000 in the year 2000. Pipelined computers might do even better [1224].

For 56-bit keys, these numbers are within the budgets of most large companies and many criminal organizations. The military budgets of most industrialized nations can afford to break 80-bit key is still beyond the realm of possibility, if current trends continue that in 30 years.

Of course, it is ludicrous to estimate computing power 35 years in the future. Breakthroughs in science-fiction technology could make these numbers look like a joke. Conversely, physical limitations at the present time could make them unrealistically optimistic. In cryptography it is wise to be pessimistic. Fielding an algorithm with an 80-bit key seems extremely short-sighted. Insist on at least 112-bit keys.

## Key Length

The value of some messages decreases rapidly with time: How much value can be trusted before it makes sense? To give an extreme example, if encrypted message is worth $139, then it wouldn't make sense to build the cracker. On the other hand, if plaintext is worth $100 million, then decrypting that would justify the cost.

Using a 64-bit key instead of a 56-bit key makes this attack 256 times more difficult. With a 40-bit key, the picture is far bleaker. A network of 400 computers, each capable of performing 32,000 encryptions per second, can complete a brute-force attack against a 40-bit key in a single day. In 1992, the RC2 and RC4 algorithms were approved for export with a 40-bit key—see Section 13.8.

## Software Crackers

Without special-purpose hardware and massively parallel machines, brute-force attacks are significantly harder. A software attack is about a thousand times slower than a hardware attack.

The real threat of a software-based attack is not that it is certain, but that it is “free.” It costs nothing to use a microcomputer to test keys whenever it is idle. A recent experiment used 40 workstations to test $2^4$ keys in a single day [603].

> The crux of the threat is sheer. Imagine a university network of 512 workstations, networked together. On campuses this would be a medium-sized network. They could be spread around the world, coordinating their activity through electronic mail. Assume each workstation is capable of running the algorithm at a rate of 15,000 encryptions per second. For the overhead of testing and changing keys, this comes down to 8192 tests per machine. To exhaust a 56-bit key with this setup would take 545 years (assuming the network was dedicated to twenty hours per day). Notice, however, that the same calculations give us hackers a chance in 200,000 of cracking a key in one day. Over a long weekend their odds increase to one in sixty-six days. The faster the more machines involved, the better their chance becomes.

These are not good odds for earning a living from horse-racing-key-cracking, but they are much better odds than the Government gives on its lotteries. For instance: “One-in-a-million!” “Could happen in a thousand years!” There is no longer possible to say such things: This is an acceptable risk?
