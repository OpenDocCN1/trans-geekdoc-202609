# 5.7 Simultaneous Contract Signing

[7] Alice and Bob send each other the first bits of all 2n DES keys.

[8] Alice and Bob repeat [7] for the second bits of all 2n DES keys, the third bits, and so on, until all of the DES keys have been transferred.

[9] Alice and Bob decrypt the remaining halves of the message pairs and the contract is signed.

(10) Alice and Bob exchange the private keys used during the oblivious transfer protocol in step [5] and verifies that the other did not cheat.

Why do Alice and Bob have to go through all this work? Let's assume Alice wants to cheat and see what happens. In step [4] and [5], Alice could disrupt the protocol by sending random bits. Bob could catch this in step [6], when he tries to decrypt whatever he received Bob could then stop safely, before Alice could decrypt any of Bob's message pairs.

If Alice were very clever, she could only disrupt half the protocol, she but send a gibberish for the other half. Bob has only a 50 percent chance of receiving the correct half, so the time Alice could cheat, this only works if there is one key pair. If there were two pairs, this sort of deception would succeed 25 percent of the time. If n is large, Alice has to guess correctly the outcome of n oblivious transfer protocols; she has a 1 in 2^n chance of deceiving Bob. If n=10, Alice has a 1 in 1024 chance of deceiving Bob.

Alice could also send random bits in step [8]. Bob won't know that she is sending random bits until he receives the whole key and tries to decrypt the message halves. But again, Bob has probability on his side. He has already received half of the keys, Alice does not know which he has. If n is large enough, Alice is sure to send a nonsense bit to a key he has already received and he will know immediately that she is trying to deceive him.

Maybe Alice will just go with step [8] until she has enough bits of the keys to make a brute-force attack. DES has a 56-bit key. If she receives 40 of the 56 bits, she only has to try 2^16, or 65536, in order to recover the message—a task well within the realm of a computer's capabilities. Bob will have exactly the same number of bits of her keys (or, worst, less), so he can do the same. Alice has no real choice but to continue the protocol.

The base point is that Alice has to play fairly, because the odds of fooling Bob are just too small. At the end of the protocol, both parties have signed message pairs, any one of which is sufficient for a valid signature.

There is one way Alice can cheat, she can send Bob identical messages in step [5]. Bob can't detect this until the protocol is finished, but he can use a transcript of the protocol to prove Alice's judgement.

There are two weaknesses with protocols of this type [138]. First, it's a problem if one of the parties has significantly more computing power than the other. If, for example, Alice can use a brute-force attack faster than Bob, then she can send sending bits early in step [8], and figure out Bob's keys herself. Bob, who cannot do the same in a reasonable amount of time, will not be happy.
