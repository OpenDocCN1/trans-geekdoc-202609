# Using Algorithms

Think of security—data security, communications security, information security, whatever—as a chain. The security of the entire system is only as strong as the weakest link. Everything has to be secure: cryptographic algorithms, protocols, key management, and more. If your algorithms are great but your random-number generator sticks, then any smart cryptyst is going to attack your system through the random-number generation. If you patch that hole but forget to securely erase a memory location that contains the key, a cryptyst will break your system via that route. It's not fair. As the designer of a secure system, you have to think of every possible means of attack and protect against them all, but a cryptyst only has to find one hole in your security and exploit it.

Cryptography is a part of security, and often a very small part. It is the mathematics of making a system secure, which is different from actually making a system security. Cryptography has "size queens"; people spend so much time arguing how long a key should be that they forget about everything else. If the secret police want what is on your computer, it is far easier for them to break into your computer and install a camera that can record what is on your computer screen than it is for them to randomize your hard drive.

Additionally, the traditional view of cryptography as "spy versus spy" technology is increasingly inappropriate. Used in the world is not protecting military secrets; it's in applications such as bank cards, pay-TV, road tolls, office building and access tokens, lottery terminals, and prepayment electricity meters [43,44]. In these applications, the role of cryptography is to make petty crime slightly more difficult, the paradigm of the well-founded adversary with a rabbit waren of cryptysts and roomy of computers just doesn't apply.
