In 1992 we would have told you that it was practical, but only faster than the quadratic sieve for numbers greater than 130 to 150 digits or so. Today it is known to be faster than the quadratic sieve for numbers well below 116 digits [472,635]. The general number sieve can factor a 512-bit number over 10 times faster than the quadratic sieve. The algorithm would require less than a year to run on an 1800-node Intel Paragon. And the general number field sieve is still getting faster. Mathematicians keep coming up with new tricks, new optimizations, there's no reason to think this trend won't continue. A related algorithm, the special number field sieve, can factor numbers of a certain specialized form—numbers generally used for cryptography—much faster than the general number field sieve cannot factor general numbers of the same size. It is not unreasonable to assume that the general number field sieve can be optimized to run this fast [1190]; it is possible that the NSA already knows how to do this. At a European Institute for System Security Workshop in 1991, the participants agreed that a 1024-bit modulus would be sufficient for long-term secrets through 2002 [150]. However, they warned: “Although the participants of this work best qualified in their respective areas, this statement [with respect to lasting security] should be taken with caution. This is good advice.

The wise crypt is ultra-conservative when choosing public-key lengths. To determine how long a key need requires you to look at both the intended security and lifetime of the key, and the current state-of-the-art of factoring. Today you need a 1024-bit number to get the level of security you got from a 512-bit number in the early 1980s. If you want your keys to remain for 20 years, 1024 bits is little too short.
