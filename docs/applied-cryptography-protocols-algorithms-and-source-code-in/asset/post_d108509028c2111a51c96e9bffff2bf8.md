
## 13.11 CA-1.1

MMB is dead [402]. Although no cryptanalysis has been published, this is true for several reasons. First, it was not designed to be resistant to linear cryptanalysis. The multiplication factors were chosen to be resistant to differential cryptanalysis, but the algorithm's authors were unaware of linear cryptanalysis.

Second, Eli Biham has an effective chosen-key attack [160], which exploits the fact that all rounds are identical and that the key schedule is a cyclic shift by 32 bits. Third, even though MMB would be very efficient in software, the algorithm would be less efficient than DES in hardware.

Daemen suggests that anyone interested in improving MMB should first do an analysis of modular cryptanalysis with respect to linear cryptanalysis and choose a new multiplication factor, and then make the constant C different for each round [402]. Then, improve the key scheduling by adding constants to the key rounds to remove the bias. He is not going to do it; he decides 3-Way instead (see Section 14.5).

## CA-1.1

CA is a block cipher built on cellular automata, designed by Howard Gutowitz [677,678,679]. It encrypts plaintext in 384-bit blocks and has a 1088-bit key (it' really two keys, a 1024-bit key and a 64-bit key). Because of the nature of cellular automata, the algorithm is most efficient when implemented in massively parallel integrated circuits.

CA-1.1 uses both reversible and irreversible automaton rules. Under a reversible rule, each state of the lattice carries one unique predecessor, while under an irreversible rule, each state can have many predecessors. During encryption, irreversible rules are iterated backward. To go back from a given state, one of the predecessor states is selected at random. This process can be repeated many times. Backward iteration serves to mix random information with the message information. CA-1.1 uses a particular kind of partly linear reversible rule, which is that a random predecessor for state can be found rapidly. Reversible rules are also used for some stages of encryption.

The reversible rules (simple permutations on sub-blocks of the state) are nonlinear. The irreversible rules are derived entirely from information in the key, while the reversible rules depend on key and the random information inserted during the stages of encryption with irreversible rules.

CA-1.1 is built around a block-link structure. That is, the processing of the message is partially segregated from the processing of the random information. This random information serves to link stages of encryption together. It can also be used to join together a ciphertext stream. The information in the link is generated as part of encryption.

Because CA-1.1 is a new algorithm it is to make any pronouncements on its security. Gutowitz discusses possible attacks, including differential cryptanalysis, but is unable to break the algorithm. As an incentive, Gutowitz has offered a $1000 prize to the "first person" who develops a procedure to break CA-1.1.

CA-1.1 is patented [678], but is available free for non-commercial use. Anyone interested in either licensing the algorithm or in the cryptanalysis prize should consider Howard Gutowitz, ESPCI, Laboratoire d'Electronique, 10 rue Vauquelin, 75005 Paris, France.

## 13.12 SKIPJACK

Skipjack is the NSA-developed encryption algorithm for the Clipper and Capstone chips [Sections 24.16 and 24.17]. Since the algorithm is classified Secret, its details have never been published. It will only be implemented in tamperproof hardware.

The algorithm is classified Secret, not because that enhances its security, but because the NSA doesn't want Skipjack being used without authorization.

Is Skipjack secure? The NSA wants to ensure that Skipjack is secure, but they haven't shared the details. Here's what is known:

- It is an iterative cipher.
- The block size is 64 bits.
- It can be used in ECB, CBC, OFB, or CFB modes.
- Under an assumption that the cost of processing power is halved every 18 months, it will be 36 years before breaking Skipjack by exhaustive search will be equal to breaking DES today. There is no significant risk that Skipjack will be broken by exhaustive search in the next 30–40 years.
- There is no significant risk that Skipjack can be broken through a shortcut method of attack, including differential cryptanalysis.
- There are no weak keys; there is no complementation property.

The experts, not having time to evaluate the algorithm to any great extent, instead evaluated NSA's own design and evaluation process. The strength of Skipjack against a cryptanalytic attack does not depend on the secrecy of the algorithm.

Of course, the panelists did not look at the algorithm long enough to come to any conclusions themselves. They all did to look at the results that the NSA showed them.

One unanswered question is whether the Skipjack keyspace is flat [see Section 8.2]. Even if Skipjack has no weak keys in the DES sense, sense of the keyscheduling process could make some keys stronger than others. Skipjack could have $2^{70}$ keys, far more than DES; the odds of choosing one of those keys at random would be 1 in 1000. I personally think the Skipjack keyspace is flat, but the fact that no one has ever said this publicly is worrisome.
