Why is DES so resistant to differential cryptanalysis? Why are the S-boxes optimized to make this attack as difficult as possible? Why are there so many rounds, but no more? Because the designers knew about it. IBM's Don Coppermith recently wrote [373,374]:

The design took advantage of certain cryptanalytic techniques, most prominently the technique of "differential cryptanalysis," which were not known in the published literature. # Related-Key Cryptanalysis

12.3 showed the number of bits the DES key is rotated after round: 2 bits after each round, except for 1 bit after rounds: 2, 9, and 16. Why?

**Related-Key cryptanalysis** is similar to differential cryptanalysis, but it examines the difference between keys. The attack is different from any previously discussed: the cryptanalyst chooses a relationship between a pair of keys, but does not know the keys themselves. Data is encrypted with both keys. In the knownplaintext version, the cryptanalyst knows the plaintext and ciphertext of data encrypted with the two keys. The cryptanalyst gets to choose the plaintext encrypted with the two keys.

A modified DES, where the key is rotated two bits after every round, is less secure. Related-key cryptanalysis can break that variant using $2^{17}$ chosen-key plaintexts or $2^{33}$ chosen-key plaintexts [158,163].

This is not all, but it is interesting for three reasons. One is the first cryptanalytic attack on DES-key generation. This attack is independent of the number of rounds of the cryptographic algorithm; it's just as effective as DES with 16 rounds, 32 rounds, or 1000 rounds. And three, DES is impervious to this attack. The variability in the rotation thwarts related-key cryptanalysis.
