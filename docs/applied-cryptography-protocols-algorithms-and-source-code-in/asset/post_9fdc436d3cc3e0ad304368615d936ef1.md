This shows two things. One, it is impossible for Victor to convince a third party of the proof's validity. And two, it proves that the protocol is zero-knowledge. In the case Peggy did not know the magic words, Victor will obviously learn anything from watching the recording. But since there is no way to distinguish a real recording from a faked recording, Victor cannot learn anything from the real proof—it must be zero knowledge.

The technique used in this protocol is called cut and choose, because of its similarity to the classic protocol for dividing anything fairly:

- (1) Alice cuts the thing in half.
- (2) Bob chooses one of the halves for himself.
- (3) Alice takes the remaining half.

It is Alice's best interest to divide fairly in step [1], Bob will choose whichever he wants in step [2]. Michael Rabin was the first person to use the cut-and-choose technique in cryptography [1882]. The concepts of **interactive protocol** and **zero-knowledge** technique were formalized later [626,627].

The cut-and-choose protocol works because there is no way Peggy can repeatedly guess which side Victor will come out of. If Peggy doesn't know the secret, she can only come out with what she knows. She has a 50 percent chance of guessing which side Victor will ask her in each round (sometimes called **accreditation** of the protocol), so she has a 50 percent chance of fooling him. The chance of her fooling him in 2 rounds is 25 percent, and the chance of her fooling him in all $n$ turns is 1 in $2^n$. After 16 rounds, Peggy has a 1 in 65,536 chance of Peggy's proofs are valid, then she must know the secret words to open the points between C and D. (The analogy isn't perfect. Peggy can simply walk in one side and out the other, there's no need for any cut-and-choose protocol. However, mathematical zero knowledge requires it.)

Assume that Peggy knows some information, and furthermore that the information is the solution to a hard problem. The basic zero-knowledge protocol consists of several rounds.

- (1) Peggy uses her information and a random number to transform the hard problem into another hard problem, one that is isomorphic to the original problem. She then uses her information and the random number to solve this new instance of the hard problem.
- (2) Peggy commits to the solution of the new instance, using a bit-commitment scheme.
- (3) Peggy reveals to Victor the new instance. Victor cannot use this new problem to get any information about the original instance or its solution.

## Graph Isomorphism

An example might go a long way to explain this concept, one comes from graph theory [619.622]. A graph is a network of lines connecting different points. If two graphs are identical for the nodes, they are called isomorphic. Finding whether two graphs are isomorphic can take for extremely large graph, centuries of computer time; it's one of NP-complete problems discussed in Section 11.1.

Assume Peggy knows the isomorphism between the two graphs, $G_1$ and $G_2$. The following protocol will convince Victor of Peggy's knowledge:

- (1) Peggy permutes $G_1$ to produce another $H$, that is isomorphic to $G_1$; Peggy knows the isomorphism between $G_1$ and $G_2$, she also knows the isomorphism between $H$ and $G_2$. For anyone else, finding an isomorphism between $G_1$ and $H$ or $H$ and $G_2$ and just is as hard as finding an isomorphism between $G_1$ and $G_2$.
- (2) Peggy sends $H$ to Victor.
- (3) Victor asks Peggy either to:
- [a] prove that $H$ and $G_1$ are isomorphic, or
- [b] prove that $H$ and $G_2$ are isomorphic.
- (4) Peggy complies. She either:
- [a] proves that $H$ and $G_1$ are isomorphic, without proving that $H$ and $G_2$ are isomorphic, or
- [b] proves that $H$ and $G_2$ are isomorphic, without proving that $H$ and $G_1$ are isomorphic.
- (5) Peggy and Victor repeat steps (1) through (5) n times.

## Hamiltonian Cycles

A variant of this was first presented by Blum [196]. A Hamiltonian cycle is a cycle in a graph that visits each vertex exactly once. Finding such a cycle is NP-complete, but there is a zero-knowledge proof for it.

The protocol involves Peggy permuting the vertices of the graph and then responding to a challenge to either show the Hamiltonian cycle in the permuted graph or to provide the permutation that maps the cycle back to the original graph. By repeating this process, Peggy can prove she knows the cycle without revealing it.
