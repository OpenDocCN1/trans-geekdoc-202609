Why not 10,000-bit keys? You can, but remember that you pay a price in computation time as your keys get longer. You want a key long enough to be secure, but short enough to be computationally usable.

Earlier in this section I called making predictions foolish. Now I am about to make some. Table 7.6 gives me recommendations public-key lengths, depending on how long you require the key to be secure. There are three key lengths for each, one secure against an individual, one secure against a major corporation, and the third secure against a major government.
Here some assumptions from [66]:

> We believe that we could acquire one thousand machines without superhuman or unethical factors. That is, we would not set an Internet worm or virus to find resources for us. Many organizations have several thousand machines on the net. Making of these facilities would require skillful diplomacy, but should not be impossible. Assuming the 5 mips average, and one year elapsed, it is not too unreasonable to embark on a project which would require half million mips years.

The project to factor the 129-digit number harnessed the power of the Internet [19], and they didn't even try hard. It isn't unreasonable to assume that a well-published project has 2 percent of the world's computing power for a year.

Assume a dedicated cryptanalyst can get his hands on 10,000 mips-years, a large corporation can get what a large government can get 10⁷ mipsyears. Also assume that computing power will increase by a factor of 10 every five years. And finally, assume that advances in factoring mathematics allows us to factor general numbers at the speeds of the special number field sieve. (This isn't possible yet, but the breakthrough could occur at any time.) Table 7.6 recommends different key lengths for security during different years.
