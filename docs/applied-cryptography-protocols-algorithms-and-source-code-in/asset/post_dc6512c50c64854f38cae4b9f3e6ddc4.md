
- (12) Alice decrypts all of the votes with her private key, checks to see that her vote is among the set of votes, signs all the votes, and sends the result to Bob, Carol, and Dave.

- (13) Everyone removes the random strings from each vote and tallies the votes.

Not only does this protocol work, it is also privacy-preserving. To see how this works, let's try to cheat. If someone tries to substitute one vote for another, it is detected immediately. If someone tries to stuff the ballot, Alice will notice in step (3). If Alice tries stuff the ballot, Bob will notice in step (4). If Bob tries the ballot, Carol or Dave will notice in step (5). If he replaces Carol's or Dave's vote, Carol or Dave will notice in step (6) or (7). If Bob is lucky and picks Alice's vote to replace, she won't notice until the second round. Still, she would not know that the votes are shuffled from one step to the other and it is impossible for one to backtrack through the protocol to determine who tampered with the votes.

Another form of cheating is to try to figure out who voted for whom. Because of the scrambling in the first round, it is impossible to backtrack through the protocol and link votes with voters. The removal of the random strings during the first round is crucial to preserving anonymity. If they are not removed, the scrambling of the votes could be reversed by re-encrypting them with the scrambler's public key. As the protocol stands, the confidentiality of votes is secure.

Even more strongly, because of the initial random string $R_1$, even identical votes are encrypted at every step of the protocol. No one knows the outcome of the vote until step (11).

What are the problems with this protocol? First, the protocol has an enormous amount of computation. The example described only four voters and it was complicated. This would never work in a real election with thousands of voters. Second, Dave learns the results of the election before anyone else does. While he still cannot affect the outcome, this gives him some power that the others do not have. On the other hand, this is also true with centralized voting.

The third problem is that Alice can copy anyone else's vote even though she does not know what it is. To see why this could be a problem, consider a three-person election between Alice, Bob, and Eve. Eve doesn't care about the result of the election, but she wants to know how Alice voted. So she copies Alice's vote, and the result of the election is guaranteed to be equal to Alice's vote.

## Other Voting Schemes

Many secure election protocols have been proposed. They come in two basic flavors: everyone's vote gets mixed so that no one can associate a vote with a voter.

One divided protocol: the idea is that each voter breaks his vote into several shares. For example, if the vote were "yes" or "no," a 1 could count "yes" and a 0 could count "no"; the voter would generate numbers whose sum was either 0 or 1. These shares are sent to balloting, each are encrypted and posted. Each center tallies the shares it receives (there are protocols to verify that the tally is correct!) and the final vote is the sum of all the voters. There are also protocols to ensure that each voter votes only once. Another protocol, by David Chaum, ensures that the election cannot be disrupted. However, this approach is not practical for large-scale elections. Another, more complex, voting protocol uses multiple rounds.

## 6.2 SECURE MULTARTY COMPUTATION

Secure multiparty computation is a protocol in which a group of people can get together and compute any function of many variables. Each participant in the group provides one or more variables, but no one learns about the inputs of other members other than what is obvious from the output. Here are some examples:

### Protocol #1

How can a group of people calculate their average without anyone learning the salary of anyone else?

- (1) Alice adds a random number to her salary, encrypts the result with Bob's public key, and sends it to Bob.
- (2) Bob decrypts Alice's result with his private key. He adds his salary to what he received from Alice, encrypts the result with Carol's public key, and sends it to Carol.
- (3) Carol decrypts Bob's result with her private key. She adds her salary to what she received from Bob, encrypts the result with Dave's public key, and sends it to Dave.
- (4) Dave decrypts Carol's result with his private key. He adds his salary to what he received from Carol, encrypts the result with Alice's public key, and sends it to Alice.
- (5) Alice decrypts Dave's result with her private key. She subtracts the random number from (1) to recover the sum of everyone's salaries.
- (6) Alice divides the result by the number of people (four, in this case) and announces the result.

This protocol assumes everyone is honest; they may be curious, but they follow the protocol. If any participant lies about his salary, the average will be wrong. A more serious problem is that Alice can misrepresent the result to everyone. She can subtract any number she likes in step (5), and no one would be wiser. Alice could be prevented from doing this by requiring her to commit her random number using any of the bit-commitment schemes from Section 4.9, and when she revealed her random number at the end of the protocol Bob could learn her salary.
