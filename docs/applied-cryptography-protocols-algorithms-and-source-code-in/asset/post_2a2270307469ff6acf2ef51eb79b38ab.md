Linear cryptanalysis is newer, and is still being improved. Notions of key ranking [1019] and multiple approximations [811,812] have been defined. Other work that extends the scale of linear cryptanalysis can be found in [1270]; [938] tries to combine linear and differential cryptanalysis into one attack. It is unclear what design techniques will protect against these sorts of attacks.

Knudsen has some progress, considering some necessary [but perhaps not sufficient] criteria for what he calls secure Feistel networks: ciphers that resist both linear and differential cryptanalysis [857]. Nyberg introduced linear cryptanalysis as an analogy to the concept of differentials from differential cryptanalysis [1180].

Interestingly, there seems to be a duality between differential and linear cryptanalysis. This duality appears both in the design of techniques to construct good differential characteristics and linear approximations [164,1018], and also in the criteria for making algorithms that are secure against attacks [307]. Exactly where this line of research will lead is still unknown. As a start, Daemen has developed an algorithm-design strategy based on linear and differential cryptanalysis [402].

## S-Box Design

The strength of various Feistel networks—and specifically their resistance to differential and linear cryptanalysis—is tied directly to their S-boxes. This prompted a spate of research on what constitutes a good S-box.

An S-box is a mapping of $m$-bit inputs to $n$-bit outputs. Previously I talked about one large lookup table of 64-bit inputs to 64-bit outputs, which would be a $64\times64$ S-box. An S-box with $m$-bit input and $n$-bit output is called a $m\times n$ bit-box. S-boxes are generally the only nonlinear step in an algorithm; they are what give block cipher its security. In the general, they are the better,

DES has eight $6\times4$ S-boxes. Khufre and Khafre have a single $8\times32$ S-box, LOKI has a $12\times8$ S-box, and Blowfish and CAST have $8\times32$ S-boxes. In IDEA the modular multiplication strategy is effectively the S-box; it is a $16\times16$ S-box. The larger the S-box, the harder is it to find useful statistics to attack using either differential or linear cryptanalysis [653,729,1626]. While random S-boxes are generally not optimal to protect against differential and linear attacks, it is easier to find strong S-boxes that are larger. Most S-boxes are nonlinear, nondegenerate, and have strong resistance to linear cryptanalysis—the fraction that does not go as rapidly as the number of bits decreases [1185,118,187].

The size of $m$ is more important than the size of $n$. Increasing the $n$ reduces the effectiveness of differential cryptanalysis, but greatly increases the effectiveness of linear cryptanalysis. In fact, if $n \geq 2^m - m$, then there is definitely a linear relation of the input and output bits of the S-box. If $n \geq 2^m$, then there is a linear relation of only the output bits [164].

## CHAPTER 14 Still Other Block Ciphers

Much of this work involves the study of **Boolean functions** [94,1098,1262,1408]. In order to be secure, the Boolean functions used in S-boxes must satisfy specific conditions. They should not be linear or affine, not even close to linear or affine [9,1177,1178,1188]. There should be a balance of zeros and ones, and no correlations between different combinations of bits. The output bits should behave independently when any input bit is complemented. These design criteria are also related to the study of **bent functions**: functions which can be optimally nonlinear. Although their definition is simple and natural, their study is very complicated [1344,1216,947,905,1176,1271,295,296,297,296,149,349,471,298]

One property that seems very important is the avalanche effect: how many output bits of an S-box change when some subset of the input bits change. It is easy to impose conditions on Boolean functions so that they satisfy certain avalanche criteria, but constructing such a handler is hard. The *strict avalanche criteria (SAC)* [1586]. See also [982,571,1262,126,399]. One paper attempts to look at all these criteria in terms of information leakage [1640].

A few years ago cryptographers proposed S-boxes so that the distribution table for each S-box is uniform. This would provide immunity against differential cryptanalysis by smoothing out the differences in any particular round [6,443,644,177]. LOCKI is an example of this design. However, this approach sometimes aids in differential cryptanalysis [172]. Actually, a better approach is making that the maximum differential is as small as possible. Kwango Kim proposed five criteria for the construction of S-boxes [83], similar to the design criteria for the DES S-boxes.

Choosing good S-boxes is not an easy task; there are many competing ideas on how to do it. Four approaches can be identified.

- 1. Choose random. It is easy that small random S-boxes are insecure, large random S-boxes may be good enough. Random S-boxes with eight or more inputs are quite strong [11,186,117]. Twelve-bit S-boxes are better. Even more strength is added if the S-boxes are both random and key-dependent. IDEA uses both large and key-dependent S-boxes.
- 2. Choose and test. Some others generate random S-boxes and test them for the required properties. See [9,729] for examples of this approach.
- 3. Man-made. This technique uses little mathematics: S-boxes are generated using intuitive techniques. true Barnett stated that “… theoretically interesting criteria are not sufficient [for choosing Boolean functions for S-boxes]...” and that “... ad hoc criteria are required” [1262].
- 4. Math-made. Generate S-boxes according to mathematical principles so that they have proven security against differential and linear cryptanalysis, and good diffusive properties. See [1179] for an excellent example of this approach.

There has been some call for the “math-made” and “man-made” approaches, but the real debate is still between S-boxes and key-dependent.
