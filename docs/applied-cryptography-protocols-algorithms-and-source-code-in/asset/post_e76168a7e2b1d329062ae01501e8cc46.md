
## 12.3 Security of DES

If DES were closed, then any $K_1$ and $K_2$ there would always be a $K_3$ such that
$E_{K_1}(E_{K_2}(P)) = E_{K_3}(P)$

In other words, the DES encryption operation would form a group, and encrypting a set of plaintext blocks with $K_1$, followed by $K_2$ would be identical to encrypting the blocks with $K_3$. Even worse, DES would be vulnerable to a meet-in-the-middle known-plaintext attack that runs in only $2^{n/2}$ steps [807].

If DES were pure, then for any $K_1, K_2$ there would always be a $K_4$ such that
$E_{K_1}(E_{K_2}(P)) = E_{K_4}(P)$

Triple encryption would be unbiased. [Note that a cipher is nearly necessarily, but a pure cipher is not necessarily.]

An early theoretical paper by Don Coppersmith gave some hints, but it wasn't enough [377]. Various cryptographers wrestled with this question "overwhelming evidence" that DES is not a group [807,371,808,1116,809], but it wasn't until 1992 that cryptographers proved that DES is not a group [293]. Coppersmith said that the IBM team knew it all.

## Key Length

IBM's original submission to NBS had a 112-bit key. By the time the DES became a standard, there was a 56-bit key. Many cryptographers argued for the longer key. Their arguments centered on the possibility of a brute-force attack—see Section 7.1.

In 1976, Diffie and Hellman argued that a special-purpose DES-parallel computer could recover the key in a day for $20 million. In 1981, Diffie upped this to a two-day search time and a cost of $50 million [491]. Diffie and Hellman argued that this was out of reach for everybody except like the NSA, but that by 1990 DES would be totally insecure [714].

Hellman [716] presented another argument against the small key: By trading memory space for time, it would be possible to speed up the searching process. He suggested the possibility of computing and storing $2^{56}$ possible results of encrypting a single plaintext under every possible key. Then to break an unknown key, all would be required for the cryptanalyst to insert the plaintext into the encryption stream, recover the resulting ciphertext, and look the key up. Hellman argued the cost of cracking matters at $5 million.

Arguments for the existence of a DES-cracker lurking in some government basement somewhere have continued. Several people pointed out that the time between failures for the DES would never be high enough to ensure the machine would work. This objection was shown to be superfluous in [1278]. Others suggested ways to speed the process even further and to reduce the effects of cipher failures.

Meanwhile, hardware implementations of DES slowly approached the million-encryptions-per-second requirement of Diffie and Hellman's special-purpose machine. In 1984, DES capable of performing 256,000 encryptions per second had been produced [533,534]. By 1987, keys performing 512,000 encryptions per second were being developed, and a version capable of checking over a million keys per second was feasible [738,1573]. And in 1993, Michael Wiener designed a $1 million machine that could perform a brute-force attack against DES in an average of 3.5 hours [see Section 7.1].

No one has publicly admitted this machine, although it is a reasonable assumption that someone has. A million dollars is not a lot of money to a large—or a medium-sized—country.

It was not until 1990 that two Israeli mathematicians, Biham and Shamir, discovered differential cryptanalysis, a technique that helped rest the question of key length. Before we discuss technique, let's turn to some other design criticisms of DES.

## Number of Rounds

Why not 32? After five rounds, the ciphertext bit is a function of plaintext bit and every key bit [107,1080], and after eight rounds, the ciphertext is a function of plaintext bit and every key bit [107,1080]. This was the avalanche effect.

Why not eight rounds? Over the years, variants of DES with reduced rounds were successfully attacked. Biham and Shamir showed that DES with three or four rounds was easily broken [982]. DES with six rounds was also broken [336]. Differential cryptanalysis showed that DES with fewer than 16 rounds could be broken by chosen-plaintext attack [714].

## Design of S-Boxes

In addition to the key length, the NSA was also concerned with the S-boxes. Since the 1970s, considerable effort has gone into analyzing the S-boxes. Many cryptographers noticed the S-boxes had some weaknesses [229]. The S-boxes were designed to be resistant to certain attacks, but the last bits of the S-box can be derived by changing the input bits [487]. Shamir noticed that the S-boxes entries were somewhat unbalanced, but it took another eight years before linear cryptanalysis revealed it [1423] that the S-boxes contained hidden features.

## 12.4 Differential and Linear Cryptanalysis

## Differential Cryptanalysis

In 1990, Eli Biham and Adi Shamir introduced differential cryptanalysis [167,168,171,172]. This is a new method of cryptanalysis, heretofore unknown to the public. Using this method, Biham and Shamir found a chosen-plaintext attack against DES that was more effective than brute force.

Differential cryptanalysis looks specifically at ciphertext pairs: pairs of ciphertexts whose plaintext have particular differences. It analyzes the evolution of these differences as the plaintexts propagate through DES when they are encrypted with the same key.

Simply, choose pairs of plaintexts with a fixed difference. The two plaintexts can be chosen at random, as long as they satisfy particular difference conditions; the cryptanalyst does not even have to know their values.(For DES, the term “difference” is defined using XOR. This can be different for different algorithms.)

## Additional Results

There were other attempts to analyze DES. One cryptographer looked at randomness on spectral tests [559]. Others analyzed sequences of linear factors, their attack failed after eight rounds [1297,336,531]. A 1987 unpublished attack by Donald Davies exploited the expansion permutation repeats bits into adjacent S-boxes, this attack is also impractical after eight rounds [172,429].
