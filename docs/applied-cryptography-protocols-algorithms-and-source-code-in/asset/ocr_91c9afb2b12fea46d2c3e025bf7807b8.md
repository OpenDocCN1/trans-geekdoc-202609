In 1988, Carl Pomerance designed a modular factoring machine, using custom VLSI chips [1259]. The size of the number you would be able to factor depends on how large a machine you can afford to build. He never built it.

In 1993, a 120-digit number was factored using the quadratic sieve, the calculation took 825 mips-years and was completed in three months real time [463]. Other results are [504].

Today's factoring algorithms use computers [302,955]. In factoring a 116-digit number, Arjun Lenstra and Mark Manasse used 400 mips-years—the time on an array of computers around the world for a few months.

In March 1994, a 129-bit number was factored using the double large prime variation of the multiple polynomial QS [66] by a team of mathematicians Lenstra. Volunteers on the Internet carried out the computation: 600 and 1600 machines over the course of eight months, probably the largest had processor ever used. To up on the state of the art of factoring, RSA Data Security, Inc. set up the RSA Factoring Challenge in March 1991 [532]. The challenge consists of a list of hard numbers, each the product of primes of roughly equal size. Each prime was chosen to be congruent 2 modulo 3. There are 42 numbers in the challenge, each of length 100 digits through 500 digits in steps of 10 digits (plus one additional number, 129 digits long). At the time of writing, RSA-100, RSA-110, RSA-120, and RSA-129 have been factored, all using QS. RSA-130 might be next (using the NFS), or the factoring champions might skip directly to RSA-140.

This is a fast-moving field. It is difficult to extrapolate factoring techniques because no one can predict advances in mathematical theory. Before discovered, many people conjectured that the QS was asymptotically as fast as the NFS was. They were wrong.

Near-term advances in the NFS are likely to come in the form of bringing down the constant: 1.923. Some numbers of a special form, like Fermat numbers, have a constant along the line of 1.5 [955,954]. If the numbers used in public cryptography had that kind of constant, 1024-bit numbers could be factored today. One way to lower the constant is to find better ways of representing numbers as polynomials with small coefficients. The problem hasn't been studied extensively yet, but it is probable that advances are coming [949].
