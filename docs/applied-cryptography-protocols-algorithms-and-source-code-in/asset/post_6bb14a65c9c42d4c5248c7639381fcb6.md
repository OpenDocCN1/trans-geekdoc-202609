
## CHAPTER 5 Advanced Protocols

## 5.7 SIMULTANEOUS CONTRACT SIGNING

### Contract Signing with an Arbitrator

Alice and Bob want to enter a contract. They've agreed on the wording, but neither wishes to sign unless the other signs as well. Face to face, this is easy: Both sign together. Over a distance, they could use an arbitrator.

- [1] Alice signs a copy of the contract and sends it to Trent.
- [2] Bob signs a copy of the contract and sends it to Trent.
- [3] Trent sends a message to Alice and Bob indicating that the other has signed the contract.
- [4] Alice signs two copies of the contract and sends them to Bob.
- [5] Bob signs both copies of the contract, keeps one for himself, and the other to Alice.
- [6] Alice and Bob inform Trent that each have a copy of the contract signed by both of them.
- [7] Trent tears up his copy of the contract with only one signature each.

This protocol prevents either of the parties from cheating. If Bob were refused to sign the contract in step [5], Alice could appeal to Trent for a copy of the contract already signed by Bob. If Alice were refused to sign in step [4], Bob could do the same. When Trent indicates he received both contracts in step [3], Alice and Bob know that the other is bound by the contract. If Trent does not receive both contracts in steps (1) and (2), he tears up the one he received and neither party is bound.

### Simultaneous Contract Signing without an Arbitrator (Face-to-Face)

If Alice and Bob were signing face-to-face, they could sign the contract this way [1244]:

- [1] Alice signs the first letter of her name and passes the contract to Bob.
- [2] Bob signs the first letter of his name and passes the contract to Alice.
- [3] Alice signs the second letter of her name and passes the contract to Bob.
- [4] Bob signs the second letter of his name and passes the contract to Alice.
- [5] This continues until both Alice and Bob have signed their names.

If you ignore the obvious problem with this protocol [Alice has a longer name than Bob], it works fine. After signing only one letter, Alice knows that no judge will bind her to the terms of the contract. But the letter is an act of good faith, and Bob responds with a similar act of good faith.

After each party has signed several letters, a judge could probably be convinced that both parties had signed the contract. The details are murky, though. Surely they are not bound after only the first letter; just as surely they are after they sign their entire names. At what point in the protocol do they become bound? After signing one-half of their names? Two-thirds of their names? Three-quarters

Since neither Alice nor Bob is certain of the exact point when she or he is bound, each has at least fear that she or he is throughout the protocol. At no point can Bob say: "You signed four letters and I only signed three. You are bound but I am not." Bob has no reason to continue with the protocol. Furthermore, the longer they continue, the greater the probability that a judge would rule that they are bound. Again, there is no reason not to continue with the protocol. After all, both wanted to sign the contract; they didn't want to sign before the other.

### Simultaneous Contract Signing without an Arbitrator (Not Face-to-Face)

This protocol uses the same sort of uncertainty [138]; Alice and Bob alternate baby steps toward signing until both have signed.

In the protocol, Alice and Bob exchange a series of signed messages of the form: "I agree with probability $p$, I am bound by this contract."

The recipient of this message can take it to a judge, and with probability $p$, the judge will consider the contract to be signed.

- 1) Alice and Bob agree on a date by which the signing protocol should be completed.
- 2) Alice and Bob decide on a probability difference that they are willing to live with. For example, Alice might decide that she is not willing to bound with a greater probability than 2 percent over Bob's probability. Call Alice's difference $a$; call Bob's difference $b$.
- 3) Alice sends Bob a signed message with $p=a$.
- 4) Bob sends Alice a signed message with $p=a+b$.
- 5) Let $p$ be the probability of the message received in the previous step from Bob. Alice sends Bob a signed message with $p'=p+a$ or 1, which is smaller.
- 6) Let $p$ be the probability of the message received in the previous step from Alice. Bob sends Alice a signed message with $p'=p+b$ or 1, which is smaller.
- 7) Alice and Bob continue alternating steps (5) and (6) until both have received messages with $p=1$ or until the date agreed to in step (1) has passed.

As the protocol proceeds, both Alice and Bob agree to bound the contract with a greater and greater probability. For example, Alice might define $a$ as 2 percent and Bob define his $b$ as 1 percent. [It would be nice if they had chosen larger increments, we will be here for a while.] Alice's first message states that she is bound with 2 percent probability. Bob might respond that he is bound with 3 percent probability. Alice's next message might state that she is bound with 5 percent probability and so on, until both are bound with 100 percent probability.

If Alice and Bob complete the protocol by the completion date, all is well. Otherwise, either party can take the contract to a judge along with the party's signed message. The judge randomly chooses a value between 0 and 1. If the value is less than the probability, both parties are bound. If the value is greater than the probability, both parties are not bound. This is what is meant by being bound to the contract with probability p.

### Simultaneous Contract Signing without an Arbitrator (Using Cryptography)

This basic protocol uses the same baby-step approach [529], although any symmetric algorithm will do.

- (1) Alice and Bob randomly generate $2n$ DES keys, grouped in pairs. The pairs are not special.
- (2) Alice and Bob generate $n$ pairs of messages, $L_i$ and $R_i$; "This is the left half of my signature" and "This is the right half of my signature," for example. $i$ runs from 1 to $n$. Each message will possibly include a digital signature of the contract.
- (3) Alice and Bob encrypt their messages in each of the $n$ pairs, the message with the left key and the message with the right key.
- (4) Alice and Bob send each other $2n$ encrypted messages, making clear which messages are halves of which pair.
- (5) Alice and Bob send each other every key using oblivious transfer protocol for each pair. Alice sends Bob the key she used to encrypt the message or the key used by the other, independently for each pair. Now Alice and Bob have one key in each pair, but neither knows which halves the other has.
- (6) Alice and Bob decrypt the message that they can using the keys they received to make sure that decrypted messages are valid.
- (7) Alice and Bob send each other the first bits of all $2n$ keys.
- (8) Alice and Bob repeat (7) for the second bits of all $2n$ keys, the third bits, and so on, until all the DES keys have been transferred.
- (9) Alice and Bob decrypt the remaining halves of the message pairs and the contract is signed.
- (10) Alice and Bob exchange the keys and verify that the other did not cheat.

Why do Alice and Bob have to go through all this work? Let's assume Alice wants to cheat and see what happens. In steps [4] and [5], Alice could disrupt the protocol by sending random bits. Bob could catch this in step [6] when he tries to decrypt whatever he received. Bob could then stop safely before Alice could decrypt any of Bob's message pairs.

If Alice were very clever, she could only disrupt half the protocol by sending gibberish for the other half. Bob has only a 50 percent chance of receiving the correct half, so Alice could cheat only if there is one key pair. If there were two pairs, this sort of deception would succeed 25 percent of the time. If $n$ is large, Alice has to guess correctly the outcome of $n$ oblivious protocols; she has a 1 in $2^n$ chance of deceiving Bob. If $n=10$, Alice has a 1 in 1024 chance of deceiving Bob.

Alice could also send random bits in step [8]. Bob won't know that she is sending random bits until he receives the whole key and tries to decrypt the message halves. But again, Bob has probability on his side. He has already received half of the keys, Alice does not know which. If $n$ is large enough, Alice is sure to send a nonsense bit to a key he has already received and he will know immediately that she is trying to deceive him.

Maybe Alice will just go with step [8] until she has enough bits of the keys to make a brute-force attack. DES has a 56-bit key. If she receives 40 of the 56 bits, she only has to try $2^{16}$, or 65536, in order to recover the message—a task well within the realm of a computer's capabilities. Bob will have exactly the same number of bits of her keys (or, worst, less), so he can do the same. Alice has no real choice but to continue the protocol.

The base point is that Alice has to play fairly, because the odds of fooling Bob are just too small. At the end of the protocol, both parties have signed message pairs, any one of which is sufficient for a valid signature.

There is one way Alice can cheat: she can send Bob identical messages in step [5]. Bob can't detect this until the protocol is finished, but he can use a transcript of the protocol to prove Alices judgment.

There are two weaknesses with protocols of this type [138]. First, it's a problem if one of the parties has significantly more computing power than the other. If, for example, Alice can use a brute-force attack faster than Bob, then she can send bits early in step [8], and figure out Bob's keys herself. Bob, who cannot do the same in a reasonable amount of time, will not be happy.
