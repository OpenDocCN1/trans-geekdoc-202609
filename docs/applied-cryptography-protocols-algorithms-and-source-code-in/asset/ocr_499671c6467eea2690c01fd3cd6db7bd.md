security. Consider the following (quite incomplete) “Top Ten Threats to Security in Real Systems” list, all are easier to exploit than to prevent.

+   1. The sorry state of software. Everyone knows that nobody knows how to write software. Modern systems are complex, with hundreds of thousands of lines of code, any one of them has the chance to compromise security. Fatal bugs may be far-removed from the security portion of the soft-ware.

+   2. Ineffective protection against denial-of-service attacks. Some cryptographic protocols allow anonymity. It may be especially dangerous to deploy anonymous protocols if they increase the opportunities for unden-ified vocals to disrupt service; anonymous systems therefore need to be especially resistant to denial-of-service attacks. Robust networks can easily support anonymity; consider that hardly anyone worries much about the millions of anonymous entry points to more robust networks like the telephone system or the postal service, where it's relatively diff-cult (or expensive) for an individual to cause large-scale failures.

+   3. No place to store secrets. Cryptos protect with smaller ones (keys). Unfortunate, modern computers aren't good at protecting the smallest secrets. Multi-user workstations can be broken into and their memories compromised. Standalone, single-user machines can be stolen or compromised through viruses that leak secrets asynchously. Remote servers, where there may be no user available to enter a passphrase (but see threat #5), are an especially hard problem.

+   4. Poor random-generation. Keys and session variables need good sources of unpredictable bits. A running computer has a lot of entropy in it but rarely provides with a convenient or reliable way to exploit it. A number of techniques have been proposed for getting true random numbers in software (taking advantage of unpredictability in things like I/O interval timing, clock and ticker skew, and air turbulence inside disk enclosures); but all are very sensitive to changes in the environments in which they are used.

+   5. Weak passphrases. Most cryptographic software addresses the key storage and key generation problems by relying on user-generated passphrase strings, which are presumed to be unpredictable to produce good key material and are also easy enough to remember that they do not require secure storage. When dictionary attacks are a well-known problem with short passwords, less is known about lines of attack against user-selected passphrase-based keys. Shannon tells us that English text has only over 1 bit of entropy per character, which would seem to most passphrases well within reach of brute-force search. Is less known, however about good techniques for enumerating passphrases in order to exploit this. Until we have a better understanding of how to attack passphrases, we really have no idea how weak or strong they are.
