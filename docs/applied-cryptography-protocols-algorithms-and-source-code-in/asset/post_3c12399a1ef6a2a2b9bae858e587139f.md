## 6.4 DIGITAL CASH

Cash is a problem. It's annoying to carry, it spreads germs, and people can steal it from you. Checks and credit cards have reduced the amount of physical cash flowing through society, but the complete elimination of cash is virtually impossible. It will never happen, drug dealers and politicians would never stand for it. Checks and credit cards have an audit trail; you can't hide where you go money.

On the other hand, checks and credit cards allow people to invade your privacy to a degree never before imagined. You might never stand for the police following your entire life, but the police can watch your financial transactions. They can see where you buy your gas, where you buy your food, where you call on the telephone—all without leaving their computer terminals. People need a way to protect their anonymity in order to protect their privacy.

Hopefully, there is a complicated protocol that allows for authenticated but anonymous lobbyists. Alice can transfer digital cash to Congressman Bob so that newspaper Eve does not know Alice's identity. Bob can then deposit electronic money into his bank account, even though the bank has no idea who Alice is. If Alice tries to buy coke with the same piece of digital cash she used to bribe Bob, the bank and if Bob tries to deposit the same piece of digital cash into two different accounts, he will be detected—but Alice will remain anonymous. Sometimes this is called anonymous digital cash to differentiate it from digital money with an audit trail, such as credit cards.

A great social need exists for this kind of thing. With the growing use of the Internet for commercial transactions, there is more call for network-based privacy and anonymity in business. (There are good reasons people are reluctant to send their credit card numbers over the Internet.) On the other hand, banks and governments seeming unwilling to give up the control the current banking system's audit trail provides. They'll have to, all it will take for digital cash to catch on is for some trustworthy institution to be willing to convert the digits to real money.

Digital cash protocols are complex. We'll build up to one, a step at a time. For more formal details, read [318,339,325,335,340]. Realize that this is just one digital cash protocol, there are others.

## CHAPTER 6 Esoteric Protocols

### Protocol #1

The first few are physical analogies of cryptographic protocols. This first protocol is a simplified protocol for anonymous money orders:

- (1) Alice prepares 100 anonymous money orders for $1000 each.
- (2) Alice puts each, and a piece of carbon paper, into 100 different envelopes. She gives them all to the bank.
- (3) The bank opens 99 envelopes and confirms that each is a money order for $1000.
- (4) Alice signs the one remaining envelope. The signature goes through the carbon paper to the money order. The bank hands the unopened envelope back to Alice, and deducts $1000 from her account.
- (5) Alice opens the envelope and spends the money order with a merchant.
- (6) The merchant checks for the bank's signature to make sure the money order is legitimate.
- (7) The merchant takes the money order to the bank.
- (8) The bank verifies its signature and credits $1000 to the merchant's account.

This protocol works. The bank doesn't know it's Alice. The bank makes the money order valid. If Alice tries to photocopy the money order, the bank will know. The bank will make the penalty.
