## Example of the Protocol

Assume they're RSA. Bob's public key is 7 and his private key is 23; $n=55$. Alice's secret value, $i$, is 4; Bob's secret value, $j$, is 2; [Assume that the values 1, 2, 3, and 4 are possible for $i$ and $j$].

- [1] Alice chooses $x=39$, and $c = E_b[39] = 19$.
- [2] Alice computes $c-i = 19-4 = 15$. She sends 15 to Bob.
- [3] Bob chooses the following 4 numbers:

```
$y_1 = D_b[15+1] = 26
$y_2 = D_b[15+2] = 18
$y_3 = D_b[15+3] = 2
$y_4 = D_b[15+4] = 39$
```

He chooses $p=31$ and calculates:

```
$z_1 = (26 \text{ mod } 31) = 26
$z_2 = (18 \text{ mod } 31) = 18
$z_3 = (2 \text{ mod } 31) = 2
$z_4 = (39 \text{ mod } 31) = 8$
```

He does all the verifications and confirms that the sequence is fine.

- [4] Bob sends Alice this sequence of numbers in this order:
$26, 18, 2 + 1, 8, 31 = 26, 18, 3, 9, 31$

- [5] Alice checks whether the 4th number in the sequence is congruent to $x$ mod $p$. Since $9 \neq 39 \text{ mod } 31$, then $i > j$.

- [6] Alice tells Bob.

This protocol can be used to create for secret auction over a computer network. They arrange themselves in a logical circle, and through individual pairwise comparisons, determine which is offering the highest price. In order to prevent people from changing their bids in mid-auction, some sort of bit-commitment protocol could be used. If the auction is a Dutch auction, then the highest bidder gets the item for his highest price. If it is an English auction, he gets the item for the second-highest price. (This can be determined by a second round of pairwise comparisons.) Similar ideas have applications in bargaining, negotiations, and arbitration.

## 23.15 PROBABILISTIC ENCRYPTION

The notion of probabilistic encryption was invented by Shafi Goldwasser and Silvio Micali [624]. Although its theory makes the most secure cryptosystem invented, its early implementation was impractical [625]. More recent implementations have changed.

## Probabilistic Encryption

The point behind probabilistic encryption is to eliminate any information leaked with public-key cryptography. Because a cryptanalyst can always encrypt random messages with a public key, he can get some information. Assuming he has ciphertext $C = E_k(M)$ and is trying to recover plaintext $M$, he can pick a random message $M'$ and encrypt it: $C' = E_k(M')$. If $C = C'$, he has guessed the plaintext. If it's wrong, he tries again.

Also, no partial information is leaked about the original message. With public-key cryptography, sometimes a cryptanalyst can learn about the bits: The XOR of bits 5, 7, and 39 is 1, and so on. With probabilistic encryption, even this type of information remains hidden.

Not a whole lot of information is to be gained, but there are potential problems with allowing a cryptyst to encrypt messages with your public key. Some information is leaked to the ciphertext every time he encrypts a message. No one really knows much.

Probabilistic encryption tries to eliminate the leakage. The goal is that no computation on the ciphertext, or on any other plaintexts, can the cryptanalyst get any information about the corresponding plaintext.

In probabilistic encryption, the encryption algorithm is more probabilistic than deterministic. In other words, a large number of ciphertexts will decrypt to a given plaintext, and the particular ciphertext under any encryption is random

```
$C_1 = E_k(M), C_2 = E_k(M), C_3 = E_k(M), \dots, C_i = E_k(M)
M = D_k(C_1) = D_k(C_2) = D_k(C_3) = \dots = D_k(C_i)$
```

With probabilistic encryption, a cryptanalyst can no longer encrypt random plaintexts looking for the correct ciphertext. To illustrate, assume the cryptanalyst has ciphertext $C_i$. Even if he guesses $M$ correctly, when he encrypts $E_k(M)$, the result will be a different ciphertext $C_h$. He cannot compare $C_i$ and $C_h$ and so not know that he has guessed the message correctly.

This is amazingly cool stuff. If a cryptanalyst has the public encryption key, the plaintext, and he cannot prove the ciphertext without the private decryption key. Even if he tries exhaustive search, he can only prove that every conceivable plaintext is a possible plaintext. Under this scheme, the ciphertext will always be larger than the plaintext. You can't get around this, it's the result of the fact that many ciphertexts decrypt to the same plaintext. The first probabilistic encryption scheme [625] resulted in a ciphertext so larger than the plaintext that it was unusable.

However Manuel Blum and Goldwasser had an efficient implementation of probabilistic encryption using Blum Shub [BBS] random-bit generator described in Section 17.9 [99]. The BBS is based on the theory of quadratic residues. In English, there are two primes, $p$ and $q$, that are congruent to 3 modulo 4. The private key, their product $pq = n$, is the public key. Mind your $p$ and $q$; the security of this scheme rests in the difficulty of factoring $n$. To encrypt a message, $M$, first choose $x$, relatively prime to $n$. then compute

```
$x_0 = x^2 \pmod n$
```

Use $x_0$ as the seed of the BBS pseudo-random generator and use the output of the generator as a stream cipher. XOR $M$, one bit at a time, with the output of the generator. The generator spits out bits $b_i$ (the least significant bit of $x_i$, where $x_i = x_{i-1}^2 \pmod n$), so

```
$M_1 \oplus b_1, M_2 \oplus b_2, M_3 \oplus b_3, \dots, M_i \oplus b_i$
```

Append the computed value, $x_i$, to the end of the message and you're done. The only way to decrypt this message is to recover $x_0$ and then set up the BBS generator to XOR with the ciphertext. Because the BBS generator is secure, the value $x_i$ is of no use to the cryptanalyst. Only who knows $p$ and $q$ can decrypt the message. In C, the algorithm to recover $x_0$ from $x_i$, is:

```c
int xo(int p, int q, int n, int t, int xt) {
int a, b, u, z;
/* we already know gcd(p, q) == 1 */
void extended_euclid(p, q, &a, &b);
u = modexp((p)/4, t, p-1);
v = modexp((q)/4, t, q-1);
w = modexp(xt, u, p);
z = modexp(xt, v, q);
return (w*w + a*p) % n;
}
```

Once you have $x_0$, decryption is easy. Just set up the BBS generator and XOR out the ciphertext. You can make this scheme even faster by using all the bits of $x_i$, not just the least significant bits. With this improvement, Blum-Goldwasser probabilistic encryption is faster than RSA while leaving no partial information about the plaintext. You can also prove that the difficulty of breaking this scheme is the same as the difficulty of factoring $n$.

On the other hand, this scheme is totally insecure against a chosen-ciphertext attack. From the least significant bits of quadratic residues, it is possible to calculate the square root of any quadratic residue. If you can do this, then you can factor. For details, consult [157, 356].

## 23.16 QUANTUM CRYPTOGRAPHY

Quantum cryptography taps the natural uncertainty of the quantum world. With it, you can create a communications channel where it is impossible to eavesdrop without disturbing the transmission. The laws of physics ensure this quantum channel: even if the eavesdropper can do whatever he wants, even if the eavesdropper has unlimited computing power, even if P = NP. Charles Bennett, Gilles Brassard, Claude Crépeau, and others who have expanded on this idea, describing quantum key distribution, quantum coin flipping, quantum bit commitment, quantum oblivious transfer, and quantum multiparty computation. Their work is described in [128,129,123,124,125,136,394,134,392,243,517,132,240,393,396]. The best overview of quantum cryptography can be found in [131] is a good nontechnical overview. A complete bibliography of quantum cryptography is in [237].

This would be still on the fringe of cryptography, by Bennett and Brassard actually went and built a working model of the thing [127,121,122]. Now we have experimental quantum cryptography.

So back, get yourself to learn, and relax. I'm going to explain what this is all about.

According to quantum mechanics, particles do not actually exist in any single place. They exist in several places at once, with probabilities of being in different places if someone looks. However, it's not until a scientist measures the particle that "collapses" into a single location. But you cannot measure every aspect (for example, position and velocity!) of a particle at the same time. If you measure one of two quantities, the act of measuring it destroys the possibility of measuring the other. The quantum world has a fundamental uncertainty and there's no way to avoid it.

That uncertainty can be used to create a secret key. As they tell, photons vibrate in some direction; up and down, left to right, or more like some angle.
