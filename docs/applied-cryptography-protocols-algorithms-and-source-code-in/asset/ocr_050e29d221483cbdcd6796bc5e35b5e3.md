# 12.3 Security of DES

If DES were closed, then any $K_1$ and $K_2$ there would always be a $K_3$ such that
$E_{K_1}(E_{K_2}(P)) = E_{K_3}(P)$

In other words, the DES encryption operation would form a group, and encrypting a of plaintext blocks with $K_1$, followed by $K_2$ would be identical to encrypting the blocks with $K_3$. Even worse, DES would be vulnerable to a meet-in-the-middle known-plaintext attack that runs in only $2^{n/2}$ steps [807]

If DES were pure, then for any $K_1, K_2$ there would always be a $K_4$ such that
$E_{K_1}(E_{K_2}(P)) = E_{K_4}(P)$

Triple encryption would be unbiased. [Note that a cipher is nearly necessarily, but a pure cipher is not necessarily.]

An early theoretical by Don Coppersmith gave some hints, but it wasn't enough [377]. Various cryptographers wrestled with this question "overwhelming evidence" that DES is not a group [807,371,808,1116,809], but it wasn't until 1992 that cryptographers proved that DES is not a group [293]. Coppersmith said that the IBM team knew it all.

# Key Length

IBM's original submission to NBS had a 112-bit key. By time the DES became a standard, there was 56-bit. Many cryptographers argued for the longer key. Their arguments centered on the possibility of a brute-force attack see Section 7.1.

In 1976, Diffie and Hellman argued that a special-purpose DES-parallel computer could recover the key in a day and $20 million. In 1981, Diffie upped this to a two-day search time and a cost of $50 million [491]. Diffie and Hellman argued that this was out of reach for everybody organizations like the NSA, but that 1990 DES would be totally insecure [714].

Hellman [716] presented another argument against the small key: By trading memory space for time, it would be possible to speed up the searching process. He suggested the possibility of computing and storing $2^{56}$ possible results of encrypting a single plaintext under every possible key. Then to break an unknown key, all would be required for the cryptanalyst to insert the plaintext into the encryption stream, recover the resulting ciphertext, and look the key up. Several people pointed out that the time between failures for the DES would never be high enough to ensure the machine would work. This objection was shown to be superfluous in [1278]. Others suggested ways to speed the process even further and to reduce the effects of cipher failures.
