In any instance, implementing Clipper raises enough problems to question its value in court. Remember, Clipper only works in OFB mode. Despite what you may have been told the contrary, this does not provide integrity or authentication. Imagine that Alice is on trial, and a Clipper-encrypted telephone call is part of the evidence. Alice claims she never made the call; the voice is not hers. The phone's compression is so bad that it is hard to recognize Alice's voice, the prosecution argues that since only Alice's escrow will decrypt the call it must have been from her.

Alice argues that the call was forged like [984,1339]: Given the ciphertext and the plaintext, it is possible to XOR to get the keystream. This keystream can then be XORed with an entirely different plaintext to form a forged ciphertext, which can then be converted to forged plaintext when fed into the Clipper decrypt. True or not, this argument could easily put any doubt in a jury's mind to regard the telephone call as evidence.

Another attack, called the Squeeze attack, allows Alice to frame Bob. Here's how [575]: Alice calls Bob using Clipper. She saves a copy of his LEAF as well as the session key. Then, she calls Carol [who is being wiretapped]. During the key, sets the session key to be identical to the one she used with Bob; this requires the phone, but it is not hard. Instead of sending her LEAF sends Bob's. It's a valid LEAF, so Carol's phone will not notice. Now she can say whatever she wants to Carol; when the police decrypt the LEAF, they will find that it is Bob's. Even if Bob isn't framed by Alice, the fact that he can claim this in court undermines the purposes of the scheme.

The law enforcement authorities of the United States should not be in the business of collecting information in criminal investigations that is useless in court. Even if escrow were a good idea, Clipper is a way of implementing it.

Capstone (also known as the MYK-80) is the other NSA-developed VLSI cryptographic chip that implements the U-government's Escrowed Encryption Standard [1153]. Capstone includes the following functions [1155,462]:

- The Skipiack algorithm in any of the four basic modes: ECB, CBC, CFB, and OFB.
- A public-key Key Exchange Algorithm (KEA), probably Diffie-Hellman.
- The Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA).
- The Secure Hash Algorithm (SHA).
- A general purpose exponentiation algorithm.
- A general purpose, random-number generator that uses a pure noise source.

Capstone provides the cryptographic functionality needed for secure electronic commerce and other computer-based applications. The first application of Capstone is in a PCMCIA card called Forteza (It was originally called Tessera until a company called Tessera, Inc. complied).

NSA had considered lengthening Capstone's LEAF checksum in production versions for use in Forteza, in order to fuel the brute-force attack against the LEAF previously discussed. Instead, they added a feature that resists the card after 10 incorrect LEAFS. This only increases the time to find a but valid LEAF by 10 percent, to 46 minutes. I am not impressed.

## 24.18 AT&T MODEL 3600 TELEPHONE SECURITY DEVICE (TSD)

AT&T Telephone Security Device (TSD) is the Clipper phone. Actually, there are four models of the TSD. One contains the Clipper chip, another an exportable proprietary AT&T encryption algorithm, the third contains a algorithm for domestic use plus the exportable algorithm, and the fourth contains the Clipper, and exportable algorithms.

TSDs use a different key for each telephone call. A pair of TSDs generate a key using Diffie-Hellman key exchange, independent of the Clipper chip. Since Diffie-Hellman incorporates no authentication, the TSD has two methods to thwart a man-in-the-middle attack.

The first is a secret. The TSD hashes the key and displays that hash on a small screen as Hex Digits. The会話 confirm that their screens show the same digits. The voice quality is good enough that they can recognize each other's voice.

Eve has a possible attack. Imagine in the middle of Alice and Bob's conversation. She uses one TSD on the line with Alice and a modified TSD on the line with Bob; in the middle she bridges the two phone calls. Alice tries to secure her. generates a key as normal, except that Eve is acting as Bob. Eve recovers the key, and using the modified TSD, forces the key she generates with Bob to have the same hash value. This attack may not sound very likely, but the TSD uses a variant of the interlock protocol to prevent it.

The TSD generates random numbers using a noise source and a chaotic amplifier with digital feedback. This generates a bit stream, which is fed through a post-whitening filter using the digital signal processor.

Despite all of this, the TSD manual does not mention security at all. In fact, it says AT&T makes no warranty that TSD will prevent any attack.
