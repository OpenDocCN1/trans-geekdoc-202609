Not all hard problems are used for zero-knowledge proofs, but a lot of them.

# Graph Isomorphism

An example might go a long way to explain this concept, one comes from graph theory [619.622]. A graph is a network of lines connecting different points. If two graphs are identical for the nodes, they are called isomorphic. Finding whether two graphs are isomorphic can take for extremely large graph, centuries of computer time; it's one of NP-complete problems discussed in Section 11.1.

Assume Peggy knows the isomorphism between the two graphs, $G_1$ and $G_2$. The following protocol will convince Victor of Peggy's knowledge:

+   (1) Peggy permutes $G_1$ to produce another $H$, that is isomorphic to $G_1$; Peggy knows the isomorphism between $G_1$ and $G_2$, she also knows the isomorphism between $H$ and $G_2$. For anyone else, finding an isomorphism between $G_1$ and $H$ or $H$ and $G_2$ and just is as hard as finding an isomorphism between $G_1$ and $G_2$.

+   (2) Peggy sends $H$ to Victor.

+   (3) Victor asks Peggy either to:

+   [a] prove that $H$ and $G_1$ are isomorphic, or

+   [b] prove that $H$ and $G_2$ are isomorphic.

+   (4) Peggy complies. She either:

+   [a] proves that $H$ and $G_1$ are isomorphic, without proving that $H$ and $G_2$ are isomorphic, or

+   [b] proves that $H$ and $G_2$ are isomorphic, without proving that $H$ and $G_1$ are isomorphic.

+   (5) Peggy and Victor repeat steps (1) through (4) $n$ times.
