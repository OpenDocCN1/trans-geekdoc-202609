# 16.4 Stream Ciphers Using LFSRs

# Other Attacks

There are other attacks against keystream generators. The linear consistency test attempts to identify some subset of the encryption key using matrix techniques [1638]. There is also the meet-in-the-middle attack [39,41]. The linear drome algorithm relies on being able to write a fragment of the output sequence as a linear equation [1636,1637]. There is the best approximation attack [502] and the derived sequence attack [42]. The techniques of differential cryptanalysis have even been applied to stream ciphers [501], as linear cryptanalysis [631].

The basic approach to designing a keystream generator using LFSRs is simple. First you take one or more LFSRs, generally of different lengths and with feedback polynomials.[If the lengths are relatively prime and the feedback polynomials are all prime, the generator is maximal length.] Key is the initial state of the LFSRs. Every time you want a bit, shift the LFSR once [this times is called calling]. The output bit is a function, preferably a nonlinear function, of some of the bits of the LFSRs. This function is the combining function, and the whole generator is a combination generator.[If the output bit is a function of a single LFSR, the generator is called a filter generator.] The theoretical background for this kind of thing was laid down by Selmer and Neal Zielier [1647].

Complications have when generators have locked at different rates, the clicking of one generator depends on the output of another. These are all electronic versions of prev WWII cipher machines, and are called clock-controlled generators [641]. Clock control can be feedback, where the output of one LFSR controls the clicking of another, or feedback, where the output of one LFSR controls its own clock.

Although these generators are great, in theory, susceptible to embedding and probabilistic correlation attacks [634,632], many are secure for now. Additional attacks on clock-controlled shift registers are in [89].

Jan Cassells, the head of pure mathematics at Cambridge and a former Bletch Park cryptanalyst, said that "cryptography is a mixture of mathematics and middle, until the mathematics can be used against you." What meant in stream ciphers, you need some kind of mathematical structure—such as a LFSR—to guarantee maximal-length and other properties, and some complicated nonlinear middle to stop someone from getting at the register and solving it. This advice also holds true for block algorithms.

What follows is a smattering of LFSR-based keystream generators that have appeared in the literature. I don't know if any of them have been used in actual cryptographic products. Most of them are of theoretical interest only. Some have been broken, may still be secure.
