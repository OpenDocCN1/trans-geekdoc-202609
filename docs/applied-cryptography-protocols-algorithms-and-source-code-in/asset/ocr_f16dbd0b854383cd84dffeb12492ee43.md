Key escrow has considerable disadvantages. The user has to trust the escrow agents' security procedures, as well as the integrity of the people involved. He has to trust the escrow agents not to change their policies, the government not to change its laws, and those with lawful authority to keep his keys so lowfully and responsibly. A major terrorist attack in New York, what sorts of limits the police would be thrown aside in the aftermath?

It is hard to imagine escrow encryption schemes as advocates imagine without some kind of legal pressure. The obvious next step is a ban on the use of non-encrypted encryption. This is probably the only way to technologically sophisticated criminals and terrorists to use it. It's not clear how outlawing non-encrypted cryptography will be, or how it will affect cryptography as an academic discipline. Can I research software-oriented cryptography algorithms without having software non-encrypted devices in my possession; will I need a special license?

And there are legal questions. How do escrow agents' liability, should some encrypted keys get out? If the U.S. government is trying to protect the escrow agencies, if the secret was compromised by either the user or the escrow agency, then it must have the user?

What if a key-escrow service, either government or commercial, had its entire escrow database lost? What if the U.S. government tried to keep quiet for a while? Clearly, this would have an impact on users' willingness to use key escrow. If it is not voluntary, a couple of scandals like this would increase political pressure to make it voluntary, or to add new regulations to the industry.

Even more dangerous is a scandal where it becomes political opponent of the current administration, or some outspoken critic of intelligence or police agencies have under surveillance for years. This could raise public sentiment strongly against encrypted escrow.

If signature keys are escrowed as well as encryption keys, there are additional issues. Is it acceptable for the authorities to use signature keys to run operations against suspected criminals? Will the authenticity of signatures based on escrow keys be accepted in courts? What recourse do users have if the authorities actually do use signature keys to sign some unfavorable contract, to help out a supported industry, or to just steal money?

The globalization of cryptography raises an additional set of questions. Will escrow policies be compatible across national borders? Will multi-national corporations have to keep separate escrow keys in every country to stay in compliance with the various local laws? Without some kind of compatibility, one of the supposed advantages of key-escrow schemes (international use of strong encryption) falls apart.
