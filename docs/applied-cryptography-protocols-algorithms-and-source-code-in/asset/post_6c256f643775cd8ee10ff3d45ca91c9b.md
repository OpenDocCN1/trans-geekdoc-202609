- (5) The voter chooses one of the votes (ah, democracy) and encrypts it with the CTF's public key.

A malicious voter, call Mallory, cannot cheat this system. The blind signature protocol ensures that his votes are unique. If he tries to send in the same vote twice, the CTF will notice the duplicate serial number in step [7] and throw out the second vote. If he tries to get multiple votes signed in step (2), the CTF will discover this in step (3). Mallory cannot generate his own votes because he doesn't know the facility's private key. He can't intercept and change other people's votes for the reason.

The cut-and-choose protocol in step (3) is to ensure that the votes are unique. Without that step, Mallory could create a set of votes that are the same for the identification number, and have them all validated.

A malicious CTF cannot figure out how individuals voted. The blind signature protocol prevents the facility from seeing the serial numbers on the votes before they are, the CTF cannot link the blinded vote signed with the vote eventually count. Publishing a list of serial numbers and their votes allows voters to confirm that their vote was tabulated correctly.

There are problems. If step (6) is not anonymous and the CTF can record who sent in which vote, then it can figure out who voted. Also, while the CTF may not be able to link votes to individuals, it can generate a large number of signed, valid votes by submitting those itself. And if Alice discovers that the CTF changed her vote, she has no way to prove it. A similar protocol, to solve these problems, is [1195,1370].

## Voting with Two Central Facilities

One solution is to split the CTF in two. Neither party would have the power to cheat on its one.

The following protocol uses a Central Legitimacy Agency (CLA) to certify voters and a separate CTF to count votes [1373].

- [1] Each voter sends a message to the CLA asking for a validation number.
- [2] The CLA sends the voter back a random validation number. The CLA maintains a list of validation numbers. The CLA also keeps a list of the validation numbers' recipients, in case someone tries to vote.
- (3) The CLA sends the list of validation numbers to the CTF.
- (4) Each voter chooses a random identification number. He creates a message with that number, the validation number he received from the CLA, and his vote. He sends this message to the CTF.
- (5) The CTF checks the validation number against the list it received from the CLA in step [3]. If the validation number is there, the CTF crosses it off (to prevent someone from voting twice). The CTF adds the identification number to the list of people who voted for a particular candidate and adds one to the tally.
- (6) After all votes have been received, the CTF publishes the outcome, as well as lists of identification numbers and for whom their owners voted.

Like the previous protocol, each voter can look at the list of identification numbers and find his. This gives him proof that his vote was counted. Of course, all messages passing the parties in the protocol should be encrypted and signed to prevent someone from impersonating else or intercepting transmissions.

The CTF cannot modify votes because each voter will look for his identification string. If a voter doesn't find his identification string, or finds his string in a tally other than the one he voted for, he will immediately know there was foul play. The CTF cannot stuff the ballot box because many voters have been certified and their validation numbers, and will detect any modifications.

Mallory, who is not an eligible voter, can try to cheat by guessing a validation number. This threat can be minimized by making the number of possible validation numbers larger than the number of actual validation numbers: 100-digit numbers for a million voters, for of course, the validation numbers must be generated randomly.

Despite this, the CLA is a trusted authority in some respects. It can certify ineligible voters. It can certify eligible voters multiple times. This risk could be minimized by having the CLA publish a list of certified voters (but not their validation numbers). If the number of voters on this list is less than the number of votes tabulated, something is awry. However, if more voters were certified than votes tabulated, it probably means some certain people did not voting. If the two of them get together, they could correlate databases and figure out who voted for whom.

## Voting with a Single Central Facility

A complex protocol can be used to overcome the danger of collusion between the CLA and the CTF [1373]. This protocol is identical to the previous one, with two modifications:

- The CLA and CTF are one organization, and
- ANDOS (see Section 4.13) is used to anonymously distribute validation numbers in step [2].

Since the anonymous key distribution protocol prevents the CTF from knowing which voter voted, there is no way for the CTF to correlate validation numbers.

## Improved Voting with a Single Central Facility

This protocol also uses ANDOS [175]. It satisfies all six requirements of a good voting protocol. It doesn't satisfy the seventh requirement, but you can solve this with blind signatures.

Here's the protocol:

- (1) The CTF publishes a list of voters.
- (2) Within a specified deadline, each voter tells the CTF whether he intends to vote.
- (3) The CTF publishes identification numbers.
- (4) Each voter generates a public and private key pair.
- (5) Each voter generates a new identification number, $I'$, and sends to the CTF:
```
$E_k(v)$
```
- (6) The CTF acknowledges receipt of the vote by publishing:
```
$E_d\{v\}$
```
- (7) Each voter sends the CTF:
```
$E_d\{v\}$
```
- (8) The CTF decrypts the votes. At the end of the election, it publishes the results, and for each vote:
- (9) If a voter observes that his vote is not counted, he can prove it.
- (10) If a voter wants to change his vote, he sends the CTF:
```
$E_d\{v'\}$
```

A different voting protocol uses blind signatures instead of ANDOS, but is essentially the same.
