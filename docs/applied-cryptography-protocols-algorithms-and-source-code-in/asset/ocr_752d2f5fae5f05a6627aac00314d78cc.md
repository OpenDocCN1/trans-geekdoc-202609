# Preface

Chapters 7 through 10 [Part II] discuss cryptographic techniques. All four chapters in this section are important for even the most basic uses of cryptography. Chapters 7 and 8 are about keys: how long a key should be in order to be secure, how to generate keys, how to store keys, how to dispose of keys, and so on. Key management is the hardest part of cryptography and the Achilles' heel of any other system. Chapter 9 discusses different ways of using cryptographic algorithms, and Chapter 10 gives the odds and ends of algorithms: how to choose, implement, and use algorithms.

Chapters 11 through 23 [Part III] list algorithms. Chapter 11 provides the mathematical background. This chapter is only required if you are interested in public-key algorithms. If you want to implement DES (or similar), you can skip ahead. Chapter 12 discusses DES: the algorithm, its history, its security, and some variants. Chapters 13, 14, and 15 discuss other block algorithms; if you want more secure than DES, skip to the section on IDEA and triple-DES. If you want to read about a bunch of algorithms, some of which may be more secure than DES, read the whole chapter. Chapters 16 and 17 discuss stream algorithms. MD5 and SHA are the most common, although Chapter 18 focuses on one-way hash functions, MD5 and SHA. Chapter 19 discusses public-encryption algorithms, Chapter 20 discusses public-digital signature algorithms, Chapter 21 discusses public-key identification algorithms, and Chapter 22 discusses public-key exchange algorithms. The important algorithms are RSA, DSA, Fiat-Shamir, and Diffie-Hellman. Chapter 23 has esoteric public-key algorithms and protocols; the math in this chapter is quite complex, so wear your seat.

Chapters 24 and 25 [Part IV] turn to the real world of cryptography. Chapter 24 discusses some of the contemporary implementations, algorithms, and protocols, while Chapter 25 touches on many of the political issues surrounding cryptography. These chapters are no meant to be comprehensive.

Also included are source code listings for 10 algorithms discussed in Part III. I was unable to include all the code I wanted to due to space limitations, and cryptographic source code cannot otherwise be exported. (Amazingly however, the State Department allowed the first edition of this book with source code, but denied for export a computer disk with the exact same source code on it.) An associated source code list includes much more code than I could fit due to military restriction. I can only send source code to U.S. and Canadian citizens living in the U.S. and Canada, but hopefully that that will change someday. If you are interested in implementing or playing with the cryptographic algorithms in this book, get the last page of the book for details.

One criticism of this book is that its encyclopedic nature takes away from its readability. This is true, but I wanted to provide a single reference for those who might come across an algorithm in the academic literature or in a product. For those who are more interested in a tutorial, I apologize. A lot is done in the field; this is the first time so much of it has been gathered between two covers. So, space forced me to leave many things out. I covered topics that I felt important, practical, or interesting. If I couldn't cover a topic in depth, I have references to articles and papers that did.
