The “natural law” of cryptography is that it is much easier to use than it is to break. To take a hand-waving example, think about basic encryption. Adding a single bit to a key, like from a 64-bit key to a 65-bit key, adds at most a small amount of work to encrypt and decrypt. But it doubles the amount of work to break. Or, more mathematically, encryption and decryption work grows linearly with key length, but cryptanalysis work grows exponentially. It is always easier for the communicator over the defender.

It turned out that this was all true, but less important than we had believed. Few years later, we realized that cryptography was just math, and that math has no agency. In for cryptography to actually do anything, it has to be embedded in a protocol, written in a programming language, embedded in software, run on an operating system and computer attached to a network, and used by living people. All of these adds vulnerabilities and—more importantly—they’re more conventionally balanced. That is, there is no inherent advantage for the defender over the attacker. Spending more effort on either result in linear improvements. Even worse, the attacker generally has an inherent advantage over the defender, at least today.

So when we learned about the NSA through the documents provided by Edward Snowden, we found that at the time the NSA hacks the computers to decrypt the encryption by circumventing it. The NSA finds vulnerabilities. It exploits implementations. It exploits weak or default keys. Or it “filtrates”—NSA-speaks for steals—keys. It has some mathematics that we do not know about, but that’s the exception. The most amazing thing about the NSA as revealed by Snowden is that it is not made of magic.

This doesn't mean that cryptography is useless: for it. What cryptography raises the cost and risk of attack. Data zipping around the Internet encrypted can be collected with minimal effort. Encrypted data has to be targeted individually. The NSA—or whoever is your data—needs to target individually and attack your computer and network specifically. That takes time and manpower, and is inherently risky. No organization has budget to do that to everyone, they have to pick and choose. With ubiquitous encryption won't eliminate targeted collection, it has the potential to make bulk collection infeasible. The goal is to leverage the economics, the politics, and the math.

There’s one more problem,one that the Snowden documents have illustrated. Well, you can trust politics, but politics can also trust technology. Governments can use laws to safeguard cryptography. They can sabotage the cryptographic standards in the communications and computer systems you use. They can deliberately insert backdoors into those same systems. They can do all of those, and then forbid the corporations implementing these systems to tell you about it. We know the NSA does this; we have to assume that other governments do the same thing.
