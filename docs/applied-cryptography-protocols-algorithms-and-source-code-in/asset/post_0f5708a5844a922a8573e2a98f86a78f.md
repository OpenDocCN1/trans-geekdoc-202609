## Karn-Green-Hellman

This scheme uses matrix multiplication [818]. Choose $n+1$ $m$-dimensional vectors, $V_0, V_1, \dots, V_n$ such that any possible $m \times m$ matrix formed of those vectors has rank $m$. The vector $U$ is a vector of dimension $m+1$.

$M$ is the matrix product $UV_i$. The shadows are $U-V_i$, where $i$ is a number from 1 to $n$.

Any $m$ shadows can be used to solve the $m \times m$ system of linear equations, where the unknowns are the coefficients of $U$. $UV_i$ can be computed from $U$. Any $m-1$ shadows cannot solve the system of linear equations and therefore recover the secret.

## Advanced Threshold Schemes

The previous examples illustrate the simple threshold schemes: Divide a secret into $n$ shadows such that any $m$ can be used to recover the secret. These algorithms can be used to create more complicated schemes. The following examples will use Sham's algorithm, although any of the others will work.

To create a scheme where one person is more important than another, if it takes five shadows to recreate a secret and one person has three shadows while else has only one, then that person and two other people can recreate the secret. Without that person, it takes five to recreate.

Two or more people could get multiple shadows. Each person could have a different number of shadows. No matter how the shadows are distributed, any $m$ of them can be used to reconstruct the secret. With $m-1$ shadows, it be one person or a hostile ele, cannot do it.

In other types of schemes, imagine a scenario with two hostile delegations. They share the secret so that two people from the 7 Delegation A and 3 people from the 12 Delegation B are required to reconstruct the secret. Make a polynomial of degree 3 that is the product of a linear expression and a quadratic expression. Give everyone from Delegation A a shadow that is the result of an evaluation of the linear equation, give everyone from Delegation B a shadow that is the evaluation of the quadratic equation.

Any two shadows from Delegation A can be used to reconstruct the linear equation, but no matter how many shadows the group has, they cannot get any about the secret. The same is true for Delegation B: They can get three shadows together to reconstruct the quadratic equation, but they cannot get any information necessary to reconstruct the secret. Only when the two delegations share their equations can be multiplied to reconstruct the secret.

In general, any type of sharing scheme that can be imagined is implemented. All you have to do is envision a system of equations that corresponds to the particular scheme. Some excellent papers on generalized secret-sharing schemes are [1462,1463,1464].

## Sharing a Secret with Cheaters

This algorithm modifies the $[m, n]$-threshold scheme to detect cheaters [1529]. I demonstrate this using the Lagrange scheme, although it works with the others as well.

Choose a prime, $p$, that is both larger than $n$ and larger than

`$s - 1|m - 1|/e + m$`

where $s$ is the largest possible secret and $e$ is the probability of successful cheating. You can make $e$ as small as you want, it just makes the computation more complex. Construct your shadows as before, except of using 1, 2, 3, $\dots, n$ for $x_u$, choose random numbers between 1 and $p$ for $x_v$.

Now, when Mallory sneaks into the secret reconstruction meeting with his false secret, share has a high probability of not being possible. An impossible secret is, of course, a fake secret. See [1529] for the math.

Unfortunately, when Mallory is exposed as a cheater, he still learns the secret (assuming that there are $m$ other value shards). Another protocol, from [1529,975], prevents that. The basic idea is to have a series of $k$ secrets, such that none of the participants knows beforehand which is secret. Each secret is larger than the one before, except for the secret. The participants combine their shadows to generate- one after the other, until they create a secret that is less than the previous secret. That's the correct one.

This scheme will expose cheaters early, before the secret is generated. There are complications when the participants deliver their shadows one at a time; refer to the papers for details. Other papers on the detection and prevention of cheaters in threshold schemes are [355,114,270].

## 23.3 SUBLIMINAL CHANNEL

This subliminal channel (see Section 4.2), designed by Gustavus Simmons [1458,1459,1460], uses the Schnorr-Shamir identification scheme (see Section 20.5). In the original scheme, the sender (Alice) chooses a public modulus, $n$, and a private key, $k$, such that $n$ and $k$ are relatively prime. Unlike the original scheme, $k$ is shared between Alice and Bob, the recipient of subliminal message. The public key is calculated:

`$h = k \pmod n$`

If Alice wants to send subliminal message $M$ by means of innocuous message $M'$, she confirms that $M'$ and $n$ are relatively prime, and that $M$ and $n$ are relatively prime. Alice calculates:

$S_1 = \frac{1}{2} (M' + M) \pmod n$
$S_2 = \frac{1}{2} (M' - M) \pmod n$

Together, the pair $S_1$ and $S_2$ is the signature under the Schnorr-Shamir scheme and the carrier of subliminal message. The warden can authenticate the message (it is always possible that Walter can make his own messages). If the message is authentic, the receiver can recover the subliminal message using this formula:

$M = S_1 - S_2$

This works, but remember that basic Schnorr-Shamir has been broken.

## ElGamal

Simmons's second subliminal channel, described in [1407,1473], is based on the ElGamal signature scheme [see Section 19.6]. Key generation is the same as basic ElGamal signature scheme. First choose a prime, $p$, and two random numbers, $g$ and $r$, such that both $g$ and $r$ are less than $p$. Then calculate:

$K = g^r \pmod p$

The public key is $K$, and $g$. The private key is $r$. Besides Alice, Bob also knows $r$; it is the key used to send and receive subliminal message. To send subliminal message $M$ using innocuous message $M'$, $M$ and $p$ must be relatively prime to each other, and $p-1$ must be relatively prime. Alice calculates:

$X = g^m \pmod p$
$M' = rX + MY \pmod{p-1}$

As in basic ElGamal scheme, the signature is the pair: $X$ and $Y$. Walter can verify the ElGamal signature. He confirms that

$K^x = g^{rx} \pmod p$

Bob can recover the subliminal message. First he confirms that

$(g^r)^X = g^{rX} \pmod p$

If it does, he accepts the message as genuine (not from Walter). Then, to recover $M$, he computes:

$M = Y^{-1}(M' - rX) \pmod{p-1}$

For example, let $p=11$ and $g=2$. The private key, $r$, is chosen to be 8. This means the public key, which Walter can use to verify the signature, is $g^r \pmod p = 2^8 \pmod{11} = 3$. To send the subliminal message $M=9$, using innocuous message $M'=5$, Alice identifies 9 and 11 are relatively prime and that 5 and 11 are relatively prime. She also confirms that 9 and $11-1=10$ are relatively prime, so calculates

$X = g^m \pmod p = 2^9 \pmod{11} = 6$

Then, she solves the following equation for $Y$:

$5 = 8 \cdot 6 + 9 \cdot Y \pmod{10}$

$Y=3$, the signature is the pair, $X$ and $Y$: 6 and 3. Bob confirms

$(g^x)^X \equiv g^{xy} \pmod p$
$(2^9)^6^3 \equiv 2^5 \pmod{11}$

It does (do the value if you don't trust you), then he recovers the subliminal message by calculating:

$M = (Y^{-1}(M' - rX) \pmod{p-1} = 3^{-1}(5 - 8 \cdot 6) \pmod{10} = 7(7) \pmod{10} = 49 \pmod{10} = 9$

## ESIGN

A subliminal channel can be added to ESIGN [1460] (see Section 20.6), and the public key. In ESIGN, the secret key is a pair of large prime numbers, $p$ and $q$, and $n=p^q$. With a subliminal channel, the private key is three primes, $p, q$, and $r$, and the public key is $n$, such that

$n = p^q$

The variable, $r$, is the extra piece of information that Bob needs to recover the subliminal message. To send a normal message, Alice first picks a random number, $x$, such that $x$ is less than $pqr$ and computes:

$w$, the largest integer that is larger than $H(m) - x^k \pmod{n/pqr}$
$s = x + |w|x^{k-1} \pmod{pqr}$

$H(m)$ is the hash of the message, $k$ is a security parameter. The value is $s$. To verify the signature, Bob computes $s^k \pmod{n}$. He also computes $a$, which is the least integer larger than the number of bits of $n$ divided by 3. If $H(m)$ is less than or equal to $s^k \pmod{n}$, and if $s^k$ is less than $H(m) + 2^k$, then the signature is considered valid.
