## Coin Flipping Blum Integers

Blum integers can be used in a coin-flipping protocol.

- (1) Alice generates a Blum integer, $n$, a random relatively prime to $n$, $x_0=x^2 \mod n$, and $x_1$ sends $n$ and $x_1$ to Bob.
- (2) Bob guesses $x_0$ is even or odd.
- (3) Alice sends $x$ to Bob.
- (4) Bob checks that $n$ is a Blum integer (Alice would have to give Bob the factors of $n$ and proofs of primality, or execute zero-knowledge protocol to convince that $n$ is a Blum integer), and he verifies that $x_0=x^2 \mod n$ and $x_1=x^3 \mod n$. If all this checks out, Bob wins the flip if he correctly guessed.

It is crucial that $n$ be a Blum integer. Otherwise, Alice may be able to find an $x'$ such that $x'^2 \mod n = x_0 \mod n = x_1$, where $x_1$ is also a quadratic residue. If $x_0$ were even or odd (or vice versa), Alice could freely cheat.

## 23.8 ONE-WAY ACCUMULATORS

There is a simple one-way accumulator function [116] (see Section 4.12):

```
A(x,y)=xy⁻¹ mod n
```

The numbers $n$ (n is the product of two primes) and $x_0$ must be agreed upon in advance. The accumulation of $y_1, y_2$, and $y_3$ would be

```
((x0¹ mod n)y² mod n)
```

This computation is independent of the order of $y_1, y_2$, and $y_3$.

## 23.9 ALL-OR-NOTHING DISCLOSURE OF SECRETS

This protocol allows multiple parties (at least two required for the protocol to work) to identify secrets from a single seller (see Section 4.13) [1374,1175]. First, here's a definition. Take two bit strings, $x$ and $y$. The fixed bit index [FBI] of $x$ and $y$ are the bits where the $i$-th bit of $x$ equals the $i$-th bit of $y$.

For example:

x = 110101001011
y = 101010000110

FBI[x, y] = [1, 4, 5, 11]
[We're reading the bits from left to right, with the rightmost bit as zero.]

Now, here is the protocol. Alice is the seller. Bob and Carol are buyers. Alice has $k$-bit secrets: $S_1, S_2, \dots, S_b$. Bob wants to buy secret $S_{bj}$ and Carol wants to buy secret $S_c$.

- Alice generates a public-key/private-key key pair and tells Bob (but Carol) the public key. She generates another public-key/private-key key and tells Carol (but not Bob) the public key.
- Bob generates $k$-bit random numbers, $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_b$, and tells Carol $k$-bit random numbers, $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_b$, and tells them to Bob.
- Bob encrypts $C_b$, the secret he wants to buy with the public key from Alice. He computes the FBI of $C_b$ and the result he just encrypted.
- Carol encrypts $B_c$, the secret she wants to buy with the public key from Alice. She computes the FBI of $B_c$ and the result she just encrypted.
- Bob takes each of the $n$-bit numbers $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_b$, and replaces every bit whose index is not in the FBI he received from Carol with its complement. He sends this new list of $n$-bit numbers, $B'_1, B'_2, \dots, B'_b$, to Alice.
- Carol takes each of the $n$-bit numbers $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_b$, and replaces every bit whose index is not in the FBI she received from Bob with its complement. She sends this new list of $n$-bit numbers, $C'_1, C'_2, \dots, C'_b$, to Alice.
- Alice decrypts $B'_b$ with Carol's private key, giving her $k$-bit numbers: $B'_1, B'_2, \dots, B'_b$. She computes $S_b \oplus B'_i$, for $i=1$ to $k$, and sends the results to Bob. (Note: The text has a typo in original logic, but following visual text provided)
- Bob decrypts $S_c$ by XORing $C_c$ and the $c$-th number he received from Alice.
- Carol computes $S_c$ by XORing $B_c$ and the $c$-th number she received from Alice. This complicated will go a long way to help. Alice the following eight 12-bit secrets for: $S_1 = 990, S_2 = 471, S_3 = 3860, S_4 = 1487, S_5 = 3751, S_7 = 2546$, and $S_k = 4043$. Bob wants to buy $S_7$, Carol wants to buy $S_2$.

- (1) Alice uses RSA algorithm. The key pair she will use with Bob is: $n=7387, e=5145,$ and $d=777$. The key pair she will use with Carol is: $n=2747, e=1421,$ and $d=2261$. She tells Bob and Carol each public key.
- (2) Bob generates 12-bit numbers, $B_1=743, B_2=1988, B_3=4001, B_4=2942, B_5=3421, B_6=2210, B_7=2306,$ and tells them to Carol. Carol generates 12-bit numbers, $C_1=1708, C_2=711, C_3=1969, C_4=3112, C_5=4014, C_6=2308, C_7=2212,$ and $C_8=222,$ and tells them to Bob.
- (3) Bob wants $S_7$, so he encrypts $C_7$ with the public key Alice gives him:
```
$2212^{545} \pmod{3078} = 5928
```
Now,
```
$2212 = 0100010100
$5928 = 1011110100
```
So, the FBI of two numbers is $[0, 4, 5, 6]$. He sends them to Carol. Carol wants $S_2$, so she encrypts $B_2$ with the public key Alice gave and gets the FBI of $B_2$ with the result of encryption. She sends $[0, 2, 6, 9, 10]$ to Bob.
- (4) Bob takes $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_7$ and replaces every bit whose index is not in $[0, 2, 6, 9, 10]$ with his component. For example:
```
$B_2 = 111111001000 = 1988
$B'_2 = 010011110100 = 1660
```
He sends $B'_1, B'_2, \dots, B'_7$ to Alice. Carol takes $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_7$ and replaces every bit whose index is not in $[0, 4, 5, 6]$ with her component. For example:
```
$C_7 = 01000101000 = 2212
$C'_7 = 10111101100 = 5928
```
She sends $C'_1, C'_2, \dots, C'_7$ to Alice.
- (5) Alice decrypts $C'_7$ with Bob's private key and XORs the results with $S_7$. For example, for $i = 7$:
```
$5928^{777} \pmod{2212} = 2546; 2546 \oplus 2212 = 342
```
She sends the results to Bob. Alice decrypts $B'_i$ with Carol's private key and XORs the results with $S_i$. For example, for $i = 2$:
```
$1660^{261} \pmod{2748} = 1988; 471 \oplus 1988 = 1555
```
She sends the results to Carol.
- (6) Bob gets $S_7$ by XORing $C_7$ and the seventh number he received from Alice.

$2212 \oplus 342 = 2546$

Carol computes $S_3$ by XORing $B_2$ and the second number she received from Alice.

$1988 \oplus 1555 = 471$

The protocol works for any number of buyers. If Bob, Carol, and Dave want to buy secrets, each gives each two public keys, one for each of the others. Each buyer gets a set of numbers from each other buyer. Then, they complete the protocol with Alice for each set of numbers and XOR all of their final results from Alice to get their secret. More details are in [1374.1175].

Unfortunately, a pair of dishonest parties can cheat. Alice and Carol, working together, can easily find Bob secret: If they know the FBI of $C_b$ and Bob's encryption algorithm, they can find $b$ such that $C_b$ has the right FBI. And Bob and Carol, working together, can easily get all the secrets from Alice. If you assume honest parties, there's an easier protocol [389].

- (1) Alice encrypts all of the secrets with RSA and sends them to Bob:
$C_i = S_i^e \mod n$
- (2) Bob chooses his secret $C_b$, picks a random $r$, and sends $C'$ to Alice.
$C' = C_b^r \mod n$
- (3) Alice sends $P'$.
$P' = C^d \mod n$
- (4) Bob calculates $S_b$.
$S_b = P'^{-1} \mod n$

If the parties be dishonest, Bob can prove in zero-knowledge that he knows some $r$ that $C' = C_{b,r} \mod n$ and keep $b$ secret until Alice gives him $P'$ in step (3) [246].
