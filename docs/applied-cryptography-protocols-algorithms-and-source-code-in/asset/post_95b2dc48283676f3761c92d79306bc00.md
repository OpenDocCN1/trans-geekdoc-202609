If it turns out that the person who bought the money order tried to cheat the merchant, the bank would want to know who that person was. (1) Alice prepares n money orders for a given amount. Each of the money order contains a different random uniqueness string, X one long enough to make the chance of two being identical negligible. On each money order, there are pairs of identity bit strings, $h_i, l_i, \dots, l_n$ [Yes, there are n different orders on each check.] Each of these pairs is generated as follows: Alice creates a string that gives her name, address, and other piece of identifying information that the bank wants to see. Then, she splits it into two pieces using the secret splitting protocol [see Section 3.6). Then, she commits to each piece using a bit-commitment protocol.

This is quite an amazing protocol, so let's look at it from various angles. Alice cheat? Her money order is nothing more than a string of bits, so she can't copy. Note that this protocol does prevent Alice from trying; it detects cheating with almost certainty. Alice can't prevent her identity from being revealed if she cheats. She can't change her uniqueness string or any of the identity strings, because then signature will no longer be valid. The merchant will immediately notice in step [6].

The interaction between the merchant and the bank takes place after Alice sends the money, the merchant could be stuck with a bad money order. Alice could try to snatch money order past the bank, on which the identity strings don't reveal her, or better, whose identity strings reveal someone else's name. The odds of getting this true past the bank in step [3] are 1 in n. These aren't impossible odds, but if you make the penalty severe enough Alice won't try it.

What about the bank? It can figure out that the money order it accepted from the merchant was the one she signed for! Alice is protected by the blind signature protocol in steps [2] through [5].
