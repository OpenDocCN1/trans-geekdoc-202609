NSA conducts research in cryptography, by designing securing algorithms to protect U.S. communications and designing cryptanalytic techniques to listen on non-U.S. communications. The NSA is known to be the largest employer of mathematicians in the world; it is also the largest purchaser of computer hardware in the world. The NSA possibly possesses cryptographic expertise ahead of the public state of the art (in algorithms, but probably not in protocols) and undoubtedly breaks many of the systems used in practice. For reasons of national security, all information about the NSA—even its budget—is classified. [Its budget is rumored to be $13 billion per year—including military funding of NSA projects and personnel—it is rumored to employ 16,000 people.]

The NSA uses its power to restrict the public availability of cryptography, so as to prevent national enemies from using encryption methods too strong for the NSA to break. Massey discusses this struggle between academic and military research in cryptography [1007];

If one regards cryptography as the prerogative of government closed doors. Without doubt, the number of workers engaged today in such research in cryptography far exceeds that of those in open research. For only about 10 years has there been widespread open research in cryptography. There have been, and will continue to be, conflicts between these two communities. Open research is a common knowledge that depends for its vitality on the open exchange of ideas via conference presentations and publications in scholarly journals. But can agents charged with responsibilities of breaking the ciphers of other nations, counteract the publication of a cipher that they cannot break! A researcher in good conscience publish such a cipher that might undermine the effectiveness of his own government's code-breakers! One might argue that publication of a secure cipher would force all governments to behave like Stimson's gentlemen," but one must be aware that research in cryptography is fraught with political and ethical considerations of severity more than in most scientific fields. The finder is not that conflicts have occurred between government agencies and open researchers in cryptography, but that these conflicts (at least those of which we are aware) have been so few and mild.
