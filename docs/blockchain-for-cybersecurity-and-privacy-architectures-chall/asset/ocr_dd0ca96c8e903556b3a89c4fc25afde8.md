# 4.4.2 E-Voting

The blockchain serves as a public ledger of transactions that cannot be reversed for future e-voting. The consensus (legitimate votes) is achieved through ‘miners’, agreeing to validate new records being added to a database. Whenever a new insertion is made, e.g., a person votes, then a new transaction record is created by person A, who adds the details of his vote and uploads it to the blockchain of nodes to add the new transaction to the blockchain. It should be deemed a valid transaction (which is often validated mathematically) by the majority of nodes, then the new vote is added to the blockchain and remains forever (Curran, 2018a). What is powerful about this solution is the fact that no centralized authority is needed to approve the votes but a majority consensus. Everyone agrees on the final tally, as they count the votes themselves, and as a result of the blockchain audit trail, anyone can verify that no votes were tampered with and no illegitimate votes were inserted.

Of course, at this time, many who believe that a blockchain can be applied in most domains, while in reality, a blockchain only makes sense when multiple mistrusting entities wish to interact and change the state of a system and are not able to agree on an online third party. Some claim that the only legitimate use for a blockchain is cryptocurrencies; however, others think that the ledger’s decentralized, tamper-proof nature makes it safe enough to allow fraud-free online elections. An interesting side of a blockchain is that it could allow for continuous voting, casting a vote every week or month. E-voting brings some new problems such as ensuring privacy, especially in the case of public permissionless blockchains, but there are solutions for that. Other problems include the speed by which transactions can be verified. For instance, at this time, Bitcoin and Ethereum can only process less than 25 transactions per second, compared to, Visa or Mastercard, can process thousands per second.

Some countries, however, tried blockchain for voting. Some have failed, such as the cyber vulnerability found the blockchain-based voting system that Russian officials planned to use in the 2019 Moscow City Duma election (Cimpanu, 2019). The Nasdaq also recently ruled the Estonia experiment safe enough for firms to start using blockchain for proxy voting. Blockchain may be championed as the solution to many problems in voting, but one domain where it might actually make sense is electronic voting. However, there are some who have grave concerns about using blockchain for voting within democracies (Lee, 2018).
