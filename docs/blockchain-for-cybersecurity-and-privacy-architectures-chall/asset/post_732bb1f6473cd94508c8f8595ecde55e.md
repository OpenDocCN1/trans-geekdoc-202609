# Foreword

Blockchain has moved beyond hype to real-world implementation in a broad range of industries (eg, finance, supply chain management, and Internet of Things) and applications (eg, cybersecurity and digital forensics), partly evidenced by its evolution over the last decade or so. At the time of writing this foreword, we are already in the fourth generation of blockchain (also known as Blockchain 4.0) and inching toward the fifth generation of blockchain.

Interest in blockchain among the academic community is also reflected by the growing number of conferences and initiatives, as well as this book titled Blockchain for Cybersecurity: Architectures, Challenges and Applications.

This book focuses on the applications of blockchain in cybersecurity, privacy, and digital forensics, for different industry sectors as Internet of Things and healthcare. Specifically, the book comprises five sections, each covering a different theme of associated research challenges. In Section I, the reader is introduced to the blockchain architectures and a number of associated challenges of how blockchain can be utilized for cybersecurity and privacy in the IoT and facilitating payment system security and privacy. The next two sections provide a more in-depth discussion of IoT and healthcare sectors. The fourth section then discusses the potential of blockchain in cybersecurity and privacy. The last section, perhaps my favorite due to my professional biases, covers the potential utility of blockchain in digital forensic applications.

In summary, this book is a timely contribution to the literature and a quality addition to the reader's bookshelf and library.

Kim-Kwang Raymond Choo
The University of Texas at San Antonio

# Preface

Since the creation of the first application in cryptocurrency, which is the Bitcoin in 2009 by Satoshi Nakamoto, blockchain technology has been presented as a revolution. Unlike centralized systems, a decentralized system allows one not to question the authorship of this declaration. In practice, when a contract is signed on the Internet, it cannot be challenged by one of the parties.

Each transaction added to a blockchain is timestamped and digitally signed. This is one of the basic principles of technology. This means that one can track back to a specific time for each transaction and locate the corresponding part on the blockchain via their public address.

The management of certificates would then be performed on the blockchain, making it virtually impossible for hackers to use fake certificates. This ensures that data remains private and secure.
