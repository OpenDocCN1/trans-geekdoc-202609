# 7.1.2 INTERNET OF THINGS

The IoT reflects the extension of the Internet to things and places it in the real world. This has allowed all humanity to dive into the depths of the internet and research its mysteries by one of the indispensable tools in our everyday life.

The IoT was formally introduced in the 2005 Internet Report of the International Telecommunications Union (ITU). The IoT has been as ‘a infrastructure for the information society, enabling advanced services by interconnecting (physical and virtual) things based on existing and evolving interoperable information and communication technologies’ (ITU Strategy and Policy Unit 2005). In fact, there is no standard definition for IoT.

The use of IoT has affected all aspects of everyday life. According to Gubbi et al. (2013), IoT applications can be found in many areas: (1) personal and home, (2) enterprise, (3) utilities, and (4) mobile. The IoT, which links billions of devices to the Internet, promises to create fantastic applications. The IoT offers unprecedented opportunities. However, security represents the biggest problem in deploying IoT. Thus, a frequent and robust approach to deal with security issues is required (Balte et al. 2015). Sicari et al. (2015) described major security-challenges while building IoT (Figure 7.2).

As computer technologies diversify, so are the possibilities of hacking. However, the IoT may not escape the rule. The dominant factor is that the evolution of techniques is cumulative and their power impose a kind of forward flight whose constraint is, at the same time, anonymous and uncontrollable. When industrial actors and public authorities talk about terms of growth and security, many opinions point to points and threats to people. In fact, deployments that do not start from scratch will represent a brake. In our connected world, security is often compromised. The more objects we connect, the more value we will create from the generated data and the greater be the risk of data theft and digital fraud.
