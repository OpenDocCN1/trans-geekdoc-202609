
## 12.2.1.2.3 Elements of Blockchain

The building blocks of blockchain and the underlying technology behind blockchain transactions show that this is not a single technique but is a combination of cryptography, mathematics and economic model, a combination of peer-to-peer (P2P) networks, and application of distributed consensus algorithm forming an integrated infrastructure construction. Blockchain technology is essentially composed of six elements, as follows (Lin & Liao: 2017):

- Decentralized: Blockchain does not rely on a centralized node, but the data can be recorded, stored, and updated in a distributed manner.
- Transparent: The records and blocks are transparent, making blockchain trustworthy.
- Open source: Most blockchains are open to everyone. People can check records publicly and use blockchain technologies to create any application.
- Autonomy: Nodes are updated by consensus without any user intervention. The trust of safe data transfer and update is placed on the entire system and not just a single person.
- Immutable: All records are reserved forever and cannot be changed unless somebody takes control of more than 51% nodes simultaneously.
- Anonymity: Data transfer and transactions can remain anonymous, since only blockchain addresses are needed for access and transfers.

- Shared and distributed ledger: The ledgers can be private, public, or semi-private/public. They can be shared among participants with privacy. The ledger entry is time-ordered and have graphical and computational architecture.

## 12.2.1.2.5 Types of Blockchain

Blockchain technologies can be broadly classified into following three types (Lin & Liao 2017):

- Public blockchain: A public blockchain is an open-ended permissionless network where anyone can participate without consensus, and maintain the shared ledger. Everybody can check and verify the transactions in a public blockchain and also participate in the consensus process. Examples are Bitcoin and Ethereum. Public blockchain has the advantage of being more secure. The disadvantages are low privacy, less eco-friendly, and requirement of high computational power and energy (How Blockchain Architecture Works? 2018)
- Consortium blockchain: This is a hybrid blockchain that is private and partly public. The ability to read and write transactions is extended to some nodes, which also control the consensus process. This type of blockchain exhibits properties of the node of authority, which are business-to-business, and can be open or private, has partnerships such as HyperLedger and can be seen as partly decentralized. Examples are HyperLedger and 3CEV.
- Private blockchain: Private blockchain requires an invitation to participate in the network. These networks put a restriction on every one of participants. It operates like a centralized database system that restricts access to users. Examples are Blockchain and Ripple.

## 12.2.1.2.7 Difference Between Blockchains and Databases

In a traditional payment system, for instance, in merchant-transaction, the data on the bank as well as the question that arises is whether the blockchains have similar or different databases. There are many types of databases such as relational databases, key-value stores, columnar databases, document databases, and graph databases. The databases can be centralized in a single location or can be distributed over many sites. The blockchain concept is similar to the distributed database. Distributed database partition retrieval and divides problems into smaller nodes. A user is not aware of the database topology across various nodes. The nodes are not homogenous in a distributed database (Peters & Panayi 2015).

In a distributed database, modifications are propagated to the various nodes through a 'master-slave' approach. There is one problem here when two of the data get modified by different writes. A blockchain can be viewed as a distributed database that can prevent such issues. A blockchain will reject a transaction from a node where the balance has already been spent by another node. This is one of the differences between databases and blockchains.

Another difference is that blockchains have the ability to create self-forcing contracts. Each node can solve a large set of complex problems to add a block to the blockchain, and they act as built-in virtual machines. The traditional databases are only data storage points and not smart contracts (Peters & Panayi 2015).
