### 7.1.1 CONTENT-CENTRIC NETWORKING

Content-centric networking (CCN) is an information-centric networking (ICN) (Mosko et al. 2016) instance that supports location independence by separating content from its source and reducing traffic through caching feature. Exchanges on this architecture are based on the publish/subscribe model; the name of object is published, and an object is requested by sending the Interest packet (Figure 7.1).

In CCN, data packets are sent in response to a requested Interest packet, and data fragments are cached along the path back to the requester. Instead of using the source and destination addresses of hosts, the CCN packets contain the name of the content that user wishes to access. It allows users to have more bandwidth and less latency simultaneously and securely. CCN promotes the use of multiple sources (Labbi et al. 2016) to retrieve the same content.

Table 7.1 contrasts the basic CCN concepts with those of Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol (TCP/IP). The key idea of CCN lies in using names rather than addresses to forward content on the network. They are used to identify the associated contents unambiguously. These names are also used in all network operations, such as announcing, requesting, and distributing content. Each content is divided into several parts (MTU) as not specified, and there is some possibility it may be larger than the MTU in IP networks. ### 7.1.2 INTERNET OF THINGS

The IoT reflects the extension of the Internet to things and places it in the real world. This has allowed all humanity to dive into the depths of the internet and research its mysteries by one of the indispensable tools in our everyday life.

The IoT was formally introduced in the 2005 Internet Report of the International Telecommunications Union (ITU). The IoT has been as ‘a infrastructure for the information society, enabling advanced services by interconnecting (physical and virtual) things based on existing and evolving interoperable information and communication technologies’ (ITU Strategy and Policy Unit 2005). In fact, there is no standard definition for IoT.

The use of IoT has affected all aspects of everyday life. According to Gubbi et al. (2013), IoT applications can be found in many areas: (1) personal and home, (2) enterprise, (3) utilities, and (4) mobile. The IoT, which links billions of devices to the Internet, promises to create fantastic applications. The IoT offers unprecedented opportunities. However, security represents the biggest problem in deploying IoT. Thus, a frequent and robust approach to deal with security issues is required (Balte et al. 2015). Sicari et al. (2015) described major security-challenges while building IoT (Figure 7.2).

As computer technologies diversify, so are the possibilities of hacking. However, the IoT may not escape the rule. The dominant factor is that the evolution of techniques is cumulative and their power impose a kind forward flight whose constraint is, at the same time, anonymous and uncontrollable. When industrial actors and public authorities talk about terms of growth and security, many opinions point to points and threats. In fact, deployments that do not start from scratch will represent a brake. In our connected world, security is often compromised. The more objects we connect, the more value we will create from generated data and the greater be the risk of data theft and digital fraud. Subjects as privacy and connectivity must be taken into consideration to ensure the possible success of the IoT. Moreover, connecting billions of various devices requires an equal amount of allocated IP addresses, which challenge the current state of the worldwide network. Even with the transition to IPv6, the design of new architectures allowing communication between connected objects and the routing of traffic generated by data growth remains a critical need.

### 7.1.3 BLOCKCHAIN TECHNOLOGY

Blockchain (Fernández-Caramés et al. 2018) is a technology that has been written extensively, and many experts agree that it will profoundly change sectors that rely on the transmission of information. It is a technology widely used in the context of the emission of cryptocurrencies as Bitcoin (Nakamoto et al. 2008). However, by its very nature, blockchain can impact other sectors such as the IoT. With its decentralized data flow and storage model, its traceability and inviolability are envisaged for uses other than virtual currencies. It is undoubtedly a compelling method to require something digital and to publish information about that owned thing.

Using blockchain technology as the basis for IoT devices reduces the risk of hacking by reducing potential entry points. By giving up a central authority in the IoT networks, blockchain could allow these networks to protect themselves. The key benefits of using blockchain for IoT are shown in Figure 7.3.
