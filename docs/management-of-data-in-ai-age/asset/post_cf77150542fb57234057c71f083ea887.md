## Applied Artificial Intelligence – Making AI Work for Consumers as a Core Business Component

Mikael Wiberg, Ph.D., Professor  
Department of Informatics, Umeå University, 901 87 Umeå, Sweden  
mikael.wiberg@umu.se  
+46 (0)70 662 10 70

### Abstract

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is now rapidly being applied in our society. While the breakthrough of AI in terms of its use and its applicability on a societal level has in fact been repeatedly announced since the mid 1950s, it is now truer than ever. As recently acknowledged, AI has now, after three waves of developments, finally left the research labs and entered real-world contexts. Accordingly, and as AI is now increasingly and widely applied, we suggest that it is now time to address issues related to “Applied Artificial Intelligence” (AAI). In this paper we propose this term, and we define it as the study, design, development, implementation and use of Artificial Intelligence technologies to address real-world problems. In this article we present how AI has developed over the past few decades, and across three waves of developments, and we illustrate Applied Artificial Intelligence by presenting our e-Biz Corp case where a global actor is now using AI as a core component of their online business. We conclude this article with a set of recommendations for moving forward with Applied Artificial Intelligence, and we present the main contributions offered by our work to the growing body of research on how to make use of AI.

**Keywords:** AI, Artificial Intelligence, Applied Artificial Intelligence, Core business

### The Breakthrough of Artificial Intelligence in Society

*“Artificial Intelligence is no longer an academic term, but a reality”*. This could have been said today, given the current and escalating hype surrounding AI. However, it was actually stated by Holloway and Hand (1988) in their Business Horizons article published almost 30 years ago. Nevertheless, this statement concerning the large-scale and real-world breakthrough of AI could have also been made today, in the year 2020. In looking back from a historical viewpoint, the proof-of-concept in terms of the wide scale societal breakthrough of Artificial Intelligence (AI) has been a continuously repeated promise and message surrounding the development of this technology over the last few decades, from the mid 1950s until today (see e.g. Turing, 1950; Weizenbaum, 1966 and Makridakis, S. (2017)). As formulated by Xu (2019), we are now already in the 3rd wave of AI, and the promise of AI has been repeated for each of these three waves of developments of AI technologies.

Still, this statement about the breakthrough of AI as formulated by Holloway and Hand (1988) might now be truer than ever. As pinpointed by Xu (2019), this 3rd wave of AI is now characterized by technological enhancements and the deployment and use of AI solutions in society, whereas the 1st and 2nd waves were to a large extent academia-driven. In short, AI is now increasingly applied on a societal level.

Accordingly, and as AI is now increasingly applied, we suggest that it is now time to address issues related to “Applied Artificial Intelligence” (AAI) and accordingly expand the focus from technical matters of making AI work (as a technology), to making AI work in the context of its use and implementation. With this as our central point of departure, we propose the term “Applied Artificial Intelligence” (AAI) and we define it as *the study, design, development, implementation and use of Artificial Intelligence technologies to address real-world problems.* In this paper we propose this term in an attempt to look beyond the technological developments surrounding the area of AI, and as an effort to redirect our focus towards what this technology now enables, for consumers as well as for businesses when AI is now increasingly applied in real-life settings.

The development of AI has been driven by technological advancements on the one hand, including the developments in machine learning, pattern recognition and neural networks (e.g. Gilio, 1995), and visions and promises about the next steps of AI in terms of its applicability on the other hand (Xu, 2019). Through these cycles of developments, the breakthrough of AI has been a repeated message, surrounded by promises of a nearby future (see e.g. Makridakis, 2017; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2020), and at the same time it has also been surrounded by doubts and fears (see e.g. Clark, 2003).

AI solutions that aim “beyond the human” (Kaplan and Haenlein, 2019) and where AI can be used to do beyond-human-scale work tasks (e.g. collect, search, filter, analyze, generate and edit massive amounts of data). We see this in how AI is increasingly applied in search engines, in social media platforms, and in streaming services. AI is no longer only aimed at “the human-like”, but increasingly applied to do these large-scale tasks that no single human, or even a collection of humans could do (within a reasonable time). For instance, to filter and analyze millions of data points, thousands of times, over and over again, with high precision, and at high speed. It is this type of AI that we look at in this article.

As recently formulated by Kaplan and Haenlein (2019), AI – “Artificial Intelligence” – can be defined as *“a system’s ability to correctly interpret external data, to learn from such data, and to use those learnings to achieve specific goals and tasks through flexible adaptation”*. This definition is in line with the current large-scale use of AI, and we can also notice that “reason” is no longer an explicit term used in the definition of AI. Of course, it would have been a central notion if still aiming for “human-like” AI, but as we move beyond human capabilities it might be more interesting to focus on what AI can offer, beyond any comparison with the human mind. In short, AI might now be its own distinctive form of intelligence where analogies with the human brain is no longer fruitful or useful, but rather misleading in how to understand both how AI works, but maybe foremost what it can offer.
