With a point of departure taken in this recent shift in the development of AI, we suggest that it is now time to also:

- shift our analytical lens – from the individual (and human-like) to the large-scale (and beyond human);

In this paper we do this exploration with a particular interest in understanding how *applied artificial intelligence* might be designed to support multiple stakeholders (in our case consumers and businesses), and how applied AI might work in the creation of new digital services for consumers, as well as how AI can be used as a core business component. Our turn to applied AI is motivated by the increasing adoption of AI to real-world problems, and studies that have most recently shown how AI technologies are becoming increasingly practically relevant (see e.g. Gomes et al., 2019 and Ågerfalk, 2020).

In this article we present how AI has developed over the past few decades, and across three waves of developments, and we illustrate Applied Artificial Intelligence by presenting our e-Biz corp case where a global actor is now applying AI as a core component of their online business. During the 1970s and 1980s there were lots of developments that set the next stage in the development of AI, including breakthroughs in technical research that increased CPU capacity, advancements in parallel distributed processing, and the development of artificial neural networks. Still, the focus was on different ways of creating models of the brain and to arrive at better models of human cognition. As noticed by Kaplan & Haenlein (2019), “a common way to define artificial intelligence is to do so by referencing human intelligence.”

Along these lines Nicholas Negroponte, the founding father of the Architecture Machine Group at MIT, dedicated his 1970 book *The Architecture Machine*, which was a book that proposed a theory of interfaces for artificial intelligence, “to the first machine that can appreciate the gesture” (Vardouli & Touloumi, 2019). Again, the goal was to create the human-like intelligent machine. However, this recurring reference to the human, or the brain, was challenged as we moved forward from the early days of AI developments.

In the 1990s, more advanced artificial neural networks were developed that could read the numbers on checks, but that was as far as they went. As stated by Miller (2019), these early machines had trouble finding patterns, an essential aspect of data analysis. The computers were still not powerful enough, and there was not enough data available to train the algorithms. Still, the 1990s mark an important shift in the development of AI – away from modeling the brain, and towards tasks that AI can perform, beyond the human level, and beyond basic research on how the brain works, and towards what AI can do (i.e. what we in this paper refer to as ‘applied artificial intelligence’).

If we now map this development to the three waves of AI as proposed by Xu (2019), we can see how the 1st wave of AI lasted for approximately 20 years (1950s–1970s), and how it was mostly driven by academia with a focus on technological solutions, symbolism and connectionism. In moving forward, we can also see how the 2nd wave of AI (1980s–1990s) was still academia driven and technology oriented, and as indicated above there was a clear focus on speech recognition, machine translations, artificial neural networks, and research on pattern recognition. Still, it was not until the late 1990s that the focus started to shift towards the use of AI for different purposes, and according to Xu (2019), it was not until the beginning of the 2000s that we could see the beginning of a 3rd wave of AI (2006).

With the 3rd wave of AI came an important shift – from being a research lab product, it started to become widely applied. As suggested by Xu (2019), it was a breakthrough in applications (including functional systems built on deep learning, speech recognition, pattern recognition, and big data) and it was enabled by high-performance computers. As such, it started to prove itself useful and it is increasingly applied to real-world problems.

So, in a sense, and as I started this paper with the quote from Holloway and Hand (1988) about the breakthrough of AI, we can now with the 3rd wave of AI see how it is increasingly a concern related to how it should be applied. From being about technical questions (e.g. machine learning and algorithms for pattern recognition), to the more fundamental and philosophical questions (i.e. what is “intelligence”? And to what extent can machines be intelligent?), we can see how the AI field is shifting towards questions concerning different forms of AI, and if it should be human-like (and manifest itself as robots, avatars, conversation partners in different settings), or beyond-human-like forms (e.g. cloud-based AI solutions).

The most recent developments that have followed from the shift to 3rd wave AI include calls for human-centered approaches to AI (Xu, 2019), a focus on ethical and responsible AI design and development (Dignum, 2019), and a concern for the social and societal impact of AI.

To understand the social, ethical and societal impact of applied AI, it is important to study how it is being applied. Here we contribute to this growing need by focusing on a company that applies AI on a large scale, by embedding AI in the development of their e-biz platform that reaches thousands of consumers. In doing so we follow the growing trend of focusing on what AI can do (in this case for consumers and for businesses), and we present the challenges they had to face when applying AI technologies in this particular context.

Before moving to this case of 3rd wave AI as applied in a business context, we first examine the current approaches taken to the development of AI. In the next section we outline the existing approach including the machine-centered approach, the human-centered approach and the systems-centered approach. We conclude that these approaches have been successful, but also that there might be room for a fourth approach – to work with an applied AI approach – when it comes to questions concerning how AI should be used in a real-world context.
