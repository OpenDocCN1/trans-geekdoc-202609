## I. The problem

Problem solving is a daily occurrence in business and, also, in human brains. Businesses resort to a variety of modes in order to find an answer to these problems. Human brains adopt, also, a variety of measures to solve their own brand of problems. Artificial Intelligence technologies seem to have been extending a helping hand to business in the search for problem solving mechanisms. Machine learning and deep learning are currently recognized as prime modes for business insight and problem solving. Does the human brain possess competencies and instruments that could compare to the deep learning technologies adopted by AI?

This will be the focus of the following article.

The article is eclectic. It draws on concepts and frameworks from psychology, neurology and management. It starts with identifying the contours of problem solving seen through psychology and management eyes. It then explores the cognitive functions of the brain and the deep learning dimension of this cognition. Possible overlapping of the two processes, the managerial and the neurological, in the process of problem solving is then explored.

The article draws on recent research on all three areas: psychology, neurology and strategic management.

The article could contribute to a better understanding of the role of the human brain in advancing solutions to managerial problems. It could also produce tools supportive to the process of computational cognition.

## II. Key variables: problem solving, deep learning and brain cognition

- **What is problem solving?**

Problem solving is “a higher-order cognitive process that requires the modulation and control of more routine or fundamental skills” (Goldstein & Levin, 1987). It occurs if an organization, an individual or a system does not know how to proceed from a given state to a desired goal state. It starts with an idea, or a problem, and proceeds all the way to an end result.

The concept is rather eclectic with roots in psychology and tentacles in philosophy, artificial intelligence, computer science, engineering, mathematics and medicine.

A “problem-solving cycle” is a sequence of events that would lead to a solution. In this cycle one recognizes a problem, defines the problem, develops a strategy to solve the problem, organizes the pertinent knowledge, mobilizes required resources, conducts remedial action, monitors progress, and evaluates the solution (Schacter, D.L. 2009).

- **What is deep learning?**

Deep learning is a subset of machine learning. It is a process that involves neural networks that could learn, unsupervised, from unstructured or unlabeled data.

Machine learning works with algorithms that detect patterns and learn how to make predictions. The process can be supervised, semi-supervised or unsupervised. In supervised learning an algorithm learns from labeled training data how to predict outcomes for unknown variables. In unsupervised learning unlabeled data lead to a model that works on its own to discover outcomes.

### a. Memory

Memory is our ability to encode, store, retain and subsequently recall information and past experiences in the human brain. It can be thought of in general terms as the use of past experience to affect or influence current behavior. Memory is the sum total of what we remember, and gives us the capability to learn and adapt from previous experiences as well as to build relationships. It is the ability to remember past experiences, and the power or process of recalling to mind previously learned facts, experiences, impressions, skills and habits. It is the store of things learned and retained from our activity or experience, as evidenced by modification of structure or behavior, or by recall and recognition. In more physiological or neurological terms, memory is, at its simplest, a set of encoded neural connections in the brain. It is the recreation or reconstruction of past experiences by the synchronous firing of neurons that were involved in the original experience.

Memory is related to but distinct from learning, which is the process by which we acquire knowledge of the world and modify our subsequent behavior. During learning, neurons that fire together to produce a particular experience are altered so that they have a tendency to fire together again. Thus, memory depends on learning because it stores and retrieves learned information. But learning also depends largely on memory as stored knowledge relates, by inference and association, to newly acquired knowledge.

### b. Learning

The brain learns.

Brain learning processes could take different shapes depending on a process and the criterion. If the criterion is time then learning is long and short term but if the criterion is transparency then it is explicit or implicit and, finally if the criterion is depth of analysis learning could become either deep or generic.

Learning is a key cognitive function of the human brain and the link to managerial performance. It is a process of acquiring or modifying knowledge, behaviors, skills, values, or preferences. The brain “learns” and the cerebrum is the most important device for learning as it is here where higher-ordered functions like memory and reasoning occur. Each area of the cerebrum specializes in a function – sight, hearing, speech, touch, short-term memory, long-term memory, language and reasoning abilities are the most important for learning. Through a network of neurons, sensory information is transmitted by synapses along the neural pathway and stored temporarily in short-term memory, a volatile region of the brain that acts like a receiving center for the flood of sensory information we encounter in our daily lives. Once processed in short-term memory, our brain’s neural pathways carry these memories to the structural core, where they are compared with existing memories and stored in our long-term memory, the vast repository of our lifelong experiences and the prime point of reference within managerial behavior (Ford, 2011) (UCERI, OECD, 2008).

Brain learning could be explicit or implicit. Explicit learning connotes conscious awareness and articulation of what is being learned. Implicit learning is without conscious access (Roman F. Loonis, et al., October 2017).
