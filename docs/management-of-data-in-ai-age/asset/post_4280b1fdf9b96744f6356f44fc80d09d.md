The Turing test was developed by Alan Turing in 1950, and it is a test of a computer’s ability to show intelligent behavior equivalent to, or indistinguishable from, that of a human. In this strive, the notions of human-like AI, or human-level AI have been recurring notions (see e.g. Miller (2019) and Kaplan & Haenlein (2019)).

In going back to the roots for this human-like model for the development of AI we should of course mention the Eliza system. This system was designed by Joseph Weizenbaum in 1966, and it is said to be the first computer program to pass the Turing test. The Eliza program worked by examining a user’s typed comments for keywords, and responded to the user in a dialogue fashion. By relying on this model, Eliza could respond either with a generic phrase or by repeating an earlier comment left by the user. The Eliza program was in this way designed to replicate the behavior of a psychotherapist. In passing the Turing test, Eliza was able to fool some people into believing that they were talking to a real person, instead of a computer.

Eliza, and similar systems developed at this time relied on an idea that humans could be modeled as computer programs, and in this way it would be possible to create AI that could mimic the human brain, and thus present itself as “intelligent”. As formulated by Newell and Simon (1972):

> “Humans, when engaged in problem solving in the kinds of tasks we have considered, are representable as information processing systems” (Newell and Simon, 1972)

As further described by Miller (2019), these researchers expanded the concept of a computer’s functional architecture to reflect the way the brain is put together, that is the brain’s cognitive functional architecture. The analogy with the human brain was the dominant model.

> “Like a computer, the brain has storage for information or facts – memory – and ways of retrieving information and working on it, using rules for problem solving – algorithms.” (Miller, 2019).

This first set of human-like developments was the result of close collaborations between several scientific disciplines (including collaborations across cognitive science, psychology, neuroscience and computer science). It was in this sense multidisciplinary, but they all shared the same focus – to inform the design of an intelligent computational machine.

Since then, the multi-disciplinary tradition of research collaborations around AI has continued, but the outcome is nowadays more diverse. Today, researchers are interested in not only the technical challenges associated with the design and implementation of intelligent machinery, but also in understanding its philosophical, social, cultural, ethical and moral implications. Here, there are some recent calls for “responsible AI” (Dignum, 2019), and the importance of understanding AI as “agency” (Ågerfalk, 2020) as well as, and in relation to that, to understand “what algorithms want” (Finn, 2017) from the viewpoints of agency, but also power, control, collection and processing of data, etc. Here, AI is again approached from a human-centered perspective, but with a wider idea about the object of study.

## 3. System-centered approaches to AI

If we now turn to the most recent developments surrounding AI, and a set of contemporary factors that motivates a turn to “applied AI” as proposed in this article, we should acknowledge the increasing interest in system-centered approaches to AI. No longer do we need to write line-by-line code to build AI systems, and no longer do we need to hand-feed these systems with data. Instead, several platforms and development frameworks (including e.g. Microsoft Azure) now offer ready-made “AI components” to integrate AI-functionality in just about any digital service. This development enables compositional design (Stolterman & Wiberg, 2020) of AI-solutions, and it lowers the development barrier to build functional AI-systems for real-world use contexts.

With the systems-centered approach to AI developers can now focus on what they want the AI system to do (i.e., how it should be applied), rather than just getting it to work (from a technical viewpoint). Further, the access to data is handled by another set of ready-made components (e.g. components for database access and retrieval, or components for data scraping of websites), and again it becomes more of an issue to think about what data sources should be used, rather than how to solve any technical problems associated with getting the data in the first place.

## 4. Towards a fourth approach? A call for Applied Artificial Intelligence

Although we now have the AI technology in place, and as we have it accessible as ready-made AI components that can be easily integrated in systems and digital services, there might still be a need for an additional approach here. If we now have the technology in place, the next question to tackle has to do with how to put it to use in different contexts? To address this question, I suggest that we need to approach it from the viewpoint of “Applied Artificial Intelligence”. Although there is nothing new about the fact that AI should be used, there is still no explicit approach developed that has as its particular aim to focus on the process through which it can be put to work. In this article I suggest that on a business level, we should not just treat this technology as a tool, but we should strive for more full-scale implementation and integration projects where whole business models are built around this technology as a core business component.

## Applying AI as a Core Business Component – The eBiz Corp Case

Artificial intelligence is increasingly a background technology that is integrated in digital platforms, products and services – ranging from the integration of AI-components in search engines and streaming services, to the use of AI in healthcare (Rahwan et al., 2019).

In our research project we have worked in close collaboration with an eBiz company where we have followed their development of one such AI-based platform that operates in the background to support “intelligent pricing” for online web shops. In adopting the methodological perspective proposed by Rahwan et al. (2019) we have followed this development during the last 11 months, and we have followed both the development of the technical AI-system, as well as the CEO, the Sales manager and the technical development team during this period in order to gain an informed understanding of how they develop their core business through the development of this AI system.

In our research collaboration with the eBiz company we have also been inspired by the work by Zook and Allen (2003) on processes that govern the growth of the business core, the work by Strassmann (1990) on how to align the development of IT with the development of business values, and the work by Zook (2007) on how to further develop the core business through the development of digital solutions. In doing so we have focused on how AI might be applied as a core component in the development of the AI-platform as well as the eBiz company as an AI-driven business. In following the development of eBiz and their AI-platform we adopted an engaged scholarship methodology (Van de Ven, 2007) where we worked closely together with this company instead of just relying on e.g. interviews.

The eBiz company we have collaborated with is a global actor who has offices in 6 countries, including an innovation office in London. eBiz develops and provides an AI-powered web retail platform for 30 customers (global chains), and they have formulated their core business as being about “doing intelligent brand and product matching online to provide the best price for the customer”. They are accordingly supporting multiple stakeholders here (including other companies as their clients, as well as the online shoppers (end users/customers)). In doing so they acknowledge how the AI-system is of strategic value if aligned with the organization and its trading partners (Melville et al., 2004), and ways of integrating the synchronous development of IT, algorithms and business models (Gomez-Uribe, 2015).

eBiz also has a mission statement that is completely aligned with their AI-platform. This statement says that they deliver a complete and exact view of how all their clients' products are compared in price points with any competition on the online market. The comparison is data-driven, automatic, and their algorithms scrape and analyze the web on a daily basis.

This allows online retailers to curate assortment, increase volume, growth and target specific strategic and tactical goals.
