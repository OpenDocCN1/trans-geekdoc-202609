During the 1970s and 1980s there were lots of developments that set the next stage in the development of AI, including breakthroughs in technical research that increased CPU capacity, advancements in parallel distributed processing, and the development in artificial neural networks. Still, the focus was on different ways of creating models of the brain and to arrive at better models of human cognition. As noticed by Kaplan & Haenlein (2019) “_a common way to define artificial intelligence is to do so by referencing human intelligence_”.

Along these lines Nicholas Negroponte, the funding father of the Architecture Machine Group at MIT, dedicated his 1970 book *The Architecture Machine*, which was a book that proposed a theory of interfaces for artificial intelligence, “_to the first machine that can appreciate the gesture_” (Vardouli & Touloumi, 2019). Again, the goal was to create the human-like intelligent machine. However, this recurring reference to the human, or the brain, was challenged as we moved forward from the early days of AI developments.

In the 1990s, more advanced artificial neural networks were developed that could read the numbers on checks, but that was as far they went. As stated by Miller (2019) these early machines had trouble finding patterns, an essential aspect of data analysis, the computers were still not powerful enough, and there was not enough data available to train the algorithms.
