## Principal component analysis

We performed the PCA to discriminate the VOCs with the similar response and determine the dimensions of the resulting data set of multi-parameters sensing responses. The responses of the two groups of VOCs (i.e., the oxygenated and non-oxygenated VOCs) were analyzed by PCA separately. High contributions of the first three principal components (PCs) were achieved: i.e., 76.58%, 15.67% and 7.6% (oxygenated VOCs); 87.7%, 8.9% and 3.2% (non-oxygenated VOCs), for PC1, PC2, PC3, respectively. This shows that the high data dimensionality can be obtained from the sensor on exposure to only four vapors.

Fig. 3 shows a 3D plot of the first 3 PCs keeping most of the information (accounting for a total of 99.8%). Each point corresponds to an entire fingerprint of a VOC at a specific concentration (consists of eight parameters) and is colored according to the VOC it represents. Points belonged to different VOCs are well-separated in space, as can be seen in Fig. 3, indicating that the sensor can

differentiate the VOCs very well. Points belonging to same VOC but with the different concentrations (from 100 ppm to 800 ppm) are located approximately in one line. These results show that the sensor has high data dimensionality and a potential to distinguish between different VOCs and quantify concentrations of individual vapors.

## Linear discrimination analysis

To quantitatively identify the types of various VOCs, LDA was carried out for oxygenated VOCs. Fig. 4a shows a 3D plot of LDA results. Each point is colored according to the VOC type and a pentagram represents the center of mass of each points group. Results shown in Fig. 4a clearly indicate that each VOC can be well determined. The LDA results of oxygenated VOCs are depicted in Fig. 4b. LDA identification accuracies of 95.4% were achieved, corresponding to 1 misclassification out of 22 total samples. Cross-validation is an effective way to verify the obtained results, and leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV) is often used as the validation algorithm [11]. The cross-validation results are depicted in Fig. 4c, indicating a cross-validation rate of 90.9% was achieved.

Furtherly, to provide a more stringent test of the sensor, the identification ability toward similar VOC mixtures has been investigated. We use the LDA to discriminate the mixtures of EtOH and acetone (A), EtOH and IPA (B), acetone and DCM (C), as well as acetone and MeCN (D). Fig. 4d shows a 3D plot of LDA results of the VOCs mixtures. From different view angles, four kinds of VOC mixtures are well separated in a 3D feature space. A correct classification rate of 100% was achieved, as shown in Fig. 4e. The cross-validation results of mixture are depicted in Fig. 4f, indicating a correct cross-validation rate of 90% has been achieved. These results demonstrate that utilizing the Ti3C2Tx based multivariable sensor is a promising approach for identification of pure VOCs and similar complex mixtures.

## Partial least square regression analysis

We further used the PLS to quantify concentrations of ethanol in a background of variable humidity levels and methanol concentrations. Fig. 5a presents a simple visualization of the quantitative prediction of ethanol vapors in the presence of variable backgrounds. The position of each point is given by its real concentration (x-axis) and predicted concentration (y-axis). Perfect predictions will lay on the diagonal (i.e., prediction matches the real gas concentration). All the VOC concentrations were predicted quite well, as all the points are close to the diagonal positions. The average accuracy is 93.4% using 5 LVs.

## CONCLUSION

In summary, we developed a Ti3C2Tx based multivariable sensor for selective VOC detection. The sensor was fabricated by depositing a thin film of Ti3C2Tx on the surface of an IDE. Eight representative parameters of the proposed sensor were selected as VOC characteristics and the multi-parameters sensing responses create a unique fingerprint for each VOC. The multivariable sensor showed a high data dimensionality in PCA. Correct rates of 90.9% and 90% for the identification was achieved for oxygenated VOCs and VOCs mixtures in LDA, respectively. The ethanol concentration estimation accuracy is up to 93.4% based on the proposed sensor. The high level of identification and concentration prediction truly shows the potential of MXene based multivariable sensors for detection of VOCs of interest in the presence of known and unknown interferences. We anticipate that the strategy to solve the cross-sensitivity of gas sensors of this work could be easily adapted to other gas sensors and implemented in a range of emerging applications, including agriculture, pollution monitoring, food quality assurance, and medical diagnosis.
