However, due to the difficulty of aligning the text generation process with visual recognition tasks [245, 263, 263], MLLMs struggle with generating correct answers that match the ground-truth class names, underperforming the current dominant contrastive paradigms, even when employing them as vision encoders [245, 246, 264, 263, 265].

Focusing on enhancing the robustness of vision models, in this Chapter, we propose to effectively leverage MLLMs to conduct Machine Vision Therapy (MVT) which aims to diagnose and rectify the error predictions through a novel Denoising In-Context Learning (DICL) strategy. Then, we utilize the rectified supervision to guide the fine-tuning process in downstream OOD problems. Specifically, rather than giving a set of options to ask MLLMs for the exact answer [245, 264, 265], we show that it is sufficient to query for the ground truth by using two exemplars, i.e., (1) a correct one that demonstrates the match between a query class name with its image example and (2) an erroneous one that combines the same query class with an image from the most confusing category for the vision model. Since the erroneous predictions are essentially label noise, we draw inspiration from learning with noisy labels [266, 267, 268, 269, 270, 271, 272, 123, 273, 274, 64, 275, 276]. Particularly, we find the erroneous categories by estimating a transition matrix that captures the probability of one class being mistaken as. By feeding the two exemplars, MLLMs can be instructed to leverage their few-shot learning power to distinguish the semantically similar images that are easily confused by vision models, as shown in the lower row of Figure 5.1. To process such instructions, we leverage the multi-modal in-context learning ability of several existing MLLMs [277, 278, 279, 280] to realize our methodology. After the error predictions are diagnosed and rectified, vision models can be further-tuned to enhance their OOD robustness on downstream data distribution. Through a comprehensive empirical study on many challenging datasets and their OOD variants, such as ImageNet [281], WILDS [215], and DomainBed [176], we carefully validate the effectiveness of MVT and demonstrate its superiority under various OOD scenarios on many well-known vision models.

To sum up, our contributions are threefold:

## 5.2 Related Work

In this section, we provide a brief discussion of the OOD generalization problem and multimodal large-language models.

### 5.2.1 OOD Generalization

OOD data refers to those with different distributions from training data. OOD generalization aims at improving the performance of deep models to unseen test environments. Researchers attempted to tackle the problem from different perspectives, such as data augmentation, OOD detection, invariant causal mechanisms [152, 282, 283, 284], and so on. Data augmentation is effective in improving model generalization. Typical methods include Cutout [285], which randomly occludes parts of an input image; CutMix [286], which replaces a part of the target image with a different image; Mixup [287], which produces a convex combination of two images; DeepAugment [217], which passes a clean image through an image-to-image network and introduces several perturbations during the forward pass. Some methods conduct OOD detection to serve OOD data. Typical methods include softmax confidence score [28, 188], which is a baseline for OOD detection; Outlier Exposure (OE) [288], which uses unlabeled data as auxiliary OOD training data. Energy scores are shown to be better for distinguishing OOD samples from IID ones [36]. Some work resort to causality to study the OOD generalization problem. Typical methods include MatchDG [150], which proposes matching-based algorithms when objects are observed and approximate the objective when objects are not observed; INVRAT [142], which leveraged some conditional independence relationships induced by the causal mechanism assumption.

### 5.2.2 Multimodal Large Language Models

The field of vision-language models has witnessed widespread advancements in recent years, driven by the growing synergy between computer vision and natural language processing. Notably, this synergy has led to the exceptional zero-shot performance [289] of CLIP [225], a model that employs a two-tower contrastive pretraining approach to align image and text information. In the rapidly evolving landscape of LLMs, such as GPTs [254], LLaMA [259], and Vicuna [290], it has become evident that LLMs possess the capacity to process information from diverse domains. BLIP-2 [249], for instance, serves as a foundational model, aligning visual features and text features using a Querying Transformer (Q-former) and utilizing OPT [291] and FLAN [255] as language models. Building upon BLIP-2, Instruct-BLIP [292] has enhanced instruction-proficiency of multi-modal models, LLaVA [251] and Mini-GPT4 [253] have introduced meticulously constructed instruction sets, which have found widespread application in various multi-modal models. mPLUG-Owl [252] introduces a two-stage paradigm, fine-tuning the visual encoder and then refining the language model with LoRA [293]. This approach effectively fuses image and text features. Some models consider additional modalities, such as ImageBind [294], which simultaneously incorporates data from six modalities without the need for explicit supervision, and PandaGPT [295] which enhances its in-following capabilities. Several multi-modal models prioritize the in-context learning abilities of LLMs. Flamingo [245], one of the pioneering efforts, integrates a gated cross-attention module to align with the spaces of images and text. Otter [250] refines Open-Flamingo [246], an open-source version of Flamingo, improves instruction-following abilities. Multi-Modal In-Context Learning (MMICL) [280] is a comprehensive vision-language model that incorporates Instruct-BLIP, the analysis and comprehension of multiple images, as well as the execution of instructions. ## 5.3 Methodology

In this section, we carefully demonstrate the Machine Vision Therapy process which mainly contains three components: Transition Matrix Estimation, Denoising In-Context Learning and Fine-Tuning of vision models. Next, we demonstrate problem setting and framework.

### 5.3.1 Problem Overview

Generalizing to Out-of-Distribution is a challenging topic in computer vision, where we normally have $P(Y^{id}|X^{id})$ pre-trained by $\theta_{cv}$, where $\hat{y}^{id} = f_{\theta_{cv}}(x^{id})$ is the prediction. In OOD tasks, we are given a set of unlabeled examples $D^{ood} = \{x^{ood}\}_{i=0}^n$ whose element $x^{ood} \in \mathcal{X}$ is drawn from unknown data distribution $p^{ood}$. Due to the change of distribution, factors that affect the data generating process are shifted, causing a difference between $p^{ood}$ and $p^{id}$, further hindering label prediction, i.e., $\hat{y}^{ood} = f_{\theta_{cv}}(x^{ood}) \neq P(Y^{ood}|X^{ood})$, where $Y^{ood}$ is the truth. Fortunately, having been observed with extraordinary low-shot generalization capability, leveraging LLM with parameters $\theta_{llm}\in \Theta_{llm}$ to enhance the OOD robustness of vision models.

Our framework is illustrated in Figure 5.2 and our problem can be formulated as follows:

$$\min_{\theta_{cv}} \mathcal{L}(f_{\theta_{cv}}, z); z = [\theta_{llm}, (X_i^+, Y_i^+); (X_i^-, Y_i^-); X_i)]_i^N;\nY_C = T[c]; \argmax f_{\theta_{cv}}(X_i)$$

where $X_i^+$ and $X_i^-$ denote the positive and negative examples, respectively, $X_i$ is the query image, and $T$ is the transition matrix. Intuitively, when a distribution shift occurs, the emerging prediction errors are essentially noisy labels that can be captured by estimating a transition matrix. Hence, by focusing on calibrating the examples with high noise probabilities, the visual robustness of downstream tasks can be improved effectively.
