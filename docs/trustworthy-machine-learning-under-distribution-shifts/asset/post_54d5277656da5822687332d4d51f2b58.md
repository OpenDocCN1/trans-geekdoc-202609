
## 7.3.2 Theoretical Proof

We first start with a given image $x_q$, when conditioned on a concept $\phi^*$ and prompt $S_n$, the most probable text output for $y_q$ is the same as the class in the prompt distribution $p_{prompt}$ with maximum probability. Then, we show that in our denoising in-context learning, when achieving the most likely prediction $z$ output by the MLLM predictor, the class text $y_q$ in the pretraining distribution $p$ is the same as the one found by the prompt distribution $p_{prompt}$, which is the exact match for the given image $X_q$.

Before we start analyzing the binary prediction $z$, we first investigate the most probable class $y$ given by prompt and query arg $\max_y p(y|S_n, x_q)$.

THEOREM 22. Assume that the above assumption holds, if for all $\phi \in \Phi, \phi \neq \phi^*$, the concept $\phi^*$ satisfies the distinguishability condition: $\sum_{j=1}^k \mathcal{L}_j(\phi^*) > \epsilon_{start}^* + \epsilon_{delim}$, then as $n \to \infty$, the prediction $y$ according to the pretraining distribution is:

$$\max_y p(y|S_n, \phi^*) \to \max_y p_{prompt}(y|X_q). \quad (7.54)$$

Thus, the in-context predictor $f_n$ achieves the optimal $0-1$ risk: $\lim_{n\to\infty} \mathcal{L}_{0-1}(f_n) = \inf_f \mathcal{L}_{0-1}(f)$.

The detailed proof of this theorem is similar to Xie et al. [297], please refer to the Section D of the original paper.

Under this assumption, the in-context predictor is guaranteed to have the highest probability of generating the class description $y$ that exactly matches the query image $x_q$. In another way, when $x_q$ does not belong to $y$, the probability $p(y|S_n, x_q)$ is less than the optimal value.

LEMMA 6. Under the same condition of Theorem 22, the prediction $z$ according to the pretraining distribution is:

$$\max_z p(z|S_n, x_q, y_q, \phi^*) \to \max_z p_{prompt}(z|X_q, y_q). \quad (7.55)$$

## 7.3 PROOF FOR MVT

Lemma 6 can be easily derived based on Theorem 22 by considering $y$ as a fixed prompt.

THEOREM 23. Assume that the above assumptions holds, as $n \to \infty$, when achieving the largest prediction probability of $z$ given prompt under concept $\phi^*$, the corresponding class description $y$ follows the same $y$ obtained from the prompt distribution:

$$\arg_y p(z|S_n, x_q, y, \phi^*) \rightarrow \arg_y p_{prompt}(z|x_q, y) \quad (7.56)$$

PROOF. Since we already have Theorem 22, if we can prove that

$$\arg_y p(y|S_n, x_q, \phi^*) = \arg_y p(z|S_n, x_q, y, \phi^*) \quad (7.57)$$

$$\arg_y p_{prompt}(y|x_q) = \arg_y p_{prompt}(z|x_q, y) \quad (7.58)$$

then we can complete the justification.

$$p(z|S_n, x_q, \phi^*) = \sum_{h^{start} \in \mathcal{H}} p(z|h_q^{start})p(h^{start}|S_n, x_q, y, \phi^*) \quad (7.59)$$

By expanding the last term, we have: where ($h^{start}$) is considered as a constant. Moreover, based on Assumption 21, the joint distribution $p(x_q, y)$ is predefined by the pretraining distribution, which does not affect the marginal distribution of $z$, thus we can have:

$$p(x_q, y|h_q^{start}, S_n, \phi^*) = \frac{p(y|S_n, x_q, h_q^{start}, \phi^*)p(x_q|h_q^{start})}{p(x_q, y)} \quad (7.62)$$

Since the change of $y$ does not affect the quantity of $p(z|h_q^{start})$, thereby applying argmax on both sides of the equation holds for finding the optimal $y$:

$$\arg max_y p(z|S_n, x_q, y, \phi^*) = \arg max_y \sum_{h_q^{start}\in H} p(z|h_q^{start}) p(y|S_n, x_q, h_q^{start}, \phi^*) \quad (7.64)$$

$$= \arg max_y p(y|S_n, x_q, h_q^{start}, \phi^*) \quad (7.65)$$

Similarly, we have:

$$p_{prompt}(z|x_q, y) = \sum_{h_q^{start}\in H} p(z|h_q^{start}, \phi^*) p_{prompt}(h_q^{start}|x_q, y), \quad (7.66)$$

$$p_{prompt}(h_q^{start}|x_q, y) = \frac{p_{prompt}(x_q, y) p_{prompt}(h_q^{start})}{p_{prompt}(x_q, y)} \quad (7.67)$$

$$\propto p_{prompt}(x_q|h_q^{start}) \quad (7.68)$$

$$\propto p(y|h_q^{start}) p_{prompt}(x_q|h_q^{start}) \quad (7.69)$$

$$\arg max_y p_{prompt}(z|x_q, y) = \arg max_y \sum_{h_q^{start}\in H} p_{prompt}(z|h_q^{start}, \phi^*) p_{prompt}(y|x_q, h_q^{start}) \quad (7.70)$$

$$= \arg max_y p(y|x_q, h_q^{start}), \quad (7.71)$$

where the change of $y$ does not affect the quantity of $p_{prompt}(z|h_q^{start}, \phi^*)$. Since:

$$p(y|S_n, x_q, \phi^*) = \sum_{h_q^{start}\in H} p(y|h_q^{start}, S_n, x_q, \phi^*) p(h_q^{start}|S_n, x_q), \quad (7.72)$$

$$p_{prompt}(y|x_q) = \sum_{h_q^{start}\in H} p_{prompt}(y|h_q^{start}, x_q) p_{prompt}(h_q^{start}|x_q), \quad (7.73)$$

it is easy to find that:

$$\arg max_y p_{prompt}(y|x_q) = \arg max_y p(y|x_q, h_q^{start}) \quad (7.74)$$

$$p(y|S_n, x_q, \phi^*) = \sum_{h_q^{start}\in H} p(y|h_q^{start}, S_n, x_q, \phi^*) p(h_q^{start}|S_n, x_q) \quad (7.75)$$

Thus, as $n \to \infty$:

$$p(z|S_n, x_q, \phi^*) = \arg max_y p_{prompt}(z|x_q, y) \quad (7.76)$$

Lemma 6 and Theorem 23 together show that given $x_q$, if $p_{prompt}(y|x_q)$ is the true label, then the in-context predictor can identify the correct class.

## 7.4.2 Proof of Theorem

Now we start the proof of Theorem 7.

ASSUMPTION 24 (Relaxed triangle inequality). For the distance function $d : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^+$, there exists $c_d \geq 1$ such that $\hat{y}_1, \hat{y}_2, \hat{y}_3 \in \mathcal{Y}, d(\hat{y}_1, \hat{y}_2) \leq c_d(d(\hat{y}_1, \hat{y}_3) + d(\hat{y}_2, \hat{y}_3)).

ASSUMPTION 25 (Inverse Lipschitz condition). For the function $d$, it holds that $\forall$:

$$\mathbb{E}[d(h(x_1, x_2))] \leq |\mathcal{L}(h) - \mathcal{L}(h^*)| \quad (7.91)$$

where $h^*$ is the Bayes optimal classifier on both $x_1$ and $x_2$, and

$$\mathbb{E}[d(h(x), h^*(x))] \leq |\mathcal{L}(h) - \mathcal{L}(h^*)| \quad (7.92)$$

ASSUMPTION 26 (Classifier optimality). For any classifier $h$ in comparison to the Bayes' optimal classifier $h^*$, there exists constants $\epsilon > 0$ such that $|\mathcal{L}(h) - \mathcal{L}(h^*)|^2 \leq \epsilon$.
