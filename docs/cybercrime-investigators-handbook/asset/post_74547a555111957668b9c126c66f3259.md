Different CSPs have different rules regarding data storage. Because an action may be legal in one country does not guarantee that it is illegal in another.5 Should the alleged offense be a legal activity in the host jurisdiction, seeking redress or assistance through the court that jurisdiction may have a lower chance of success.

There is agreement between academic research and industry that an investigator may find it impossible to identify the legal jurisdiction without obtaining the support of the CSP.23 This is a major distinction when compared to investigating computing events in infrastructures owned and operated by the user, where the data center or servers containing the evidence reside in a known and geographically accessible location.

In summary, the identification of the jurisdiction where an alleged incident took place be a complex matter requiring the assistance of the CSP. Collecting the evidence in an admissible manner from a foreign jurisdiction is a matter that must be planned, possibly with the assistance of extensive legal support prior to the active component of the investigation and evidence collection commencing. Understand that the cloud-computing environment is one that is very different for the investigator to navigate and having an understanding of the multijurisdictional environment in which you are operating will be of assistance to you.

## Acquisition of Evidence

This section examines the legal and technical feasibility of obtaining evidence from a cloud-computing platform that may be resident in a foreign jurisdiction. The evidence being sought may be that of a complainant or a suspect and may be sought by a civilian or law enforcement examiner. Of particular relevance to this section is identifying the legality of such an action and an examination of what technical barriers may exist.

Conducting a forensic examination in the cloud creates problems that do not exist in traditional infrastructures. First, as the data may reside in foreign jurisdictions, legal advice needs to be sought so that any forensic analysis does not violate the laws of the foreign jurisdiction.9 The ability to conduct forensic examination depends upon the identification of the host jurisdiction and its legislation, as data is subject to the laws of the country in which it resides.28 Also of consideration is the protection of the data of other clients of the CSP that are not subject to the examination.9

Obtaining de-forensic evidence from storage is significantly different from obtaining it from a cloud platform. With cloud platforms, the underlying infrastructure is not available for bit-by-bit imaging.

Acquiring the evidence requires an understanding of the target jurisdiction. Legislation in the United States that a law enforcement officer may access data on a cloud platform includes the Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA), an ordinary warrant, or the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA). An ordinary warrant can be issued under Rule 41 of the Federal Rules of Procedure (FRCP).

As many cloud-computing data centers are located in the United States, when obtaining a search warrant from a CSP based in the United States enforcement officer must be aware of the Fourth Amendment to the US Constitution, which states:

> The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, from unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the things to be seized.

Aside in securing evidence that may be dynamic in nature and may be easily overwritten prior to the service of a court order, a request may be submitted to the CSP asking them to preserve the evidence until a court order may be obtained and served. In the United States, the instrument used to preserve is S2703(f) letter from title 18 of the Code of Federal Regulations. S2703(d) order from title 18 of the Code is required for noncontent data that is more detailed than subscriber details.³

Obtaining cloud-based evidence from a jurisdiction where the EU means that the investigator must navigate European privacy directives as well as host country legislation. As an example, France has the Data Protection Act of 2004, which is administered by the Commission nationale de l'informatique et des libertés (CNIL), as well as the EU Data Protection Directive to protect data in their jurisdiction. This legislation places additional responsibilities on parties seeking to remove data from other countries, including potentially being required to seek separate notification and/or authorization from the CNIL prior to the data being removed from France should the data owner not have informed consent or the recipient country not have a level of data protection that is recognized by French authorities as being equivalent to their domestic legislation at the French or EU level. ³³
