As criminals develop these skills, law enforcement reacts to them and develops their evolving field of investigative knowledge.

Cybercriminals share their knowledge on open source and closed criminal forums, resulting in a higher standard of criminal who can see experienced assistance when their attempted crime meets a hurdle. Cybercriminals also provide tutorials for those new to the industry, including step-by-step-methodologies on to commit cybercrime without leaving digital evidence leading to their identity. If be required, some sites provide one-on-one tutorials and peer review. In essence cybercrime is a profession, with many resources available to the investigator in understanding developing methodologies and the ways cybercriminals control their activities.

An advantage of cybercrime to a criminal, when compared to other forms of crime, is the lack of structural complexity. When compared to a crime as illegal drug trafficking, cybercrime lacks the managerial complexities that are prevalent in crimes involving physical property. A drug trafficker may be required to structure the business, distribution, processing, transportation, competition, threats, network, and money laundering. In cybercrime, there are fewer of these considerations involved. As opposed to the illegal drug trade, where parties involved may personally known to each other and build relationships, those involved in partnerships in cybercrime may never physically meet each other, assisting each other on their areas of expertise. Also, a valuable consideration is that there are no turf wars involved in cybercrime, as are in the illegal drugs trade.¹

There are many reasons why cybercrime is attractive to the criminal. One of these is the financial reward available compared to other forms of crime. Joseph Schafer and his colleagues found that on average the bank robber obtain $2,500, the fraudster $25,000, and the cybercriminal $250,000. They also found that the cost of the technology to an organization is approximately $1.9 million.²

Alongside the financial rewards, a further attraction of cybercrime to the criminal is that certain actions are easy to carry out and hard to detect. The Internet provides anonymity for skilled cybercriminals, who use available technological resources (such as free web-based email accounts).
