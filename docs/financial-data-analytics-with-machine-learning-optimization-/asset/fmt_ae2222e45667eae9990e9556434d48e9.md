
Upon putting this procedure into code, the issue of arithmetic underflow may emerge when the products in ([12.13](#c12.xhtml#c12-disp-0029)) are computed. We usually deal with this by calculating their logarithms. Besides, while the conditional independence assumption yields effective computations of the probabilities, it often deviates from reality when the feature variables are dependent. For instance, feature variables in real data rarely exhibit conditional independence. To illustrate this drawback, consider a classification problem involving ![p equals 2](images/c12-i0194.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mi>p</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>2</mn></math> binary feature variables ![normal x 1](images/c12-i0195.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub></math> and ![normal x 2](images/c12-i0196.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">x</mi><mn>2</mn></msub></math>. A new observation ![left-parenthesis x 1 comma x 2 right-parenthesis](images/c12-i0197.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></math> is classified as class A when ![x 1 equals x 2](images/c12-i0198.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>=</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>2</mn></msub></math>, or class B otherwise. Suppose that for a given training dataset, the number of samples taking every combination of values ![left-parenthesis x 1 comma x 2 right-parenthesis](images/c12-i0199.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></math> is the same, so that each class has half of the observations, while the values of ![normal x 1](images/c12-i0200.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub></math> and ![normal x 2](images/c12-i0201.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">x</mi><mn>2</mn></msub></math> are also evenly distributed in each class. By simple calculations, the product to be maximized on the right‐hand side of ([12.14](#c12.xhtml#c12-disp-0031)) is ![one half times one half times one half equals one eighth](images/c12-i0202.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>2</mn></mfrac><mo>&#215;</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>2</mn></mfrac><mo>&#215;</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>2</mn></mfrac><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>8</mn></mfrac></math> for all combinations of ![left-parenthesis x 1 comma x 2 right-parenthesis](images/c12-i0203.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></math>, implying that the classifier is no better than flipping a coin. Due to this limitation and other ones like it, we propose in the next section how to enhance the Bayes classifiers by incorporating a well‐known notion from actuarial science, namely tail comonotonicity for dependence modelling.

## 12.5 COMONOTONE‐INDEPENDENCE BAYES CLASSIFIER (CIBER)

To remedy the drawbacks of the Naïve Bayes discussed in the previous section, we introduce a variant [\[5\]](#c12.xhtml#c12-bib-0005) that incorporates comonotonicity, as introduced briefly in [Section 8.8](#c08.xhtml#c08-sec-0015) of [Chapter 8](#c08.xhtml). Our aim is to better recover and model the dependence structure among feature variables without complicating the calculations of conditional joint probabilities too much. This variant is called the *Comonotone‐Independence Bayes Classifier*, or *CIBer* for short. This new classifier can effectively deal with discrete feature variables with numerical values, also known as “discrete features”. However, in financial and insurance contexts, the presence of all three types of feature variable, namely discrete, continuous and categorical, is common. To that end, we introduce a unified feature engineering scheme: by binning continuous feature variables and employing the method of *Joint Encoding* for categorical ones, as we shall discuss in Sections [12.5.2](#c12.xhtml#c12-sec-0008) and [12.5.3](#c12.xhtml#c12-sec-0009), we convert all feature variables into discrete features, as the inputs of the new classifier.
