$$\boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{H}^{\top} \boldsymbol{z}_t,$$

where $\boldsymbol{y}_t = (y_t^1, \ldots, y_t^p)^{\top}$ with $y_t^j$ being the $j$-th principal component of $\boldsymbol{z}_t$, and $\boldsymbol{H}$ is the collection of columns of the corresponding loadings of the principal components, and is independent of $t$. If the first $m$ components are sufficient to explain a significant portion of the underlying total variance, one may partition the component vector $\boldsymbol{y}_t$ into $\boldsymbol{y}_t^{(1)}$ and $\boldsymbol{y}_t^{(2)}$ being $m$ and $p-m$ dimensional random vectors respectively. Similarly, we can partition the corresponding loadings $\boldsymbol{H}$ into $\boldsymbol{H}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times m}$ and $\boldsymbol{H}^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times (p-m)}$. In other words, we write

$$\boldsymbol{y}_t = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{y}_t^{(1)} \\ \boldsymbol{y}_t^{(2)} \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \boldsymbol{H} = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{H}^{(1)} & \boldsymbol{H}^{(2)} \end{pmatrix}.$$
