
For a small sample size `n`, we have a rather low confidence that the true value of `μ` is equal to `μₙ`, in the sense that the posterior variance `βₙ⁻¹` is not too small. This confidence increases with `n`, and eventually becomes certain as `n → ∞`, by then `βₙ⁻¹ → 0` which further implies `P(|μ - μₙ| > ε | x = x) → 0` for any `ε > 0` in light of *Chebyshev's inequality*. This is also consistent with the conclusion drawn by using MLE. In this way, we may consider *Bayesian inference* as supplementing MLE.

Secondly, from ([1.18](#c01.xhtml#c01-disp-0065)), the posterior mean `μₙ` is indeed the weighted average of the sample mean `x̄ₙ` and the prior mean `μ₀`, with their respective weights proportional to `nβ` and `β₀`: the weight for `x̄ₙ` in `μₙ = (nβx̄ₙ + β₀μ₀) / (nβ + β₀)`, namely `nβ / (nβ + β₀)`, is known as the *credibility factor* in actuarial science. For any fixed value of `σ₀²` (or equivalently, `β₀`), `μₙ` always depends increasingly more on `x̄ₙ` than `μ₀` when `n` is large, meaning that for a sufficiently large sample, the choice of prior does not significantly affect the posterior estimate of `μ`.

Let us illustrate this claim using a concrete example. We generate a random sample of size `n` from `N(2, 1)`, and perform Bayesian inference on the mean `μ = 2` with a prior distribution of `N(-1, 1)` by pretending that we did not know the mean. [Figure 1.4](#c01.xhtml#c01-fig-0004) shows the posterior distributions of `μ`, and the posterior mean and variance are denoted by `μₙ` and `σₙ²`, respectively. We can observe that the posterior distribution of the mean detaches from the prior distribution and gets closer to the true value of 2 as `n` increases.
