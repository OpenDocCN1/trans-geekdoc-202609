However, since a larger weight of ![nine fifteenths equals 0.6](images/c13-i0281.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mfrac><mn>9</mn><mn>15</mn></mfrac><mo>=</mo><mn>0.6</mn></math> is assigned to ![script upper S Subscript normal upper L](images/c13-i0282.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><msub><mrow><mi mathvariant="script">S</mi></mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">L</mi></msub></math> (with a relatively lower entropy) for the “Risk level” attribute, while the same larger weight is also assigned to ![script upper S Subscript normal upper F](images/c13-i0283.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><msub><mrow><mi mathvariant="script">S</mi></mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">F</mi></msub></math> (with a relatively higher entropy) for the “Sex” attribute, it turns out that the actual average entropy for “Risk level” is lower than that of “Sex”, and so “Risk level” is eventually selected due to a higher information gain. That is a typical example of *Simpson's Paradox*, in which a trend appears in several different groups of data, but disappears or reverses when these groups are combined; also see [\[19\]](#c13.xhtml#c13-bib-0021).
