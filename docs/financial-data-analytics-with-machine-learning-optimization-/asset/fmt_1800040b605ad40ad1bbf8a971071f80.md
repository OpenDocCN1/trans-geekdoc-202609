
On the other hand, since `normal gamma 1` and `normal gamma 2` are independent themselves, and they are also independent of `normal u`, `h(normal u, normal gamma 1)` and `h(1 - normal u, normal gamma 2)` are still conditionally independent given `normal u`, thus the conditional covariance given `normal u` is

`Cov(x, z | u) = Cov(h(u, γ₁), h(1 - u, γ₂) | u) = 0`

Finally, by the tower property,

`Cov(x, z) = E[Cov(x, z | u)] + Cov(E[x | u], E[z | u]) ≤ 0`

For applications of this approach, readers may refer to [11](#c05.xhtml#c05-bib-0011), [15](#c05.xhtml#c05-bib-0015), [16](#c05.xhtml#c05-bib-0016) for more discussions. Meanwhile, we shall continue to use the target integral of [Example 5.1](#c05.xhtml#c05-fea-0002) for the sake of consistent presentation and comparison. [Example 5.2](#c05.xhtml#c05-fea-0008) serves as a counter-example that a mis-use of this approach may lead to a worse performance in terms of the accuracy of estimation. While many other textbooks focus on the merits such as the convenient implementation of [Proposition 5.1](#c05.xhtml#c05-fea-0004), [Example 5.2](#c05.xhtml#c05-fea-0008) suggests that extra caution should be exercised in actual practice.

## [Example 5.2](#c05.xhtml#R_c05-fea-0008) *(Continuation of [Example 5.1](#c05.xhtml#c05-fea-0002))*

We first simulate `u₁, …, u₅,₀₀₀` and then compute the antithetic samples with `vᵢ = 1 - uᵢ`, for `i = 1, …, 5,000`. The estimate is then given as in ([5.1](#c05.xhtml#c05-disp-0016)):

```
θ̂ = (1 / (n / 2)) Σ(i=1 to n/2) [uᵢ³(1 - uᵢ)²·⁵ + vᵢ³(1 - vᵢ)²·⁵] / 2
```
