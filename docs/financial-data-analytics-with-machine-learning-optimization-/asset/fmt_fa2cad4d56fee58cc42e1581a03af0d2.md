In particular, for an AR(![p](images/c07-i0105.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mrow><mi>p</mi></mrow></math>) (resp. MA(![q](images/c07-i0106.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mrow><mi>q</mi></mrow></math>)) model, its ACF (resp. PACF) tails off / decays exponentially (can be sinusoidally) with the lag ![k](images/c07-i0107.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mrow><mi>k</mi></mrow></math>, while its PACF (resp. ACF) cuts off / vanishes after lag ![k greater-than p](images/c07-i0108.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mi>k</mi><mo>&#62;</mo><mi>p</mi></math> (resp. ![k greater-than q](images/c07-i0109.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mi>k</mi><mo>&#62;</mo><mi>q</mi></math>); this latter result is called the `cut-off property`. Their sample counterparts also demonstrate similar trends, see [Figure 7.4](#c07.xhtml#c07-fig-0004) for a numerical illustration. In particular, for the AR(2) model, the sample PACF value does not deviate significantly from 0 for ![k greater-than 2](images/c07-i0110.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mi>k</mi><mo>&#62;</mo><mn>2</mn></math>, which justifies the cut-off property of its PACF at lag 2, and the same argument applies to the ACF of the MA(2) model. The mathematical details of these properties have been thoroughly discussed in various classical textbooks on time series, including [\[15](#c07.xhtml#c07-bib-0014), [39\]](#c07.xhtml#c07-bib-0037) to name a couple. While the properties mentioned above are for pure AR and MA models, they can also be applied to the determination of the values of ![p](images/c07-i0111.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mrow><mi>p</mi></mrow></math> and ![q](images/c07-i0112.png)<math display="inline" overflow="scroll"><mrow><mi>q</mi></mrow></math> in the general ARMA model, namely by observing the cut-off point of the sample PACF and ACF plots, respectively. See [Table 7.1](#c07.xhtml#c07-tbl-0001) for a summary of the properties of ACF and PACF for ARMA models.

ARIMA models are a useful tool commonly used in a wide range of disciplines, including but not limited to financial forecasting, economic analysis, operations research, and climate modelling. Here, let us illustrate their application to finance under the context of a recent trendy topic, namely the price prediction of `Bitcoin`. Bitcoin is a digital crypto-currency that enables individuals to transfer monetary value to one another without the requirement of the presence of a trusted third party like a bank or payment processor. It is the first decentralized peer-to-peer payment network solely powered by its own users without a central authority. The Bitcoin network monitors the movement of the cryptocurrency among digital wallets to prevent fraud and double spending. Bitcoin represents an innovative new form of digital money that is censorship-proof and accessible to anyone with internet access. New Bitcoins are issued through a process called “mining” in which computers solve complex math problems to discover a new block in the blockchain. Miners are then rewarded with new Bitcoins for verifying transactions and securing the network.
