`s² = 1/(n−1) Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (uᵢ − ū)²`

can serve as an unbiased estimate of `σ²τ`, and so we can estimate `σ` by `σ̂ = s/√τ`. However, choosing an appropriate value for `n` is not easy. Generally speaking, the estimate is more accurate when `n` is large. Unfortunately, in reality `σ` does change in time. Including too many past observations may be useless or even harmful for the prediction of the spontaneous volatility. As a rule of thumb, the choice of `n` should reflect the length of time the volatility is used. For instance, if we want to estimate the value of a one-year option, the daily stock price for the last year should be taken. More sophisticated methods for estimating volatility will be discussed in later sections of this chapter. Obviously the volatility varies with time rather than staying constant. Let us focus on computing the evolution in time of the standard deviation of `uᵢ`'s based on a moving window of width `w`. First, we compute the standard deviation of the first `w` observations `u₁, …, u_w`, and then that of `u₂, …, u_{w+1}`, and so forth. This gives rise to a series of estimated standard deviations.
