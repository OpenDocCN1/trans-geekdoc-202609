
The tail of the `tν`-distribution is heavier than that of any normal distribution. In principle, it becomes normal when `ν → ∞` and `tν`-distributions are commonly used to model security data with fat tails. For common securities, the value of `ν` ranges from 3 to 6 as pointed out in [[11](#c03.xhtml#c03-bib-0011)]; specifically, Harry Markowitz noted that a degree of freedom around 4 yields the best estimate and is consistent with the observations from S&P500 stock returns. We can lay down `tν` Q-Q plots to see whether the fittings look better. First, we need to estimate the degrees of freedom `ν`. One simple way is to match the sample excess kurtosis `ζ̂₂` computed from the sample `uᵢ`'s to the theoretical value of `6 / (ν − 4)`, which gives an estimate of `ν = 6 / ζ̂₂ + 4`. The `p`-th quantile of the `tν`-distribution is obtained by setting `P{T ≤ tν(p)} = p`, denoted by `qₚ := tν(p)`. In [Programmes 3.9](#c03.xhtml#c03-gra-0009) (in Python) and [3.10](#c03.xhtml#c03-gra-00010) (in **R**), we can respectively use the `t_QQ_plot()` and `t.QQ.plot()` functions to produce the `tν` Q-Q plots.

From [Figures 3.5](#c03.xhtml#c03-fig-0005) and [3.6](#c03.xhtml#c03-fig-0006), we note that the `tν`-distribution fits the fat tails better than the normal distribution. Again, we can use the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test to test whether `u₁`, `u₂` and `u₃` follow a `tν`-distribution via [Programmes 3.11](#c03.xhtml#c03-gra-00011) (in Python) and [3.12](#c03.xhtml#c03-gra-00012) (in **R**).
