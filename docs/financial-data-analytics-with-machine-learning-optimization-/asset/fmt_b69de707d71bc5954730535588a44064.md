
Note that as $\alpha \to 0$, $\mathcal{E} \to \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(g(x_i)-y_i)^2$, hence the MAP estimate converges to the least square estimate. Furthermore, as $\alpha > 0$, the second term of $\mathcal{E}$ in ([12.12](#c12.xhtml#c12-disp-0021)) increases with the norm of $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, thus it can be regarded as a regularization term, meaning that MAP estimation can be regarded as a regularization on the least square estimation. The resulting $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ tends to $\mathbf{0}$ for a larger value of $\alpha$; while the prior distribution becomes closer to a uniform distribution as $\alpha$ decreases, echoing a weaker regularization in $\mathcal{E}$. As a remark, the selection of the parameter $\alpha$ may vary according to the data structure. For instance, when all $x_i$'s lie between 0 and 1 while the responses $y$'s have a larger magnitude, the estimate of $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ may inevitably be large. To this end we may choose a smaller $\alpha$ to strike a balance.

Apart from regularization, the penalty term in the expression of $\mathcal{E}$ in ([12.12](#c12.xhtml#c12-disp-0021)) can also help to prevent overfitting, as a simpler model is sought. Models with a larger degree $M$ are usually subject to a larger penalty, and by comparing the values of $\mathcal{E}$ across models with various values of $M$, we can select an appropriate degree $M$ of the estimated polynomial to avoid overfitting and to enhance out‐sample predictions. This can be considered as an alternative to the well‐known *Bayesian Information Criterion* (BIC), defined as $\text{BIC} := -2 \ln \hat{L} + k \ln n$, where $\hat{L}$ is the maximized likelihood, $k$ is the number of parameters to be estimated, and $n$ is the sample size; also see [Appendix 12.A.212.6.2](#c12.xhtml#c12-sec-0018) for a motivation of BIC via Laplace's approximation.

Straightforward calculations of ([12.11](#c12.xhtml#c12-disp-0020)) show that the posterior is indeed another multivariate normal distribution:

$$
\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y} = \tilde{y} \sim \mathcal{N}_{M+1}\left(\lambda \boldsymbol{S} \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i \boldsymbol{\phi}(x_i), \boldsymbol{S}\right)
$$
