Ethics, in simple terms, are do's and don'ts, i.e., virtues and evil we all learn and are expected to follow because ethics can be defined based on well-founded standards of right and wrong that prescribe what humans ought to do, usually in terms of rights, obligations, benefits to society, fairness or specific virtues.* We need to speak of ethics for AI and especially machine intelligence, independent of merely human ethics. Ethics of AI design encompasses the moral behaviour of the engineers involved in designing, industries and making AI-augmented products, including for defence services, policy and decision-makers who allow the use of such products and finally the users. As it consider the behaviour of machines; ethics also becomes a subject of scrutiny. The ethics of AI is the branch of ethics of technology to artificially intelligent systems. During the 41st Session of UNESCO Global Conference held in November 2021 in Paris, an agreement called 'Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence,' was adopted, renewing cooperation on this many organisations have also come up with codes of ethics to guide stakeholders facing ethical dilemmas when deciding on adaptation and use of AI. Interestingly, years before the development of AI to its latest, the potential dreadfulness of AI-powered robots was foreseen by the famous Russian-American science writer Isaac Asimov, who wrote fiction series on robots and created ‘The Three Laws of Robotics’ – binding robots to follow rules to limit damage. In Asimov’s code of ethics, the first law forbids robots from actively harming humans or allowing humans to come to harm. The second law orders robots to obey orders unless the orders are in accordance with the first law. The third law orders robots to protect themselves insofar as doing so is in accordance with the first laws. Engineers must take note of the following guidelines to ensure the ethical design of AI, which follows:

- Transparency – the most important ethical domain. Its adherence protects fundamental human rights, dignity, autonomy and well-being of society (UNI Global, 2017).
- Justice and Fairness – issues of fairness, unbiased and equity must be built into the algorithm model.
- Non-maleficence – Avoidance of harm to human beings been an important aspect of concern in AI ethics, particularly in areas like killer robots and drones (UAVs).
- Responsibility and accountability – Liability and accountability for making war must be traceable and under judicial review data protection standards outlined in the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) (2018).
- Benefic – AI should bring benefits to the society as highlighted in IEEE (2019).
- Freedom and autonomy – AI developers should ensure no impediments to the freedom and liberty of human beings.
- Trust and sustainability – trust is important for the ethical deployment and use of AI as documented in Special Interest Group on Artificial Intelligence (HLEG) (2019).

Although it is not in doubt that AI is to be part of the future of militaries around the world, almost eight decades ago, USSR fielded military robots in the battlefield during the Second World War in the form of unmanned radio-controlled 'tasks'. The Red Army used them during the Winter War against Finland. It was passed by the German Goliath, a tactical mine vehicle. Robots from the point of view are of three types:

- Automatic robots—which respond in a mechanical way to inputs.
- Automated robots—which execute commands in a preprogrammed way with sensors that help sequence the action. They are mostly limited by algorithms that determine their rules of operations and behaviour out of a fixed set of alternative actions, which makes them predictable.
- The third type of robot is an autonomous robot which has minimum human interference other than possibly switching it on or off.

Military prefer and mostly use autonomous robots for various purposes and such military robots' market has been showing a constant rise in leading countries' defence budgets in years. The latest available data suggests that the military robots' market size will likely grow from $14.5 billion in 2020 to $24.2 billion by 2025, at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 10.7%. AI-powered armed vehicles and autonomous surveillance vehicles are now part of military inventory because the advantages they offer as a force multiplier in battle. As robots are devoid of emotions and are without fear, they can undertake risky tasks, saving human lives of friendly forces. Besides, robots are more accurate and have better decision-making abilities in stressful battlefield environments. They have made military tasks safer, easier and precise. Types of military robots will depend on the purpose for which they were designed. Presently, for military use there are transportation robots, search and rescue robots, firefighting robots, mine clearance robots, surveillance and reconnaissance robots, armed robots and unarmed aerial/ground/underwater vehicles.

The US first deployed robots in July 2002, when the robot 'Hermes' was used to search the caves in Qiqay, at Afghanistan, to find hidden enemies and stockpiles of weapons. Since then, militaries around the world have been deploying robots, including the latest AI-augmented robots, in combat and surveillance roles. Few of the robots operative in the major militaries are as follows:

- One is Multi-Utility Tactical Transport (MUTT), which carries equipment/load up to the weight of 1,200 pounds assisting the soldiers travelling on foot in difficult terrains, was tested by US Marines in July 2016. This Unmanned Ground System (UGS) produced by General Dynamics Land System is being inducted in the US Army.
- Next is RISE, a climbing robot that scales rough terrain, including walls, fences and trees.
- There is a 9 mm Glock pistol-equipped combat robot weighing 26 pounds named DOGO developed by General Robotics which functions as a watchdog for soldiers in battle.
- Guardbot, a surveillance robot that can negotiate all types of terrain, including sand, snow and dirt as bodies of water, having the ability to swim.
- Centaur robot, named after the mythological creature with the upper body of a human and lower body of a horse, is capable of finding landmines, unexploded ordnance, improvised explosive devices (IEDs) and destroying them.
- An unmanned ground vehicle (UGV) with a remote control mechanism which looks like a small tank that can carry out multiple tasks like reconnaissance in Nuclear-Biological-Chemical (NBC) environment, direct fire, obstacle breaching and surveillance etc. in all types of warfare, to reduce hazards for regular soldiers.
- SAFFIR is a military robot that stands 5 feet 10 inches tall and weighs 143 pounds – developed by researchers at Virginia Tech. This is one of the most autonomous military robots utilising advanced military technology designed to extinguish a fire on a warship.
