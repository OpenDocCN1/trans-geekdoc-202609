# What is an intelligent military machine?

The term artificial intelligence (AI) was first used as a title for the conference held at Dartmouth College in 1955. Alan Turing’s (a mathematician and a pioneer in computer science) 1950 essay titled ‘Computing Machinery and Intelligence’ can also be taken as a signpost for the birth of the term AI.¹ AI is machines that can generate behaviour similar to humans, such as making choices and judgements. The rise of AI is part of the ‘Information Revolution.’ Information is a crucial ingredient of material productivity in the era of Information Revolution. In this era, the generation of new knowledge is dependent on computing power. Basic services like banking, railways, transportation, water and energy distribution are becoming more and more dependent on information and communication technologies (ICT). The principal sources of energy include digital information, computer software and communications. The computer with its electronic brain is supplementing (might supplement in the future) human thinking. The integrated circuits and computer technology are the bedrock of AI, and these two technologies are continuously developing. AI is a combination of technologies which includes both hardware and software. So, an AI system includes hardware like microchips, sensors. The fundamental blocks of AI are learning algorithms which allow the machine to detect patterns, make decisions, adjust their behaviour to environment. The field studying the effect of AI on warfare is overloaded with acronyms, abbreviations and conceptual categories. Social scientists argue that a tight and static definition of a concept or category will result in the death of social sciences. Nevertheless, a working definition of various terms, categories is necessary. The understanding of what constitutes autonomous differs among people and regions.

The UK in a Joint Doctrine Note published in 2011 differentiated automated/automatic systems (which have elements of AI in them). An automated or automatic system follows a predefined set of rules and results in predictable outcomes. One example is the Phalanx Anti-Ship Missile Defence System. In contrast, an autonomous system is capable of understanding higher-level intent and direction. With a sophisticated perception of its environment, a system is capable of taking a course of action from a number of alternatives without depending on human oversight or control. Overall, the activity of an autonomous system is predictable though individual actions may be, as the system not merely follow a pattern of rules in a predictable way. Paul Scharre a former US Army Ranger turned military AI expert, writes that as machines become more sophisticated, they are more capable and able to execute complex tasks in more open-ended environments. Scharre classifies AI on the basis of its intelligence and autonomy into three classes: Automatic, automated and autonomous. An automatic machine is simple threshold-based, and an automated one is more complex rule-based. An autonomous system senses the environment and acts. It does not exhibit much in the way of decision-making, and its actions are predictable. An autonomous system, in contrast, may consider a variety of inputs and weighs variables before taking an action. An autonomous machine is goal-oriented and self-directed. Goal-oriented suggests that the human user specifies the goal to be achieved, but how it is achieved is decided by the machine. Its internal cognitive processes are less intelligible to its handlers. Hence, in Scharre’s framework, at one pole is the automatic machine, which is least autonomous and minimally intelligent.
