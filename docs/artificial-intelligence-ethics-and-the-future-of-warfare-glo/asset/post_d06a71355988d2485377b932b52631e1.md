There is a debate between the USA, the most affluent of sixth-generation fighter designs, about the affordability of such aircraft which is expected to be huge. Fifth-generation fighters F-22 and F-35, despite their performances, have minimal sales due to price tags. The huge cost of fifth-generation fighters has scared away other nations. Some of the nations with current sixth-generation programmes have leapfrogged the fifth. There is a thought process of shunning fifth and sixth-generation fighters, rejuvenating fourth-generation and giving them some additional features without major changes. This thought process points towards the emergence of 4.5th generation, which achieves a compromise between cost (far less than fifth-generation aircraft) and capability (lot better than fourth-generation).

Already, elements of AI are being retrofitted on some older aircraft. Aircraft more than four decades old have been given updates of onboard memory and networking speeds are being enhanced so as to enable essential AI functions (data access, scanning, targeting, radar warning, images and video). AI has been introduced and is helping to lower pilot workload related to flying and navigating. The F-15 is being given AI and ML algorithms to enhance its capabilities under the ‘cognitive cockpit’. The EA-18G Growler is also getting AI injected into its systems to imbue Electronic Warfare (EW) capabilities: an illustration is a system called Reactive Electronic Measures (REAM). Northrop Grumman was awarded a contract for the development of this Machine Learning Algorithms (MLAs) to the EA-18G's airborne attack suite to achieve capabilities against agile, adaptive and unknown hostile radars. REAM technology is expected to join active fleet squadrons. Many of the AI applications mentioned above are available in varying degrees of sophistication and could be grafted onto existing or evolving aircraft types without much difficulty. As AI permits cockpit to perform more vital functions at speeds exceeding that human pilots are capable, aircraft could move from sharing cockpits with human pilots (as U-2 mentioned above) to manning them independently. Thus, an 'optionally manned cockpit' design is a key attribute of sixth-generation fighter. As also evident from the discussion on sixth-generation fighters, a quintessential element of ongoing sixth-generation programmes is the AI-driven capability to team with UAVs for sundry offensive and defensive roles. This teaming is discussed later.

# AI as cockpit crew

On 20 August 2020, in the finale of AlphaDogfight trials under Defense Research Projects Agency's (DARPA) auspices, an algorithm developed by Heron Systems and installed in a simulator F-16 comprehensively defeated US Air Force pilot in five rounds of combat. Almost exactly a year ago, DARPA had emphatically stated that 'No AI currently exists, however, that can outduel human strapped into a fighter jet in a high-speed, high-G dogfight.' Deep analysis would show that the simulation conditions were biased in favour of AI. The human pilot was in an actual aircraft and had a Virtual Reality (VR) headset to give him situational awareness. However, the demonstration was insignificant as it ratified the construct of AI replacing a pilot in the cockpit for something as dynamic as dogfight.

Civil aviation already has models of AI in the cockpit. The Autonomy system from Garmin enables, in case of pilot incapacity, for on-board systems to take either automatically or on activation by the pilot or a passenger, stabilise the aircraft and then autoland, if required. Interestingly, this, now certified by US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), is not projected as an AI system but as automation. The patent application for Emergency Autoland System did not use the term 'artificial' or 'intelligence' anywhere.

However military aviation requirements are much more demanding and stringent. On 15 December 2020, the USAF flew a single-seat U-2 on a simulated missile strike mission²⁰ with an AI algorithm called ART as a working crew member. A human pilot flew the aircraft and coordinated with ART, which was responsible for sensor employment and tactical navigation. The system is designed to be transferable to another type of aircraft with ease and has the potential to be occupant in modern fighters as well as future ones. The implication is that the pilot workload of flying and fighting can be reduced by AI in the cockpit taking complex tasks of managing data from multiple sources and sensors. ART (or similar system) could also manage autonomous systems teamed with the fighter it is located in (manned-unmanned teams are discussed later). There have been reports of other programmes for unmanned combat aircraft. For example, Japan is reportedly developing an unmanned fighter to operate by 2035.²

Current technological progress encourages optimistic aviation accommodating incremental AI participation in cockpit workloads. The AlphaDogfight series of demonstrations is DARPA's Air Combat Evolution (ACE) programme, which aims to progressively automate aerial combat and foster confidence in unmanned cockpits. Evolving fighters, previously clubbed into sixth-generation fighters, stand to benefit immensely from the inexorable tip of AI into modern cockpits. A promising example is DARPA's Air Labour In-Cockpit Automation System (ALIAS), which is aimed at developing a flexible, drop-in, removable kit that would permit appending high levels of AI-driven automation into an existing aircraft; the ultimate aim is to execute every mission from take-off to landing² with just AI working as a cockpit crew. This forms the basis for the Manned-Unmanned Teaming (MUM-T) concept, now in an advanced stage of maturity.
