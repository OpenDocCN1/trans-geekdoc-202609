In addition to the lack of consensus about terminology, other points of disagreement (some explicit, others only implicit) hamper the international discussions about AWS. For instance, some parties speak as though AWS is already in operation and has been for some time; others, by contrast, assume that deployment of AWS is still off in the future. The disparity would suggest that in their exchanges the parties are not referring to an identical set of technologies. In other words, for some a ban would prohibit all future weapon systems, while others recoil because a ban would also cover existing technologies, of which some are well entrenched and considered valuable to some militaries.

Other ambiguities surround key terms of the discussion, for instance the meaning assigned to ‘autonomous functioning,’ ‘targeting’ and so. If a ban on AWS is to become a matter of prescriptive law, these ambiguities would have to be ironed out, so that the parties could operationalise what exactly is or not excluded from their arsenals.⁷⁷ Finally, among those who embrace the language of ‘meaningful human control,’ disagreement surrounds the relevant point of comparison. For some, the term implies human must be involved in every decision where a specific human being is targeted with lethal harm. This would entail that no automation is allowable whenever a ‘trigger’ is pulled, as in ‘hand-to-hand’ combat situations. For others, ‘meaningful human control’ refers instead to the person commanding an operation; he or she delegates the execution of the command to subordinates whether human or machine – and adequately oversee what they do. In this connection, it is important to recognise that even when a human executes a commander’s ‘meaningful’ control is not equivalent to ‘perfect’ control; human subordinates cannot be counted to invariably act in accordance with the intent of their commanders; by consequence, setting perfect control as a standard would perhaps set an idealistic expectation for the acceptable use of AWS in battlefield scenarios.

What are the effects of AI on the chain of command? Even in conventional warfare, not all decisions can be carried out by way of obedience. Two situations can render it inoperative/inapplicable: (1) time constraints do not allow for receiving instructions from above; (2) communications links are severed, not allowing for receipt of commands. Obedence, moreover, can fail in its basic intent, as commanders can misconstrue a situation and, based on error, order actions that run counter to what they would have intended. Here, a kind of higher obedience triumphs the more immediate, lower obedience. When Eriksen gave his order to fire, he was asked by his subordinates whether he had received the command to do so; he famously responded, ‘Either I will be decorated or I will be court martialed. Fire!’

Imagine AI-driven autonomous technologies had been operated at Oscarsburg. The communications would have happened much more quickly. Eriksen would have known in this certainty until after the Blücher was a German vessel (he did not know until he had fired). Sensors downstream would have shown this and relayed the information instantaneously to him. Had an intelligent machine been on command instead the order received from his superior (human) commander in Oslo. If the command had been, ‘don’t fire’, the AI machine, at that moment the possessor of delegated operational authority, would have instantly complied with this command, and Oscarsburg’s torpedoes would not have been fired. The Blücher would have sailed to Oslo, resulting in the Norwegian King Haakon’s capture and a significant hampering of the execution of government personnel as well as gold reserves from the capital. Imagine if the command to fire had been issued by an officer who was a German sympathizer; would the AI machine then on duty at Oscarsburg have factored this? The machine’s compliance would have been automatic or could be withheld under strict guidelines. What guidelines? How would it have dealt with the ambiguity of the Oscarsburg situation, Norway being officially neutral at the time? Would the AI machine have fired warning shots instead (as was then required by Norway’s prewar rules of engagement, which Eriksen maintained had been satisfied by warning shots that had earlier been issued to no avail by fortresses situated down the Oslo Fjord allowing the ship to pass the narrow strait? Human obedience is automatic; might pass through the filter we call conscience. Context must be carefully weighed. This is the great benefit of human obedience; its combination of responsiveness and flexibility makes obedience a powerful virtue in moments of crisis. At present, no machine, however intelligent it may be, can replicate the subtlety of human obedience. (Perhaps such replication will never be possible). This is the great advantage that human obedience has over automatic machine compliance. Where human beings comes short, however, in addition to the question of speed and knowledge, is in their ability to step outside the chain of command and/or in pursuit of their private ends. This would not arise with a machine, unless it was spoofed, or penetrated by an adversary.

## Virtue ethics as an appropriate language and framing of AI military ethics

We conclude our ethical analysis with a brief discussion of the ethical framework and language to be used when discussing the military ethics of employing AI systems, not least with the virtue ethics of military education.

While ethics in general and military ethics in particular are often associated with the formulation of basic duties and rules (under the heading of deontology or duty ethics) or the calculation of desired effects (under the heading of utilitarianism or more consequential ethics), the approach to that informs much of the training and education in – and self-understanding of military ethics is ‘virtue ethics.’ This is an ethical approach that sees good character (‘excellence’ or ‘virtue’) as inescapable and foundational to soul behaviour.

Some would say that character and virtue are concepts inextricably linked to human beings and their behaviour, and that they do not apply to the actions of machines. One could claim that increased use of computer-based machinery in general and AI-based, increasingly autonomous machines and systems in particular make virtue ethics less relevant. Instead, duties, rules and consequences become the key terms for the ethical use of AI, including in military settings.

Given the centrality of elements such as training, competence, proper feedback loops, safety, trust and proper delineation of responsibility, we believe that virtue ethics remains an unavoidable and essential framework for understanding the ethics of using AI-based machines and systems in a military setting. From the way in which systems are developed and programmed to the way they are deployed and used, whether for information gathering and other forms of enhancement of human decisions or as autonomous decision-makers, the virtues central to proper military action will inform the processes for them to align with core legal and ethical concerns. Rules and utilitarian calculations without a basis in virtue and character can detail the most inhumane of acts and processes.
