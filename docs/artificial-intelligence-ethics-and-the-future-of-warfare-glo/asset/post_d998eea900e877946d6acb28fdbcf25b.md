The use of AI-generated Earth observation data has also shown promise in the planning and execution of armed attacks in future wars. Notably, there is a growing range of satellites, both commercial and military. M. Borowitzowicz discusses the rapid increase of small satellites in Earth’s orbit. Of the reported 666 satellites for intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance and remote sensing as of 2018, 353 belonged to entities and the government of the US, China had 122, Russia had 23 and the rest of the world had remaining 168. 58 The US Army has already experimented with the capability of satellite systems to generate battlefield imagery, leveraging the use of both commercial and military satellites to rapidly targeting and help commanders make real-time decisions.59 Project Convergence which started in 2020 at Arizona, USA reported that combined with new capabilities on data fusion and artificial intelligence, the Army reported cutting down shooter time from 20 minutes to 20 seconds.60 There are instances where geospatial intelligence to find and eliminate terrorists around the world.61

Introducing AI into planning and military strategies in African countries poses several ethical questions and opportunities. In most instances, there is always the concern as to how it can help in saving lives or unnecessarily killing people and destroying property during armed conflicts. The major concerns are the capabilities of AI-controlled weapons to distinguish between military and civilian objectives; a basic principle of IHL. Also, there is concern as to how satellite information can avoid mistaken targets and unnecessary loss of lives, not forgetting its unethical use can cause untold consequences and breach major rules of engagement in prohibited areas, etc.

This research is centred around Nigeria, given its relatively strong military capacities and economy within the continent.³ Nigeria struggles with several security threats, ranging from petty crimes, through kidnappings and assassinations, to serious terrorist activities, especially in the northern region. Nigeria's weapon systems are mostly imported from other countries like China, the USA and Russia. Currently, the Defence Industries Corporation of Nigeria (DICON), established in 1964, serves as Nigeria's official weapons developer.⁷⁵ DICON is still known to produce weapons as ceremonial scrolls, hand grenades, sub-machine guns, the Nigerian rifle 1 model 7.62 mm and the OBJ 007 (a Nigerian version of the Russian AK-47). There have also been sightings of locally-made patrol vehicles and Irigiri, an armoured vehicle manufactured by DICON.¹⁷⁶
