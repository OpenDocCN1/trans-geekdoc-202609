This problem is set in the absence of any agreed framework or an understanding of how escalatory behaviour can be detected in cyberspace. For instance, a cyber campaign launched for signalling (i.e., for coercive diplomacy) may go unnoticed by the target or, worse, be mistaken for an offensive attack. Even if an operation of this nature is promptly and easily discovered, the motivations behind would remain unclear or misunderstood. Thus, the onus falls on nation states and especially powers to communicate clearly and transparently about their AI capabilities as to prevent an AI arms race which may consequently upset the current nuclear strategic balance. This AI arms race would not be limited to state actors; sooner the proliferation could be among many non-state actors. Owing to the 'scalability, efficiency and ease of diffusion' of AI systems, the cost in terms of resources, manpower and psychological distance would be lower and the barriers to entry would come down. A classic example is the use of UAVs (or semi-autonomous drones), the announced version of these were seen in the Vietnam War in 1959–75, but by the end of the 2000s, this technology had proliferated among non-state actors.43 With the commercial off of non-state actors continuously employing them in their activities. During the Battle of Mosul in 2017, the Islamic State loaded 40 mm grenades onto UAVs and dropped them on Iraqi Government positions, killing up to 30 Iraqi soldiers in a single week.44

This is a common problem with emerging technologies. Initial development is expensive, but once commercialised, the price drops drastically and with algorithm-based systems, the cycle would more likely go faster because many of these systems are currently developed using open source software. Even if they are not being developed with open source software, once an algorithm has been developed, the cost of producing it is almost non-existent. This necessitates new approaches to arms control, including mechanisms that bridge the gap between conventional and nuclear munitions and address proliferation to non-state actors. This will also alter the nature of warfare because, as access to weapon technologies becomes more widely available, states and non-state actors will be tempted to use surrogates to fight on their behalf.
