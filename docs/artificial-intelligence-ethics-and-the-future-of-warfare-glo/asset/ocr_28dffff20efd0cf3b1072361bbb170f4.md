# Ethics, laws on war and AI-driven warfare 195

Visibly, in the last couple of decades, drones, in their avatar as weapons, have changed the face of war. In the year 2020, the world witnessed the pivotal role Turkish drones played in Azerbaijan's impressive victory over Armenia in the Nagorno-Karabakh conflict. also following the Russian invasion in Ukraine the TV footage with how Kamikaze drones supplied by the US brought upon heavy losses on the Russian tanks, proving how military drones are playing a pivotal role in Ukraine's defensive war against the mighty Russian forces. Undeniably, AI-powered UAVs (drones) have now joined the armed forces of the world in their inventory as they are becoming indispensable in modern war.

A new kind of war was born when the USA and its allies commenced using drones in countries where they were not formally at war or without the proper consent of the sovereign states. In such places, UAVs targeted individual terror masterinds or terror groups in their so called 'war against terror.' Turkey and some other countries have been using drones for transnational assassination in Syria. The use of drones in the war against terror and the misuse of drones for terrorism calls for an in-depth analysis of the ethical and legal issues involved. But the most contentious issue here is the use of autonomous drones/weapon systems without human imprint or negligible human control because (lethal) autonomous weapon systems (AWS/LAWS support extrajudicial killings, take responsibility away from humans, and makes war or killings more likely.²²

In a message to the Group of Government Experts, Antonio Gutierrez, UN Secretary General on March 25 2019 wrote: 'Autonomous machines with the power and discretion to select targets and take lives without human involvement are potentially unacceptable, morally repugnant and should be prohibited by international law.' He further reminded the expert dealing with AI-assisted weapons that human responsibility for decisions on the use of weapon systems must be retained, such accountability cannot be transferred to machines.²³

So far, the international community has not prohibited the use of such weapons. It is difficult for the UN to garner the support of all members to make them agree to ban AI-powered autonomous weapons/AI-embedded technology that may be used for offensive purposes in the form of a law. And that leads to the lowering of how use such systems (autonomous vehicles, fire-and-forget/loitering missiles, or drones loaded with explosives) and reducing the probability of being held accountable would increase the probability of their use. The cruel asymmetry where one side can kill with impunity, and thus has no reasons to do so, already exists in conventional drone zones with remote-controlled weapons (e.g., USA in Pakistan). Arguably, the main threat is not the use of such weapons in conventional warfare, but by non-state agents, including criminals.²⁴
