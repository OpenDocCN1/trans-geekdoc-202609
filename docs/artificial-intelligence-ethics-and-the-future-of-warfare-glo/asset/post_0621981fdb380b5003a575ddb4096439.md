## Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a growing field that finds application in various sectors. It has often been termed as 'general-purpose technology' that enhances or adds functionality when integrated into systems.¹ The major idea behind the use of AI within machines is to allow some control and autonomy with limited or no human assistance or supervision.

Autonomy is not a new concept, especially in weapon systems where its employment has been witnessed since at least the era of the Second World War. For instance, the Norden Bombsight and V-1 buzz bomb were controlled by sensors linked to computer systems and application of lethal force while the final decision-making control always remained with the operator.² Autonomy depends on a diverse array of technology but primarily software. The viability of autonomy relies on (a) 'the ability of software developers to formulate an intended task in terms of a mathematical problem and a solution; and (b) 'the possibility of mapping or modelling the operating environment in advance.³ Autonomy can be created or enhanced by AI and ML.⁴ The current advancement in autonomy is because of the increasing computing power and continuous development in ML has made the development of commercially viable autonomous systems, such as AI voice assistants, autonomous vehicles or autonomous weapons possibility.⁵ AI can have different levels of autonomy based on⁶

- Human involvement in the system's task execution:
- Human in the loop: Semi-autonomous systems which remain under the active control of a human operator.
- Human on the loop: Human-supervised systems that remain the oversight of a human operator.
- Human out of the loop: Fully autonomous systems that carry out operations without any interference or oversight of a human operator.
- Reactive Systems: Follow condition-action rules, commonly referred to as 'if-then' rules.
- Deliberative Systems: Employ a model of the world (information on how the functions work and how actions by the system are responded by the world), a value function (which offers information about the intended goal), and a set of alternative rules to search for and plan ways to accomplish the goal.
- Learning systems: Become more effective over time by gaining experience.
- Operational tasks include 'mobility, health management (fault detection), etc.'.
- Mission tasks include 'target identification and selection, explosive detection, etc.'

AI has been segregated into three branches: Narrow (weak) AI, General Intelligence (AGI) and AI Super Intelligence (ASI). ASI is said to be a futuristic capability where the machines would surpass the intelligence of humans who have the brightest and most gifted minds. AGI has been linked to an advanced level of intelligence that would least match, or not surpass, that of a human. It is supposed to be so advanced that it would be able to comprehend the environment around it and also make decisions and predictions based on this understanding, something more akin to what was portrayed in the movie *The Terminator*. To date, we have a narrow phase of AI today. This phase has improved over last time and is continually developing, as machines are able to perform tasks such as object identification or language translation, but as per the user-defined inputs and parameters. Often these parameterized inputs are broadly defined but allow a substantial range of autonomy by establishing some limits on the AI's behaviour. For instance, AlphaZero – a computer program, developed by DeepMind – mastered the game of chess. Playing chess allowed the technology to initiate moves as per its own understanding (providing freedom to navigate the chess board) but within the rules of the game. Hence, AI in the current context is defined as 'purpose-built, problem-specific and context-driven.'¹⁸

Understandably, the introduction of AI within weapon systems could facilitate significant improvements in the military domains, for instance, optimising solutions for concise operations that are data-intensive like logistics and maintenance. Speedy and concise analysis using AI would provide policy-makers with better information and more time to make decisions. However, these AI systems are also brittle, that is, there is a high plausibility that these systems may fail in unpredictable situations or behave in an unpredictable manner that could further snowball into escalation and deter the current stability matrix among nations. Cyberspace, in this context, is the foundation on which AI is grounded, and hence, cybersecurity is a necessary condition for the secure deployment of AI systems for all social, economic and military purposes. The shortcomings and vulnerabilities of cyberspace would intrinsically linked to the implementation of AI, which would further exacerbate the deterrence equation.

Against this backdrop, this chapter will first analyse the relevance of the topic, employing the ongoing Ukraine-Russia War as a case in point that has witnessed a number of cyber operations feeding into kinetic warfare. Further, this chapter will examine the degree of autonomy showcased by the weapons employed in the Russian-Ukraine War. This examination is important as it will highlight how Ukraine-Russia War will set precedents for the future and impact the deterrence equation.

## Russian-Ukraine War: The role of cyber and autonomous systems

The ongoing conflict witnessed a number of cyber operations feeding into kinetic warfare. Although analysts have noted that the conflict's cyber dimension was limited, nevertheless, there are serious concerns about the stability of the international environment. These risks are further exacerbated by the potential spillover effects of cyberattacks targeting Ukraine.

The current conflict also saw the use of drones with at least some autonomous capabilities. Two major drones with autonomous capabilities employed by Russia include Lancet and KBLA. KBLA produced by ZALA Aero, a small kamikaze drone aircraft that smashes itself into enemy targets and detonates an explosive device. This drone claims to operate autonomously with AI capabilities as target identification and engagement. However, there is no certainty whether the drone did fully autonomously. An executive from Rostec, a part of Russia's government-owned industrial complex, indicated that KBLA is a domestic analogue of Israel's Orbiter 1 drone.¹² This Israeli counterpart is a control station where an operator monitors the incoming videos using the sensors of the drone and manually selects the target. This stands true for KLA, wherein the target selection and engagement were undertaken by a human operator and not the machine alone.

Ukraine used Turkish Bayrakt TB2 for autonomous flights and features 'laser guided smart ammunition.'¹⁴ The USA declared that it would send 700 Switchblade and 'Phoenix Ghosts' with GPS (Global Positioning System) tracking and object recognition software for Ukraine. Switchblade is a US single-use drone that comes equipped with 'explosives, cameras, and a guided system.' This loitering munition comes with some autonomous capabilities but needs an operator to lock on the target.¹⁵

Germany announced in November 2022, that would supply '14 tracked and remote-controlled infantry vehicles for support tasks' as part of 2022's 1.64 billion USD spent on military support for Kyiv.¹⁶ These unmanned vehicles are said to rely on an upgraded version of similar technology that was used for landmine disposal in the Afghanistan and Iraq Wars.

Milrem Robotics, an Estonian contractor and developer of the Tracked Hybrid Infantry Systems (TheMIS) – a UGV provided Ukraine with these units designed for evacuating casualties and for finding and removing mines. However, analysts believe that these technologies are introduced for a specifically defined purpose but may also find additional applications in combat zones. For instance, the TheMIS also has a ‘follow-me’ capability that makes it an excellent ‘battle mule’ for transporting ammunition and other equipment.¹⁷
