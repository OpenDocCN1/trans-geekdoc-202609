In 2021, the European Commission came up with a proposal for the regulation of a European approach to AI, the European Union AI Act. The object is to pursue trustworthy AI. The regulation will ban AI systems that create potential threats to the safety, livelihood and rights of the citizens. The approach (similar to Panwar’s article on risk-based approach discussed earlier) is to differentiate between unacceptable risk, high and low or minimal AI weapon systems.⁶³ Even if we assume the EU will not come up with unacceptable risk AI-embedded weapons, how can we be sure that Russia, China, USA and Israel, others would subscribe to such an approach as regard the development of military AI?

In a report on ethics generated by the Peace Research Institution Oslo (PRIO) dated 2021, some practical steps are mentioned for controlling AI-embedded war machines. The report states that the handlers must be trained in the technical as well as ethical aspects of the AI system that they are operating. Further, resilient feedback loops between software designers, software and hardware manufacturers (engineers and scientists), decision-makers (politicians, and generals), and us (military) need to be developed.⁶⁶

In Chapter 8, Guru Saday Batatay asserts the use of AI-embedded weapon systems in the physical world and in cyberspace will increase in the coming days. Both the state and non-state actors will be parties to this process. The politics may cross the ethical boundaries using AI-powered weapon systems (like drones for targeted killings across borders) against terrorists. There will not be any moral distinction between the normalised norms of the nation states and the insurgents. He continues by citing Hugo Groti’ War and International Humanitarian Law (IHL), more comprehensive distinctive and elaborate legal promulgations are necessary to keep the use of AWS within the bounds of established legal-ethical norms. Batatay argues that the use of armed drones for killing terrorists does not fall within the purview of IHL. Further, he hints that if teams comprising humans and intelligent warring machines are used, then we should probably think of formulating “machine ethics” as machines can never think like humans. Here we find an echo of Ramanathan’s observations mentioned above. But how far can IHL accommodate LAWS?
