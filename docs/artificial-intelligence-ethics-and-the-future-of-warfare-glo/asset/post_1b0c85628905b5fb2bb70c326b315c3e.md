## Cyber and AI: A lethal combination

Over the past few decades, researchers have made enormous strides in developing computer programs that draw insights from immense volumes of data owing to cheaper access to machines that can process gigantic data quickly. With the advancement of AI, it is now possible for machines accurately translate languages, compose poetry and possibly even aid in the discovery of new drugs.²¹

The introduction of AI within weapon systems has led to significant improvements in many processes as well as eliminating redundant time-consuming tasks. Major applications of AI have been noted in capabilities and analysing voluminous data from different sensors, early warning systems, intelligence surveillance and reconnaissance, inventory management and prediction. These systems can also be used to conduct remote sensing operations in areas that are hardly accessible by manned systems. Many of these tasks are currently being undertaken by a human in the loop. For instance, AI can enable systems to sift through large amounts of data to find relevant information, fuse data from sensors and observe whether situations have changed or not; finally, it is the analyst who decides the next steps to be taken on the concise and comprehensive data.

For cyber operations as well, AI could act as a force multiplier for both offensive and defensive weapons. In the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) organised a cyber challenge where a lethal dubbed May was deployed. Its features include ‘automatic finding vulnerabilities in commercial-off-the-shelf software without developer participation’ and ‘automatic patching of its own vulnerabilities.’ Many researchers also anticipate that an advanced version of May could be capable of ‘fighting dynamically against attackers.’ It is said to be ‘smart for both offense and defense purposes.’²²

In the field of cybersecurity, AI is increasingly used in systems that prioritize threats and carry out automatic remediation. Examples include anomaly detection algorithms that can identify malicious traffic or user activity in real time. The development of new technologies that can conduct complex cyber campaigns with innovative traits, such as malware capable of tactical adaptability and value calculations vis-à strategic objectives, is currently the subject of intense research worldwide. OpenAI recently released ChatGPT that allows users to interact with an ‘intelligent’ chatbot. A user can also ask it to convert or finish an assignment. A researcher experimented with the chatbot to see if it would be able to solve a simple capture the flag challenge. Surprisingly, the model not only identified the vulnerability within the code but also wrote a small code that allowed the exploitation of the vulnerability. This is to note that as the machine becomes more sophisticated, it could also be employed to write malicious code or find vulnerabilities in the adversary’s network. This discovery is without shortcomings, OpenAI researchers have observed that the model can produce vulnerable or misaligned code and while ‘future code generation models may be able to be trained to produce more secure than the average developer,’ getting there ‘is far from certain.’²³ There is also no

Thus, there is a general indication that AI could have a transformative impact on both defensive and offensive cyber weapons. However, the likely impacts of introducing AI-cyber tools within weapon systems may range from being a positive reinforcement to a destructive scenario. From a defensive perspective, the combination of AI-cyber tool could help enhance the performance of weapon systems and detect irregularities and deviations. The combination could build a robust network against any form of cyber subversion. Several cybersecurity firms are cognizant of the growing advantages of implementing AI solutions to provide rapid insights and cut through the noise, reducing the response time during eternity. From a national perspective, as the United States’ report, “AI can enhance our ability to predict, identify, and respond to cyber and physical threats from a range of sources.”²⁴ The DOD’s Project Maven is believed to ‘employ AI to decipher video footage and is expected to among others, increase the precision of drones.’²³ It is also being expanded to other areas such as speeding up the reading of hard drives obtained from non-state actors or terrorist entities.²⁶

From an offensive perspective, the introduction of AI within weapon systems may make the systems vulnerable to different types of cyberattacks and data manipulation. For example, an adversary could deploy malware, install a backdoor to take control of the system or manipulate/fool the behaviour of autonomous systems. Furthermore, the data fed to autonomous systems could be poisoned, wherein intruders feed an algorithm to alter the system’s behaviour according to their own aims. Offensive attacks such as data manipulation or spoofing would be relatively easy to execute, but very difficult to detect, attribute or effectively count. After witnessing the capabilities of Mayhem, Pentagon’s startup-centric Defense Innovation Unit Experimental (DIU)) initiated a project Dubbled Triton is said to offer Mayhem-like capabilities to several defence agencies to find coding flaws in both operating systems and custom programs used by the US military.²⁷

The introduction of AI-cyber tools within weapon systems highlights a prevalent strategic paradox wherein, on the one hand, these technologies are making operations easier and eliminating redundant tasks. While the other hand, the same technology would make systems more prone to cyberattacks, vulnerability exploitation and an increased probability of technical glitches.
