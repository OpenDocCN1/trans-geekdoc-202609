
# 3 ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE AND MILITARY AVIATION

A.K. Sachdev

Artificial intelligence (AI), the distributive of science that deals with computer-related technologies pursuing the development of intelligent machines, is yet to receive a universally accepted definition but is being hailed as the third revolution in warfare. While the first two revolutions (gunpowder and nuclear weapons) were arms, AI does not strictly speaking – fit that definition. Nevertheless, it is common to read or hear references to the ‘AI arms race’. AI is making significant inroads into all domains of warfare – land, sea, air, space and cyber – and all military powers are engaged in endeavours to harness AI to their advantage over perceived rivals.

At this stage, it would be good to differentiate between ‘automated’ and ‘autonomous’ in the context of computer-driven technologies. Automation implies that the computer carries out a task without human assistance within a rule-based structure deterministically, i.e., producing consistently identical results for a particular set of inputs, while an autonomous system operates stochastically in much as it makes predictions about outcomes based on inputs so as to arrive at a course of action probabilistically. Thus, an autonomous system, even when given the same inputs repeatedly, would produce a range of decisions/actions. That is where AI can. According to USDOD,

AI refers to the ability of machines to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence — for example, recognizing patterns, learning from experience, drawing conclusions, making predictions, or taking action whether digitally or as the smart software behind autonomous physical systems.¹

In other words, AI describes a computer’s competence for natural language recognition, pattern identification, experiential learning, predictions/conclusions based on inputs, visual perception, decision-making and decisive action; essentially, these represent of human intelligence. However, there is a fundamental problem in making computers mimic the human brain. We still do not understand comprehensively the brain functions, and thus cannot design computers to impersonate it.

The common AI (CAI) refers to those tasks that computers can do as well, and indeed faster than, humans beings, while General (GAI) refers to the higher level, cognitive functions of the brain, generally termed as ‘thinking.’ The Holy Grail of AI is to achieve a state of technology wherein computer programs become so advanced that AI transcends human intelligence, potentially erasing the boundary between humanity and computers (hence the term ‘singularity’). Erudite estimates expect that to happen in another two decades.² Meanwhile, current research endeavours to elevate CAI to gai by understanding how the brain functions and then having AI mimic the processes. While we move towards that milestone, AI is still a significant handiden for military purposes and the militarisation of AI is well under with leading military powers investing large resources in emerging AI-related technologies.

AI has linkages with many current, emerging and future fields of science. Some of them are quantum computing, nano technology (size, weight and power consumption [SWAP] is critical to embedded AI in avionics, and technology is obsessed with reducing this set of parameters to make AI more usable and deployable), brain machine interface, natural language processing, human augmentation, robotics, data science (data science includes multiple fields, including statistics, scientific methods, and data analysis to extract value from data), big data analytics, cloud computing, edge computing, small (and big) satellites, 5G/6G communications, 3D printing, computer technologies, Internet of Things, digital twins, augmented/virtual/mixed reality and metaverse (Meta is developing its own AI supercomputer, which it predicts would be the fastest in the world). This article looks at the major developments in AI, in conjunction with these domains, and the technology-intensive field of military aviation.

# Modern combat aerial platforms

To locate the inroads of AI into combat aircraft, it would help to know that as technologies provided improvements during their evolution, the significant milestones gradually came to be as generational changes. There is no unanimous agreement on airtight definitions of these generations, but generally, the jet fighters that appeared at the end of the Second World War (mid 1940s) are referred to as the first generation, while significant technological improvements in design and speeds led to the second generation in the 1950s. The second-generation aerial vehicles were the fighters operating during the Korean War, with many swept-swept designs. Supersonic speeds and advanced engines generalised the third generation through the 1950s and 1960s. The fourth generation came in the 1970s and was differentiated by significant improvement in avionics and automation, for example Fly By Wire (FBW) and Full Authority Digital Engine Controls (FADE). These had sophisticated levels of automation (essentially 'if... then...' capabilities) but by no autonomy AI. A 4.5 (also immense 4+ or 4+) generation is also listed by some sources to refer to fourth-generation fighters with fifth-generation features. Numerically, the majority of current-day fighters are essentially fourth-generation, although they are constantly being refitted with features (improvements upon the fourth generation) to enhance their original capabilities. In the 1990s, the fifth generation emerged with a significant amount of stealth, some of the other features that characterised this generation being fitted with Active Electron Scanned Array (AESA) radars, super cruise, plug-and-play electronics, and automation permitting a fair amount of autonomy supported by fledgling AI in some areas of operations.

While the fifth generation was typified by high stealth (stealth and emphatic feature), the distinctive feature of the sixth generation is AI. Some fourth-generation fighters carried a Weapon System Operator (WSO) to help the pilot, but fifth-generation fighters are all sensors with AI sharing the pilot workload. In the sixth generation, AI is expected to move from the cockpit with a human pilot to manning it independently; 'optionally manned cockpit' design is thus slated to be a key attribute of the sixth-generation fighter. Its other attributes are expected to be modular design, virtual/augmented/mixed reality and the capability to control drone swarms.

The USA, Russia, China, the UK, Japan and France are engaged in sixth-generation fighter programmes, with the USA, expected, in the lead but China not far behind. USA has two sixth-generation programmes: F-X, also known as Next Generation Air Dominance (NGAD) or Penetrating Counter Air Platform (PCA) for the US Air Force (USAF) and FA-XX, a replacement for the US Navy's F-18E/F (also referred to as NGAD). The projected date of induction for both NGAD is 2030. The NGAD is often projected as a 'family of systems' with a manned platform working with multi-mission UAVs called Collaborative Combat Aircraft (CCA) which will carry out roles including probing, suppressing and destroying enemy defences, surveillance and weapon delivery in high-risk areas. In September 2022, American defence firms Kratos and General Atomics announced a significant milestone – the UAV MQ20A. The 'family of systems' may later include long-range aircraft. While the USA has been going alone with the NGAD until recently, indications from reported statements by USAF Secretary Kendall are that the US may be willing to discuss collaboration with Australia, Japan and the UK, especially the Collaborative Combat Aircraft (CCA).

Europe has two sixth-generation programmes. The first is the Future Combat Air System or Tempest developed by the UK, Sweden and Italy, none of which have a fifth-generation programme. Japan has its own F-X sixth-generation programme but is partnering on some parts of Tempest since 2020. In December 2021, it was announced that the UK and Japan are jointly joining to produce an engine for Tempest. The collaboration could extend to other areas of development. In 2022, Defense News reported that the Italian Air Force Chief would travel to Japan to talk with its Japanese counterpart about collaboration.

The second European sixth-generation fighter is developed by a consortium comprising France, Germany and Spain. Its official name is FCAS, but to differentiate it from the UK programme, it is often referred by its French abbreviation SCAF (standing for Système de combat aérien du futur). It aims at producing a Next Generation Fighter (NGF) which will connect through the cloud to a series of UAVs for offensive and surveillance roles. This programme was initially slated to do its first test flight in 2027 and go operational by 2045. However, some problems about work share are remembered, and there may be delays. While the workshare problems appear in the near future, the business issues involving the various governments and OEMs are immense to summit, and not before 2050, according to Dassault's CEO Eric Tripp.

Russian news agency Telegramnoy Agentstvo Sovetskogo Soyuzka (TASS) reported in 2016 that Russia was working on a sixth-generation aircraft. Later, unconfirmed reports claimed the programme Perspektivnyy Aviatsionnyy Kompleks Dal'nogo Perekhвата or PAKDP (which translates to Prospective Air Complex of Long-Range Interception) and gave it the name MIG-41, projecting for it a first flight in the mid-2020s and entry into service after 2030. More recently, reports indicate that the systems meant for the sixth-generation fighter are being tested on the fifth generation Su-57.
