Eriksen would have known in this certainty until after the Blücher was a German vessel (he did not know until he had fired). Sensors downstream would have shown this and relayed the information instantaneously to him. Had an intelligent machine been on command instead the order received from his superior (human) commander in Oslo. If the command had been, ‘don’t fire’, the AI machine, at that moment the possessor of delegated operational authority, would have instantly complied with this command, and Oscarsburg’s torpedoes would not have been fired. The Blücher would have sailed to Oslo, resulting in the Norwegian King Haakon’s capture and a significant hampering of the execution of government personnel as well as gold reserves from the capital. Imagine if the command to fire had been issued by an officer who was a German sympathizer; would the AI machine then on duty at Oscarsburg have factored this? The machine’s compliance would have been automatic or could be withheld under strict guidelines. What guidelines? How would it have dealt with the ambiguity of the Oscarsburg situation, Norway being officially neutral at the time? Human obedience is automatic; might pass through the filter we call conscience. Context must be carefully weighed. This is the great benefit of human obedience; its combination of responsiveness and flexibility makes obedience a powerful virtue in moments of crisis. At present, no machine, however intelligent it may be, can replicate the subtlety of human obedience. (Perhaps such replication will never be possible). This is the great advantage that human obedience has over automatic machine compliance. Where human beings comes short, however, in addition to the question of speed and knowledge, is in their ability to step outside the chain of command and/or in pursuit of their private ends. This would not arise with a machine, unless it was spoofed, or penetrated by an adversary.
