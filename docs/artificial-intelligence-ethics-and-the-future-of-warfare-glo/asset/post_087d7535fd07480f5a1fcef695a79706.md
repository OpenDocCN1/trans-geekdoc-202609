
The 'Loyal Wingman' project of USAF Research Laboratory (AFRL) involves a manned command aircraft with unmanned aircraft serving as an adjunct flying platform, scouting for threats and taking them on, if (thus inviting the term loyal wing). Initial live demonstrations were with F-16s—both manned and unmanned—and were impressive enough to ensure the concept being grafted onto the sixth-generation programmes.

NATO's conglomerate of analysts, while the F-16 tests were contested in an autonomous mode over predetermined parameters, future plans include 'flocking' and 'swarming'. In flocking, more than one unmanned wingman would operate under the manned aircraft, which would give more abstract commands than exercise direct control over them, while 'swarming' would obviate the possibility of the command aircraft having situational awareness over each of a huge swarm of UAVs but permit it to exercise command over the swarm as an aggregate. A demonstration related to this concept was made in October 2016 with F/A-18 Super Hornets swarming a swarm of 103 autonomous micro drones, which then proceeded to exhibit 'collective decision-making, adaptive flying, and self-healing.' The swarming UAVs are especially alluring as they represent myriad possibilities. Some UAVs could have offensive roles, some could be scouts (ISR) tasks, could carry EW payloads, some others could aid in target recognition and tracking, and others could be loitering munitions with possible air-to-air capability, that they can sacrifice themselves in defense of their command aircraft. The possibilities are myriad.

As can be, UAVs present a persuasive alternative to exposing expensive (the almost unaff), scarce, manned aircraft to high threat environments. They can be used again and like manned aircraft and are comparatively economical, so greater risks can be taken with them, with no danger of losing a valuable human pilot. They are referred to Low Cost Attributable Aircraft (LCAA) or Attributable (A/R) UAVs, for short life spans (in comparison to manned fighters), recoverable (if not downed during a mission), low cost, and usable with multiple manned aircraft. The AI technologies that are lodged in UAVs enable them to be part of MUM-T programmes like the Skyborg, one of 'Vanguard programmes' launched by AFRL.

Skyborg was launched in 2018 by AFRL's Strategic Development Planning and Experimentation (SPDE) and AFRL calls it 'an autonomy-focused capability that will enable the Air Force to operate and sustain low-cost, teamed aircraft that can thwart adversaries with quick, decisive actions in contested environments.' UAV programmes include the Kratos-built XQ-58 Valkyrie which completed a successful flight in March 2019 under AFRL's Low Cost Attributable Aircraft Technology (LCAAT) project. Skyborg related contents have also been awarded to General Atomics, Northrop Grumman and Boeing. Skyborg's eventual loyal wingman could be an unmanned fighter (as in the 2018 trials of manned/unmanned F-16s flying together).

In 2019, DARPA launched the Air Combat Evolution (ACE) program to adopt AI in individual and team aerial combat tactics and to develop Air Combat Maneuvering (ACM) algorithms for visual 1-vs-1, visual-2-vs-1 and 2-vs-2 engagements with a broad spectrum of performance. The AlphaDogfight fights mentioned earlier were a part of ACE which embraces manned and unmanned aerial combat. While ACE is aimed at developing AI software capable of close combat by unmanned platforms autonomously, Skyborg has many capabilities (Beyond Range [BVR] missiles and long-range sensors) in mind for loyal wingmen. However, as both programmes look at unmanned wingmen, they may be merged at some future point in time as the programmes mature. Will the dividing line between close aerial combat and BVR engagements by AI-enabled UAVs vanish as wingmen. Already, the X-62A, a modified F-16, is being used by both Skyborg and ACE.¹⁰

Boeing, which has a stake in the US Skyborg programme, is also designing and developing, in collaboration with the Royal Australian Air Force (RAAF), the Airpower Teaming System (ATS) – its first unmanned system in Australia. On 1 March 2021, Boeing announced¹¹ the first test flight of its loyal wingman, UAV slightly smaller than an F-16 (as loyal wingman with length of 11.6 metres) with an F-8 or F-35 class of aircraft (which could fly with one unmanned aircraft), or flies like EW and ISR along with P-8A Poseidon or E-7 Wedgetail aircraft. UAV was later named MQ-28A Ghost Bat. Australia had had last six years and plans to buy seven more¹² the next two to three years.

Moving to Europe, the decision to develop FCAS was taken in 2017 by Germany and France, but they joined an investment agreement only in February 2020, with Spain joining up in December that year. Within the programme, Next Generation Weapon System (NGWS) aims to create sixth-generation manned fighters (possibly NGFs) with UAVs (called Remote Carriers or RCS under the programme) which will be appendable in a scalable and flexible manner. RCs from 200 kg to 2 tons all UAV (AUW) are being analysed under NGWS, with EW, ISR, Target Acquisition, or to Ground Strike and Suppression of Enemy Air Systems (SEAD) as the possible roles. Airbus Defence and Space was working with US-based ANSYS, an engineering simulation company, but reportedly, there are problems being faced by the programme¹³ and its future is uncertain.

Russia has also been working on developing new UAVs to function as autonomous loyal wingmen; reportedly ‘Grom’ (meaning thunder), developed by Kronstadt Group was presented at the Army 2020 Arms Expo in Moscow. Grom is designed to operate in conjunction with Su-35/Su-57 fighters for reconnaissance and missile firing (500 kg payload) under their command.³⁴. It resembles the XQ-58 Valkyrie but is longer by half a length (13.8 m to Valkyrie's 8.8 m). According to TASS, Russia's state agency, Kronstadt stated the Grom will be able to control a swarm of 10 smaller drones called 'Molniya' (or lighting).⁵ Another UAV, the S-70 Okhtnik-B (Hunter) designed by Sukhoi Design Bureau and Russian Aircraft Corporation MiG, first flew in 2019 and is also designed to be a loyal wingman aircraft carrying 2 tons of internal payload missiles and bombs, or to be equipped with electro-optic targeting, communication, and reconnaissance equipment. The actual AI capability and content of these two UAVs are not ascertainable as all information about them is from Russian state media (and hence susceptible to skepticism).

Sixth-generation programmes intend upon MUM-T capabilities, but not all of them will survive the onslaught of shrinking defence budgets. Already, there is a mild clamour around revitalisation of fourth generation aircraft to give them capabilities surpassing fourth generation. Without a doubt, there would be built into the MUM-T UAVs the modular platforms to detail into partnerships with aircraft hierarchically lower than the sixth generation designs are being developed for originally. An interesting consequence of this happening could be the realisation that a UAV is as effective in MUM-T roles with sixth generation as with less-than-six ones.

## AI on UAVs

From a humble beginning, and driven by commercial motivations, drones or UAVs have grown tremendously. Their military uses have proliferated impressively too. In 1969, Israel's Military Intelligence Directorate used toy drones mounted with 35 mm cameras programmed to pick pictures every ten seconds to harvest details of trenches, signals cables and deployments over Egyptian territory over the Suez. Since then, the use of UAVs has grown remarkably in terms of autonomy for myriad roles and tasks. UAVs have been used in combat during military conflicts for a long time; a US Predator first fired a missile in 2001.³⁶ More recently, Azerbaijan and Armenia have gone wide (and decisive!) use of UAVs in the latter half of 2020.

Autonomous UAVs can now execute accurate strikes against ground targets, carrying out target selection and firing presumed on AI (which is either on board or on a platform they are in data communication with). The MQ-9 Reaper has been used to detect categorise and track potential objects of interest. This AI pod has the potential to identify targets and determine priorities for engagement. The loyal wingman UAV developed by Boeing for RAAF has similar target detection, prioritisation and engagement capabilities, as claimed by Boeing.
