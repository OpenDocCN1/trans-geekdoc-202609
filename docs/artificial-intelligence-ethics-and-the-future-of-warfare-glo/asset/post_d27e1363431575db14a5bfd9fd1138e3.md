## Current developments in AI-military uses

This research posits that a vast majority of the developments of AI in military technologies are in the realm of weapons systems, military surveillance, and planning. The evolution of automated and autonomous systems began with the Gatling gun used in the American Civil War (1861-1865). Though this itself was not an autonomous weapon, paved the way for the subsequent development and introduction of autonomy into weapons systems. For the era, a weapon that could fire 300 rounds per minute was an advantage against an enemy who needed to recoil and refill manually. Others followed, including the German G7e/T4 potentially (Falcon’s) torpedo. Then came the precision-guided munitions (PGMs), mostly autonomous due to the inability of the human operator to recall once they launched. There are also looting and hunting missiles which are autonomous for target identification and engagement.

As Scharre predicts, “the trend of creeping automation that began with the Gatling gun will continue. The incorporation and expansion of autonomous capabilities has been introduced into numerous military support systems. Artificially intelligent support systems that are automated at various levels are relevant for all dimensions of the operational areas (land, air, sea, and space, as well as the cyber and information space). One could speak of assisted perception and action in the increasingly complex techno sphere in which military operations are carried out.

This rapid spread of AI technology into areas that were considered a human-exclusive preserve has been remarkable. Countries like Germany, the USA, China, the UK, Russia, France, India, and North Korea are ahead in the military AI race. The South Asia Monitor of 16 January 2021 reported that 'the Indian Army invests heavily into artificial intelligence, autonomous weapons systems, quantum technologies, robotics, cloud computing, and algorithm warfare, to achieve convergence between the Army's warfighting philosophies and the military attributes of these technologies.'

This follows the prediction that India's unique security situation, insurgency, and the presence of two hostile neighbours will drive its need for autonomous weapons systems.

The US, one of the biggest players in military AI development, insisted that it would not support calls for a ban on AWS. As of 2011, the government had already built the X-47B; the first unmanned aircraft to perform an autonomous take-off and landing on an aircraft carrier. With a defence budget of as 754 billion dollars (for the fiscal year 2021), the US reportedly spends more on weapons systems development than any other G7 country. According to Scharre:

Applications of military robotics and autonomy are non-controversial, as uninhabited logistics convoys, tanker aircraft, or reconnaissance drones. Autonomy is also increasing in weapons systems, though with next generation missiles and combat aircraft pushing the boundaries of autonomy. A handful of experimental programs show how the US military is thinking about the role of autonomy in weapons. Collectively, they are laying the foundations for the military of the future.

Already, the US boasts of military systems with autonomous capacities ranging from anti-ship and anti-land missiles like the Tomahawk anti-ship missile (TASM) and the Tomahawk land attack missile (TLAM), to the Long Range Anti-ship Missile (LRASM) which, according to its developers, precision routing guidance, a multi-modal sensor suite, weapons data link, and an enhanced anti-jam GPS to detect and destroy specific targets within a group of numerous ships at sea. DARPA is also building technologies that enable swarm drones to hunt in coordinated packs.

The Chinese also lean on the assurance that autonomy and AI-enabled weapons systems, more speed, reach, precision, and lethality of future operations. China has consistently worked towards becoming an AI power. This has created a pseudo-arms race between itself and other competitors like the US, Russia, and Israel. The People's Liberation Army boasts of the Blowfish A3 helicopter, which can perform more complex combat missions; including fixed-point timing, detection, fixed-range reconnaissance, and targeted precision strikes. These copters have already been marketed to governments in the Middle East, including Pakistan and Saudi Arabia. Russia has proven to be one of the strongest opponents to the idea of a ban on LAWS. The Kremlin owns the Orion UAV; a drone reported to be capable of engaging others in combat. The Uran 9 UAV has already been marked publicly as well. The Russians have indicated their intention to develop robotic swarms and other technologies that would pull soldiers out of the front lines. Turkey has built the STM Kargu for asymmetric warfare. This system can be used in both manual and autonomous modes. A UN Security Council Panel of Experts on Libya reported that the Kargu carried out the first drone attack in history. Also, it has been suspected of several strikes during the 2020 Nagorno-Karabakh War, even though Azerbaijani authorities were yet to verify this information at the time of this research.

Israel has consistently automated its weapon systems and has indicated its stance against an international regulation to ban or regulate LAWS. Its long war with Palestine has been supported by automated border control sentry technology placed along the Gaza borders. Automated machines loaded with 7.62 calibre machine guns are trained to identify human movements along its 1.5 km kill-zone and to open fire at unpermitted entries. Israel's Iron Dome Missile Defence system is heralded as one of the most impressive in the world, with the capacity to autonomously defend Israeli territories against incoming missile attacks. China, South Korea, Turkey and Chile have all purchased and have even reengineered their version of the Israeli HARPY drone to a personalised variant.
