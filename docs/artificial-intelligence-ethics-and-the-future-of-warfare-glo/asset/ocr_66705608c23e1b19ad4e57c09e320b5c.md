# Actual development of AI-based systems (software and military hardware)

As explained above, software developers are often not experts in military applications and vice versa. Significant safety gaps ('incompleteness') can arise as a result. Nancy Leverson has argued that flaws in fundamental safety requirements, not coding errors, are the greatest threat to the use of AI.63 This maintains, only when software designers interact with end users throughout the entire design process. Trust emerges when everyone are secure in their operating environment, and the designers understand the full range of challenges that the operators will face:

> Providing feedback and allowing for experimentation in system design, is critical in allowing operators to optimise their control ability. In the less automated system designs of the past, operators naturally had this ability to experiment and update their mental models of the current system state. Designers of highly automated systems sometimes do not understand this requirement and design automation that takes 'out of the loop'. Everyone is surprised when the operator makes a mistake based on an incorrect mental model. Unfortunately, the reaction to such a mistake is to add more automation and to marginalise the operators even more, exacerbating the problem.64

This observation holds even for AWS because these will be deployed on the battlefield by the decision of commanders, who must retain human control over these systems. The system may operate autonomously once deployed, but the deployment decision remains fully in the hands of its human operator, who must retain a capacity to direct and redirect the system towards the achievement of his/her tactical and strategic goals. The one deploying the AWS is its end user, and the designers of these systems must communicate regularly and effectively with these end users throughout the design phase.
