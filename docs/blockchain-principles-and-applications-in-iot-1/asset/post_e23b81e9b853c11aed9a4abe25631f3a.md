## 14.6 Blockchain-Based Access Control in Various Domains

### 14.6.1 Health Care

With the rise of benefits of data sharing and collaborative research, healthcare industries have shifted to the use of cloud-based storage of medical information, especially medical records in the form of electronic medical records (EMRS) or personal health information (PHI) as addressed by a few articles. PHIs differ from EMRs in the data record. PHIs record the complete information about a patient, such as readings from their tests or smart devices. EMRs record data such as meetings with a doctor or the tests conducted. However, we will use these terms interchangeably. These records often contain sensitive information about a patient; their age, physical, and mental characteristics; and health-related information. The most commonly used method of storing such data is either in local storage or the cloud. However, there are problems associated with both methods. Storing records locally restricts researchers from making use of the largely available medical data pool. Cloud storage risks revealing sensitive patients' data to third-party providers and losing control over how the data is managed or who can access the data.

- 1. Challenges: The following challenges are being faced in the healthcare industry, most of which have been attempted to solve with the use of blockchain technology.

- * Trust in Storage Providers: EMRs are often stored in hospitals or external storage providers. Patients need to build trust in these parties before being willing to allow the storage on their EMRs. Even with proper access control mechanisms in place, issues can arise from within the controlling organization. Risks involve the unauthorized sharing of data for medical research or otherwise as well as changing of medical records. Due to this, patients often prefer not to digitize their medical records.

- Historical Data: The health insurance industry is one application where this facility is required. Insurance fraud happens when patients develop fake medical certificates to get insurance. This can happen easily as there is no recordkeeping facility for EMRs that can preserve patient’s privacy as well as provide a verifiable and immutable history of medical data to authorized requesting [27–30][P52].

- Fragmented Storage: Currently, EMRs are stored at the locations they are often generated or in reside clusters. This limits researchers from being able to access the entire pool of available data [31–33][P52, P63].

Other shorter summaries are provided to sum up the work done in this domain. MedRec is one of the first papers to be published that tackles the problems of local storage of EMRs. Different hospitals are unable to access a patient’s medical history due to the unavailability of sharing methods. This is wholly to protect the patients’ privacy. MedRec proposes a blockchain based overlay over existing storage to provide a access control system that allows patients to query their data themselves from anywhere and share data with other medical institutions or researchers. This makes the design modular and easy to integrate with current solutions. Three different types of contracts have been in in this framework. The first type is used to provide a mapping between a user and their Ethereum account. The second type links patients and medical record providers (medical institutions) and associated permissions allowing a patient to access their data via an available query string which can be applied on the provider’s database to obtain the EMRs. The third contract provides a summary of all related medical data associated with any patient or provider allowing easy access to fragmented data, especially for researchers.

The method proposed being one of the first, solves several issues regarding fragmented records storage, rules out centralized storage as an option, and provides a base for future systems to be developed using blockchain. However, the system does suffer from the following limitations:

## Access Control and Data Security

One of the major limitations of MedRec, poses a slight threat to the privacy of individuals by the permission-less blockchain that. In a permission-less blockchain, users may join the network and access control medical data. In a permissioned environment, users are given by the trust provider. This kind of implementation has been deployed by Dubovits et al. [34][P50]]. The authors have implemented a Hyperledger blockchain that contains access control policies as well encrypted in the cloud. In a permission-ed environment, users are known and lesser chances for users to perform malicious activities. The implementation on Hyperledger uses the data with the patients' secret key. The framework ensures the following features: privacy, security, availability, and scalability. Privacy is provided by allowing a patient complete control over. Security attacks aren't very plausible since the network is permitted and the identities of every user is known.

Wait, the next particular in our study of healthcare related research is [35][P61] by Zhang and Lin. This paper is targeted towards doctors being able to access historical healthcare related information of a given patient from all the healthcare institutions he/she been in. The paper suggests that storing PHI in cloud-based services is insecure and poses security challenges which can be taken of by storing them in blockchain. The first blockchain is a private blockchain which is local to every health institution and stores records of patients who visit that institution. A private blockchain ensures faster transactions, lower cost, and greater security. The second blockchain is a consortium blockchain that store secure searchable indexes of PHI in the respective private blockchain. These, searchable indexes are key search encrypted in a manner that fetches any doctor the results he/she would want from the revelation of the identity of it to any third-party listener. All identities and PHI records are encrypted using public-key encryption for security and privacy. The goals defined in the paper include data security and access control, privacy preservation, search time, time revocation (to ensure a doctor can access the medical records only at the time by the patient and not in future), and system availability. A consensus mechanism called proof of conformance has been suggested by the paper which checks for the valid authorization of the doctor while uploading the PHI to the private blockchain as well checks if the search indexes are from a predefined set of indexes for system interoperability. Another method in [[27][P62] uses blockchain only for auditing and non-tampering of data. Access policies are given by the patients and stored on the blockchain. Hospitals are responsible for encrypting the data using combined attribute-based/identity-based encryption and signature (C- AB/IB-ES) schemes and adding to the data pool controlled by the blockchain's consensus nodes.
