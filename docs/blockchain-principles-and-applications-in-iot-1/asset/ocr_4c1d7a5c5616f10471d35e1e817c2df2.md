Blockchain

P35 introduces CapChain built for the Monero blockchain making of Monero's Ring Confidential Transactions to preserve privacy. The method makes use of the token approach to allow users to not just grant access rights but also delegate rights to other users. The implementation models Cap-BAC by treating access rights as capabilities on the blockchain that can be transferred to users just like cryptocurrency. To preserve identities, instead of using public addresses, transactions on the blockchain use a hash chain as these permissions are delegated from user to user. The use of sub-addresses allow for easy revocation of all rights from a user with other users that have derived their rights from this user. The implementation does suffer from drawbacks.

The limitation of this approach is that it doesn't provide the ability to revoke access as easily as revocation requires a transaction on the chain. As the access policies are stored in the form of contracts on the blockchain, these are publicly visible which can form a threat [52][P6]

The authors of [64]P5 continue their work on FairAccess using the second generation of blockchain to implement the architecture. This new generation of blockchain, called the account model, allows gained access control through the use of advertised scripting for smart contracts [56][P7]

To tackle the issue of distance between IoT devices and the cloud storage which has an increase in latency, authors of [48]P5 used a blockchain layer to provide an access control and data management framework over time-series IoT data stored at the edge of the network using a locality-aware decentralized storage system. Even cloud nodes can be used as storage nodes for this purpose. To secure the storage on devices, the IoT data streams are chained, compressed, and encrypted at the application layer. Transactions are made on the blockchain to facilitate sharing of data with a service.

They include the dataset and the public key of the service. Access requests are checked on the blockchain for a corresponding transaction by the owner and only then the key is shared with the requester. This method suffers from the drawback of slow transaction speeds.

IoT devices constantly change managers or may be under the control of more than one manager at a time. However, manager devices are also lower power devices that cannot easily execute the access control. Handling hard coding of managing access control, security, scalability, transparency, and currency will not be easy to execute on these devices. [57,58]P7 by Oscar Novo proposes an architecture using a single smart contract on the blockchain is used to define all access controls. The IoT devices are however not included as part of the blockchain. A new entity defined as the management hub translates information between the blockchain and the devices. Managers are however considered lightweight nodes in the blockchain that are responsible for managing the access control of the IoT devices under them by accessing the smart contract. A single contract automatically handles the issues of managing multiple devices and currency.

However, this architecture does not support the ability to audit the access control permissions. To obtain access, the management hub simply query the access control policy in the blockchain and translate the request back to the devices. Transactions aren't supported as in current blockchain implementations; it takes a couple of seconds to minutes for the operation. Additionally, modification of access control policy requires a transaction against the smart contract which might take some significant amount of time to reflect in the blockchain. This inherent limitation of the blockchain also poses as a threat to the strength of the access control model.
