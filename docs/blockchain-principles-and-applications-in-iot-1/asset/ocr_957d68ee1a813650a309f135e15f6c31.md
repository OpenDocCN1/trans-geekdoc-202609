# Access Control and Data Security

One of the major limitations of MedRec, poses a slight threat to the privacy of the individuals by the revelation of the patient's identity is mainly due to the permission-less blockchain that is used by the framework. In a permission-less blockchain, any user may join the blockchain, access data and be able to verify the transactions. In a permissioned blockchain environment, users are given identities by a trusted identity provider. The identity provider helps to manage trust by controlling access control within the network as well the user's rights to validate new blocks. This kind of implementation has been deployed by Dubovitskaya et al. in [[34][P50]]. The authors have implemented an access control system over the HyperLedger permissioned blockchain that contains metadata as well as access control policies to access encrypted medical data stored in the cloud. These access control policies are defined by those who own the data themselves.

In a permissioned environment, users' identities are known and lesser chances exist for users to perform any malicious activities. The implementation on HyperLedger uses the medical record and another set of the organized patients' data in the cloud, encrypted with the patients' secret key. The framework ensures the following features, privacy, security, availability, and scalability. Privacy is provided by allowing a patient complete control over the access control policy over/her data. Security attacks aren't very plausible since the network is permitted and the identities of every user is known. With cloud-based storage and APIs, availability of data is also ensured. Scalability is ensured by the PBFT consensus protocol. However, the paper seems to target only kind of medical record as mentioned and in future efforts, the authors will work on including kinds of medical records, including images.

The next particular in our study of healthcare related blockchain research is [[35][P61] by Zhang and Lin. This paper is targeted towards doctors being able to access historical healthcare related information of a given patient from all the healthcare institutions he/she has been in the past. The paper suggests that storing PHI in cloud-based services is insecure and poses security challenges which can be taken of care by storing them in blockchain.

The first blockchain is a private blockchain which is local to every health institution and stores records of patients who visit that institution. A private blockchain ensures faster transactions, lower cost, and greater security. The second blockchain is a consortium blockchain that store secure searchable indexes of PHI in the respective private blockchain. These, searchable indexes are key search encrypted in a manner that fetches any doctor the results he/she would want from the revelation of the identity of it to any third-party listener. All identities and PHI records are encrypted using public-key encryption for security and privacy. The goals defined in the paper include data security and access control, privacy preservation, search time, time revocation (to ensure a doctor can access the medical records only at the time he by the patient and not in future), and system availability. A consensus mechanism called proof of conformance has been suggested by the paper which checks for the valid authorization of the doctor while uploading the PHI to the private blockchain as well checks if the search indexes are from a predefined set of indexes for system interoperability. Another method in [[27][P62] uses blockchain only for auditing and non-tampering of data. Access policies are given by the patients and stored on the blockchain. Hospitals are responsible for encrypting the data using combined attribute-based/identity-based encryption and signature (C- AB/IB-ES) schemes and adding to the data pool controlled by the blockchain's consensus nodes.
