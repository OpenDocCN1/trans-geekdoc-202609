
## 3.2 Requirements for Digital Twin-Driven Product Customization and Production Planning

Manufacturing customization now includes minimal flexibility to change fixed parameters. Additional flexibility requires new modeling and processing languages to express, exchange, and process product and manufacturing knowledge, facilitating the transition from digital to physical products. This goal will require the integration of innovative and disruptive technologies and capabilities focused on DTs that describe the shape and characteristics of a customized product, the shape of the product, the materials, components, and the behavior of the individual parts and assemblies that make up the product. This technology will correlate, and unify large, dispersed product and production-related datasets to provide greater visibility into the state of a product, its various parts, and production processes. The result is a product with smarter, more reliable, safer, and more secure plug-and-play integration of digital and physical components.

Ultimately, development of digital twins will also enable transformative customer-centric product ideation and customization that can optimize the entire product life cycle. This customer-centric vision is revolutionary but highly complex. It requires technologies and tools that focus on large-scale collaboration between different stakeholders, from manufacturer to designer, designer to end-user, and from end-user to manufacturer. To address such concerns, this article lays the groundwork for how customer-centric companies can collaborate to produce a range of customizable products using the three facets of DTs discussed in S. 3.1.

A Digital Twin Knowledge Model (DT-KM): this is a formal digital representation—known as the Manufacturing Blueprint Knowledge Model—that collects, stores, and processes “intelligent, actionable data” from all points in the product and production life cycle. In the DT-KM, each physical product has one unique digital twin that provides a comprehensive accuracy of the product and its production efficiency, linking the facets of product, production, and performance.

A Digital Twin Meta-Programming Language (DT-MPL), known as the Manufacturing Blueprint Programming Language, rationalizes structures, intermediates, and aggregates generic DT-KM knowledge to improve decision-making and create products, solutions, and services that meet customer needs.

The approach outlined enables a fully programmable digital twin to support highly individualized customized products, capable of gathering information about actual usage and customer needs, which can be used to design new features based on both direct product feedback and actual usage. As a result, product design and customization provide product design components with sufficient compatibility and ability to complement “living” products that combine ever-changing physical and digital characteristics.

## Model-Driven Product Engineering

The complexity of the manufacturing ecosystem shows that advanced model-driven engineering (MDE) approaches have the potential to be used in manufacturing to simplify the product design and development processes and drive productivity gains [4, 5]. Model-based engineering provides a simultaneous, collaborative design process that allows users to identify and define requirements, model solution architectures, validate and share ideas among various stakeholders with different views, and enable them to trade-off between features and costs. The MDE approach provides a means of linking different engineering processes through artifacts and abstractions that meet the requirements of industry acceptance and enable analytic methods. The Systems Modeling Language (SysML) is a popular choice among MDE practitioners. However, its informal semantics make it poorly suited to rigorous analysis.

To realize the digital twin and digital thread concepts, the conventional MDE approach needs to be specialized and extended to satisfy the requirements of smart manufacturing. MDM is an approach that moves away from drawing and other definition tools, such as natural language descriptions [28] and 3D models, and moves to interactive 3D models. MDM converts the 3D models through model transformation into modular manufacturing operations, digital manufacturing processes, and digital threads that are ultimately realized using additive manufacturing techniques or on the factory floor. Model transformation takes as input the model corresponding to the previous level in the MDM hierarchy and transforms it into the equivalent model of the level immediately below in the MDM hierarchy.

# Product Ideation and Design (PID)

MDM enables product engineers and customers to imagine, think, and act on (complex) product development projects. The PID layer of the MDM architecture is essentially a series of sessions that represent a formalized part of the product design phase. Different stakeholders, including designers, developers, engineers, and customers, come to share their visions, insights, and ideas.

Product ideas take this information and apply it to the design and customization of specific products at the product-agnostic manufacturing model level. At this level, 3D-CAD models interactively capture and visualize all relevant information in real time for production and quality planning, controlling production processes, work orders generation, and coordination of related processes. In previous R&D efforts, we have designed and implemented a novel approach to reach this level that is formally grounded in the DT blueprints detailed in Sec. 5, through the 3D interactive product-oriented configuration management (PoCL), [3, 29].

# Platform-Independent Manufacturing Model (PIM)

At this level, DT controls digital activities, processes, and recipes to create the first product of interest. PIM is rooted in the ISA98 Manufacturing Operations Management level, which provides resources, equipment, personnel, and energy to transform digitized raw material and component descriptions, quality checks, production flows, and production schedules into products. It carries the activities to adapt to a product.

Therefore, it includes the management of information about schedules, uses, skills, definitions, history, and the current status of all resources (suppliers, equipment, and materials) and related to the manufacturing facility. The data collected with the product design at the PIM level can be used to drive the manufacturing flow and provide PIM with an abundance of information that will drive production innovation. PIM combines this data collected from resources and other processes to provide a more complete real-time view of all manufacturing plants, production lines, and increasingly supply chains. Combined with the analytical power of digital tools, these increasingly rich datasets offer opportunities for innovation that brought advantages and benefits.

The digital threads derived in this layer are then used to create a fully functional prototype using additive manufacturing (AM) techniques or to instruct machines and robots on the shop floor to carry out product manufacturing functions. AM produces higher-performance designs than traditional manufacturing techniques by enabling multiple design iterations without expensive retooling, allowing for leaner/slimmer manufacturing while enhancing the design quality. AM allows the creation of functional objects on 3D printer. With AM, manufacturers can reduce assembly time and produce parts that reduce overall costs and add value to finished products [30].

# Platform-Specific Manufacturing Model (PSM)

PSM transforms production planning decisions for PIM into control commands for production processes on the factory floor to fulfill the control and monitoring needs of different types of machines and equipment. This type of conversion structures the actual interface between the digital and physical world, resulting in improved production performance, increased uptime, improved resource availability, and operational speed. Based on factory automation and control systems, PSM includes device control, intelligent devices, work centers, robots, machines, programmable logic controllers, and monitoring sensors as carbon emission sensors, temperature, and vibration sensors.

To ensure efficiency, high quality, and high throughput, devices typically use different communication protocols that run independently. For these components to work properly, coordination, synchronization, and access to resources must be effectively managed. To take advantage of the Factory of Things, manufacturers need to manage the communication barriers between these field devices and connect them to a cloud-based Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) implementation, which offers significant advantages for product design parameters.

### Feedback loop Product manufacturability depend on functional and nonfunctional design parameters. This requires a feedback loop in MDM. As shown in Fig. 1, there is a feedback loop in product design and manufacturing process design, manufacturing, and ultimately product delivery and maintenance. Data from the product conception/ideation and design process (and the subsequent use of the DTs and the corresponding physical product manufacturing) is captured and continuously updated, triggering a continuous cycle between the digital and physical domains.

MDM feedback loops provide not only product improvements but also the ability to recognize, improve, and modify production lines, processes, and machines. Real-time operational data can also be collected and analyzed to optimize production processes and predict demand, downtime, and future value. During implementation and deployment on the PSM platform, feedback drives product optimization through quality assurance testing, ensuring that product defects are identified and fixed as they are discovered.
