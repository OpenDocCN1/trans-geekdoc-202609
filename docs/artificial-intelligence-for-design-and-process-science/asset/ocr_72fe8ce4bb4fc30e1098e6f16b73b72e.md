# ### 3.3.2 Creativity Assessment Studies

The evolution of computational approaches to creativity assessment, by Guilford’s (1950) prediction to today's AI-based methods, can be delineated into four distinct phases.

In the first phase, the focus was on text-mining statistics derived from responses, including word length, count, and punctuation usage. The second phase was the application of latent semantic analysis to divergents, originality through semantic distance. The third phase witnessed a transition from latent semantic analysis to Global Vectors for Word Representation. This shift capitalized on superior corpus and neural network capabilities, offering a better approximation of human thought processes. Research indicated that this approach surpassed latent semantic analyses in predicting human ratings. In the fourth phase, there was a shift from unsupervised to supervised learning approaches. This leveraged large language modeling techniques to significantly enhance scoring accuracy [44].

In a study [46], a creativity assessment system called AuDRA—an Automated Drawing Assessment format—was employed to extract visual creativity ratings, they trained AuDRA and tested its generalizability to untrained drawing sets, raters, and tasks. Using a collection of line drawings and drawings from simple drawing productions. This work employed four distinct datasets of rated drawings—totaling over 13,000 drawing-rating pairs. While training the model to perform the abstract drawing task to optimize its predictive capacity for specific task. They opted to use ResNet [50] as the computer vision backbone for AuDRA [46].

The model successfully learned to predict the creativity scores of human drawings. It was not trained on remarkably high accuracy.

The model showed evidence of learning visual features of drawings, simply predicting their level of elaboration. Critically, AuDRA generalized to predict the creativity of sketches from entirely unpublished datasets of human ratings that were not used in the model. They found AuDRA scores to be highly correlated with human creativity ratings on the same drawing task. Importantly, correlations between AuDRA scores and human raters surpassed those drawings' elaboration (i.e., ink on the page) and human creativity raters, suggesting that AuDRA is sensitive to features of drawings beyond a simple degree of complexity.
