
# Introduction

Advances in new technologies, including artificial intelligence (AI), have fueled the Fourth Industrial Revolution affecting the way people live, work, and relate to one another. As part of its 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, the United Nations, in 2015 set the 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) (e.g., reduce inequality, provide affordable and clean energy, improve health and education) which were a call for action in a global partnership in AI innovation and education for social good [1]. These goals align with the mission of the California State Universities (CSUs), with 21 of campuses being Hispanic-Serving Institutions (HSIs). Faculty and researchers from the CSU system have been exploring how AI can be used for social good by addressing societal challenges, improving people's lives. This raises two questions for educators: (1) How can undergraduate students learn AI so they are equipped with the necessary skills for the future workforce and become tomorrow's citizens and leaders? (2) How can student engagement and learning in AI be increased to reduce the equity gap in AI education?

The AI For Social (AI4SG) project aims to bring AI education by developing and studying the efficacy of innovative learning practices and resources for AI for social good. We have developed three learning modules for students to (1) identify social issues that align with the SDGs in their community (e.g., poverty, hunger, quality education), (2) learn AI through hands-on labs and business applications, and (3) create AI-powered solutions to address social issues they have identified. Students are expected to simulate AI learning in their communities and contribute to their communities. Students then use the modules to engage in an interdisciplinary approach, facilitating AI learning for social good in management information systems, geography, and computer science at three CSU HSIs (San Jose State University (SJSU), Cal Poly Pomona (CPP), and CSU San Bernardino (CSUSB).

# Related Work

## Culturally Responsive, Relevant, and Sustaining Pedagogy (CRRSP)

Culturally Responsive, Relevant, and Sustaining Pedagogy (CRRSP) is a student-centered teaching framework, drawing upon decades’ work of research [2–6], that recognizes and affirms students' racial, linguistic, and cultural identities to develop their agency, encourage academic rigor and critical thinking skills, uplift marginalized voices, and empower students to become agents of change through a strong development of their sociopolitical or cultural consciousness. CRRSP recognizes and responds to students’ identities and needs by using cultures, experiences, and narratives of culturally diverse students as resources for their own learning and the teaching of peers [7]. Teaching that recognizes the cultural characteristics, experiences, and perspectives of culturally diverse students affirm the cultural knowledge and skills that their communities offer the classroom [3]. CRISP realizes that student learning is impacted by factors that go beyond the classroom [3, 7]; integrating students' communities of Color's knowledge and skills into classroom learning normalizes the knowledge and skills of Color, enriches classroom learning, and promotes a curriculum that is accessible and relevant to all students [5], thereby increasing students' social, cultural, and academic engagement [8–11].

CRISP does not replace rigor with inclusion or vice versa; rather, it empowers students intellectually, "socially, emotionally, and politically" [5] (p. 159). Because CRISP integrates community and diverse academic knowledge of society, others, and themselves [5, 7, 12]. A culturally affirming instructor mandates practices that systematically foster success and empower students to answer critical questions [2, 13]. Through the development of student agency, CRISP builds students' critical literacies to connect their lives/examples of oppression in curricula, institutions, and disciplines [14].

In contrast CRISP's concern with connecting students' identities, cultures, and communities to the classroom, past research has missed the lack of diversity in particular fields, including computer science (CS). CS has historically been inaccessible to marginalized groups, women, and students from working communities [15]. Work has been done to take a culturally affirming approach to CS education by examining and diversifying what are produced, how courses are taught, and how to deepen student participation [16, 17]. From this work, culturally responsive computing (CRC) has emerged on the principles of CRISP to challenge the status quo in computing [18, 19].

CRC promotes equity in STEM by rooting the curriculum in a social critique lens [19, 20] to leverage the linguistic assets of the local community and students [21, 22]. Through CRC, students engage and challenge political, economic, and social issues, addressing oppressive conditions they have experienced. CRC has five tenets that support a culturally responsive environment: (1) every student is capable of digital innovation; (2) the learning context must support the use of technology; (3) learning should be about one's self along various sociocultural lines that allow for technical innovation; (4) technology should be a vehicle for students to reflect and demonstrate understanding of their intersection identities; and (5) standards for technological success must consider who creates, for whom, and to what end, rather than that ensures socially and culturally irrelevant [19]. In sum, CRC's goals are similar to those of CRISP: to create an educational (computing) environment that values student/family/community expertise, provide opportunities for collaboration with local community organizations and community experts, and to focus not only on the content (i.e., tool design) but also on the design and implementation of CRC curricula [22].

Past examples of CRC demonstrate the connection it has to CRSP. Examples include girls using digital technology to educate their local community about relevant, but stigmatized, social issues (e.g. pregnancy) [23], using “social GIS” to explore social justice issues with science and mapping tools [24], using computational skills to share Indigenous knowledge and language [20], and with young women to develop a Zambian Women Rights Rights mobile app [25]. Past research has established that when students are encouraged to embody their identities, cultures, and communities into STEM learning and computing, students’ motivation is enhanced, which has the power to increase student agency and STEM participation [10, 26]. Teaching through a CRC lens creates opportunities to align the goals of students and community members through a collaboration that leverages “community assets” that are relevant to the students and their lived realities [22] (p. 5) while promoting equity in CS [27].

# 3 AI4SG Curriculum Design

We designed AI learning modules called “AI for Social Good (AI4SG)” to educate undergraduates from STEM and non-STEM backgrounds. These instructional modules are a result of interdisciplinary work from the research team and are based on CRC and targeting sustainability as an application case [20]. CRC is particularly suitable for interdisciplinary, community-based, and inclusive AI due to its five tenets: (1) all students are capable of digital innovation, with varying levels of learning support and application; (2) the learning contexts support transformational use of technology, modules will be designed to address relevant social issues in students’ communities; (3) learning should be about one’s self along intersecting sociocultural lines that allow for technical innovation, modules are interdisciplinary and the exchange of cultural values will be incentivized; (4) technology should be vehicle in which students reflect and demonstrate understanding of their intersectional identities, modules will be designed for students to express and bring their full self into the practice and community; (5) barometers for technological progress should consider who creates, for whom, and to what rather than endures culturally and irrelevant curriculum, modules will emphasize the importance of social good, community, and ethics in AI, allowing students to contribute to their communities as they learn AI.
