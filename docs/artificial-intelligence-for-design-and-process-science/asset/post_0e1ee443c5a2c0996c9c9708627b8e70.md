## 2.3 Product Models, Data Exchange Standards, and Meta-programming Languages

Product model and data exchange standards that can be used in conjunction with product engineering include the ISO-10303 and the MTConnect standards. The ISO10303 standard (www.steeptools.com) describes how digital product information is represented and exchanged, enabling companies to unify all product associated with individuals throughout their life cycle. The MTConnect standard (www.mtconnect.org) defines a semantic vocabulary for manufacturing plants to provide structured contextual data without their proprietary format. It is clear that product engineering and digital twins require more expressive constructs than those offered by the product model and data exchange standards examined above. Much effort has been put into the development of ontology approaches such as AutomationML (www.automationml.org). It is an open, neutral, XML-based data exchange format that allows the transfer of design data from production systems.

Other efforts have focused on enhancing the complexity of automated systems by proposing programmable logic controllers (PLCs) and domain-specific languages (DSLs) [17] for model-driven development of automated processes [18]. DSLs raise the level of abstraction beyond programming by specifying the solution directly in terms of domain concepts. A typical example is the Systems Modeling Language (SysML) [19]. SysML captures functional and nonfunctional requirements, mapping abstract features to factory machine operations, and generates code for the appropriate runtime environment. In addition, an orchestration engine has been developed to accommodate the adaptation of automated systems in operation and the physical changes in the environment [20]. However, until now, little has been placed on developing appropriate abstraction mechanisms to bridge the components of the digital and physical worlds.

Significant efforts are reported in [21], where the authors introduced the idea of digital twin derived from a product version creates assembly plans and synthesizes the resources in a manufacturing cell.

To avoid problems as those mentioned above, meta-programming languages can be used [22]. A meta-programming language invokes a program that can operate on other programs or can represent its own code as a data structure of the language itself [23, 24]. Despite the many examples of program generation reported in the literature, most of the proposed systems use ad hoc technology to build program fragments.

To remedy these limitations, multi-level languages have been proposed as intermediate representations for partial evaluation and as basic format for runtime generation, the most prominent early example being MetaML [25], AutomationML [26], NodeRED (nodered.org), and Converge [27]. However, all of the efforts lead to a platform-dependent design and are not suitable for smart manufacturing.

It is clear from the above analysis that product models and DSLs currently have very limited capacities in the context of product engineering. Almost all efforts focus on very specific aspects of PLCs on the factory floor; they abstract from the physical processes in the controller environment while not targeting digital twins. In contrast, the meta-programming approach in this chapter provides a canonical model and language that encodes an actionable digital twin to fully represent and simulate the characteristics and performance properties of a physical product, its product line, and associated production processes in the virtual world, before the product is physically produced. ## Digital Twins for Product Customization and Production Planning

Transdisciplinary research helps develop effective data integration strategies and advanced analytics techniques that can provide actionable insights for decision-making, process optimization, and predictive maintenance. Smart manufacturing requires a transdisciplinary approach in several ways:

- (i) Integrated technology development: Smart manufacturing involves the convergence of different technologies such as the Internet of Things (IoT), artificial intelligence (AI), robotics, and data analytics. Transdisciplinary research facilitates collaboration between experts in these fields, leading to the development of integrated technologies that can work together seamlessly in smart manufacturing systems.
- (ii) Interdisciplinary problem-solving: Smart manufacturing challenges often require solutions that span several disciplines. Transdisciplinary approaches allow researchers and practitioners from different fields such as engineering, computer science, materials science, and business to work together to solve complex problems related to the automation, optimization, and efficiency of manufacturing processes.
- (iii) Data integration and analytics: Smart manufacturing generates huge amounts of data from sensors, machines, and other sources.
- (iv) Supply chain optimization: Smart manufacturing extends beyond individual factories to include supply chain optimization. A comprehensive approach to supply chain management is required, taking into account logistics, information flow, and coordination between different stakeholders, resulting in more efficient and responsive manufacturing networks.

## 3.1 Digital Twins Facets

To achieve digital product customization, DTs allow product designers to model multiple permutations and a holistic view of all aspects of a digital product. They streamline the process of customer demands and implement usage data that will increase customization options across three facets:

**Product facet (for efficient design/customization of products)**—Allows manufacturers to analyze how a product works under different design conditions, making the necessary adjustments in designing the digital counterpart, and ensure that the physical product performs as designed. Digital twin product facets allow to test product variations before physically prototyping the product.

**Production facet (for improved production planning and production)**—Helps to verify how the manufacturing process will run before production on the shop floor. It simulates the process using the product facet and analyzes the digital thread throughout a variety of conditions and what-if scenarios. A digital thread is a single seamlessly connected chain of data captured throughout the life cycle of a single product, designed, modeled, manufactured, used, and delivered from the initial design concept to the finished product. Creating a digital twin replicates the entire manufacturing process. This allows manufacturers to find process errors without affecting the production volume of the manufacturing plant.

**Performance facet (for improved quality and performance of products and production)**—Supports the collection and analysis of data on the usage and effectiveness of digital products and production processes, providing actionable insights for informed decision-making and continuous optimization of both product and process aspects. The performance facet gathers real-time data from operational products and the production line, enabling manufacturers to identify opportunities to improve the product or production process.
