
# The Power of Smart Design: How Intelligence Shapes Custom Digital Products

Michael P. Papazoglou, Bernd J. Krämer, and Amal Elegammal

**Abstract** Smart manufacturing fundamentally transforms traditional manufacturing into the next generation of data- and knowledge-driven digital manufacturing. This chapter presents a proven digital twin-driven manufacturing approach called manufacturing blueprints. It includes a novel manufacturing knowledge model and a programming approach to rethink and redesign the whole product customization and development process. Manufacturing blueprints are a comprehensive digital twin approach that addresses the entire smart product life cycle, including product design, production, and performance aspects. Manufacturing blueprints promises to realize the vision of smart manufacturing by fully digitizing all physical assets and processes, connecting factory sites and plants to customize outputs, and allocating resources to provide a logical shift between the digital and physical realms of product customization.

**Keywords** Smart manufacturing · Meta-programming · Manufacturing blueprints · Production planning

# Introduction

Large Industry 4.0 or smart manufacturing is signaled by a transition toward a physical-to-digital-to-physical connection. Smart manufacturing represents the next industrial revolution, realized through the application of digital technologies in industrial processes. A smart manufacturing ecosystem will interweave all aspects of manufacturing, from plant operations to the supply chain, exhibiting a set of connected machines, process automation, and manufacturing units, enabling virtual tracking of capital assets, processes, and resources. The aim is to create an integrated, automated, and optimized production flow that produces increasingly individualized customer products [1].

The manufacturing paradigm advocated by smart manufacturing allows systems (machines, sensors, devices, actuators, valves, and networking technologies) to have both a digital counterpart and a physical interaction with the real world. This results in a digital twin model that mirrors the physical and the virtual worlds [2]. This digital-twin approach enables manufacturers to build an intelligent (sensor-equipped) virtual, digital counterpart capturing any product at any stage of production. The digital twin approach can analyze the behavior of a digital product and provide actionable insights that guide informed decisions about processes and materials using advanced visualization tools, such as 3D CAD/CAM tools, and simulations that observe the impact on the shape and properties of the physical counterpart of the product.

The digital twin representation shows the evolution of a product and production process in every stage of the product life cycle [3]. This helps designers and engineers to optimize performance, predict behavior, and gain insights from past design and production experience. Data from the field can be fed back into the digital twin and used as the basis for further innovation and improvement, enabling manufacturers to virtually recreate a product and its production and even simulate its performance in the real world without taking any physical action.

The vision of smart manufacturing and digital twins requires new languages for modeling, correlating smart digital product aspects (product, process, supplier, and machine knowledge) while supporting the distributed nature of industrial processes.

To address these challenges, this article proposes (i) a novel digital twin knowledge model (a semantic model that captures relevant direct and indirect product production data) and (ii) a digital twin programming paradigm that operates on top of the twin knowledge model and serves to correlate and manage readily available product/manufacturing information and processes. The work presented in this chapter focuses on smart manufacturing enablers and building blocks in [3] by integrating and synergizing an advanced model-driven product engineering approach that leverages the power of meta programming languages. Model-driven engineering has the potential to subsum the complexity of the manufacturing ecosystem to simplify the product design and development processes productivity gains [4, 5]. Meta-programming paradigm can improve the productivity, quality, and reliability of software systems.

In the following discussion, Sect. 2 presents related work and compares it with the findings in this chapter. Sect. 3, we present the requirements for twin-driven product customization and production planning. Section 4 extends traditional engineering to monitor the requirements of digital twins and digital threads following a novel model-driven product approach. Section 5 presents the proposed DT model and its meta-programming environment. In Sect. 6, the approach is applied to an industrial strength aerospace case study. Finally, Section 7 summarizes the chapter and presents perspectives of our work.

# Literature Review

The ability to capture, integrate, and process heterogeneous manufacturing data is central to supporting the tenets of smart manufacturing discussed in this chapter: digital twins, customizable products, and meta-programming languages for manufacturing environments.

## 2.1 Digital Twins

Grieves first introduced the concept of DTs back in 2003 [2]; however, the rapid development of DT-enabling technologies, including simulation, data acquisition, communication sensors, visualization, cloud computing, and big analytics, have only recently emerged. These triggered the transformation of the DT from being a concept to its application and development in various fields [6].

With the growing attention to the concept and development of DTs, several analytical and state-of-the-art articles have been published recently [6–9]. The study in [7] provides a categorical literature review of DT in manufacturing, distinguishing between Digital Models (DM), Digital Shadow (SD), and DTs. DM is defined as a digital representation of an existing or physical object that does not use automated data exchange between physical and digital objects. SD is another automated one-way data flow between the state of an existing physical object and a digital object. The students then concluded that the literature focusing on the highest-level development in the form of DT is rare and compared to DM and DS. The work presented in this article aims to fill this gap.

The authors in [9] present the challenges and revolutionary technologies for the Internet of Things (IoT), artificial intelligence (AI), and DT in the domains of manufacturing, healthcare, and smart cities. They define DT as a simple bidirectional integration of data between physical and virtual machines. In a parallel context, the research in [8] investigates the correlation and differences between cyber-physical systems (CPSs) and DTs and concludes that they are not similar in several aspects considering their origin, development, engineering aspects, CPSs mapping, and base elements.

State-of-the-art review considering 50 relevant papers and 8 patents has been conducted in [6], which concludes that DT applications in the product design account for 18% of the literature, while 35% address the process design (mainly the execution phase while neglecting planning), and 47% address the monitoring process (possibly health monitoring).

In contrast, DT approach proposed here provides a formal manufacturing knowledge model and an expressive meta-programming environment (see Sect. 2.3) for the seamless translation of validated product designs into efficient and optimized production plans. This approach can be extended to subsequent processes of the product life cycle, from production to monitoring, with rigorous solutions, and concrete IT implementations. However, these are beyond the scope of this article.

## 2.2 Product Customization

Product customization is the production of goods and services to meet the needs of individual customers with efficiency close to mass production. Critical to the successful implementation of a customization strategy is the concept of product architecture, which is the means to ensure the reuse of parts, assemblies, technologies, and knowledge to achieve both product customization and variability at the same time [10].

Modularity and commonality in product design ensure customization to bring product costs lower to mass production. Both enable product differentiation/customization by commonality is primarily used to reduce lead times and benefiting from economies of scale. During the design phase, commonality is primarily used to reduce the effort required to create product variants [11].

Giovanni et al. [12] present an innovative approach to rationalize product diversity by associating each product variant with the profile of the customer most likely to be interested in.

Tian et al. [13] argue that in the case of mass customization, by the strong similarities between mass customization products and their components, a parameterized approach can be used. Research studies relate to product customization are looking at knowledge models that represent the platform of a product family [14, 15]. The study in [14] presents a module-based integrated design scheme to support the creation of a product platform, product family generation, and customized assessment. Naguchi et al. [15] propose a knowledge model of product architecture design system that focuses on design repositories to reduce data redundancy and inconsistencies.
