
Learning Shortcuts: On the Misleading Promise of NLU in Language Models

## 2.1 Identifying Shortcuts

Research dedicated to automatically identifying and mitigating spurious cues in training and fine-tuning data has been proposed in recent years [13, 17, 18]. The understanding of shortcut learning behaviors in LLMs has been recently extended by leveraging the long-tailed phenomenon [9]. This work observed that LLMs tend to focus on the head of the word distribution, neglecting the poorly learned tail of the distribution. Furthermore, their findings indicate that shortcuts are predominantly learned in the early training iterations, impeding potential learning enhancements in later iterations.

While most works do not differentiate between spurious and genuine tokens, [19] addresses this distinction by employing an attention-guided approach to detect and alleviate shortcut learning. This mechanism identifies both model and dataset biases, utilizing human attention as a supervisory signal to compel the model to allocate more attention to “relevant” tokens. This approach enhances robustness to out-of-distribution data and maintains performance on IID data.

## 3 Implications on NLU Evaluation

### 3.1 Examining the Impacts

Inflated NLU Performance Scores The performance gains of pre-trained language models on NLU tasks have been the subject of extensive scrutiny within the natural language processing domain. In several recent works, the performance of BERT-like models is shown to be entirely attributable to the exploitation of statistical cues. We demonstrated that BERT uses the word “not” as a shortcut in several NLU tasks, and the removal of such cues leads to a significant drop in model performance, plummeting from near baseline to a score [8]. Similarly, a drop of over 20% in BERT-based NLU performance was observed when evaluated on out-of-distribution datasets [9]. Another study showed that PLM accuracy scores are inflated by 20% by many samples containing high levels of superficial information [20].

Moreover, [16] established that training and evaluating models using standard natural language inference (NLI) datasets such as MNLI [21] results in models relying on heuristics. When assessed on a curated dataset lacking shortcut, performance dropped below chance performance for examples without heuristics. This highlights that NLU models may not learn the correct rules for inference. Similarly, [15] shows that even with a complete vocabulary overlap, the performance of fact verification models declines by 20% when employing distinct language—while maintaining consistent claim and evidence relations (support or refute). [19] also revealed a substantial decline of 40%–60% in BERT-based model performance on NLI and fact verification tasks upon the removal of shortcuts. These studies underscore the pronounced dependence of LLMs on superficial information within the datasets, and the inflated NLU performance reported due to the same.

Overconfidence in LLM Decisions Examining model performance extends beyond accuracy metrics. Recent works also include a scrutiny of confidence scores for incorrect predictions. [19] demonstrated models to produce overconfident predictions for data samples with shortcut or trigger patterns, irrespective of the ground truth. This mismatch between model confidence and actual accuracy leads to the phenomenon known as miscalibration, impacting the reliability of models in real-world applications. Ideally, a well-calibrated model assigns high probabilities to correct and low probabilities to incorrect decisions, aligning predicted probabilities with observed events.

The rising deployment of neural network architectures in high-risk real-world scenarios has prompted extensive research into calibration [14, 22, 23]. Evaluations of neural network reliability indicate that their confidence predictions are often poorly calibrated and overconfident [24, 25]. Recent studies have extended these findings to pre-trained language models and that fine-tuning exacerbates miscalibration [26–29]. This is attributed to the excessive parameterization of the models, leading to overfitting on the training data. The attention garnered by pre-trained language models from strings to exhibit confidence during training, regardless of prediction accuracy [30]. However, these models showcase calibration deterioration in out-of-domain scenarios [27]. Notably, smaller models demonstrate improved calibration on in-domain data, while larger models exhibit better calibration on out-of-domain data [31]. These findings underscore the current inadequacies of pre-trained language models in terms of confidence calibration and reliability in decision-making.

### 3.2 Improving NLU amid Shortcut Learning

The current paradigm in natural language understanding and evaluation in LLMs revolves around alleviating shortcut learning to prevent inflated NLU task scores. Strategies such as ensemble learning and adversarial datasets are employed to diminish the impact of shortcuts, followed by the assessment of NLU task performance. More robust evaluations extend to measuring model generalization by assessing performance on out-of-distribution datasets.

Data-Centric Approaches Recent approaches to enhance model robustness and mitigate the impact of spurious cues involve the creation of datasets curated examples that reduce model reliance on shortcuts during task learning. Incorporating adversarial data points during both training and evaluation, such as mirroring linguistic artifacts around labels, has also been shown to diminish the impact of spurious cues on model learning [8, 15]. [15] demonstrated a notable decrease in model performance when assessed on an adversarial test set, underscoring the necessity for NLU evaluations to incorporate considerations for shortcut learning in language models. Additional efforts in this domain involve the development of adversarial datasets, such as HANS, which assesses whether natural language inference (NLI) models rely on syntactic heuristics to accomplish the task [16].

Recently, novel data generation techniques have been proposed for mitigating spurious correlations and promoting generalization [32]. This technique involves training data generators to create and filter data points that exacerbate correlations but yield significant performance gains in task accuracy. Moreover, even though data augmentation for bias removal reduces the model's dependence on spurious cues, it does not eliminate it [33]. This persistence of lexical bias emphasizes the ongoing challenges in effectively mitigating the impact of spurious cues in NLU models.
