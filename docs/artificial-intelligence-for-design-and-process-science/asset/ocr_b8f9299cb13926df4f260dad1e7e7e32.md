# Approach

Mathematical models of atomic and molecular systems, grounded in both classical and quantum physics, utilize three-dimensional spatial portrayals of atoms. Such models emerge from the classical mechanics assumption that space and time are distinct dimensions. However, this premise, questioned by Einstein's theory of general relativity, limits these models in terms of accuracy and computational efficiency. We propose that the constructs of 3D space and time are predominantly human-derived perceptions, suggesting that mathematics can depict the universe beyond these confines. To enhance computational capabilities and represent physical reality across scales, it's essential to rethink the 3D space-time paradigm.

We developed the communication dynamics model that challenges the conventional space-time belief, offering a unified mathematical formulation that encapsulates physical significance. In this model, one-dimensional frequency space evolving hyperbolically over a clock time (or T-vector). Our model permits representation precision across varying system scales, introducing an adaptive precision mechanism. With this model, precision can range from the minimum quantum time, represented by Planck's constant (h), to complex entities like atoms, molecules, and even biomolecules like proteins and DNA. This model facilitates seamless cross-scale integration using a uniform formula, presenting an avenue to refine foundational physics theories by shifting from a 3D space-time representation to a frequency-vector model.

Communication dynamics systematically analyze and compare chemical structures at any chosen scale. It holds the potential to address data-heavy and computationally demanding challenges in chemical systems. In prior work [1], we introduce unique communication channel representations for every element in the periodic table, conceptualizing electron orbitals as distinct communication channels. This innovative approach allowed for the depiction of intrinsic orbital properties, including energy and radius. The approach is translatable to modeling complex chemical systems.
