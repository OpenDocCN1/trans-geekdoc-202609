Teaching that recognizes the cultural characteristics, experiences, and perspectives of culturally diverse students affirm the cultural knowledge and skills that their communities offer the classroom [3]. CRISP realizes that student learning is impacted by factors that go beyond the classroom [3, 7]; integrating students' communities of Color's knowledge and skills into classroom learning normalizes the knowledge and skills of Color, enriches classroom learning, and promotes a curriculum that is accessible and relevant to all students [5], thereby increasing students' social, cultural, and academic engagement [8–11].

CRISP does not replace rigor with inclusion or vice versa; rather, it empowers students intellectually, "socially, emotionally, and politically" [5] (p. 159). Because CRISP integrates community and diverse academic knowledge of society, others, and themselves [5, 7, 12]. A culturally affirming instructor mandates practices that systematically foster success and empower students to answer critical questions [2, 13]. Through the development of student agency, CRISP builds students' critical literacies to connect their lives/examples of oppression in curricula, institutions, and disciplines [14].

In contrast CRISP's concern with connecting students' identities, cultures, and communities to the classroom, past research has missed the lack of diversity in particular fields, including computer science (CS). CS has historically been inaccessible to marginalized groups, women, and students from working communities [15]. Work has been done to take a culturally affirming approach to CS education by examining and diversifying what are produced, how courses are taught, and how to deepen student participation [16, 17]. From this work, culturally responsive computing (CRC) has emerged on the principles of CRISP to challenge the status quo in computing [18, 19].

CRC promotes equity in STEM by rooting the curriculum in a social critique lens [19, 20] to leverage the linguistic assets of the local community and students [21, 22]. Through CRC, students engage and challenge political, economic, and social issues, addressing oppressive conditions they have experienced. CRC has five tenets that support a culturally responsive environment: (1) every student is capable of digital innovation; (2) the learning context must support the use of technology; (3) learning should be about one's self along various sociocultural lines that allow for technical innovation; (4) technology should be a vehicle for students to reflect and demonstrate understanding of their intersection identities; and (5) standards for technological success must consider who creates, for whom, and to what end, rather than that ensures socially and culturally irrelevant [19].
