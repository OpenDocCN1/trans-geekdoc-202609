## 5.2 Hydrogen Atom Energy Level

Starting from Bohr's model of hydrogen atom, the radius of the stationary orbits and electron energy levels (eV) can be calculated with the following equations:

$r_n = n^2 a_0$

$E = -\frac{13.6Z^2}{n^2}$

$a_0 = 0.0529\text{ nm}$ is Bohr's radius and $Z$ is the number of protons in an atom (in this case 1).

Bohr's model is only successful in describing hydrogen-like atoms but fails in other elements. It also fails to describe many quantum features of atoms. The current quantum mechanics description of atomic orbital generally operates the Schrödinger equation. The Schrödinger equation (Eq. (14)) is an eigenvalue equation. For example, the Hamiltonian operator ($H$) operates on a function ($\Psi$, wave function) and the result equals a real number ($E$) times the same function. The scalar number is called the eigenvalue; the function is the eigenfunction. For hydrogen atom, the atomic orbital can be described by spherical coordinates in the form of a radial function $R_{nl}$ with radial variable $r$ and spherical harmonic $Y_{lm}$ with angular variables $\theta$ and $\phi$:

$H\Psi = E\Psi$

$\Psi_{nlm} = R_{nl}Y_{lm}$

The wave functions of all hydrogen orbitals can be found here: the expectation value of radius $\langle r\rangle$ is used to describe the average radial probability, which resembles the expected value from experiment as:

$\langle r\rangle = \int \Psi_{nlm}^* r \Psi_{nlm} dV$

$dV$ is the spherical coordinate that contains:

$dV = r^2 \sin\theta dr d\theta d\phi$

For example, for 1s orbital, the solution $\langle r\rangle = \frac{3a_0}{Z}$. This also explains why heavier atoms have smaller orbital when $Z$ increases, $\langle r\rangle$ of 1s orbital becomes more compact. Further, the probability density of electron at distance $r$ can be described using $P(r)$, which has a relation with radial wave function $R$:

$P(r) = 4\pi r^2 R^2$

The maximum of $P(r)$ is obtained by setting $dP/dr=0$ and solving for $r$. For 1s orbital, the maximum of $P(r)$ is when $r=a_0$. For Hydrogen ($Z = 1$), the expectation value $\langle r\rangle$ is 1.5 times the maximum probability radius $r$ that is the Bohr radius. For 2s, $r=2a_0$ and for 3s $r=\frac{(3+\sqrt{3})a_0}{2Z}, \frac{(3-\sqrt{3})a_0}{2Z}$ (two nodes).

On the other hand, the H atom spectrum can be described by the Rydberg equation, which implies a series of discrete energy levels as:

$E_n = -\frac{hc_H}{n^2}$

The eigen equation for the radial function $R_n$ can be expressed as follows, and the eigenvalue of which is:

$$\frac{\hbar^2}{2me}\frac{\partial^2R}{\partial r^2} + \frac{2\hbar R}{r} + \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu r^2}(l(l+1) - \frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0})R=ER$$

where $\mu = \frac{m_{electron}m_{nucleus}}{m_{electron} + m_{nucleus}}$. Therefore, when $l=0$:

$E = -\frac{Z^2\mu e^4}{32\epsilon_0^2\pi^2\hbar^2}\frac{1}{n^2}=-13.6\frac{Z^2}{n^2}$

which is the same as Eq. (20) when $Z=1$, indicating that both theories are in agreement at H atom.

When $n \to \infty$, the electron is free from nucleus and this process is called ionization. The ionization energy for electrons in the valence shell is $E_i = -E(n)$; when $n$ is a constant, the ionization energy increases as $Z$ increases. Because the amount of energy required for ionization (ionization energy) is the same for a given quantum number $n$, the atom orbital exhibited energy levels of $2n^2$ degeneracy (e.g., second shell $2s+2p$ has eight degenerate states).
