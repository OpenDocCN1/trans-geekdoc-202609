Multi-word expressions (MWEs)—including compound nouns and phrasal verbs—may present features such as complex noncompositionality [4, 5] and context-dependent usage [6, 7]. Compound nouns, for example, exhibit a semantic richness that surpasses the sum of their parts. Consider the terms fig leaf and orange juice. At first glance, each may be straightforward compositions, a leaf from a tree and juice from one, respectively. However, the former also come to symbolize a covering for embarrassing or shameful. While the meaning of orange juice can be determined from its individual parts, the meaning of fig leaf cannot for all cases. It should be noted that orange juice is also ambiguous, but the ambiguity stems from orange being a fruit or a juice, while thus ambiguous, orange juice is still compositional.

Our work suggests that transformer encoders are capable of observing distinct and accurate meanings of compositional nouns. This observation is significant, albeit based on a limited number of examples, indicating that these models can grasp the subtle semantic distinctions that compound nouns convey. The ability to accurately interpret these nouns is crucial for tasks such as semantic analysis, machine translation, and information retrieval, where the precise meaning of compound nouns can greatly affect the accuracy and relevance of the results. The successful interpretation of such compositional nouns by underscores the potential of these models to enhance our understanding and processing of natural language, offering avenues for future research and application in NLP.

Moreover, the ability to identify and understand compositional compound nouns has profound implications for improving search functionalities. In search contexts, recognizing the compositional nature of compound nouns enables more sophisticated query processing. For example, when a search involves a compositional noun, it is vital to discern which component of the compound noun is variable and which is fixed to generate relevant results. This distinction allows for the dynamic adaptation of search queries to find entities similar to the target concept. For instance, in a search for apple juice, understanding that apple can be replaced with other fruits like orange to find similar entities—while maintaining juice as a fixed term—enhances the search experience by providing diverse but relevant results.

This nuanced understanding and manipulation of language elements are pivotal in advancing search technologies that consider the semantic relationships between words, rather than relying solely on keyword matching. Such advancements could significantly improve the precision of information retrieval systems, making them more responsive to the actual intent behind users' queries. Consequently, the application of transformer encoders to discern and manipulate the structure of compositional compound nouns also benefits linguistic analysis and translation tasks but also elevates the capabilities of search engines and information retrieval systems to understand and cater to the complex nuances of human language.
