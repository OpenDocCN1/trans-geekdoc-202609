# Philosophy of Intellectualism and Machine Intelligence

The idea of intellectualism has been the focus of extensive philosophical study throughout time, and many interpretations of it have changed significantly. Ancient philosophers like Socrates built their arguments for reason and knowledge on the tenet that knowledge produces virtue ignorance vice [14]. This idea holds that people who fully comprehend what is morally correct will act in accordance with it. Aristotle, on the other hand, changed intellectualism in favor of a more empirical approach and emphasized the value of logic and experiential learning [15]. The intellectual landscape then underwent a significant upheaval as a result of the Enlightenment. A new era of reason, scientific inquiry, and a healthy distrust of authority was promoted by the likes of Kant, Descartes, and Hume, whose works served as its foundation. This was a significant shift from the long-standing dependence on religious doctrine or established tradition. Descartes emphasized skepticism and doubt as a way to get specific knowledge and championed the idea of independent thought. I think, I am, as famously encapsulated in it "Cogito, ego sum." This groundbreaking method, known as Kantian skepticism, established truths. Hume, a significant character in the Scottish Enlightenment, also made a contribution to this change in perspective with his empiricist philosophy, which maintained that knowledge comes from sensory experience. He emphasized the use of empirical data and critical thinking in his work, which was crucial in the development of the scientific method [16].

By fusing the rationalist and empiricist traditions, Kant’s intellectual contributions, particularly his foundational work, Critique of Reason, substantially influenced modern secular idealism [17]. He advances the concept of "transcendental idealism," which holds that the human mind is an active creator of knowledge rather than a passive consumer. Therefore, according Kant, intelligence is the ability to understand and navigate through the world using these intentionally established mental schemas, or "Erkenntnis" [13]. This idea is furthered by Kant’s theory of judgment, which emphasizes how ideas and intuitions link to unique representations of objects. It emphasizes how intelligence is a result of our capacity to form mental representations and comprehend their relationships. This view of intelligence is supported by transcendental idealism, which maintains that our perspective of the world is shaped by cognitive faculties rather than a direct reflection of the universe. The "semantic problem," or the accuracy of mental representations referencing world-things, is addressed by this strategy [18]. Therefore, intelligence is viewed in the Kantian perspective as an active, constructive ability rather than a passive reflection. Here, reflexivity allows our minds to evaluate and scrutin their own thought processes and mental representations [19]. He emphasizes the intelligence’s constructive, reflective, and inherently active nature.
