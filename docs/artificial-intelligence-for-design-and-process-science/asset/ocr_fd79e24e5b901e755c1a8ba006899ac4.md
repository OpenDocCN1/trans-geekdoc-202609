But imagination is only half the story. The process of imagining possibilities might be married to methods for evaluating such strategy.
In games, the final goal is to see a clear path to do goal straight away. In interesting games it's not possible to see a path to make progress you must form more linear, tractable, intermediate goals, and aim for. In others, you've got to decide what to wish for.

Wishful thinking, in this sense, is an essential part of problem-solving. This becomes even more true when we move outside the context of games, where there are no set rules that supply the definition of ultimate success.

It's very plausible, then, that an important step toward achievement, whether for machines or human beings, is cultivate systematic childish thinking. New Year's resolutions, business plans, and “to-do” lists embody strategy.
The issue of goals arises most at the highest levels of learning and problem-solving. Trying to make a scientific breakthrough, write a novel or compose a symphony, or create some great work of art can be hard, frustrating work. Success is not guaranteed, and the tangible, economic rewards are usually modest. What keeps people going?

Some motivations are obvious. The dopamine rush that accompanies successful problem-solving can be its reward. And successful problem-solving can earn the esteem of others, another thing people finding rewarding in itself. But that's not the whole story.

For many of the greatest physicists, religion was a big motivator. Galileo, Newton, Faraday, and Maxwell were all deeply believing, if not entirely orthodox, Christians. Here's how John Maynard Keynes, the economist, described Isaac Newton, in his lecture “Newton, the Man":

He looked on the universe and all that is in it a riddle, as a secret which could be read by applying pure thought to certain evidence, certain mystic clues that God had laid about the world as sort of philosopher's treasure hunt to the esoteric brotherhood. He believed that these clues were found partly in the evidence of the heavens and in the constitution of elements (and that is what gives the false question of being an experimental natural philosopher), also partly in certain papers and traditions handed down by the brethren in an unbroken chain back to the original cryptic revelation in Babylon. He regarded the universe as a cryptogram set by the Almighty.

And here is Maxwell, delighting in his discoveries:

The vast interplanetary and interstellar regions will no longer be regarded as waste places in the universe, which the Creator has not seen to fit with the symbols of the manifold order of His kingdom. We shall find them to be full of this wonderful medium: so full, that no human power can remove them from the smallest portion of space, or produce the slightest flaw in its infinite continuity.
