Cancer cells evade immune-mediated recognition and elimination through immunosuppressive strategies that disrupt T-cell signaling and induce tolerance in the TME through the production of inhibitory cytokines that prevent the expansion and activation of CD4+ helper and cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells [30,37–39].

The suppression and inactivation of immune detection and immune-mediated cytotoxicity is also accompanied by the ability of cancer cells to actively reprogram immune cells in order to stimulate angiogenesis and accompanying stromal responses that promote the progression of disease through the continued survival of cancer cells. The subversion of normal immunoregulatory mechanisms also leads to the emergence of subsets of tumor cells that are less immunogenic and resistant to apoptosis within the immunosuppressed milieu of the tumor microenvironment. This selection pressure enables cancer cells to adapt and further evade the immune system through the loss of tumor antigens that would normally stimulate complement-mediated and antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity from T-cells and natural killer (NK) cells [17,28–39]. The combination of the uncontrolled proliferation and adaptation of cancer cells in the immunosuppressed tumor microenvironment ultimately results in the invasion of neighboring tissues and distant sites beyond the primary site of origin.

The importance of the role of immunity in controlling cancer was observed a century ago by Dr. William Coley in a case of complete remission in advanced inoperable sarcoma secondary to severe Streptococcus pyogenes bacterial skin infections [55]. Thus, the concept of stimulating and inhibiting hierarchical immunoregulatory mechanisms of the immune surveillance system as a feasible treatment strategy for cancer was born decades ago [56–58]. Since then, many studies have focused on specific types of immune cell populations and their various functional properties in order to determine the clinical significance of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory temporospatial relationships in numerous types of human disease, including cancer [17,28–30,32,44,52,53,59–65]. During the process of uncontrolled growth in cancer, nuanced differences in protein structure of foreign antigens in infected, aberrant, or tumor cells are detected at the molecular level during antigen processing and processed by the innate and adaptive immune system to coordinate and targeted immune-mediated response. However, the immune system is highly complex and consists of many types and subtypes of immune cells to fight infections and remove aberrant cells, which results in a dynamic interplay between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory functional states that are regulated through cytokine cell signaling and direct interactions between immune cells to generate systemic immune responses to fight cancer.

Despite the intrinsic functional complexities and widely variable composition of the immune system, we are fortunate as a society to have access to first-generation immunotherapeutic options in our current day and age that have been utilized to successfully treat cancer and provide tangible clinical benefits.
