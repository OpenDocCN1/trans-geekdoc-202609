Cancer cells evade immune-mediated recognition and elimination through immunosuppressive strategies that disrupt T-cell signaling and induce tolerance in the TME through the production of inhibitory cytokines that prevent the expansion and activation of CD4+ helper and cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells [30,37–39].

The suppression and inactivation of immune detection and immune-mediated cytotoxicity is also accompanied by the ability of cancer cells to actively reprogram immune cells in order to stimulate angiogenesis and accompanying stromal responses that promote the progression of disease through the continued survival of cancer cells. The subversion of normal immunoregulatory mechanisms also leads to the emergence of subsets of tumor cells that are less immunogenic and resistant to apoptosis within the immunosuppressed milieu of the tumor microenvironment. This selection pressure enables cancer cells to adapt and further evade the immune system through the loss of tumor antigens that would normally stimulate complement-mediated and antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity from T-cells and natural killer (NK) cells [17,28–39]. The combination of the uncontrolled proliferation and adaptation of cancer cells in the immunosuppressed tumor microenvironment ultimately results in the invasion of neighboring tissues and distant sites beyond the primary site of origin.

The importance of the role of immunity in controlling cancer was observed a century ago by Dr. William Coley in a case of complete remission in advanced inoperable sarcoma secondary to severe Streptococcus pyogenes bacterial skin infections [55]. Thus, the concept of stimulating and inhibiting hierarchical immunoregulatory mechanisms of the immune surveillance system as a feasible treatment strategy for cancer was born decades ago [56–58]. Since then, many studies have focused on specific types of immune cell populations and their various functional properties in order to determine the clinical significance of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory temporospatial relationships in numerous types of human disease, including cancer [17,28–30,32,44,52,53,59–65]. During the process of uncontrolled growth in cancer, nuanced differences in protein structure of foreign antigens in infected, aberrant, or tumor cells are detected at the molecular level during antigen processing and processed by the innate and adaptive immune system to coordinate and targeted immune-mediated response. However, the immune system is highly complex and consists of many types and subtypes of immune cells to fight infections and remove aberrant cells, which results in a dynamic interplay between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory functional states that are regulated through cytokine cell signaling and direct interactions between immune cells to generate systemic immune responses to fight cancer.

Despite the intrinsic functional complexities and widely variable composition of the immune system, we are fortunate as a society to have access to first-generation immunotherapeutic options in our current day and age that have been utilized to successfully treat cancer and provide tangible clinical benefits. Currently, available treatment options stimulate immune cells or block immune checkpoint pathways. The best examples of recent success in immunotherapy include monoclonal antibody-based treatment strategies that block cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1), which have generated substantial interest in identifying other novel clinically actionable targets that can further improve treatment response and clinical outcomes through combinations with existing chemotherapy and other novel pharmacologic options. Other types of treatments that are being explored include chimeric-antigen receptor T-cells (CAR-T) that are produced by genetically modifying T-cells harvested from individual patients; recombinant proteins to stimulate and enhance cytotoxic T-cell activity; cancer vaccines to augment antigen presentation; and oncolytic viruses to initiate systemic anti-tumor immunity in order to target and destroy cancer cells [30,37,38].

Examples of immune checkpoint therapy with CTLA-4 or PD-1/PD-L1 to overcome the immunosuppression mediated by tumor cells [37,38,64,66] include melanoma, renal cancer, and lung cancer [67–71]. Ipilimumab, the monoclonal antibody that blocks CTLA-4, was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to treat melanoma in 2011, which was followed by FDA approval for nivolumab to block PD-1 in melanoma and non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in 2014 [50,72]. These successes were followed by the introduction of Pembrolizumab, which is considered to be a breakthrough therapy for the treatment of NSCLC because treatment response is significantly improved in tumors that also express high levels of the PD-L1. Nivolumab has also been recently approved to treat patients with advanced metastatic squamous NSCLC with disease progression in combination with or after standard platinum-based chemotherapy [39,71,72]. In order to further improve the therapeutic efficacy of these immune checkpoint inhibitors, combinations of immunotherapy with existing forms of chemotherapy and other forms of targeted therapies are currently being tested and evaluated in patients with locally advanced or metastatic cancer [37,73–76].

Despite this success, the identification and stratification of patients who will likely benefit from immunotherapy is not so straightforward, even though the PD-1 signaling blockade has become an established form of treatment for many types of cancer. Commercially available standardized assays to evaluate the bioactivity of PD-1/PD-L1 lack antigen specificity, even though the overexpression of surface or soluble PD-L1 in multiple tumor types is mostly associated with poor prognosis [67,70,72,76–81]. The histologic assessment of PD-L1 is also challenging due to heterogeneous expression in cancer and benign normal tissues, which is further complicated with unintuitive thresholds and cutoffs for scoring PD-L1 positivity that can vary and lead to known problematic interobserver variability in PD-L1 assessment by pathologists [42,82–84].

As immunotherapy expands, there is a high demand for reliable and standardized bioassays and scoring methods that can characterize antibodies and evaluate expression to select the appropriate patients for PD-1/PD-L1 blockade therapy. Since checkpoint inhibitors and other forms of emerging immunotherapy rely on the host immune system and tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) to fight cancer, the utility and pharmacologic effects of these treatments may be limited in current treatment strategies if the host immune system is depleted or not present in the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, inaccurate patient stratification and the impact of misinterpretation of PD-L1 expression can lead to radically different therapeutic stratification, clinical outcomes, and serious adverse effects that can occur due to subjective scoring around variable thresholds and cutoffs [42,82–84]. In order to meet this unmet clinical need, digital pathology algorithms that can detect and characterize lymphocytes in WSIs of diagnostic surgical pathology specimens may play useful roles to identify and stratify patients for immunotherapy, especially with the recent success of manual assessment of TILs by pathologists in histopathologic specimens in many solid tumor types to provide important prognostic information and established the potential of the role of TILs as a biomarker to predict treatment response [3,4,28,85,86].

## Identifying TILs with deep learning

The biological behavior of various types and subtypes of cancer is intrinsically coupled to the characteristics of the tumor microenvironment, which consists of stroma, immune infiltrate, nerves, and lymphovascular vessels [17,31–35,50]. Since these distinct tissue components cohabit the TME with cancer cells, it is widely believed that tumor antigenicity–driven immune responses are likely quite influenced, if not directly dependent, on the type, quantity, and spatial localization of immune cells that are present in the immediate landscape of the TME [17,31,87]. In order to further examine the relationship of immune cells in the TME with cancer cells, the assessment of TILs is gaining importance as a candidate prognostic and predictive biomarker that may be useful to identify and stratify patients with the highest likelihood of responding to immunotherapy treatment options in a variety of cancer types [32–35,88].

Thus, the routine of evaluation of TILs is gaining popularity as a readily available and widely applicable biomarker that needs to be incorporated into diagnostic surgical pathology reports. Even though the implementation of TILs as a routine clinical biomarker has not been achieved yet, the immediate need to incorporate the evaluation of TILs to improve current protocols for immune checkpoint therapy and other forms of immunotherapy is very clear. The evaluation and appropriate characterization of TILs in the TME is also warranted in terms of addressing immunotherapy-related safety issues since serious adverse effects and the lack of long-lasting responses in many patients suggests that the activation of previously tumor-immunosuppressed TILs needs to be carefully controlled [63].

High densities of TILs correlate with favorable clinical outcomes [89], including longer disease-free survival (DFS) or improved overall survival (OS) in multiple cancer types [90]. Moreover, the spatial localization and distribution of TILs in terms of the immune infiltrate in the tumor center and invasive margin have also become important in cancer prognosis [89].
