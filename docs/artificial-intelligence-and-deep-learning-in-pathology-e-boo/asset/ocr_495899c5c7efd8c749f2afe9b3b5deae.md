The best examples of recent success in immunotherapy include monoclonal antibody-based treatment strategies that block cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1), which have generated substantial interest in identifying other novel clinically actionable targets that can further improve treatment response and clinical outcomes through combinations with existing chemotherapy and other novel pharmacologic options. Other types of treatments that are being explored include chimeric-antigen receptor T-cells (CAR-T) that are produced by genetically modifying T-cells harvested from individual patients; recombinant proteins to stimulate and enhance cytotoxic T-cell activity; cancer vaccines to augment antigen presentation; and oncolytic viruses to initiate systemic anti-tumor immunity in order to target and destroy cancer cells [30,37,38].

Examples of immune checkpoint therapy with CTLA-4 or PD-1/PD-L1 to overcome the immunosuppression mediated by tumor cells [37,38,64,66] include melanoma, renal cancer, and lung cancer [67–71]. Ipilimumab, the monoclonal antibody that blocks CTLA-4, was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to treat melanoma in 2011, which was followed by FDA approval for nivolumab to block PD-1 in melanoma and non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in 2014 [50,72]. These success were followed by the introduction of Pembrolizumab, which is considered to be a breakthrough therapy for the treatment of NSCLC because treatment response is significantly improved in tumors that also express high levels of the PD-L1. Nivolumab has also been recently approved to treat patients with advanced metastatic squamous NSCLC with disease progression in combination with or after standard platinum-based chemotherapy [39,71,72]. In order to further improve the therapeutic efficacy of these immune checkpoint inhibitors, combinations of immunotherapy with existing forms of chemotherapy and other forms of targeted therapies are currently being tested and evaluated in patients with locally advanced or metastatic cancer [37,73–76].

Despite this success, the identification and stratification of patients who will likely benefit from immunotherapy is not so straightforward, even though the PD-1 signaling blockade has become an established form of treatment for many types of cancer. Commercially available standardized assays to evaluate the bioactivity of PD-1/PD-L1 lack antigen specificity, even though the overexpression of surface or soluble PD-L1 in multiple tumor types is mostly associated with poor prognosis [67,70,72,76–81]. The histologic assessment of PD-L1 is also challenging due to heterogeneous expression in cancer and benign normal tissues, which is further complicated with unintuitive thresholds and cutoffs for scoring PD-L1 positivity that can vary and lead to known problematic interobserver variability in PD-L1 assessment by pathologists [42,82–84].

As immunotherapy expands, there is a high demand for reliable and standardized bioassays and scoring methods that can characterize antibodies and evaluate expression to select the appropriate patients for PD-1/PD-L blockade therapy.
