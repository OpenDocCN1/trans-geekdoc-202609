Another recent approach that focuses on reducing the requirement for training data is the development of super-resolution methods that are designed to predict high-resolution labels by primarily utilizing low-resolution training data, generally augmented with sparse high-resolution training data [153]. Generating training data in these approaches involves a combination of labeling patches to indicate density of different cells in each patch that is supplemented with dot annotation.

The emergence of mIHC and mIF and their natural combination with digital pathology and deep learning methods for quantitative IHC interpretation has the potential to address the challenges that can enable this kind of rich information from multiple biomarkers to become routinely used to provide clinically useful prognostic and predictive information [29–31,42,136,142,148]. There appears to be much less doubt and increasing excitement about methods that interrogate and quantify the nature of the interactions among the different types of immune cells in the TME to provide useful prognostic information by identifying key immunoregulatory cells. The potential of identifying clinically actionable insights from using mIHC and artificial intelligence to stratify patients for targeted immunotherapy in the neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings are too important to ignore [42,130]. Thus, development of new methods are absolutely necessary if mIHC and mIF are clinically adopted. If, and when, these methods become full-fledged methods to enable clinical decision support to guide treatment and provide prognostic information, rigorous evaluation, testing, and validation will be undoubtedly required. Since several commercially available mIF and mIHC methods have emerged recently, the future appears very bright for the incorporation of these methods into mainstream translational cancer research.

## Vendor platforms

Deep learning algorithms have also found their way into commercial platforms. Commercial software for analysis of the immune infiltrates include the Genie Spectrum (Aperio ePathology Solutions, Vista, CA) [154,155], Image-Pro Plus 3.0 (Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD) [156], Halo (Indica Labs, Corrales, NM) [151], ImageJ [157], VMscope (VMscope GmbH, Berlin, Germany) slide explorer [2], Vectra/inForm (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) [158], SlidePath (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) [159], Tissue Studio (Definiens, Munich, Germany) [146], Author AI (Visiopharm Corporation, Westminster, CO, USA), and many others. A commercially available image analysis software platform, WebMicroscope, has also been utilized to (1) identify tissue regions containing TILs and (2) count the number of lymphocytes in each region [160], where the authors observed that TIL density in seminomas had prognostic relevance regarding disease relapse. PaigeAI and PathAI are also examples of companies that advertise the ability to carry out combinations of region characterization and cell identification in H&E WSIs as part of their respective efforts to develop deep learning applications to deliver more accurate diagnosis.

## Conclusion

Artificial intelligence is rapidly assuming a crucial role in the elucidation and characterization of tumor immune interactions. Deep learning driven computational algorithms that support the construction of detailed spatial maps appear to be a relatively straightforward way to characterize general tumor immune interactions in the tumor microenvironment, which may be very helpful to predict treatment response and clinical outcomes. Thus, the utilization of advanced digital pathology and deep learning applications will likely grow very quickly over the next few years. This chapter has focused primarily on describing methods behind rapid advances in our ability to generate cancer immune maps and emerging methods that can be used in combination to provide many different types of potentially useful information to deliver precision medicine. Currently, the interpretation of cancer immune dynamics with the complex TME is challenging, but disease site-specific correlations between predictions with digital pathology and deep learning methods, quantitative analyses of mIHC and mIF, clinical outcomes, and treatment response are being actively studied to discover biomarkers and elucidate complex relationships between tumors and immunity in order to help advance our ability to treat cancer. Even though mapping patient-specific tumor-immune interactions are still at an early stage, the crucial next phase of research regarding the quantitative analysis of spatial morphologic and molecular patterns emerging from these methods is beyond fascinating and hopefully clinically applicable. Last but not least, the power of the digital pathology and deep learning methods also lies in the ability to perform these computational analyses of WSIs across thousands to hundreds of thousands of cases in parallel without the need for additional tissue, time, or resources. It is also quite conceivable that analyses of mIF and mIHC in small subsets of site-specific diseases can be used to identify generalizable relationships that can be correlated with more high-throughput standard Pathomics analyses in H&E WSIs in much larger collections in order to gain further insight into tumor biology. Hopefully, these developments can lead to workflows that can be implemented in pathology laboratories as practical clinical decision support tools for routine diagnostic surgical pathology. Thus, we hope the end of this chapter serves as a beginning to help imagine a future where WSIs of incoming tissue samples are analyzed with automated algorithms that perform diagnostic screening, quantify the expression of biomarkers in mIHC panels, provide prognostic information, help identify therapeutic targets, and predict treatment response.
