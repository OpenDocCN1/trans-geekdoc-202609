Nagpal et al. (2019) have published a study on the development and validation of a DL algorithm for improving Gleason scoring of prostate cancer [162]. This study aimed to address the issue of grading variability, improve prognostication, and optimize patient management. A two-stage deep learning system (DLS) was developed to perform Gleason scoring and quantitation on prostatectomy specimens. The first stage was a deep convolutional neural network–based regional Gleason pattern classification. It was trained using 912 slides with 112 million pathologist-annotated image patches. About 1159 slide-level pathologist classifications were used for the second stage of training. A reference standard was created from the independent reviews of three pathologists as well as a genitourinary specialist pathologist on a dataset of 331 slides from 331 patients from 3 independent sources [162].

Twenty-nine additional pathologists reviewed the validation dataset as a comparison to the performance of the DLS. The mean accuracy of the 29 pathologists was 0.61 compared with an accuracy of 0.70 for the DLS (P = .002). Gleason Grade decision thresholds were also investigated with the DLS achieving area under the receiver operating characteristic curves between 0.95 and 0.96 at each threshold. At a Gleason Grade ≥4, the largest difference was seen, and the DLS showed greater sensitivity and specificity than 9 out of 10 pathologists [162].

Because of its clinical significance, Gleason pattern quantitation was compared among the groups; DLS had 4%–6% lower mean absolute error than the average pathologist, and the DLS predicted the same pattern as the pathologist 97% of the time. Lastly, the DLS was able to predict a more gradual transition from well to poorly differentiated by utilizing “fine-grained Gleason patterns,” such as 3.3 or 3.7 instead of the traditional methods of Gleason scoring [162].

Bulten et al. (2020) reported on a DL method for the automated Gleason grading of prostate biopsies. The purpose of this latter study was to create a DLS that was fully automated for detecting cancer and performing Gleason grading without the need for training with manual pixel-level annotations. This was accomplished using previously developed epithelium and prostate cancer segmentation algorithms with slide-level Gleason grades from the original pathology reports of 5209 biopsies from the Radboud University Medical Center. The U-Net architecture was utilized in two stages: (1) Segmentation of the whole biopsy was done by assigning Gleason growth patterns to tumorous regions, and the normalization ratio of epithelial tissue was calculated. (2) The normalized volume percentages of each growth pattern were used to determine the grade group. The neural network was deepened to contain six levels with more skip connections in each layer block and upsampling operations in the expansion path. A test set of 535 biopsies that were not included in the training set was reviewed by three uropathologists, each with greater than 20 years of experience. A consensus score and Gleason Grade were determined by these expert pathologists, and this became the reference set [163].
