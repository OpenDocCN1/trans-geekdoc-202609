## Artificial intelligence and the interplay between tumor and immunity

Joel Haskin Saltz, MD, PhD¹, Rajarsi Gupta, MD, PhD²

¹Professor, Chair, Biomedical Informatics, Stony Brook University, Stony Brook, NY, United States; ²Assistant Professor, Biomedical Informatics, Stony Brook Medicine, Stony Brook, NY, United States

### Introduction

Over the last 20 years, digital pathology has rapidly grown and evolved alongside advances in immunology, cancer therapeutics, computer science, and artificial intelligence. During this time, there has been increased adoption of digital slide scanners in clinical settings and greater availability of digital whole-slide images (WSIs) of glass slides for a wide spectrum of clinical and research applications. Digital pathology, deep learning, and computational pathology have emerged as powerful and valuable tools in the era of precision medicine and immunotherapy. These technologies and computational methods can revolutionize the clinical subspecialty of pathology, which serves as the central hub of medicine and oncology by providing definitive tissue-based diagnoses that guide patient management and treatment selection in cancer.

Computational image analysis of digital pathology images generates data that are referred to as Pathomics, which refers to the automated quantification of the wide spectrum of phenotypic features of tissues and cells in WSIs. Basic clinically relevant and interpretable Pathomics features include (1) characterization of phenotypically distinct architectural regions such as cancer, benign, and stromal connective tissue, (2) shapes and textures of nuclei, (3) classifications of cell type, and (4) characterization of lymphocytic infiltration. Recently, digital pathology tools have been developed that can quantify and characterize nuanced interactions between cancer and the immune system in digital WSIs of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained tissue samples from patients, motivated by the guidelines of the International Immuno-Oncology Biomarkers Working Group [1–4].

Image analysis has been employed to study digital pathology images for many years, as described in excellent review articles of algorithmic methods [5–9]. Recently, deep learning and artificial intelligence in Pathomics has led to the development of computational methods that augment and empower qualitative traditional diagnostic histopathologic evaluation, which can substantially accelerate cancer research. Emerging digital pathology and deep learning applications can (1) stratify patient management through data-driven insights into cancer, (2) identify relevant biomarkers to predict clinical outcomes and treatment response, and (3) enhance our collective understanding of cancer biology to motivate the utilization of novel therapeutic approaches [5–8,10–41]. This chapter introduces and describes a selected set of novel Pathomics-based deep learning methods that have been developed to classify and reproducibly quantify the interplay between tumor cells and the immune response in the tumor microenvironment.

### Immune surveillance and immunotherapy

The human immune system is typically described as the sum of the innate and adaptive immune systems, which function together to maintain a state of continuous surveillance through several different types and subtypes of immune cells. These diverse cells are responsible for eliminating the vast majority of infectious agents, aberrant cells with acquired genetic and epigenetic changes, and cancer cells through the recognition and processing of foreign antigens. The cells of the innate immune system have pattern recognition receptors that recognize non-self antigens which generate immediate, short-lived, and nonspecific inflammatory responses, whereas the cells of the adaptive immune response generate highly specific inflammatory responses through antibody-mediated and cell-dependent responses to provide long-lasting defense with the capacity for memory [17,23,25,27].

The innate and adaptive immune systems are hierarchical regulatory mechanisms that are coordinated through many different types of immune cells. These cells are typically divided into broader classes and cell lineages that commonly include B-cells, T-cells (TH1, TH2, TH17, TREG, γδ T-cells, cytotoxic T-cells, and NK/T-cells), neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, mast cells, and NK cells [34,35,42–44]. The major categories of cell types that exist in the tumor microenvironment (TME) include cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells that kill non-self and cancer cells; CD4+ helper T-cells that suppress or regulate immune responses through cytokine signaling; CD4+ regulatory T-cells (CD25+/FOXP3+ Treg) that play an important role in tolerance; natural killer cells (NK cells) that kill cells that are missing self MHC-I markers without requiring cytokine stimulation; and macrophages and dendritic cells that have phagocytic and antigen presenting functions [45,46]. To put the diversity and heterogeneity of the immune landscape into perspective, there are over 100 different populations of immune cell types in the peripheral blood of humans that are characterized by the variable expression of cell surface and intracellular markers across the continuum of various activation and differentiation states [47–50].

The dynamic complexity of the immune system and its ability to respond to diverse stimuli and fight cancer cells is highly unpredictable due to intrinsic differences in the pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cell populations that vary widely across individuals [17,28,29,51–53].
