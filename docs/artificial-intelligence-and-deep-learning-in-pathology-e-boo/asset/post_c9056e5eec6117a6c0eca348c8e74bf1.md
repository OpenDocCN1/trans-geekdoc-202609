Segmentation of nuclei in tissue images proved to be challenging due to (1) variations in nuclear size and morphology, (2) nuclear overlap, (3) complex and irregular visual aspects of histologic structures, and (4) variations and irregularity of H&E staining and image acquisition. Irshad et al. [6] and Gurcan et al. [5] provide excellent surveys of the state of the art as it existed approximately 5–10 years ago in nuclear segmentation and detection. Image processing algorithms of various levels of sophistication were also employed, but the utilization of supervised algorithms was rare. Irshad et al. [6] specifically stated in their review article in 2014 that, “To the best of our knowledge, only few supervised machine learning techniques like Bayesian ... SVM (Support Vector Machine) ... and AdaBoost are used for nuclei segmentation.”

In the past few years since then, deep learning methods have played a rapidly increasing role in both nuclear segmentation and detection (for early examples, see Refs. [114–116]). Janowczyk et al. [21] articulated the advantages of deep learning approaches in an excellent manner by stating, “in order to develop a nuclear segmentation algorithm, one must first understand all of the possible variances in morphology, texture, and color appearances. Subsequently, an algorithmic scheme needs to be developed which can account for as many of these variances as possible while not being too general as to result in false positive results or too narrow as to result in false negative errors. This process can become quite unwieldy as it is often infeasible to view all of the outlier cases a priori, and thus an extensive iterative trial and error approach needs to be undertaken.” Winners of the recent MICCAI nuclear segmentation challenges have also utilized deep learning methods [117]. Since the success of deep learning algorithms tends to hinge on training data adequacy, generation of comprehensive training datasets remains crucial and challenging. This is especially challenging due to the wide variability in the appearance of nuclei that can differ between tissue types and can sometimes be difficult to morphologically distinguish and classify in cancer, which is further complicated by the variations in appearance that are due to tissue preparation. A promising approach to reduce the requirements for generating training datasets employs generative adversarial networks (GANs) [118] to generate realistic synthetic nuclei that can be used to enlarge and generalize training sets [119,120].

Early work in lymphocyte detection and characterization efforts also explored the development of a clinical decision support (CDS) system to discriminate among three types of lymphoproliferative disorders and normal blood cells [121], along with lymphocyte detection and centroblast quantification in follicular lymphoma [122]. Currently, algorithms have been developed that can detect and classify lymphocytes as well as other types of cells, which allows for the detailed characterization of the interrelationships between tumor, tumor-associated stroma, and lymphocytes [123]. Corredor et al. recently presented a set of metrics that captures density and spatial co-localization of TILs and tumor cells that were linked to likelihood of early-state NSCLC recurrence [123]. Similarly, Heindl et al. captured metrics of immune cell abundance and spatial heterogeneity that were computed for patients with ER + breast cancer [124] based on a pipeline that segmented and classified nuclei as lymphocytes, cancer, or other types of nuclei to perform analyses in postmenopausal patients with ER + breast cancer treated with tamoxifen or anastrozole over 5 years. The prognostic significance of immune scores was then compared with Oncotype DX 21-gene recurrence score (RS), PAM50 risk of recurrence (ROR) score, IHC4 score, and clinical treatment scores.

The development of methods that classify tissue regions has been facilitated by the rapid development of convolutional neural network (CNN) methods and publicly available software. Recently, a deep learning–based patch classification workflow was utilized to characterize patterns of lymphocytes in WSIs by using a CNN to classify image patches as lymphocyte-positive (i.e., containing lymphocytes) or lymphocyte-negative in combination with a CNN to segment necrotic regions in order to reduce false lymphocyte-positive classifications [41]. This method leveraged a convolutional autoencoder (CAE) that was designed to encode nuclei in image patches into sparse feature maps that encode both the location and appearance of nuclei [105]. A supervised CNN took unsupervised encoded features from the unsupervised CAE for classification. An iterative training effort was carried out with 100 × 100 pixel image patches (equivalent to a resolution of 50 × 50µm patches at 200× magnification) for 5200 images from 13 tumor types using TCGA data.

The approach taken was to progress through tumor types and (1) annotate lymphocyte infiltrated image regions and regions not infiltrated by lymphocytes, (2) train by using all available annotations to generate predictions, and (3) visually assess the predictions while extracting samples of images from regions where the predictions were inaccurate until adequate accuracy was obtained for the given tumor type. After the end of this process, a separate testing dataset was generated across tumor types to evaluate results. Recently, Abousamra et al. utilized all of the publicly available set of annotations generated in this method to train two standard CNN networks, VGG 16 and Inception v4 to generate predictions in the same 5200 TCGA specimens [125]. The accuracy that was obtained by using the standard models on the aggregated training set is modestly better than that reported by using the CAE model on the incrementally generated training set [41,125].

A variety of other efforts have targeted characterization of region types in specific tumor types. In breast cancer, a fully convolutional network was utilized to carry out pixel level semantic segmentation to identify tumor, stroma, necrosis, and TILs [10]. These training data were gathered through a structured annotation gathering, where the annotation curation process was called “structured crowdsourcing” through participants with varying degrees of expertise who contributed training data. Amgad et al. [11] have also described pixel-level semantic segmentation of tumor, stroma, and necrosis regions linked with the detection of tumor, stroma, and lymphocytic nuclei. This approach also employs a fully convolutional network approach that combines region and nucleus segmentation masks to classify pixels into 12 classes, where each class encodes a combination of region and nucleus membership information. The framework enforces biological constraints, e.g., it does not allow cell classifications within necrotic regions or stromal cells within non-stromal regions.

Other current work that parallels these types of efforts include the combination of automated tumor detection to facilitate the interpretation of the spatial patterns of distribution of TILs within a relevant histologic landscape in order to identify intratumoral and peritumoral TILs. Le et al. have recently developed methods to classify 350 × 350 pixel image patches (equivalent to a resolution of 85 × 85μm patches at 400× magnification) of invasive breast cancer in WSIs that are combined with existing lymphocyte detection method [41,126]. Figs. 10.1 and 10.2 are examples of tumor-TIL maps that can be generated from H&E WSIs with a combination of deep learning methods to provide immediate insight into the spatial distribution of intratumoral and peritumoral TILs in breast cancer and identify whether a specific tumor is “hot” or “cold” with respect to the pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory immunologic state of the TME [127].
