The development of methods that classify tissue regions has been facilitated by the rapid development of convolutional neural network (CNN) methods and publicly available software. Recently, a deep learning–based patch classification workflow was utilized to characterize patterns of lymphocytes in WSIs by using a CNN to classify image patches as lymphocyte-positive (i.e., containing lymphocytes) or lymphocyte-negative in combination with a CNN to segment necrotic regions in order to reduce false lymphocyte-positive classifications [41]. This method leveraged a convolutional autoencoder (CAE) that was designed to encode nuclei in image patches into sparse feature maps that encode both the location and appearance of nuclei [105]. A supervised CNN took unsupervised encoded features from the unsupervised CAE for classification. An iterative training effort was carried out with 100 × 100 pixel image patches (equivalent to a resolution of 50 × 50µm patches at 200× magnification) for 5200 images from 13 tumor types using TCGA data.

The approach taken was to progress through tumor types and (1) annotate lymphocyte infiltrated image regions and regions not infiltrated by lymphocytes, (2) train by using all available annotations to generate predictions, and (3) visually assess the predictions while extracting samples of images from regions where the predictions were inaccurate until adequate accuracy was obtained for the given tumor type. After the end of this process, a separate testing dataset was generated across tumor types to evaluate results. Recently, Abousamra et al. utilized all of the publicly available set of annotations generated in this method to train two standard CNN networks, VGG 16 and Inception v4 to generate predictions in the same 5200 TCGA specimens [125]. The accuracy that was obtained by using the standard models on the aggregated training set is modestly better than that reported by using the CAE model on the incrementally generated training set [41,125].

A variety of other efforts have targeted characterization of region types in specific tumor types. In breast cancer, a fully convolutional network was utilized to carry out pixel level semantic segmentation to identify tumor, stroma, necrosis, and TILs [10]. These training data were gathered through a structured annotation gathering, where the annotation curation process was called “structured crowdsourcing” through participants with varying degrees of expertise who contributed training data. Amgad et al. [11] have also described pixel-level semantic segmentation of tumor, stroma, and necrosis regions linked with the detection of tumor, stroma, and lymphocytic nuclei. This approach also employs a fully convolutional network approach that combines region and nucleus segmentation masks to classify pixels into 12 classes, where each class encodes a combination of region and nucleus membership information. The framework enforces biological constraints, e.g., it does not allow cell classifications within necrotic regions or stromal cells within non-stromal regions.
