It might seem trivial to compute this coefficient of determination (\( R^2 \) by merely squaring the R). But, simply squaring R mistakenly ignores that these two parameters deal with different data. \( R^2 \) applies to predicted \( \hat{y} \) values, whereas R applies to the actual y data.

In practice, a linear regression line may represent data poorly, having an indignant \( R^2 \), indicating no statistical relationship. However, a higher \( R^2 \) might be found using a non-linear model.

A poor \( R^2 \) may not be entirely due to a sub-optimal regression model. Data quality and relevance is also a factor to consider. Anomalistic data values, what we call *outliers*, can skew a regression model's results. It may also be that the data was imported from an incorrect or corrupt repository. Or, the data may have been sampled using insufficient randomness. (More is said about the last point in the *Resampling* chapter.)

# 20.5 MULTIPLE LINEAR REGRESSION

Consider a regression model of multiple independent variables, such as:

y = \(\beta_0 + \beta_1x1 + \beta_2x2 + \dots + \beta_NxN + \epsilon\)

Each beta coefficient represents the change in the \( y_i \) for a given predictor \( x_i \), holding all the other predictors constant. That is, we generate these pair-wise.
