A bi-implication, also called a biconditional statement, involves two if-then-else statements.

If \( p \rightarrow q \) and \( q \rightarrow p \) (the converse) are both true, then \( p \leftrightarrow q \) is true.

For example, the statement:

*This dog barks if and only if denied a bone.*

The two implications are:

+   - If denied a bone, then the dog barks.
+   - If the dog barks, then a bone was denied.

Given that \( p \leftrightarrow q \) is equivalent to \( (p \rightarrow q) \vee (q \rightarrow p) \), the truth table associated with bi-implication is:

| p | q | p → q | q → p | p ↔ q |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

To negate a proposition is to use its complement. For example, the negation of: The dog barks is The dog does not bark.

This may be symbolically denoted as:

```
\(\neg p\)
```
