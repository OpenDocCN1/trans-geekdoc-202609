experiment. That is, a sample space $S$ has the distinct outcomes:

{ $s_1 s_2 \dots s_n$ }

When all possible individual outcomes are equally probable, the sample space is an *equi-probable space*.

An event is a subset of a *sample space*. An event can consist of an individual outcome from the sample space or a collection of outcomes. Hence, we use set notation and set operations when computing probabilities involving subsets of the sample space, as seen in the coverage below of *mutually exclusive events*, *independent events*, *joint probability*, and *conditional probability*.

For example, suppose we are rolling a 6-sided die and we are interested in the probability of obtaining an even number. The sample space is $S = \{ 1 \ 2 \ 3 \ 4 \ 5 \ 6 \}$ and the event we are interested in is $E = \{ 2 \ 4 \ 6 \}$. If we roll the die and do get an even number, then event $E$ has occurred.

Another example: Find the probability of obtaining exactly two heads among three tosses of a coin. The sample space is:

$S = \{ TTT \ TTH \ THT \ HTT \ HHT \ HTH \ THH \ HHH \}$

Note that assuming a fair coin is used, each of the eight outcomes is equally likely, with a probability of $\frac{1}{8}$.

The event of obtaining exactly two heads is the subset:

$E = \{ HHT \ HTH \ THH \}$
