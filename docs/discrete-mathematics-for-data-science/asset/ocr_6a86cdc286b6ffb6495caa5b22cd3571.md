A binary relation \( R \) on \( S \) is any subset of the ordered pairs in the Cartesian product. (All ordered pairs in the Cartesian product make up a *full relation*.)

For example, given set \( S = \{ a \ b \ c \} \), a binary relation \( R \) on \( S \) is any subset of the ordered pairs in the Cartesian product. Consider:

\( S \times S = \{ (a, a) (a, b) (a, c) (b, a) (b, b) (b, c) (c, a) (c, b) (c, c) \} \)

One relation between two sets \( A \) and \( B \) is the Cartesian product of \( A \) and \( B \). The cardinality is N x M, where N is the number of elements in set \( A \), and M is the number of elements in set \( B \). Another relation on \( S \) above could be \( R = \{ (a, b) (b, c) (a, c) \} \). \( R \) is a relation on \( S \) since these pairs exist in the full relation. It can be said that element \( a \) is related to element \( b \) by \( R \).

Note that the relation \( R \) is, itself, a set.

Conversely, there is no relation on \( S \) for \( Q = \{ (a, b) (b, c) (c, d) \} \). The pair \( (c, d) \) does not exist in the full relation.

Besides binary pairings such as \( (i, j) \), a *set relation* may consist of other ordered tuples, such as ternary \( (i, j, k) \) for three sets, and n-ary \( (i, j, k, l ...)\) for larger numbers of sets.

As with predicate logic, pair order matters in relations. For example, given that \( A = \{ a \ b \ c \} \) and \( B = \{ c \ b \ a \} \), we can say \( A = B \). However, \( (a, b) \neq (b, a) \). This is because \( a \neq b \) and \( b \neq a \).

Consider that a parent-child pair is only valid for a certain order.

\( R(parent, child) \neq R(child, parent) \)
