\( x_{wtd} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i} \)

where \( x_i \) represents each data value and \( w_i \) represents the weight of that data value.

When finding an arithmetic mean of, for example, 3, 5 and 8, the result of \( \frac{16}{3} \) assumes that each data value has an equal influence on the result. In other words, the values are *equally weighted*. But suppose that the values 3 and 5 have twice as much influence as the 8. We are then dealing with a weighted mean. We can assign weights in a ratio such as 2:2:1 to the values 3, 5, and 8 respectively, and apply the weighted mean formula as follows:

\( x_{wtd} = \frac{x \cdot x_{wt} + y \cdot y_{wt} + z \cdot z_{wt}}{total \ weight} \)

\( x_{wtd} = \frac{3 \cdot 2 + 5 \cdot 2 + 8 \cdot 1}{2 + 2 + 1} = \frac{6 + 10 + 8}{5} = \frac{24}{5} = 4.8 \)

The choice of weights is flexible, as long as the weights are in the desired ratio. A convenient choice of weights in the above example would be 0.4, 0.4, and 0.2 respectively, because the sum of the weights in the denominator would be 1. Weights with a sum of 1 are in fact percentages.
