
predictors. VIFs range from 1 (no correlation) on up, with values between 4 and 10 being suspect and values greater than 10 generally indicative of multicollinearity.

Using the independent data fields, we find the VIF for every field \( i \) relative to every other field \( j \). The VIF calculation entails first obtaining the coefficient of determination \( R^2_{ij} \) between the two fields. Then, the VIF calculation is:

\( VIF_{ij} = 1/(1 - R^2_{ij}) \)

Despite a low VIF and low correlations between independent variables, multicollinearity may still be present in the form of joint dependencies. Testing for such requires the generation of synthetic data fields in the form of all pairs of dependent and joint-independent sets. That is, given a model of three independent variables:

\( y = x_1 + x_2 + x_3 \)

Based on the above model, a VIF would be computed for the following distinct pairs of independent variables:

\( (x_1, x_2), (x_1, x_3), (x_2, x_3) \)

With several data sets representing independent variables, one can manually remove factors and then retest the regression model. However, a large number of data sets may make this impractical, as when processing Big Data. Thus, it may be advisable to programmatically test randomly sampled fields while ensuring that the number of records always exceeds the number of fields.

## 23.3 INVESTMENT PORTFOLIO EXAMPLE

Consider a case of stock market investing, where we want to build a diversified investment portfolio. How many stocks are enough for the portfolio to diversify away the risk oriented to any one investment security (firm) in the portfolio? Such firm-oriented risk is referred to as non-systematic or microeconomic risk. In this example we ignore risks that are not specific to any one firm, such as those of the general macroeconomy, also known as systematic risk.

Systematic risk is easily corroborated by a statistical evaluation of the portfolio's stock price data, as a large number of such data sets inevitably reveal multicollinearity. Multicollinearity in investment portfolio data indicates some portfolio holdings are redundant and, thus, do not reduce portfolio risk. Therefore, a highly diversified portfolio should be able to reduce its holdings without incurring additional risk.

To avoid risk, an investor might purchase a large number of corporate securities, perhaps hundreds. However, such a strategy is of limited benefit. Apart from the endeavor taking much time for due diligence and incurring many transaction fees, the benefits of diversification diminish after a certain number of stocks. Additionally, macroeconomic risks cannot be avoided by diversification using stocks alone.

Because the macroeconomy influences all stocks, stock prices generally exhibit the same tendencies, going up and down in tandem to varying degrees in various periods of time. Hence, the old investing adage: “A rising tide raises all ships.”

A famous paper published in 1952 by Harry Markowitz, titled _Portfolio Selection_, suggested that the optimal number of stocks for a portfolio is approximately twenty.³ That finding has been corroborated by other studies since then, concluding that the optimal number is roughly the same.

### Key implications for investment portfolios:

- Beyond a certain number of stocks, there is no reduction in portfolio volatility.
- We can eliminate certain holdings without increasing the portfolio's overall risk.
- A random selection of twenty stocks should give about the same return as the general market.
- A selection of twenty stocks out-performs (or under-performs) the general market if it includes at least one non-correlating stock that out-performs (under-performs) the general market.

³H. Markowitz, “Portfolio selection,” The Journal of Finance, vol. 7, no. 1, pp. 77–91, Mar. 1952.

## 23.4 SAMPLE DENSITY

The strength of a predictive model may depend on the data of its respective features being statistically distinguishable. This is achieved by adding features (dimensions) while maintaining _sample density_. Sample density refers to the distribution of data in a _feature space_ of some dimension.<sup>4, 5</sup>

Sample density is associated with better model performance. Sample sparsity is associated with worse model performance. As dimensions increase among features of inconsistent records, sample density diminishes.

To see how two data sets can appear indistinguishable, consider the following time-series data for two exchange-traded financial instruments, the S & P 500 stock fund having the symbol SPY, and the S & P US Treasury Bond fund having the symbol TLT.

Suppose that at some snapshot in time, the rate of change for these two symbols happens to be the same. If we have no other attributes to assess for these symbols at that time, then such a comparison may be misleading or not useful. As seen in Figure 23.1, the only dimension of the data is the rate of change.

Still, with two dimensions there may be some patterns that are not significantly separable. For example, the two fund symbols may, coincidentally, have the same average parameters for some period of time, such as one quarter of a year. The extra addition of features may remedy that situation. For example, with corporate stocks, possible additional features includes data for trade volume, dividends, quarterly revenue, and related data transformations, such as moving averages.

Of course, by increasing the number of data dimensions, we potentially decrease the density of the feature space, as previously mentioned, given that not all features are represented by sampled data.

For a feature space of three dimensions, sample density may be computed as:

```
sample density = (s_{min}/x_{n_1}) \cdot (s_{min}/x_{n_2}) \cdot (s_{min}/x_{n_3})
```

where x_n is the available number of samples per independent variable x (these may differ) and s_min is the minimum number of available samples from all independent variables, x.

Over d dimensions, the sample density can be computed as:

```
sample density = \prod_{i=1}^{d} \frac{s_{min}}{x_{n_i}}
```

where \(x_{n_i}\) is the available number of data samples in the \(i^{th}\) independent variable, \(x_i\).

Over \(d\) dimensions, the least possible sample density can be computed as:

\(sample\ density = (s_{min}/s_{max})^d\)

where \(s_{min}\) is the minimum number of available samples from all independent variables and \(s_{max}\) is the maximum number of available samples from all independent variables. This worst case scenario is equivalent to the most possible sample sparsity. Based on that scenario, Figure 23.2 shows how sample density decreases as dimensions increase.
