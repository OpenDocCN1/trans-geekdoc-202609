The sample covariance is often denoted as $s_{xy}$, $cov_{xy}$, or $cov(x, y)$.

Using the covariance and the standard deviations of both sets, we can
express the sample correlation coefficient as:

$r_{xy} = \frac{s_{xy}}{s_x s_y}$

For population data, the symbolic expression is:

$\rho = \frac{\sigma_{xy}}{\sigma_x \sigma_y}$

As can be seen, both covariance and correlation convey a statistical
relationship between two variables. So, why not just use covariance and
forgo the extra computation for correlation? Conceptually, covariance
measures how much two variables generally move in the same or
opposite direction. Correlation takes this a step further by dividing the
covariance by the product of the standard deviations of the two sets,
effectively normalizing the measure to a range of -1 to +1. This is
important when computing a covariance for two non-standardized data
sets that have different units. Hence, correlation may be referred to as
*normalized covariance*.

However, if we were to first standardize the respective data sets (see
standardization in the *Probability & Statistics* chapter), then the
computation for covariance yields the same result as that for
correlation, making the latter computation unnecessary.
