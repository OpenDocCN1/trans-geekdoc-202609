## In brief

Recent methods applying deep learning to model neural activity often rely on "black-box" approaches that lack an interpretable connection between neural activity and network parameters. Here, building on advances in interpretable AI, Toloosham et al. propose a new method, deconvolutional unrolled neural learning (DUNL), and apply it to seven datasets.

## Highlights

- DUNL is a method for designing interpretable AI algorithms to analyze neural data
- It deconvolves neural data into a sparse set of interpretable, local components
- It performs single-trial analysis across brain areas and recording modalities
- It is scalable with dataset size in both trial-based and naturalistic settings

# SUMMARY

The widespread adoption of deep learning to neural activity relies on "black-box" approaches that lack an interpretable connection between neural activity and network parameters. We propose using algorithm unrolling, a method for interpretable deep learning, to design the architecture of deconvolutional neural networks and obtain a direct interpretation of neural weights in stimulus-driven single-neuron activity through a generative model. We introduce deconvolutional neural network (DUNL), and demonstrate its versatility by applying it to deconvolve single-trial signals across multiple areas and recording modalities. We uncovered multiple salience and prediction error signals in dopamine neurons in the somatosensory thalamus and characterize the heterogeneity of neural responses in the motor cortex and striatum under unstructured, naturalistic experiments. Our work leverages advantages in interpretable deep learning to provide a mechanistic understanding of neural activity.

# INTRODUCTION

Understanding neural activity at both the single-neuron and population levels, and their relationship to behavior and environmental factors, is a key goal in neuroscience. Recent advances allow for long recordings of neurons during complex tasks. When neural signals vary over time or subsets of neurons exhibit complex codes with varying levels of selectivity—for example, neurons in the visual hierarchy select tasks (5-9) and neuromodulatory pathways, such as dopamine neurons, respond to various environmental and internal stimuli.

To understand how single neurons support multiple representations, scalable methods are needed to deconvolve neuronal activity and non-overlapping components that capture heterogeneity in the population. We present a deep learning-based deconvolution method to address this.

A reasonable deconvolution method should first handle single data inputs without requiring trials or assemblies. It should work for both natural and artificial tasks, with minimal or no trial length (11-12).

Second, the method should relate to the signal source, whether spike count or point signals such as calcium levels measured via fluorescent channels.

Third, the method should employ expressive mappings from layers to capture neural data complexity.

Fourth, it should be interpretable (supplemental discussion), addressed here in two contexts: (1) within the network, where architecture, weights, and representations are linked to the optimization process and a generative model, (2) providing a mapping between stimuli (internal or external) and latent variables. For instance, a probabilistic model adaptable to various data types can capture neural response logic and temporal sparsity, through neural impulses. This interpretability relies on domain knowledge, which structure data or hypotheses. The method should be scalable and practical for analyzing large-scale neuroscience datasets.

Most statistical neural data methods are primarily designed for dimensionality reduction to find a low-rank structure that governs the neural data, which makes them suitable for deconvolution. Examples include principal-component analysis (PCA)$^{25-27}$, non-negative matrix factorization (NMF)$^{28-30}$, sparse component analysis (SCA)$^{31}$, v-MIF$^{32}$, and ICA deconvolution methods which recover low-rank structures with the data methods. Generalized linear model (GLM)$^{36}$ and point processes for neural sequences$^{37}$ point out data with low-rank structures and offer interpretability by separating data activity from noise. However, none of these methods fully meet all of the discussed requirements, such as scalability or adherence to the statistics of the signal.

The limitations of methods and the scalability of deep learning have motivated the exploration of approaches for neural data. Prior work has many desiderata—extracting high-dimensional latent spaces from neural data using non-deep architectures$^{38}$. However, these methods lack a direct link between latent variables and neural counts, making deconvolution difficult due to the "black-box" nature of such models$^{39-41}$.

Here, we propose a novel framework, deconvolutional neural learning (DUNL), combining convolutional sparse coding (dictionary learning), which provides a flexible framework to deconvolve single-trial activity into interpretable, local components. This is enabled by the use of sparse coding as our generative model. To showcase DUNL's versatility, we applied it to five experiments. First, we decoded stable and reward prediction (RPE) events from multiplexed signals. Second, we deconvolved outcomes from associative learning. Third, we performed simulations of decoding and prediction in the thalamus. Fourth, we extracted neural responses from the piriform with low SNR conditions. Fifth, we characterized dopamine activity in the striatum during a realistic experiment. Finally, we evaluated DUNL, highlighting its strengths: local interpretability, scalability, and generalization in data-limited scenarios.

# RESULTS

## Sparse deconvolution learns structures in single-trial activity

We aim to decompose single-trial activity into pulse-like responses, to recurring events, by identifying low-rank structures in neural data. Observed neural activity is represented as convolution of sparse codes and a dictionary with varying shapes and magnitudes. Our goal is to reconstruct data by learning these kernels through optimization. We want to account for the sparsity of spikes by modeling the mean activity through a passed function like the nonparametric exponential (Gaussian, Binomial, or Poisson) (see Table S1) for spiking activity and Gaussian with mean $\mu$.

Neural activity $y_t$ is modeled as the convolution of kernels $h_k$ and sparse codes $x_{ki}$, with a baseline activity $b_t$ (Figure 1B). Deconvolution identifies local temporal patterns and discovers low-rank structures (but can only capture long-term features). Kernel learning goes beyond the pre-defined methods by learning them automatically. The learned kernels and sparse codes are interpretable: kernels represent shared characteristics across trials, and sparse codes represent individual responses.

Indices indicate time events associated with kernel $k$, with amplitudes $x_{ki}$ representing neural response strength. In functional system identification, this method captures cause-effect relationships: sparse codes represent the timing of the effect, while kernels represent the response strength. However, the kernels in the model are non-parametric and fully learned from data, without adhering to a user-parametric form, and codes are sparse. We learn the kernels and $x_{ki}$ by minimizing the loss $\sum (y - \hat{y})^2$, regularized by desired properties in the kernels and codes. Specifically, we use a sparsity prior to promote a few activations in time, and optionally, a second-order covariance structure to capture dependencies among kernels (e.g., discouraging simulating).
