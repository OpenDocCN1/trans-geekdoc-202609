After a while, these small improvements add up and NAG ends up being significantly faster than regular Momentum optimization. Moreover, note that when the momentum pushes the weights across a valley, $\nabla_t$ continues to push further across the valley, while $\nabla_t$ pushes back towards the bottom of the valley. This helps reduce oscillations and thus converges faster.

NAG will almost always speed up training compared to regular Momentum optimization. To use it, simply set nesterov=True when creating the SGD optimizer:

```
optimizer = keras.optimizers.SGD(lr=0.001, momentum=0.9, nesterov=True)
```

# AdaGrad

Consider the elongated bowl problem again: Gradient Descent starts by quickly going down the steepest slope, then slowly goes down the bottom of the valley. It would be nice if the algorithm could detect this early on correct its direction to point a bit more toward the global optimum.

The AdaGrad algorithm$^{14}$ achieves this by scaling down the gradient vector along the steepest dimensions (see Equation 11-6):

Equation 11-6. AdaGrad algorithm

1. $s \leftarrow s + \nabla_0/(\theta) \otimes \nabla_0/(\theta)$
2. $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla_0/(\theta) \odot \sqrt{s} + \epsilon$

> $14$ "Adaptive Subgradient Methods for Online Learning and Stochastic Optimization," J. Duchi et al. (2011).

The first step accumulates the square of the gradients into the vector s (recall that the ⊗ symbol represents the element-wise multiplication). This vectorized form is equivalent to computing $s \leftarrow s + (\partial / \partial \theta)^2$ for each element $s$, of the vector $s$; in other words, $s$ accumulates the squares of the partial derivative of the cost function with regards to parameter $\theta$. If the cost function is steep along the $i^{th}$ dimension, $s$ will get larger and larger at each iteration.

The second step is almost identical to Gradient Descent, but with one big difference: the gradient vector is scaled by a factor of $\sqrt{s + \epsilon}$ (the ∕ symbol represents the element-wise division, and $\epsilon$ is a smoothing term to avoid division by zero, typically set to $10^{-10}$). This vectorized form is equivalent to computing $\theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - \eta (\partial / \partial \theta_i) / \sqrt{s_i + \epsilon}$ (simultaneously).

In short, this algorithm decays the learning rate, but it does so for steeper dimensions than for gentler slopes. This is an adaptive learning rate. It helps point the resulting updates more directly toward the global optimum (see Figure 11-7). One additional benefit is that it requires much less tuning of the learning rate hyperparameters.

![](img/0684d2da98e09085c0a622d4fd69aaad_373_0.png)

Figure 11-7. AdaGrad versus Gradient Descent

AdaGrad performs well for simple quadratic problems, but unfortunately it often stops too early when training neural networks. The learning rate gets scaled so much that the algorithm ends up stopping entirely before reaching the global optimum. Even though Keras has an AdaGrad optimizer, you should not use it to train deep neural networks (it may be efficient for simpler tasks such as Linear Regression, though). However, understanding Adagrad is helpful to grasp the other adaptive learning rate optimizers.

# RMSProp

Although AdaGrad slows down too fast and ends up never converging to the global optimum, the RMSProp algorithm¹⁵ fixes this by accumulating only the gradients from the most recent iterations (as opposed to all gradients since the beginning of training). It does so by using exponential decay in the first step (see Equation 11-7).

Equation 11-7. RMSProp algorithm

1. $s \leftarrow \beta s + (1 - \beta) \nabla_\ell(\theta) \otimes \nabla_\ell(\theta)$
2. $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\frac{\nabla_\ell(\theta)}{\sqrt{s} + \epsilon}$

The decay rate is typically set to 0.9. It is even a new hyperparameter, but this default works well, so you might not need to tune it all.

```
optimizer = keras.optimizers.RMSProp(lr=0.001, rho=0.9)
```

Except on very simple problems, this optimizer almost always performs much better than AdaGrad. In fact, it was the preferred optimization algorithm of many researchers until Adam came around.

# Adam and Nadam Optimization

Adam,¹⁶ stands for adaptive moment estimation, combines the ideas of Momentum optimization and RMSProp: like Momentum optimization it keeps track of an exponentially decaying average of past gradients, and just like RMSProp keeps track of an exponentially decaying average of past squared gradients (see Equation 11-8).¹⁷

> 15 This algorithm was created by Geoffrey Hinton and Tijmen Tieleman in 2012, and presented by Geoffrey Hinton in his Coursera class on neural networks (slides: https://homl.info/57; video: https://homl.info/58). Amusingly, since the authors did not write a paper to describe it, researchers often cite "slide 29 in lecture 6" in their papers.

> 16 "Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization," D. Kingma, J. Ba (2015).

> 17 These are estimations of the mean and (uncentered) variance of the gradients. The mean is often called the first moment, while the variance is often called the second moment, hence the name of the algorithm.

# Equation 11-8. Adam algorithm

1. $m \leftarrow \beta_1 m - (1 - \beta_1) \nabla_\theta(\theta)$
2. $s \leftarrow \beta_2 s + (1 - \beta_2) \nabla_\theta(\theta) \otimes \nabla_\theta(\theta)$
3. $m \leftarrow \frac{m}{1-\beta_1^t}$
4. $\hat{s} \leftarrow \frac{s}{1-\beta_2^t}$
5. $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \frac{m}{\sqrt{\hat{s}} + \epsilon}$

- $t$ represents the iteration number (starting at 1).
If you look at steps 1, 2, and 5, you will notice Adam's close similarity to both Momentum optimization and RMSProp. The only difference is that step 1 computes an exponentially decaying average rather than an exponentially decaying sum, but these are actually equivalent for a constant factor (the decaying average is $1-\beta$, times the decaying sum). Steps 3 and 4 are somewhat of a technical detail: since $m$ and $s$ are initialized at 0, they will be biased towards 0 at the beginning of training, so these two steps will help $m$ and $s$ at the beginning of training.

The decay hyperparameter $\beta_1$ is typically initialized to 0.9, while the decay hyperparameter $\beta_2$ is often initialized to 0.999. As earlier, the smoothing term is usually initialized to a tiny number such as $10^{-7}$. These are the default values for the *Adam* class (to be precise, defaults to None, which tells Keras to use `keras.backend.epsilon()`, which defaults to $10^{-7}$; you can change it using `keras.backend.epsilon()`).

```
optimizer = keras.optimizers.Adam(lr=0.001, beta_1=0.9, beta_2=0.999)
```

Since Adam is an adaptive learning rate algorithm (like AdaGrad and RMSProp), it requires less tuning of the learning rate hyperparameter $\eta$. You can often use the default value $\eta = 0.001$, making Adam even easier to use Gradient Descent.

If you are starting to feel overwhelmed by all these different techniques, and wondering how to choose the right ones for your task, don't worry: some practical guidelines are provided at the end of this chapter.

Finally, two variants of Adam are worth mentioning:

- Adam, introduced in the same paper as Adam: notice that in step 2 of Equation 11-8, Adam accumulates the gradients in s (with a greater weight for more recent iterations). In step 5, if we ignore e and steps 3 and 4 (which are technical details anyway), Adam just scales down updates by the square root of s. In short, Adam replaces the l1 norm with the l2 norm (a fancy way of saying the method). Specifically, it replaces step 2 in 11-8 with s ← max(β₂, ∇(θ)), it drops step 4, and in step 5 it scales the gradient updates by s, which is just the weight of the time-decayed gradients. In practice, this can make Adam more stable than Adam, but this really depends on the dataset, and in general actually performs better. It’s just one more optimizer you can try.
- Nadam optimization¹ is more important: it is Adam optimization plus the Nesterov trick, which will often converge faster than Adam. In his report, Timothy Dozat compares many different optimizers on various tasks, and finds that Nadam generally outperforms Adam, which is sometimes outperformed by RMSProp. Adaptive optimization methods (including RMSProp, Adam and Nadam optimization) are often great, converging to a good solution. However, a 2017 paper¹ by Ashia C. Wilson al showed that they can lead to solutions that generalize poorly on some datasets. When you are disappointed by your model's performance, try using Nesterov Accelerated Gradient descent instead: your dataset might be allergic to adaptive gradients. Also check out the latest research, which is moving fast (e.g., AdaBound).
