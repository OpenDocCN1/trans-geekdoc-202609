# # The Architecture of the Visual Cortex

David H. Hubel and Torsten Wiesel performed a series of experiments on cats in 1958' and 1959 (and a few years later on monkeys), getting crucial insights into the structure of the visual cortex (the authors received the Nobel Prize in Physiology and Medicine in 1981 for their work). In particular, they showed that many neurons in the visual cortex have a small local receptive field, meaning they react to visual stimuli in a limited region of the visual field (see Figure 14-1, the receptive fields of five neurons are represented by dashed circles). The receptive fields of different neurons may overlap, and together tile the whole visual field. Moreover, others showed that some neurons react only to horizontal lines, while others react to lines with different orientations (two neurons have the same receptive field but react to different orientations). They also noticed that some neurons have larger receptive fields, and react to more complex patterns that are combinations of the lower-level patterns. These observations led to the idea that the higher-level neurons are based on the outputs of neighboring lower-level neurons (in Figure 14-1, notice that each neuron is connected only to a few neurons from the previous layer). This powerful architecture is able to detect all sorts of complex patterns in any area of the visual field.
