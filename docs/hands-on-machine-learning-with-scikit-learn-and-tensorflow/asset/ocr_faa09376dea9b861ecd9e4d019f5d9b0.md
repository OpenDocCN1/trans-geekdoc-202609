+   • Adam, introduced in the same paper as Adam: notice that in step 2 of Equation 11-8, Adam accumulates the squares of the gradients in s (with a greater weight for more recent weights). In step 5, if we ignore e and steps 3 and 4 (which are technical details anyway), Adam just scales down the parameter updates by the square root of s. In short, Adam scales updates by the l2 norm of the time-decayed gradients (recall that the l2 norm is the square root of the sum of squares). Adam replaces the l1 norm with the l2 norm (a fancy way of saying the method). Specifically, it replaces step 2 in Equation 11-8 with s ← max(β₂, ∇(θ)), it drops step 4, and in step 5 it scales the gradient updates by a factor of s, which is just the weight of the time-decayed gradients. In practice, this can make Adam more stable than Adam, but this really depends on the dataset, and in general actually performs better. It’s just one more optimizer you can try if you experience problems with Adam on some task.
• Nadam optimization¹ is more important: it is Adam optimization plus the Nesterov trick, which will often converge slightly faster than Adam. In his report, Timothy Dozat compares many different optimizers on various tasks, and finds that Nadam generally outperforms Adam, which is sometimes outperformed by RMSProp.

Adaptive optimization methods (including RMSProp, Adam and Nadam optimization) are often great, converging to a good solution. However, a 2017 paper¹ by Ashia C. Wilson al showed that they can lead to solutions that generalize poorly on some datasets. When you are disappointed by your model's performance, try using Nesterov Accelerated Gradient descent instead: your dataset might be allergic to adaptive gradients. Also check out the latest research, which is moving fast (e.g., AdaBound).
