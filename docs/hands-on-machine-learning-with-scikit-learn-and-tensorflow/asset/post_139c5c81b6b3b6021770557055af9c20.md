
ers.schedules, then pass this learning rate to each optimizer. This approach updates the learning rate at each step rather than at each epoch. For example, here is how to implement the same exponential schedule as earlier:

```
s = 20 * len(X_train) // 32 # number of steps in 20 epochs (batch size = 32)
learning_rate = keras.optimizers.s.ExponentialDecay(0.01, s, 0.1)
optimizer = keras.optimizers.SGD(learning_rate)
```

This is nice and simple, when you save the model, the learning rate and its schedule (including its state) got saved as well. However, this is not part of the Keras API, it is specific to tf.keras.

To sum up, exponential decay or performance scheduling can considerably speed up convergence, so give them a try!

## Overfitting Through Regularization

With four parameters I can fit an elephant and with five I can make him wiggle his trunk.

> —John Neumann, cited from Enrico Fermi in Nature 427

With thousands of parameters you can fit the whole zoo. Deep neural networks have tens of thousands of parameters, sometimes even millions. With so many parameters, the network has an incredible amount of freedom and can fit a huge variety of complex datasets. We need regularization.

We already implemented one of the best regularization techniques in Chapter 10: early stopping. Moreover, even though Batch Normalization was designed to solve the vanishing/exploding gradients problem, it also acts like a pretty regularizer. In this section we will present other popular regularization techniques for neural networks: $l_1$ and $l_2$ regularization, dropout and max-norm regularization.

## $l_1$ and $l_2$ Regularization

Just like you did in Chapter 4 for simple linear models, you can use $l_1$ and $l_2$ regularization to constrain a neural network's weights (typically not its biases). Here is how to apply $l_2$ regularization to a Keras layer's connection weights, using a regularization factor of 0.01:

```
layer = keras.layers.Dense(activation="elu",
  kernel_initializer="he_normal",
  kernel_regularizer=keras.regularizers.l2(0.01))
```

The l2() function returns a regularizer that will be called to compute the regularization loss, at each step during training. This regularization loss is added to the final loss. As you might expect, you can use keras.regularizers.l1() if you want $\ell1$ regularization, and if you want both $\ell1$ and $\ell2$ regularization, use `keras.regularizers.l1_l2` (specifying both regularization factors).

Since you will typically apply the same regularization to all layers in your network, well as the same activation function and the initialization strategy in all hidden layers, you may find yourself repeating the same arguments over and over. This makes it ugly and error-prone. To avoid this, you can try refactoring your code to use loops. Another option is to use Python's `functools.partial()`: it lets you create a wrapper for any callable, with some default values. For example:

```python
from functools import partial

RegularizedDense = partial(keras.layers.Dense,
    activation="elu",
    kernel_initializer="he_normal",
    kernel_regularizer=keras.regularizers.l2(0.01))

model = keras.models.Sequential([
    keras.layers.Flatten(input_shape=[28, 28]),
    RegularizedDense(100),
    RegularizedDense(10, activation="softmax", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform")
])
```

## Dropout

Dropout is one of the most popular regularization techniques for deep neural networks. It was proposed by Geoffrey Hinton in 2012 and further detailed in a paper by Srivastava et al., and has proven to be highly successful: state-of-the-art neural networks got a 2% accuracy boost by adding dropout. It is a fairly simple algorithm: at training, every neuron (including input neurons but excluding output neurons) has a probability $p$ of being "dropped out", meaning it would be entirely ignored during this step, but may be active during the next step. The hyperparameter $p$ is called the dropout rate, and it is typically set to 50%. After training, neurons get dropped anymore. And that's all (except for a technical detail we will discuss momentarily).

> ¹Improving neural networks by preventing co-adaptation, G. Hinton et al. (2012).
> ²Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting, N. Srivastava et al. (2014).

![](img/0684d2da98e09085c0a622d4fd69aaad_383_0.png)
Figure 1. Dropout regularization

It is quite surprising at first that this technique works at all. Would a company perform better if its employees were told to toss a coin every morning to decide whether or not to work? Well, who knows; perhaps! The company would obviously be forced to adapt; it could not rely on any single person to fill in any other critical tasks, so expertise would have to be spread across several people. Employees would have to learn to cooperate with many of their coworkers, not just a handful of them. The company would become much more resilient. If one quit, it wouldn't make much of a difference. It's unclear whether this idea would actually work for companies, but it does. Neurons trained with dropout cannot adapt with their neighbors; they have to be as useful as possible on their own. They cannot rely excessively on just a few inputs; they must pay attention to changes in their inputs. In the end you get a more robust network that generalizes better.

Another way to understand the power of dropout is to realize that a unique network is generated at each step. Since each neuron can be either present or absent, there is a total of $2^N$ possible networks (where $N$ is the total number of dropout neurons). This is such a huge number that it is virtually impossible for the single neural network to be sampled twice. Once you run 10,000 training steps, you have essentially trained 10,000 different neural networks (each with just one instance). These networks are obviously not independent since they share many of their weights, but they are nevertheless all different. The resulting network can be seen as an averaging ensemble of all these neural networks.

There is one small but important technical detail. Suppose $p=50\%$, in which case during training a neuron will be connected to half as many inputs as it was. To compensate for this fact, we need to multiply each neuron's input connection weights by 0.5 after training. If we don't, each neuron will get a total input signal roughly twice as large as what the network was trained on, and it is unlikely to perform well. Generally, we multiply each input connection by the keep probability $(1 - p)$ after training. Alternatively, we can divide each neuron's output by the keep probability during training (these alternatives are not perfectly equivalent, but they work well enough).

To implement dropout using keras, you can use the keras.Dropout layer. During training, it randomly drops some inputs (setting them to 0) and divides the remaining neurons by the keep probability. After training, it does nothing at all, it just passes the inputs to the next layer. For example, the following code implements dropout regularization before every Dense layer, with a dropout rate of 0.2:

```python
model = keras.models.Sequential([
    keras.layers.Flatten(input_shape=[28, 28]),
    keras.layers.Dropout(rate=0.2),
    keras.layers.Dense(300, activation="elu", kernel_initializer="he_normal"),
    keras.layers.Dropout(rate=0.2),
    keras.layers.Dense(100, activation="elu", kernel_initializer="he_normal"),
    keras.layers.Dropout(rate=0.2),
    keras.layers.Dense(10, activation="softmax")
])
```

![](img/0684d2da98e09085c0a622d4fd69aaad_384_0.png)

If dropout is only active during training, the training loss is penalized for the validation loss, so comparing the two can be misleading. In particular, a model may be overfitting the training set and have many validation losses. Make sure to evaluate the training loss without dropout (e.g., after training). Alternatively, you can call the fit() method inside a with keras.backend.learning_phase_scope() block: this will force dropout to be active during both training and validation.

If you observe that the model is overfitting, you can increase the dropout rate. Conversely, you should try decreasing the dropout rate if the model underfits the training set. It can also help to increase dropout for large layers, and reduce it for small ones. Moreover, many state-of-the-art architectures use dropout after the last layer, so you may want to try this if full dropout is too strong.

Dropout does tend to significantly slow convergence, but it usually results in a much better model when tuned properly. So, it is generally worth the extra time and effort.
