### 3.18 Hypothesis testing of means, proportions, variances and correlations:

#### 1. Testing Means:

*   **Objective:** Determine if there is a significant difference between the sample mean and a hypothesized population mean.
*   **Example:** A data scientist wants to test whether a new feature significantly improves the average user engagement time on a website. The null hypothesis (`H0`) might be that the new feature has no effect (`μ=μ0`), and the alternative hypothesis (`H1`) is that the new feature increases engagement (`μ>μ0`). The `t-test` or `z-test` could be employed for hypothesis testing.

#### 2. Testing Proportions:

*   **Objective:** Assess whether a sample proportion significantly differs from a hypothesized population proportion.
*   **Example:** Suppose a data scientist is examining the click-through rate of two different ad campaigns. The null hypothesis (`H0`) might state that the proportion of clicks for both campaigns is equal, while the alternative hypothesis (`H1`) suggests a difference. A hypothesis test for proportions, like the `z-test` for proportions, could be utilized.

#### 3. Testing Variances:

*   **Objective:** Determine if there is a significant difference in the variances between two samples or between a sample and a known population variance.
*   **Example:** In a data science context, one might be interested in comparing the variability of scores from two different tests. The null hypothesis (`H0`) could state that the variances are equal `![](image_rsrc9T1.jpg)`, and the alternative hypothesis (`H1`) would propose a difference. The `F-test` is often used for comparing variances.

#### 4. Testing Correlations:

*   **Objective:** Assess whether the correlation between two variables is significantly different from zero or from a specific value.
*   **Example:** A data scientist might want to determine if there is a significant correlation between the amount of time users spend on a website and the number of products they purchase. The null hypothesis (`H0`) could be that the correlation (`ρ`) is zero, indicating no relationship, while the alternative hypothesis (`H1`) suggests a non-zero correlation. The `t-test` or Fisher's transformation can be used for hypothesis testing of correlations.

**Justification:**

*   Hypothesis testing provides a structured approach to make statistical inferences and decisions based on data.
*   Mathematical foundations, such as `t-tests`, `z-tests`, `F-tests`, and correlation tests, enable quantifiable assessments of the evidence against or in favor of a hypothesis.
*   Data scientists use hypothesis testing to validate assumptions, compare groups, and draw conclusions from data, forming a critical component of statistical analysis in the field.

By employing hypothesis testing in data science, practitioners can make informed decisions, validate models, and draw reliable insights from datasets with a solid mathematical foundation.
