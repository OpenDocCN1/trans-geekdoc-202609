
# Hyperplanes

Hyperplanes may have different properties. For instance, a hyperplane of an *n*-dimensional [affine space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_space) is a [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_\(geometry\)) [subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subset) with dimension *n* – 1 and it separates the space into two [half spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-space_\(geometry\)). While a hyperplane of an *n*-dimensional [projective space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_space) does not have this property.

The difference in dimension between a subspace *S* and its ambient space *X* is known as the [codimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codimension) of *S* with respect to *X*. Therefore, a [necessary and sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition) for *S* to be a hyperplane in *X* is for *S* to have codimension one in *X*.

In [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry), a hyperplane of an [*n*-dimensional space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-dimensional_space) *V* is a subspace of dimension *n* − 1, or equivalently, of [codimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codimension) 1 in *V*. The space *V* may be a [Euclidean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_space) or more generally an [affine space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_space), or a [vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space) or a [projective space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_space), and the notion of hyperplane varies correspondingly since the definition of subspace differs in these settings; in all cases however, any hyperplane can be given in [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate) as the solution of a single (due to the "codimension 1" constraint) [algebraic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_equation) of degree 1.

If *V* is a vector space, one distinguishes "vector hyperplanes" (which are [linear subspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_subspace), and therefore must pass through the origin) and "affine hyperplanes" (which need not pass through the origin; they can be obtained by [translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_\(geometry\)) of a vector hyperplane). A hyperplane in a Euclidean space separates that space into two [half spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-space_\(geometry\)), and defines a [reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_\(mathematics\)) that fixes the hyperplane and interchanges those two half spaces.

## Special types of hyperplanes

Several specific types of hyperplanes are defined with properties that are well suited for particular purposes. Some of these specializations are described here.

### Affine hyperplanes

An **affine hyperplane** is an [affine subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_space) of [codimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codimension) 1 in an [affine space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_space). In [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates), such a hyperplane can be described with a single [linear equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_equation) of the following form (where at least one of the *a*<sub>*i*</sub>s is non-zero and *b* is an arbitrary constant):

```
a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + ... + aₙxₙ = b
```

In the case of a real affine space, in other words when the coordinates are real numbers, this affine space separates the space into two half-spaces, which are the [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_\(topology\)) of the [complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_\(set_theory\)) of the hyperplane, and are given by the [inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_\(mathematics\))

```
a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + ... + aₙxₙ < b
```
```
a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + ... + aₙxₙ > b
```

As an example, a point is a hyperplane in 1-dimensional space, a line is a hyperplane in 2-dimensional space, and a plane is a hyperplane in 3-dimensional space. A line in 3-dimensional space is not a hyperplane, and does not separate the space into two parts (the complement of such a line is connected).

Any hyperplane of a Euclidean space has exactly two unit normal vectors.

Affine hyperplanes are used to define decision boundaries in many [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning) algorithms such as linear-combination (oblique) [decision trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_tree_learning), and [perceptrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceptron).

### Vector hyperplanes

In a vector space, a vector hyperplane is a [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_subspace) of codimension 1, only possibly shifted from the origin by a vector, in which case it is referred to as a [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_\(geometry\)). Such a hyperplane is the solution of a single [linear equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_equation).

### Projective hyperplanes

Projective hyperplanes are used in [projective geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry). A [projective subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry#Projective_subspace) is a set of points with the property that for any two points of the set, all the points on the line determined by the two points are contained in the set. Projective geometry can be viewed as [affine geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_geometry) with [vanishing points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanishing_point) (points at infinity) added. An affine hyperplane together with the associated points at infinity forms a projective hyperplane. One special case of a projective hyperplane is the infinite or ideal hyperplane, which is defined with the set of all points at infinity.

In projective space, a hyperplane does not divide the space into two parts; rather, it takes two hyperplanes to separate points and divide up the space. The reason for this is that the space essentially "wraps around" so that both sides of a lone hyperplane are connected to each other.

## Applications

*   In [convex geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_geometry), two [disjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjoint_sets) [convex sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_set) in *n*-dimensional Euclidean space are separated by a hyperplane, a result called the [hyperplane separation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperplane_separation_theorem).
*   In [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning), hyperplanes are a key tool to create [support vector machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support_vector_machine) for such tasks as [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision) and [natural language processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing).
*   The datapoint and its predicted value via a linear model is a hyperplane.

## 3.10 Half-Planes

A half-plane is a planar region consisting of all points on one side of an infinite straight line, and no points on the other side. If the points on the line are included, then it is called a closed half-plane.

The plane is divided into two semi-planes: the upper semi-plane, and the lower semi-plane.

### Upper Half-Plane

The upper semi or half-plane is that part of the plane that consists of the points that lie in the 1st and 2nd quadrants of the plane. In the upper half of the plane, the value of the *y*-coordinate will always remain positive. The name upper half/semi-plane was suggested by the mathematician Poincare, also known as Poincare half-plane.

The lower semi or half-plane is that part of the plane that consists of the points that lie in the 3rd and 4th quadrants of the plane. So, in the lower half of the plane, the value of the *y*-coordinate will always remain negative.
