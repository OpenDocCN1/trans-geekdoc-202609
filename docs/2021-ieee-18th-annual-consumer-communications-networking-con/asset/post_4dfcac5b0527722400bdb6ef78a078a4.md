
## Label Leakage from Gradients in Distributed Machine Learning

Aidmar Wainakh, Till Müßig*, Tim Grube, Max Mühlhäuser
Telecooperation Lab, Technical University of Darmstadt, Darmstadt, Germany
{wainakh, grube, max}@tk.tu-darmstadt.de, *till.muessig@stud.tu-darmstadt.de

Abstract—Empowered by the high connectivity of manifold devices in today’s world, distributed machine learning enables multiple, distributed users to build a joint model by sharing their gradients over a network. In this paper, we highlight the privacy risk of sharing gradients by proposing LLG, an algorithm to disclose the labels of the users’ training data from their shared gradients. We conduct an empirical analysis on two datasets to demonstrate the validity of our algorithm. Results show that our approach effectively extracts the labels with high accuracy in different scenarios.

Index Terms—Distributed machine learning, privacy, leakage from gradients

## I. INTRODUCTION AND MOTIVATION

The emergence of the Internet enables many services to ease and enrich everyone’s life, e.g., online shopping and social networking. Many of the service providers employ Machine Learning (ML) models to further improve their services, e.g., to enable personalization of kinds. These models are mostly trained in a centralized fashion, where user data is collected and processed in central servers. These developments and rising awareness of potential threats led to many user concerns about the usage of their personal data and, consequently, about their privacy. In this light, companies, e.g., Google, introduced distributed ML settings like the emerging Federated Learning (FL) [5]. This movement is also facilitated by current advancements of fog and edge computing.

FL enables service providers and users to utilize the power of ML without collecting personal information in central places. As such, users cooperate to train the ML model in a distributed way. The users’ individual models are then collected and aggregated to establish the joint model with similar properties as the traditional, centralized-trained ML model. This lifts some pressure from privacy concerns as the personal information will never leave the users’ devices. However, to aggregate the individual users’ models, they share their model’s gradients, i.e., they share model updates. Sharing gradients was believed to be secure and private as gradients were thought to not reveal information about the users’ training datasets [7].

Recently, Zhu et al. [7] have proposed an attack, Deep Leakage from Gradients (DLG), that reconstructs the training data and labels from the shared gradients. In their attack, they consider the classification task, where the label refers to the class of the input data, which can be highly sensitive information, e.g., in case of a model for cancer classification. However, DLG is not able to reliably extract the ground-truth

labels [6]. Zhao et al. [6] have suggested Improved Deep Leakage from Gradients (iDLG), a simple approach to extract the ground-truth label based on the sign of the gradients w.r.t. the last-layer weights of a neural network. Although their approach is reliable and detects the correct label with 100% accuracy, it works only for gradients generated from a single data sample. This limitation renders the proposed approach inapplicable in real-world scenarios, where the gradients are typically shared after training the model on a batch of samples.

In this paper, we propose Label Leakage from Gradients (LLG), an algorithm to extract the ground-truth labels from shared gradients. Unlike iDLG [6], LLG is designed to consider gradients generated by training the model with a batch of samples. Our algorithm is based on two properties of the values of the last layer gradients that reveal relationships between labels and the resulting values of gradients. We derive these properties through an empirical analysis of gradients and their changes over the course of the model training. We evaluate our algorithm on MNIST and CIFAR-100 datasets. First results show that LLG can extract the ground-truth labels with very high accuracy.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we present background and related work on information leakage from gradients. We elaborate on our approach in Section III. Then, in Section IV we present intermediate results of our analysis. Finally, we conclude the paper and introduce directions for future work in Section V.

## II. BACKGROUND & RELATED WORK

Training a neural network consists of optimizing the weights of the network W to minimize the loss function l. The gradients ∇W are the derivatives of the loss function l w.r.t. the weights W. The gradients ∇W represent the direction and magnitude that is used to update the weights in the correct direction.

Zhu et al. [7] propose an attack that uses shared gradients in distributed learning to reconstruct the training data. The authors generate dummy input data and output labels, then attempt to optimize them to generate dummy gradients that match the shared ones. By that, the dummy data and labels will converge to the real data and labels used by the participants in the training process. However, it is observed that their method to generate the correct labels is not optimal [6].

Zhao et al. [6] have addressed this issue by proposing an analytical approach to extract the ground-truth labels from the shared gradients. The authors demonstrated that the gradients
of classification (cross-entropy) loss w.r.t. the last layer weights have negative values for the correct labels. Thus, detecting the negative gradients is sufficient to extract correct labels. However, their approach works only in the case of gradients generated from one sample. Meaning, the batch used in the training by the participants must contain one sole sample. Using a batch with the size of one is highly unlikely scenario in real-world applications. Participants typically are expected to have multiple samples, and they would train the model on these samples (a batch of them at least) before sharing the gradients with other parties. In our work, we consider this issue and propose an algorithm that works with arbitrary batch sizes.

## III. METHODOLOGY

In this section, we present our problem setting, then elaborate on two gradient properties upon which we base our algorithm. Derived from these properties, we define two parameters, impact and offset. Then, we describe our algorithm LLG to extract the ground-truth labels from shared gradients.

### A. Problem Setting

We consider a distributed ML scenario, where a participant trains a neural network for one iteration on a batch of their data samples (in particular, we assume that the batch contains more than one data sample). Then, the participant shares the gradients resulted from the training process with other parties, e.g., the service provider. We assume that the neural network model consists of L layers and is trained with cross-entropy loss over one-hot labels for a classification task.

For our algorithm, we consider the gradients ∇WL wrt. the last-layer weights WL (between the output layer and the layer before). The gradient vector ∇WiL represents gradients connected to the label i on the output layer. We note gi to refer to the sum of the elements of ∇WiL.

gi = 1/|T| · ∇WiL

For the sake of simplicity, we refer to gi as the gradient of label i in the rest of the paper.

### B. Gradient Properties

We derive two properties of gradients through empirical analysis. In Section IV, we present additional evidence to back these properties.

- Property 1: A negative value of the gradient gi indicates that label i appeared in the batch of training data.
- Our empirical analysis suggests that the appearance of a negative gradient corresponds to a label in the batch of training data.
- Property 2: The magnitude of the gradient gi is an indicator for the number of occurrences of label i in the batch of training data.
- In our analysis, we found indicators that the value of the gradient correlates with the number of occurrences. For example, a single appearance of label i in the batch of training data results in a gradient value of −x whereas the two occurrences of label j result in a gradient value of −2x.

### C. Impact and Offset Parameters

Driven by our observations, we define the impact m as the change of the gradient value caused by a single occurrence of a label in a batch. This value is always negative (see Property 1). As the change on gradients values is the same regardless the corresponding label, we assume that impact m is label-agnostic.

We also noticed that in some cases the values of the gradients are shifted by an unknown positive value. We call this value offset s. Based on our observations, we presume that the offset s occurs as a result of misclassification penalty during the weight optimization process. Meaning, if a sample of label i was misclassified as label j, the optimization algorithm adds a penalty (i.e., a positive value) to the gradients of label j. As this offset value might vary from label to another, we consider it a label-specific value.

As summarized in Eq. (2), the number of occurrences ci multiplied by the impact m and corrected by the offset si acts as an estimator for the value of the gradient of label i.

ci * m + si ≈ gi

From Eq. (2), it follows easily that the number of occurrences ci of label i can be derived from the parameters m, si, and gi. Next, we discuss our proposals to estimate the impact m and offset si.

### D. Parameter Estimation

We present two scenarios that enable us to estimate both the impact m and offset si.

- Shared gradients In this scenario, the adversary has access to the shared gradients. As mentioned earlier, the impact refers to the change in the value of the gradients corresponding to one occurrence for a label. Thus, the mean of the changes seems to be a good estimation. Based on Property 1, we know that all negative gradients are indicating existing labels in the batch of training data. Therefore, we propose to derive the estimated impact as the sum of the gradients with negative values divided by the batch size b. However, empirically, we noticed that multiplying by a factor of (1+1/n) improves the accuracy of extracting labels, where n is the total number of labels.

m = ∑i;gi<0 (gi) / b * (1 + 1/n)

In this scenario, the offset si cannot be estimated and is neglected. Our results show that the accuracy is still sufficiently high to perform a successful attack.

- Auxiliary knowledge In the second scenario, the adversary has access to the shared gradients and a sample from the dataset that contains all the labels. With this sample, the adversary can train the model and analyze the resulting gradient values to estimate the impact m and offset si through multiple local runs.
