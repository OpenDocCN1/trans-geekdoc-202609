However, their approach works only in the case of gradients generated from one sample. Meaning, the batch used in the training by the participants must contain one sole sample. Using a batch with the size of one is highly unlikely scenario in real-world applications. Participants typically are expected to have multiple samples, and they would train the model on these samples (a batch of them at least) before sharing the gradients with other parties. In our work, we consider this issue and propose an algorithm that works with arbitrary batch sizes.

# III. METHODOLOGY

In this section, we present our problem setting, then elaborate on two gradient properties upon which we base our algorithm. Derived from these properties, we define two parameters, impact and offset. Then, we describe our algorithm LLG to extract the ground-truth labels from shared gradients.

# A. Problem Setting

We consider a distributed ML scenario, where a participant trains a neural network for one iteration on a batch of their data samples (in particular, we assume that the batch contains more than one data sample). Then, the participant shares the gradients resulted from the training process with other parties, e.g., the service provider. We assume that the neural network model consists of L layers and is trained with cross-entropy loss over one-hot labels for a classification task.

For our algorithm, we consider the gradients ∇WL wrt. the last-layer weights WL (between the output layer and the layer before). The gradient vector ∇WiL represents gradients connected to the label i on the output layer. We note gi to refer to the sum of the elements of ∇WiL.

gi = 1T · ∇WiL

For the sake of simplicity, we refer to gi as the gradient of label i in the rest of the paper.

# B. Gradient Properties

We derive two properties of gradients through empirical analysis. In Section IV, we present additional evidence to back these properties.

+   Property 1: A negative value of the gradient gi indicates that label i appeared in the batch of training data.
+   
+   Our empirical analysis suggests that the appearance of a negative gradient corresponds to a label in the batch of training data.

+   Property 2: The magnitude of the gradient gi is an indicator for the number of occurrences of label i in the batch of training data.
+   
+   In our analysis, we found indicators that the value of the gradient correlates with the number of occurrences. For example, a single appearance of label i in the batch of training data results in a gradient value of −x whereas the two occurrences of label j result in a gradient value of −2x.

# C. Impact and Offset Parameters

Driven by our observations, we define the impact m as the change of the gradient value caused by a single occurrence of a label in a batch. This value is always negative (see Property 1). As the change on gradients values is the same regardless the corresponding label, we assume that impact m is label-agnostic.

We also noticed that in some cases the values of the gradients are shifted by an unknown positive value. We call this value offset s. Based on our observations, we presume that the offset s occurs as a result of misclassification penalty during the weight optimization process. Meaning, if a sample of label i was misclassified as label j, the optimization algorithm adds a penalty (i.e., a positive value) to the gradients of label j. As this offset value might vary from label to another, we consider it a label-specific value.

As summarized in Eq. (2), the number of occurrences ci multiplied by the impact m and corrected by the offset si acts as an estimator for the value of the gradient of label i.

ci * m + si ≈ gi

From Eq. (2), it follows easily that the number of occurrences ci of label i can be derived from the parameters m, si, and gi. Next, we discuss our proposals to estimate the impact m and offset si.

# D. Parameter Estimation

We present two scenarios that enable us to estimate both the impact m and offset si.

• Shared gradients In this scenario, the adversary has access to the shared gradients. As mentioned earlier, the impact refers to the change in the value of the gradients corresponding to one occurrence for a label. Thus, the mean of the changes seems to be a good estimation. Based on Property 1, we know that all negative gradients are indicating existing labels in the batch of training data. Therefore, we propose to derive the estimated impact as the sum of the gradients with negative values divided by the batch size b. However, empirically, we noticed that multiplying by a factor of (1+1/n) improves the accuracy of extracting labels, where n is the total number of labels.

m = ∑i;gi<0 (gi) / b * (1 + 1/n)

In this scenario, the offset si cannot be estimated and is neglected. Our results show that the accuracy is still sufficiently high to perform a successful attack.

• Auxiliary knowledge In the second scenario, the adversary has access to the shared gradients and a sample from the dataset that contains all the labels. With this sample, the adversary can train the model and analyze the resulting gradient values to estimate the impact m and offset si through multiple local runs.
