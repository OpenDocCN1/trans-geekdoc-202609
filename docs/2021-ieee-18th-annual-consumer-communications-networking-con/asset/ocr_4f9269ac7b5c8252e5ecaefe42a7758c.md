# Label Leakage from Gradients in Distributed Machine Learning

Aidmar Wainakh, Till Müßig*, Tim Grube, Max Mühlhäuser
Telecooperation Lab, Technical University of Darmstadt, Darmstadt, Germany
{wainakh, grube, max}@tk.tu-darmstadt.de, *till.muessig@stud.tu-darmstadt.de

Abstract—Empowered by the high connectivity of manifold devices in today’s world, distributed machine learning enables multiple, distributed users to build a joint model by sharing their gradients over a network. In this paper, we highlight the privacy risk of sharing gradients by proposing LLG, an algorithm to disclose the labels of the users’ training data from their shared gradients. We conduct an empirical analysis on two datasets to demonstrate the validity of our algorithm. Results show that our approach effectively extracts the labels with high accuracy in different scenarios.

Index Terms—Distributed machine learning, privacy, leakage from gradients

# I. INTRODUCTION AND MOTIVATION

The emergence of the Internet enables many services to ease and enrich everyone’s life, e.g., online shopping and social networking. Many of the service providers employ Machine Learning (ML) models to further improve their services, e.g., to enable personalization of kinds. These models are mostly trained in a centralized fashion, where user data is collected and processed in central servers. These developments and rising awareness of potential threats led to many user concerns about the usage of their personal data and, consequently, about their privacy. In this light, companies, e.g., Google, introduced distributed ML settings like the emerging Federated Learning (FL) [5]. This movement is also facilitated by current advancements of fog and edge computing.

FL enables service providers and users to utilize the power of ML without collecting personal information in central places. As such, users cooperate to train the ML model in a distributed way. The users’ individual models are then collected and aggregated to establish the joint model with similar properties as the traditional, centralized-trained ML model. This lifts some pressure from privacy concerns as the personal information will never leave the users’ devices. However, to aggregate the individual users’ models, they share their model’s gradients, i.e., they share model updates. Sharing gradients was believed to be secure and private as gradients were thought to not reveal information about the users’ training datasets [7].

Recently, Zhou et al. [7] have proposed an attack, Deep Leakage from Gradients (DLG), that reconstructs the training data and labels from the shared gradients. In their attack, they consider the classification task, where the label refers to the class of the input data, which can be highly sensitive information, e.g., in case of a model for cancer classification. However, DLG is not able to reliably extract the ground-truth

labels [6]. Zhao et al. [6] have suggested Improved Deep Leakage from Gradients (iDLG), a simple approach to extract the ground-truth label based on the sign of the gradients w.r.t. the last-layer weights of a neural network. Although their approach is reliable and detects the correct label with 100% accuracy, it works only for gradients generated from a single data sample. This limitation renders the proposed approach inapplicable in real-world scenarios, where the gradients are typically shared after training the model on a batch of samples.

In this paper, we propose Label Leakage from Gradients (LLG), an algorithm to extract the ground-truth labels from shared gradients. Unlike iDLG [6], LLG is designed to consider gradients generated by training the model with a batch of samples. Our algorithm is based on two properties of the values of the last layer gradients that reveal relationships between labels and the resulting values of gradients. We derive these properties through an empirical analysis of gradients and their changes over the course of the model training. We evaluate our algorithm on MNIST and CIFAR-100 datasets. First results show that LLG can extract the ground-truth labels with very high accuracy.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we present background and related work on information leakage from gradients. We elaborate on our approach in Section III. Then, in Section IV we present intermediate results of our analysis. Finally, we conclude the paper and introduce directions for future work in Section V.
