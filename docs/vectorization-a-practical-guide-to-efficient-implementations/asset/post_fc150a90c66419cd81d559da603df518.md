### 11.1 Language Modeling with Transformer

Prior to the introduction of the transformer architecture [Vaswani et al., 2017], deep learning-based natural language modeling has been relying on previously introduced sequence representation architectures such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs). However, these architectures have a limitation: When learning from a long sequence of text/documents, they tend to remember only the most recently encountered tokens and forget those encountered earlier (e.g. the beginning of a paragraph). Deep learning researchers assess an architecture’s capability to learn from input signals using a metric called **receptive field** size, a concept borrowed from vision neuroscience. If a model can memorize and learn from a long sequence (in 1D sequence modeling) or a large area (in 2D/3D computer vision problems), then it is said to have a large receptive field. Having a limited receptive field creates problems for tasks like language translation or question-answering, where the text input (i.e. prompt) can be very long (e.g. hundreds of words). Just imagine you were being asked an exceedingly long question, but you can only remember the last few words being said. How could you understand the question and, therefore, give a response without being able to recall what is being asked at the beginning? Attention, specifically the scaled-dot-product self-attention that we discussed in Chapter 5 is proven to resolve this issue in the original transformer paper [Vaswani et al., 2017]. By performing pair-wise dot-product between input tokens, the model learns to focus on what to pay “attention” to (pun intended here~) during sequence modeling. The transformer model was introduced in the context of neural machine translation. However, later research has shown that the transformer model is especially powerful in learning long-sequence data. It has been demonstrated that the transformer architecture allows researchers to build state-of-the-art models in domains not only in natural language processing (NLP), but also in areas including but not limited to text-to-speech (e.g. FastSpeech2 [Ren et al., 2020]), speech-to-text (e.g. Whisper [Radford et al., 2022]), computer vision (e.g. ViT [Dosovitskiy et al., 2021] model that we discussed in Chapter 9), drug discovery (e.g. SMILES [Honda et al., 2019; Grechishnikova, 2021]), and recommendation engines (e.g. SASRec [Kang and McAuley, 2018]).

### 11.1.1 Encoder and Decoder of the Transformer Architecture

The original transformer uses an encoder–decoder architecture, where the encoder compresses the inputs, and then the decoder decompresses the hidden states to generate outputs specific to the task of translation. As a side note, a recent study has demonstrated that the transformer model is a powerful data compressor for representation learning [Yu et al., 2023], with the interesting slogan “Compression is all there is.” The simplest encoder–decoder architecture is the autoencoder, which we introduced in Chapter 8, where the encoder and decoder have the same architecture (i.e. a dense layer). Transformer instead uses a combination of attention, layer normalization, and feedforward layers to compress and decompress the data. The key differences between the encoder and the decoder blocks are:

- The encoder uses bidirectional attention. This means that past tokens can be used to predict future tokens, while future tokens can also be used to predict past tokens.

- The decoder uses masked or causal attention. Past tokens can be used to predict future tokens, but not vice versa. In Chapter 5, when we were discussing the scaled-dot-product attention mechanism, we have illustrated the two types of attention mechanism.

- Specific to the original transformer paper, the decoder block also receives inputs from the hidden state of the encoder layer with an additional attention layer.
