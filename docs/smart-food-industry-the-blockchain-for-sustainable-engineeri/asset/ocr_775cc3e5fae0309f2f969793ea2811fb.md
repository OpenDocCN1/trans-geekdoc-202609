# 2.3 Phase III: Utilization (Distribution)

The next phase is acquisition and distribution, where the food product is distributed to retailers and merchandised for consumers. Here, sustainable innovations include eco-packaging, edible packaging, and eco-labeling. While eco-packaging offers a better alternative to conventional packaging in fast-moving food at the selling point, consumers perceive it as less appealing, visually ineffective in protecting the content, and less hygienic (Magnier and Crié 2015). Edible packaging is still a lab-based innovation, which faces many challenges such as health, safety, and toxicological effects (Jeiawan et al. 2020). To add to the contamination effects that influence product desirability and intention (Gunta and Coskun 2021). Ecolabeling, on the other hand, discloses its own acceptance challenges among consumers. Although the labeling system has been initiated as a medium to address consumers' distrust in the food system (Tonkin et al. 2015), research has revealed a back-firing consequence and evidence of distrust in the control system and authenticity of food organic (Nutavasih and Thogersen 2017).

# 2.4 Phase III and IV: Utilization (Consumption) and Waste Management

The final two stages of food's life cycle are consumption and waste management, whose main environmental contribution is to limit food waste and excessive consumption. Innovation in these phases addresses consumers' behavior change and often requires a technological component such as digital mobile applications for leftover sharing, and restaurant. These innovations come with their own share of challenges. On one hand, the applications are not optimized for user experience and can be demeaning to use (Me et al. 2019). On the other hand, food sharing and leftovers in a consumer-to-consumer sharing, without any seal or third-party guarantees, rises doubts about the safety and quality of the food in question (Falcone and Imbert 2017).

Furthermore, getting consumers to adopt digital apps requires an amount of effort and commitment from consumers and complicates their food choices. It implies changing their habits and regularly making deliberate choices, which is cognitively tiring. Overall, sustainable food choices are surrounded by a behavioral cost, which limits its attractiveness for consumers.
