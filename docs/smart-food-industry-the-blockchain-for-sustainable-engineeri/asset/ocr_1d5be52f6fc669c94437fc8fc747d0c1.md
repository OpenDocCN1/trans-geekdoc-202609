Expressed on a daily basis, the HRD consists of 232 g of grains (wheat, corn and others), 50 g of tubers or starchy vegetables, 200–600 g of vegetables, 100–300 g of fruit and 0–500 g of dairy products. Protein sources can be 0–28 g red meat, 0–58 g poultry, 0–10 g fish and 0–13 g eggs, 0–50 g can be consumed as an alternative to red meat. A total of 50 g (weight) of legumes (lentils, beans and peas) and 25 g of soy is recommended. Total added fat is listed as 50 g, emphasizing the use of unsaturated plant oils. Finally, sugar and other sweeteners consumption is limited to 31 g (Willet et al. 2019).

Dietary patterns are likely to reduce the risk of major chronic diseases and improve health (Orlich et al. 2013, Satija et al. 2017, Schwingchack et al. 2017, Walting et al. 2022). Having a variety of foods is not only healthier but also supportive of stronger sustainability as it increases demand for diverse foods (Bene et al. 2019). Although the E-Lancet report provided the first evidence-based targets for HSDs received criticism because it did not assess cost or affordability (Schwingchack et al. 2020, Hirvenon et al. 2020). The study of Hirvenon et al. (2020) showed that HRDs are not affordable for the world’s low-income populations, sub-Saharan and South Africa in particular. The same study reported HRDs are on average 60% more expensive than the foods required for nutrient adequacy, due to the inclusion of greater quantities of animal-derived foods and vegetables.

To achieve the required transition to HSDs, everyone has a part to play. Environmental sustainability should be an essential part of dietary guidance while interacting with individuals or groups regarding their dietary choices and setting guidelines for the public The following are some of the diet-related practices that all consumers can execute when making food choices:

+   - Avoiding over-consumption beyond adequate and for a variety of nutrient-dense foods
- Limiting intake of ultra-processed (UP), nutrient-poor and over-packed food
- Consuming no more than recommended amounts of animal tissue
- Increasing the consumption of plant-based foods (Barbour et al. 2021, Ridout et al. 2021).

We have a formidable task in hand since shifting to healthy diets will require significant steps such as a 50% reduction in the global consumption of unhealthy foods (as red meat and sugar) and a 100% increase in the consumption of healthier ones (such as vegetables, fruits, legumes and nuts). Any deviation from these targets (although these differ from region to region) would compromise the accomplishment of SDGs (Willet et al. 2019).

# 2.2 Overconsumption

Adult obesity and overweight continue to rise at a similar rate in rich and developing countries. The prevalence rate from 11.8% in 2012 to 13.1% in 2016, and no country is out to track the increase in adult obesity (FAO own UNICEF WFP and WHO 2021, Global Nutrition Report 2021). Similarly, childhood overweight is rising (Global Nutrition Report 2021). The dilemma lies in the fact that the global food production is adequate to meet the energy requirements of everyone but put supply, the distribution is unequal. Some are undernourished due to the inability to access and afford food, and some become overweight or obese due to excess consumption (FAO UNICEF WFP and WHO 2021). Serafini and Tofil (2016) defined obesity as an “unsustainable metabolic food” (MFW), indicating that any food eaten beyond physiological needs should be considered waste due to the excess and undesirable body fat accumulation. The following study provided evidence of the substantial amount of food lost globally through overeating and its impact on the environment (Toti et al. 2019).
