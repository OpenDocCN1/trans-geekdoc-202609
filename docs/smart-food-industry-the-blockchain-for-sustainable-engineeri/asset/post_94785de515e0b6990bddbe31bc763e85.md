## 2.9 Augmented Reality (AR)

The concept of "augmented reality" AR refers to the use of digital resources overlaid on top of the current view of the real world (Long et al. 2017). Virtual and actual objects may be presented in three dimensions, as Wu et al. (2013) in their discussion of AR systems that combine the real and virtual worlds. The hardware for AR includes a sensor that gathers real-time images of the surroundings and a display that shows both the real and virtual worlds simultaneously. For example, eyeglasses, smartphones, tablets, and even head-up displays have all been able to make use of AR. The implementation of AR in industrial settings is referred to as Augmented Reality (AR). Even though AR systems have been increasingly prevalent, this is a relatively young subject, with just a few industries benefiting from its use. To adopt AR successfully in the industry, both organizational and technology are essential determinants (Masood and Enger 2019).

From sustainability, AR can assist in reducing inventory costs by strengthening warehouse management (Mourtis et al. 2019). This may be accomplished by lowering the mistake rate, enhancing flexibility, boosting dependability, speeding up operations, increasing staff safety, and increasing operator engagement (Rejeh et al. 2021a). AR, according to Wang et al. (2019), can contain a number of properties that allow for the construction of participatory and hedonic encounters, permitting customers to enhance their feelings while purchasing or eating food items. Additional benefits of employing AR in food logistics provide greater visibility of food chain operations, contamination risk prevention, empowerment of food marketing, similarity of food training, and improvement in food logistics (Luque et al. 2017).

Even though AR in food logistics has not been widely investigated, the technology offers several opportunities to the food industry, including worker safety, maintenance, training, quality control, design and layout, communication, location, language translation, and product expiration. Packing and storing food in a warehouse is a use of heavy equipment in the food business. AR may alert the operator to potential hazards by providing real-time feedback on how the machine is doing (Mourtzis et al. 2018).

AR can assist with the maintenance and repair of industrial equipment. AR can display pertinent photos, films, or highlight special places on the equipment, in addition to providing thorough instructions to assist with machinery maintenance. In maintenance activities, AR may employ employees and teach workers how to operate equipment or follow a particular procedure. AR can assist in identifying faults, damaged items, and products that do not meet quality criteria. For example, with the assistance of AR, it is possible to spot missing information on a label or broken packaging. Furthermore, during the development of new logistics industry, AR may assist in visualizing how the equipment will be installed in the production facility or warehouse, well as the floral movement and equipment use.

## 2.10 Blockchain Technology

Sensors deployed across the food production chain create a tremendous quantity of data. While this data may be viewed in real-time, they may be securely accessed by utilizing blockchain technology. Specifically, blockchain is a chronologically ordered chain of (data) blocks stored among decentralized or distributed nodes. In this distributed setting, transactions are verified by the nodes, which eliminates the need for a central authority, forming a new form of trust as digital trust (Rejeh et al. 2019, Meiblmaier 2018). With bitcoin and cryptocurrencies, it is now critically disrupting and transforming various industries and domains, including food logistics (Biswas and Gupta 2019, Rejeh et al. 2020). Since data cannot be manipulated with blockchain technology, businesses are racing to embrace it. In fact, blockchain is a secure and distributed authorized nodes can read and write, and the data is preserved and shared over a peer-to-peer system instead of a central system.

From sustainability perspectives, blockchain provides a number of benefits for smart food logistics, including enhanced traceability, shipping efficiency, transparency, and power tracking, fewer concerns stemming from theft of commodities, and speedier invoicing and payment processing. It can assist in mitigating the risks of logistics-related challenges, such as procurement, supply chain traceability, customers' coordination, and trade finance (Rejeh et al. 2020). The globalization of food supply chains has created various issues for food businesses in terms of securing and protecting food products and information sharing within the supply chain. Nevertheless, when businesses operate in a blockchain setting, they will be able to maintain transparency and visibility over food processes, instantly detect data provenance, and trace the physical route by the product (Tan et al. 2018).
