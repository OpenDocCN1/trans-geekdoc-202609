
# 3. Pseudocereals as Superfoods

Pseudocereals are dicotyledous crops with close similarities to the dominant cereal crops. Their 'superfoods' status is a relatively new development emerging from the recognition of their benefits to patients with nutrient deficiencies or health disorders. Amaranth, buckwheat, chia, quinoa orff are sorts of quality nutrients, complex carbohydrates, protein, and vitamins.

## 3.1 Nutrition

Pseudocereals are treated sources of calories and proteins in traditional communities where the major cereals often succumb to droughts, pests, and diseases. The nutrition of adults and children in indigenous communities is based on the most abundant pseudocereals, or minor grains found locally. These are mostly produced using minimal external inputs, such as fertilizers or pesticide, in the gaps in grain supply created by the dependence on animal-based protein sources. In places where capita consumption of animal-based food is low, human and livestock health improvement. Similarly, in locations where climate impacts cereals as maize or rice, pseudocereals and millets form the base of the caloric basket.

### 3.1.1 Pseudocereals for Calories

Calorie consumption is an important indicator of food sufficiency in many developing countries and globally. Cereals are currently leading global sources of calories (35%) ahead of sugarcane (24%) and fruits and vegetables (23%) for human health (Pardey et al. 2014). Of the leading cereals, rice leads the pack contributing 21% of global calories and 27% for developing countries. Wheat comes second and maize, the latter contributes about 15% of global calories per capita calorie consumption. There is a wide variation in the per capita consumption of caloric crops across regions and within a region.

In developing countries where total available cereals are not adequate to support food sufficiently, small grains and pseudocereals make up the difference during the harder months of year. ### 3.1.2 Cereals for Readily Available

Much of the world's population relies on plant-based protein for their nutrition and wellbeing (Nadatur et al. 2016). A sharp increase in animal-protein rich diets was closely related to the growth in the global economy, arguments supporting plant-based diets seem to be winning respect over systems that are sensitive and environmentally protected (Pardey et al. 2014, Santé and Soret 2014). In developing countries, the current increase in animal meat products is being driven by fast-paced urbanization trends and related improvement in per capita income of educated young population involved in economic activities (Sharma et al. 2019). Over night, malnutrition counts for over 30% of children under the age of 5 years. There is evidence of large-scale shortages in protein-rich food sources in several communities leading to stunted growth or poor child development.

Research on superfoods that are rich in protein and other minerals was driven by the quest to fill in the gap in the access and availability of protein-rich foods that exists globally. To date, protein undernutrition is in many children in the developing world and is exhibited through marasmus or kwashiorkor. Acute deficiency of protein in children is a major cause of death and wasting in adults the compound lead to low productivity and secondary infections. Amaranth for its leaf and seed. Amaranth leaves contain 17–37% protein (Oliveira and De Carvalho 1975) while the grain averages 14% in protein (Pedersen et al. 1987). Similarly, wheat, buckwheat, canary seed and chia are among pseudocereals with high protein contents that support protein-rich diets in humans.

Besides pseudocereals, research is on producing sources that can be scaled and produced at low cost and low carbon footprints. The potential of microalgae, water lentils and a range of indigenous mushrooms has been discussed. Microalgae, for example Arthospira sp., has shown potential for production and use as protein supplements both and in the form of dry processed products (Barka and Blecker 2016).

## 3.2 Healthy Diets

Human health is central to development and the attainment of aspiration of humanity. Food is a major source of energy and nourishment. Food diets are a function of income as it determines ability and willingness to pay, access to and availability of quality food. However, in marginalized locations where production is dependent on the abiotic and biotic factors of production, the diminishing production potential can jeopardize opportunities for healthy diets. For the urban population and population hotspots where land limitation prohibits production and individual purchasing capacity is high, food chain performance becomes an important determinant of access to food. Both adults and children require a balanced diet for proper health.

### 3.2.1 Human Nutrition

Malnutrition has been defined as the severe lack of adequate nutrition consumption of insufficient food, inappropriate foods, or the inability of food to utilize available food. More health aligned definitions of malnutrition as a disordered nutritional state resulting from a combination of inflammation and a negative nutrient balance, leading to changes in body composition, function and outcome have been put forward by several workers (Soeters et al. 2017).
