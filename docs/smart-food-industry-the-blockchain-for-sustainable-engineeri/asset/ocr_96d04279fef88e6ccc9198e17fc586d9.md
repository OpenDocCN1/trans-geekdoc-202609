# 2. Impact of Food Safety on Environmental Sustainability

Environmental sustainability is part of the 17 SDGs, including social and economic sustainability, as well as food security and safety. A sustainable food system is one that supports food security through the optimal use of human and natural resources, that protects biodiversity and ecosystems for present and future generations, that is accessible and universally acceptable, economically viable and environmentally friendly, and that provides the consumer with nutritious, safe, healthy and convenient food.

Strictly speaking, sustainability involves the use of resources at rates that do not exceed the Earth's ability to replace them. Regarding food, a sustainable food system must ensure security of food supply, health, accessibility, food quality, employment, and at the same time, a adaptable environment to climate change, preserving biodiversity and ensuring the quality of water and soil.

The level of environmental impact of food production refers to where and how food is produced and the availability of natural resources such as water and soil. There are often trade-offs between environmental factors said, to date, there is no simple set of principles to determine whether food is more sustainable than another.

Food security affects us all, because food production, impact of agriculture on the environment, threat of climate change and factors determining food prices are largely of global nature—there is no single solution that a country can adopt to ensure access to affordable, sustainable and nutritious food for all. It is predicted that the global food system will grow under pressure in the next 40 years, when the population will grow to 9 billion. There will be a huge demand for protein-rich foods as a major challenge to maintaining sustainable food (FAO 2011a, Féís et al. 2008).

As confirmed by the World Food Summit in 2009, access to safe and nutritious food is a right for everyone. Ensuring food safety has a cost and excessive food safety requirement may impose constraints on production, storage and distribution systems, which may lead to barriers hinder competitiveness (FAO 2009, Våsholm et al. 2020).

The growth of the global population, the increase in pressure on natural resources and global warming will lead to the need for a new framework at national and international level. Population ageing in Europe is also an additional challenge. All these factors have profound implications for agriculture and rural areas. Global demand for food is increasing, urbanization is accelerated, input prices are rising, the vulnerability of crops and livestock to climate change and the pressure on resources is growing, and all this will limit food production.

The world's population is expected to grow from 7 billion, as it is currently, to 9 billion by the middle of this century, and 90% of this will be in the least developed countries (in 50 of the least developed countries globally). Global growth will be associated with urbanization (70% of the world's population is expected to live in urban areas by 2050, to 49% currently) and rapid economic growth in some of the countries with the largest population (e.g., Brazil, China, India and Russia) (Shared Food Foundation 2015).

Climate change will have an increasing impact on the food security. Global warming is expected to generate more and unevenly distributed effects across the world.
