Unhealthy (discretionary) foods do not constitute a part of a healthy diet. They are likely to be nutrient-poor but energy-dense and therefore contribute to excess intake. They contribute to poor nutrition and have a harmful environmental impact, as recently established in a study on Australian dietary intake and environmental data (Ridout et al. 2021). This study found that discretionary foods increased environmental impact score the most, followed by fresh meat and alternatives and dairy foods and additives.

Sugary foods and drinks make up a significant portion of discretionary foods. Excess consumption of added sugar has negative consequences on health since it is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, both directly and indirectly. High intake of added sugar can be accompanied by further intake, leading to weight gain and an increased risk of obesity (Stanhope 2016, Mekonnen et al. 2013, Khawaja et al. 2019).

Food processing is necessary to provide safe, digestible, and palatable food with an extended life to preserve the nutrients. It is also an effective tool to reduce food waste. When foods are processed to the degree that they are stripped of their nutrients and cannot possess any of those benefits, they do more harm than good. The consumption of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) has been associated with weight gain and increased risk of NCDs. UPFs are defined as multi-ingredient and highly-formed mixtures that contain little (if any) added food. They tend to be high in sugar, sodium and saturated fats and low in value-added nutrients (e.g., protein, fibre and micronutrients) (Monteiro et al. 2019). Moreover, UPFs have environmental impact due to carbon emissions and water use with their production and heavily wrapped with materials that are not environmentally friendly. Unfortunately, they make a significant dietary energy source in high-income countries such as Canada, the USA and UK (Monteiro et al. 2013, Baker et al. 2020). Popkin et al. (2021) emphasised that the food industry has a major role in developing less processed products and called for action to reduce the consumption of UPFs.

# 2.3 Meat Consumption and Alternatives

The World Research Fund (WCRF) recommends limiting red meat consumption to three portions (350 g to 600 g per week), with the optimum for public health outcomes set at 300 g per week and avoiding processed meats. Red meat consumption is associated with an increased risk of colorectal and other NCDs (Boida et al. 2016, Khawaja et al. 2021). The link between processed meat consumption and cancer is more clearly demonstrated (Handel et al. 2020, Veittit 2021, Weling et al. 2020). High intake of salt, processed meat may increase the risk of high blood pressure and mortality from cardiovascular disease (WCRF 2018).

Western consumers' acknowledgement of the environmental impact of meat consumption is very low (Sanchez-Sbate and Ruben-Sbate 2019, Monteiro et al. 2022). This finding corroborates the need to introduce national impact targets to promote the uptake of sustainable and ethically produced meat. The Eat Better alliance allows for an average 50% reduction in meat and dairy consumption in the UK by 2030 and a transformation to “better meat and dairy” as a standard to health benefit, social justice and the environment (Dibb and Di Lagiano 2010).

To reduce excessive meat consumption, consumers need to incorporate more plant-based foods and meat alternatives into the diet. Plants are recommended as primary, but not exclusive, sources of protein in the HDR (Willett et al. 2013). Some diets avoid animal products, but such diets need to be planned carefully and appropriately to prevent micronutrient deficiencies (vitamin B12, calcium and selenium) (Vento et al. 2016, Bakaloud et al. 2021, WHO/EURO 2021).
