Land degradation driven by agricultural practices in population hotspots has led to reduced agricultural production and unsustainable productivity of land. In many instances, climate extremes have weighed in and caused huge losses in agricultural systems and produce due to communities' disaster unprepared. Food spoilage and waste have also been reported to be high in population hotspots, driving poverty levels higher. Climate change has seen to compound the population growth and food insecurity associated with hotspots. Smart technologies are required to reduce extreme weather impacts and play a role in resolving other layers of challenges associated with population hotspots in developing countries. If unchecked, climate change driven weather will damage major crops causing the sharp increase in frequency of droughts, cyclones, and floods. There are opportunities of exploring climate smart technologies on orphaned crops in less populated regions of the world where indigenous communities have relied on diverse crops for food and nutrition.

Since the time of the 1960s, mainstream crops have been promoted to avert widespread shortages at the expense of minor crop. It has become apparent that major crops on one cannot support a 10 billion population's demand on starch, protein, vitamins, and minerals (Gilland 2002, Naduar et al. 2017). High yielding varieties, orange flesh cultivar and fortified crops have had a resounding impact, but more is still required. Minor crops need to be part of the solution in feeding a growing population and making sustainable production systems with low carbon footprint a possibility in many communities (Janwali et al. 2016). For communities that have developed their livelihoods on minor crops, it is probably the right time for the "old indigenous knowledge" to be revealed for the benefit of all. Similarly, there is a need to capture the smart drivers from across the board and focus on super-foods that can save the world from hunger and poor health.

The "Superfoods" were originally used to describe a collection of crop-derived foods that are rich in nutrients and antioxidant which support human health and wellbeing (Tacer-Caba 2019). However loose use of the superfoods has a broader set of food crops compared to the original list that included blueberries, broccoli, and salmon. The extended list of superfoods includes but not limited to seafood (e.g., cod, tuna, or trout), nuts such as almond or cashew nuts, legumes, leafy vegetables, and small grained crops (amaranth, buckwheat, chia, quinoa or teff). Others have picked black rice as a superfood because of its unique attributes that are different from those of the other forms of rice. Regardless, here we use of pseudocereals to apply to a range of nutritionally superior crops that contribute to human nutrition and health, of which pseudocereals are an integral part of the set.

Pseudocereals are dicotyledon crops belonging to the Polygonaceae family that are exploited from the wild that resemble cereal crops in their nutritious attributes. Pseudocereals are also known as pseudograins, they share some characteristic with the positive grains, ancient grains, and orphaned starch for traditional communities. Pseudocereals such as buckwheat have been described as having high nutritional, industrial, and medicinal value and can be used for multiple purposes (Pizahrad and Malik 2020). In this context pseudocereals' status and value of pseudocereals in providing nutrition security for various communities will be discussed in this chapter.

The chapter describes the use of pseudocereals, production, and utilization. The role played by superfoods and potential of pseudocereals in providing nutrition security for various communities are discussed. This chapter analyses nutritional contribution, crop management practices, value chain development and the role of IoT in pseudocereals. research for development of pseudocereals were also discussed. The agriculture-health nexus is discussed given the importance of pseudocereals' contribution to nutrition and wellbeing now and in the future.
