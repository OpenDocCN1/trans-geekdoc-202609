In 2018, EFSA (European Food Safety Agency) approved the consumption and sale of insect foods in the EU states with the “New Food Regulation” (No. 2015/2283) (European Commission 2015). It also issued an opinion on the safety of dried yellow mealworms (Turck et al. 2021a) in January 2021, frozen and dried formulations from migratory locust (Turck et al. 2021b) in July 2021, and frozen and dried formulations from house insects from August 2021 (Turck et al. 2021c). After the EU 2015/2283 (European Commission 2015) Regulations, the consumption of insects as an alternative food source in EU member countries is expected to become increasingly widespread.

Edible insects such asickets (Ache domesticus), mealworms (Tenebrio molitor), Mopani wasps (Gorbinia bimella), black soldier larvae (BSFL) (Hermetia illens), are considered good alternative protein sources with their high protein and content. They have a high feed conversion ratio, low environmental footprint and high economic value (Folin et al. 2019, Bessa et al. 2020, Imathiu 2020).

There are more than 200 edible insect species, and their nutritional values vary widely between them. There is insufficient information about the nutritional composition of insects due to their diversity. The protein content of larvae, beetle, grasshopper, cricket, termite and bee vary between 35 and 77% of dry matter. Even within the same species, the nutritional profile of the insects can differ depending on the growth conditions (environment, season and their diet). Köhler et al. (2019) reported that the Bombay locust, scarce (larva or adult), beetle, cricket and silkworm could be used as human food depending on their nutritional contents. AA scores range from 0.58 to 1.04 when the suggested pattern of FAO (2013) was used as a reference. Insects can be a good source of iron since it exists in a highly biologically available form. Buffalo mealworms were found to exhibit higher iron bioavailability over FeSO4 (Latitude-Daka 2016). Chitin and chitosan, are abundant in the shells of insects, suppress pathogenic microorganisms in the intestines and as prebiotics (Stull et al. 2018, Montovska et al. 2012, Saadoun et al. 2022). These compounds also possess anti-nutritional effects such as binding minerals, dietary fats and fat-soluble vitamins and cause their excretion from the colon.

Baiano (2020) reviewed edible insects’ nutritional, microbiological, chemical properties and toxicological safety. They showed that insects could be biologically safe but might contain harmful residues such as or heavy metals. Cross-reactions with allergens in some insects also need to be examined. More detailed studies on insects are required on their allergenicity, pesticide residue levels, bacterial contamination and heavy metal contents (such as arsenic, mercury and lead).

Using flour protein or extract as meals in food formations is a good strategy to increase consumer acceptance. Comprehensive data on their functional properties before being added to food is required. Their structural and functional properties that affect their technological and functional behaviours such as solubility, emulsification, oil and water binding capacity and gelling need to be researched (Gonzalez et al. 2021). During processing, these properties can cause changes in techno-functionality due to structural modifications affecting their nutritional properties positively or Neg.
