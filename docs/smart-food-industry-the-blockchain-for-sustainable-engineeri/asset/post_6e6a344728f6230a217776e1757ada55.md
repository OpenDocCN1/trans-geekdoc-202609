## 3.1 Challenge 1: The Attitude-behavior Gap

When it comes to save the planet, consumers are good intention. If you ask them about the environment cause, they do not hesitate to convey their concern and their willingness to act in sustainable ways. Nevertheless, when in the marketplace, these good intentions fail to translate to real behavior. This inconsistency between consumers' declared values, attitudes, and intentions on one hand, and their real purchase behavior on the other, is well documented in the literature as the green-attitude-behavior gap (ElHaaffer et al. 2020). This gap applies to sustainable food products, as well as other product categories such as personal care products, electronics, apparel, and it can be traced at both the purchase phase (at the purchase), and at the disposal phase (recycling, upcycling, reusing).

To approach the attitude-behavior gap as a primordial challenge in promoting sustainable food innovations requires a thorough understanding of this phenomenon. The gap entails that for some sustainable innovations, the problem is beyond attitude acceptance of the innovation, but rather a behavioral reluctance when it comes to purchasing and integrating the innovation in consumers' diets. Some reasons could explain this hesitation in the innovation

To begin with, consumers face a difficult choice when it comes to switching to eco-friendly food alternatives. If the decision was based on values, then action would be guaranteed, but consumers often assess the costs associated with a behavior before undertaking it (Delmas and Colgan 2018). For instance the choice of buying organic fruits. If this solely based on the environmental factors of the organic fruits (soil is protected from bioaccumulation of chemical substances, groundwater is protected from contamination), consumers would buy or organic alternative in a heartbeat. But this specific example comes with substantial costs. Consumers would probably pay considerably higher price. Moreover, switching costs are encountered as consumers would have to visit specific points such as farmers' market, and more time and effort invested in the research. This situation creates a conflict of interest for consumers between their altruistic values (the environment) and economic values (their own) following with lower costs and most personal gains (Delmas and Colgan 2018).

Thus, consumers are not able to prioritize the environment over their own beliefs in consumption choices (ElHaaffer et al. 2020), when the costs are larger than the perceived benefits (Delmas and Colgan 2018).

Another reason for the attitude gap can be found in the defense and coping mechanisms developed by consumers to protect their mental wellbeing. Indeed, when there is an inconsistency between their values and their actual behavior, consumers experience a moral discomfort and accumulated feelings of guilt and shame (ElHaaffer et al. 2020). To alleviate these negative emotions, consumers tend to justify their unsustainable behavior through neutralization techniques (Atkinson and Kim 2015, Gruber and Schlegelmelich 2014). These techniques include, among others, the denial of responsibility and the defense of the necessity (Gruber and Schlegelmelich 2014). For instance, when consumers don't go the extra mile to purchase bulk instead of canned food, they would blame the in of these options and their need to buy and consume this type of food.

This is a serious problem, as it can easily turn into patterns of justification, and ultimately turn into indifference about sustainable behavior, establishing and maintaining the inconsistent behavior over time (Gruber and Schlegelmelich 2014).

## 3.3 Challenge 3: Intangibility Issue

Psychological distance relates to the mental representation of objects and events in the minds of consumers. This distance can spread over four dimensions: time, space, social and hypothetical (Liberman et al. 2007). The more the things are psychologically distant, the more they are constructed as abstract in the minds of consumers (Trope et al. 2007). Research has demonstrated that climate change for instance is psychologically distant on many levels (Spence et al. 2012), and by extension, products that are positioned environmentally friendly are perceived as distant from the self (Segev et al. 2015). This is one of the reasons why consumers are reluctant to integrate sustainable consumption into their lives: things that are abstract in minds, i.e., buying sustainable food, are linked to a more distant future in time. The consumer postpones switching to an indefinite future.

Another issue in regard is the intangible of sustainable alternative. Sustainable foods (organic, local, urban farming, meat alternatives) are characterized by a high level of information asymmetry where consumers can never verify if the product actually meets the standards that are promised by the producers (Jansen and Hamm 2012). In cases, even after the purchase of a product certified as organic or as cruelty free, consumers are kept in the dark about its credibility.

Add to the effect of green on consumers’ trust in the sustainable alternatives. With the rapid growth of green-products market, and the trendiness of the green attribute, there is an increasing advertising, which falsely promotes their products by green (Delmas and Burbano 2011). The mere existence of this type of misleading advertising creates skepticism, and consumers start doubting every green claim, even the noteworthy. When consumers experience green skepticism, they experience anger and frustration (Terra Co 2010), and their knowledge and concern about the environment decreases (Goh and Balaji 2016). Hence, the accumulation of these three factors (i.e., psychological distance of the green alternative, intangibility of sustainable goods, and green skepticism) aggravate the problem of the green gap, and results in consumers resounding eco-friendly food alternatives.

## 3.4 Challenge 4: Product Attribute Elusiveness: Naturalness, Health Concerns, Neophobia, and Taste Perception

Food products attribute to hinder planetary and human health remain elusive (Dubé et al. 2021). To attain a lower carbon footprint, food innovations consist of modifying product attributes such as chemical and biological compositions, which are negatively perceived by consumers and accentuate the risk level associated with consuming these foods. Sigiest and Hartmann (2020) explain this phenomenon through the ‘Natural is better’, where consumers automatically associate human intervention in food production with unnaturalness, harm, and contagion. The unnaturalness is a recurrent argument for rejecting sustainable food alternatives such as cultured meat (Bryant and Barnett 2018, Verbeke et al. 2015) and genetically modified food (Sigiest and Hartmann 2020). Consumers who oppose low-carbon food innovations based on their unnaturalness believe that consuming food is dangerous for human health and the environment or consider this kind of technology unethical for its excessive interference with nature (Bryant and Barnett 2018).
