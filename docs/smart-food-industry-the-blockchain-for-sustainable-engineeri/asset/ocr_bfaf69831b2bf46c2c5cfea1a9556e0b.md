Nutritional compositions vary with the species, diet, developmental stage, sex, and growth environments of insects, such as temperature, day, light intensity, and spectral composition (incident light, reflection, and spectral transmittance) (Zielinska et al. 2015, Barros et al. 2017). There are nutrient variations depending on the type of processing, if the insects are alive, dehydrated, cooked, etc., as shown in Table 1.

Humans already consume insects as their diet, often without realizing it. For instance, according to the US Food and Drug Administration, an average of 75 or more insect fragments per 50 g are allowed in wheat flour as a defect (AOAC 972.32) (Castro and Chambers 2018).

Studies show that the "meat" of insects contains substantial amounts of proteins and lipids and is rich in mineral salts, including iron, phosphorus, magnesium, selenium, zinc, vitamins (A1, B2, B5, B6, B7, D, E, and K), carotenoids, and flavonoids (avigenin, quercetin, luteolin, and rutin) and are easily digestible (Belluco et al. 2017, Castro et al. 2018, Hunter 2021, Ojha et al.). For example, the tanagra ant has more protein (42.59%) than chicken (23%) or beef (20%) (Costa-Neo 2013, Fogadin et al. 2017).

Insects have nutritional value, an abundance of proteins, and may contain double or triple (>40%) compared to conventional sources of animal origin. This is a good substitute for other animal proteins (Rumpold and Schlitzer 2013, Lucas et al. 2020). The protein content of beef, chickens, silkworms, moth caterpillars that live in Zimbabwe, and palm weevil is higher than beef, chicken, and pork, as demonstrated in Table 2. The mopane cricket is traditionally boiled in salted water and dried in the sun; it can last for several months without refrigeration and is important in food stores (Halloran et al. 2018).

A serving of locust provides similar amounts of vitamin D as, cooked chicken liver, and egg yolks. Silkworm larvae (Bombyx mori L.) fed mulberry leaves and lettuce, and when processed (and freeze-dried), can contain 10 mg of vitamin E in 10 g (Tong et al. 2011). Fat is the second-largest component of insects, and have an unsaturated fatty acid profile similar to white fish while containing more unsaturated fatty acids than poultry or red meat.
