Regarding the environment, once again, whether veggie meat alternatives have a lower environmental impact is not entirely clear—although virtually researchers are of, even veggie meat products have some negative environmental impacts, meat is much, much worse for the environment. For example, a study conducted by the University of Michigan detected that veggie burgers have a significantly lower environmental impact than traditional burgers. Specifically, Beyond Burger uses 99 percent less water, 93 percent less energy. Also, veggie Burger uses 90 percent fewer greenhouse gases than animal flesh burgers (Heller et al. 2018). However, it is important to mention that this study is just not completely unbiased as it was sponsored by the company Beyond Meat. Moreover, consider that the production of veggie meats, unlike traditional meats, does not involve manure pollution (Santo et al. 2020, Ernst et al. 2019).

A significant aspect of the environmental implications of veggie meats is that, in the end, our food production has negative environmental impacts, and this includes veggie meat alternatives. Farming involves tilling, which destroys soil carbon. The preparing for soy foods destroys the soil. A process releases carbon in the atmosphere. Thus, the growing of industrial crops contributes too much carbon in the atmosphere (Haddaway et al. 2017). Now, of course I am not suggesting that farming food should be stopped. In a study published in Nature, with the Oxford Martin Programme on the Future of Food, even a certain degree of skepticism regarding the environmental effectiveness of totally plant-based diets. The researchers recommend the following:

We analyse options for reducing the environmental effects of the food system, including dietary changes, more plant-based diets, improvements in technologies and management, and reductions in food loss and waste. We find that no single measure is enough to keep these effects within all planetary boundaries simultaneously, and that a synergistic combination of measures will be needed to sufficiently mitigate the projected in environmental pressures (Springmann et al. 2018).

Surprisingly, the researchers do not conclude that the most environmentally diet is veganism. Rather, they argue that in order to resolve environmental problems, humans should follow a flexible diet, which predominantly focuses on vegetables and legumes, incorporates some meat (521–22). In other words, such a study represents a counterintuitive example of the consensus that vegan diets are our best bet to save the planet from environmental degradation.
