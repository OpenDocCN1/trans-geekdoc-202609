## 2.4 Pineapple Waste and By-products

Pineapple (*Ananas comosus* L. Merrill) has the highest production rate of all tropical fruits worldwide. The main producer and exporter of pineapple is Costa Rica, with a producing rate of approximately 25.5 million tons annually (Casabar et al. 2019). As the global demand and consumption rate of pineapple is rising, the processing of pineapple is also increasing. Industrialization of fruit fruits generates significant amounts (nearly 25%), which consist mainly of the peel, core and crown of the pineapple (Conesa et al. 2016). Considering the different parts, peels represent the largest part of waste production with a rate of 30-40%, followed by core (10%) (Roda and Lambri 2019). Pineapple wastes in the form of core, peel, stem and leaves contain a wide range of bioactive compounds in each fragment. Namely, there are bromelin, ferulic acid, and vitamin A and C in pineapple peels, ascorbic acid and proteolytic enzymes in the core, and citric acid in leaves (Hikal et al. 2021). Industrial wastes of pineapple are high-quality stocks with a significant amount of lignin (1.5%), cellulose (14%) and hemicellulose (20%) used for bioproduct production (energy, food, etc.) as a potential source for bioethanol production (McCance et al. 2020). Pineapple wastes were evaluated by Anbesaw (2021) as a potential source for bromelin enzyme that can be used for different food and non-food applications. Authors used bromelin to recover silver from X-ray films with low-cost and in an efficient way. Pineapple wastes also help to minimize the production cost of a huge number of polyester films. In another study on biomaterial production, bacterial cellulose and the nanocrystalline film is produced using pineapple peels as the culture medium (Anwar et al. 2021). Similarly, Gil and Maupeau (2018) focused on the valorization of industrial waste of pineapple (core and peel) for bioethanol and bromelin production.

## 2.5 Citrus Waste and By-products

Citrus fruits are among the most popular and highly consumed fruits in the world, because of their nutritional value and beneficial properties. Citrus fruits mainly consist of sweet orange, mandarin, grapefruit, lime and lemon (Satari and Karimi 2018). The majority of citrus fruits are utilized in citrus processing industries, causing approximately 50-60% wastes by weight. Citrus waste mainly comprises peel, membrane and seed parts (Mahato et al. 2020). Therefore, the valorization alternatives of citrus wastes are gaining importance to develop a sustainable bio-economy, and to reduce adverse effects on air, soil and water. Innovative approaches are required rather than traditional valorization methods such as pectin, animal feed, organic fertilizer and compost production. For instance, the bioconversion processes of these wastes have come to fore the potential economic gains (Zema et al. 2018).

## 2.6 Banana Waste and By-products

Banana is the most produced fruit, representing 16% of the total fruit production in the world with the production rate of 115.7 million metric tons. The peel (30-40%) is the major part of banana wastes with 34.7-46.3 million metric tons producing rate (Barmossi et al. 2021). Banana wastes can be valorized as animal feed, organic fertilizer or compost. The banana peel contains high amounts of bioactive compounds compared to the fruit itself. Furthermore, the antioxidant capacity of the peel is higher than the fruit itself (Vu et al. 2020). Banana peel is an excellent bioresource for sustainable materials.

## 2.7 Cherry Waste and By-products

The rapid increase in the production rate of cherry has caused significant amounts of waste. The most common and important species of cherry are *Prunus avium* L. known as sweet and *Prunus cerasus* L. known as sour. Most of sweet cherries are consumed fresh (approximately 85%), while sour cherries are processed into juice, jam, puree, marmalade and jelly (Bagheri et al. 2019). The valorization alternatives of citrus wastes are gaining importance to develop a sustainable bio-economy, and to reduce adverse effects on air, soil and water. Innovative approaches are required rather than traditional valorization methods such as pectin, animal feed, organic fertilizer and compost production. For instance, the bioconversion processes of these wastes have come to fore the potential economic gains (Zema et al. 2018).

Mauricio et al. (2018) investigated the potential of cherry pomace, which can be used in functional foods as antioxidants, anti-inflammatory, and anti-diabetic properties. These by-products generated from cherry pomace have high organoleptic properties.

## 2.8 Grape Waste and By-products

Three-quarter of grape is utilized in wine-making, which is around 25 billion tons annually (Beres et al. 2017). Grape pomace consists mainly of skin, pulp and seeds. Phenolic compounds such as anthocyanins, hydroxybenzoic acids, flavonoids, peonidin, cyanidin and delphinidin are abundant in grape skin. These can be used in functional products (Solar et al. 2020).
