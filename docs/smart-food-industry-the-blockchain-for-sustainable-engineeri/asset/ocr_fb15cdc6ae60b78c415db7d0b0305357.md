One of the biggest threats we face today is climate change, which is exacerbated by excessive deforestation and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and it concerns how adaptation measures could affect the food system (Winsemius al. 2015, Baiano 2020). In the coming decades, there will be a gradual increase in the demand for high-quality protein, and it will be estimated at 72% in meat production in the next 30 years; one of features is that this protein source is better quality than those currently available. Vegetable products often contain less than 14%, whereas types of edible insects have up to 75%-high-quality protein, thus being sources of essential amino acids, including interesting amounts of fatty acids such as omega 3 and 6, and other specific nutrients that help in the complex function of the human body. When properly produced, insects can be from natural sources, pesticide-free, and locally produced using land more efficiently for food production and, above all, respecting the environment (Dossey et al. 2016, Hakoran et al. 2018, Hunter 2021, Van Hui et. 2021, FAO 2021b, Ojha et al. 2021).

Insects can transform organic matter into high-quality protein and other nutrients more efficiently than conventionally used animals; therefore, they are associated with a small ecological footprint. Insects produce lower levels of GHGs such as methane, carbon dioxide, and nitrous oxide than animals raised for meat production, suggesting they are a greener alternative to animal production in terms of emissions (Abassi et al. 2016, Gravel and Doyen 2020, Acosta-Estrada et al. 2021).
