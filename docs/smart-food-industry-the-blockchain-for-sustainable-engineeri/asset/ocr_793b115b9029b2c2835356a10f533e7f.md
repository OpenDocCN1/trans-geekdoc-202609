Since then, the most diverse environmental labels can be found on a wide range of products sold, including in the food segment (Fig. 2). The most basic examples consist of (i) carbon codes—which indicate whether a product has a high, medium, or low carbon footprint, usually represented by the colors red, yellow, and green, respectively; (ii) carbon reduction labels—which quantitatively indicate (through percentages or mass units) of the product's carbon footprint; and (iii) carbon-neutral labels—which indicate carbon offsets, that is, they present carbon reduction measures, their remaining emissions, as well as the purchase of carbon credits involved in the entire production process of the product (Birkenberg et al. 2021).

However, although the justification for environmental labeling as an attribute of value to products is necessary, so far, the standardization lack of labels, as the application lack in the food market, makes it difficult for consumers to buy food products. In addition, criteria such as consumer preferences and how much they are willing to pay for sustainable products are still the stage for the discussion.

In order to elucidate this gap, several studies related to labeling have been published in academic literature. So far, what has been clarified is there was a tendency for consumers to be more aware of the negative impacts of food production. However, although consumers have shown concern and understanding regarding these issues, this has not translated into willingness to pay or use of sustainable labels (Leach et al. 2016).

In fact, consumers' priorities regarding food choices are driven by a series of interconnected factors, such as price, quality, health and, environmental boundaries. This propensity to use environmental attributes as the last factor as purchase criterion is often associated with higher prices of labeled products, credibility of labels, availability of labeled products, lack of understanding of what the labels represent, or other perceptions. In this sense, willingness to pay, that is, the maximum amount a consumer is willing to pay, becomes a crucial factor in finding the best price to sell a product, for both the seller and for the buyer. Therefore, reaching a middle ground between market vs. consumer must be done to make a —sustainable—sale.
