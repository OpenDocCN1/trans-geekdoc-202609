# 4. Novel Protein Sources

With the limited protein sources we currently have, it is impossible to meet the protein requirement of the increasing population. The daily amount of protein (animal or vegetable) per capita is estimated at 52 g in 2030 and 54 g in 2050 (FAO 2017, FAO IAF UNICEF WPO 2020). Many national DGs recommend meeting protein requirements from various foods that support health and sustainability. For example, in the 2015–2020 Dietary Guidelines for Americans, the "protein foods" group consists of meat, poultry, seafood, beans and peas, soy processed products, nuts and seeds (USDA 2015).

The E-Lancet reiterates that humanity no longer consume more food than necessary (Willett et. 2019). The consumption of animal-based foods is more dominant in developed countries such as the USA than in developing countries. These countries must adopt more plant-based foods and novel protein sources to maintain adequate protein intake (Karmaus and Janes 2021). However, when making recommendations for developed and underprivileged countries, amino acid (AA) composition and digestibility of proteins must be considered (WHO 2007). This is because protein deficiency occurs when the diet provides little protein or lacks essential amino acids (EAAs). Health problems caused by protein deficiency, especially in the long-term, are severe with growth inhibition, impaired kidney and brain function, early aging, poor immunity, loss of muscle mass, and inadequate nutrient absorption (IU 2020).

Diet and environment have been identified as motivating factors in reducing meat consumption in European Union (EU) countries. However, this differs between consumers in northern and southern European countries with varying degrees. Europe, which consists of the richest countries with the largest share in economic and social sustainable development, the majority of consumers acknowledged their role in reducing meat as part of HDAS [sic*] (De Boer and Aiking 2022). A study in the UK investigated the consumption of plant-based alternative foods (vegetables, nuts and seeds) and compared it with meat and dairy reduction targets. The study reported that from 2008 to 2019, the consumption rate of plant-based foods, plant-based alternatives and plant-based milk consumption increased (approximately three times) was noted in plant-based milk consumption, while meat intake was significantly reduced. Although plant-based foods are expected to be more popular and consumed more widely, ultra-processed forms of these products cannot contribute to a healthy diet and may lead to other health problems (Alae-Carev et. 2022).

The food industry seeks alternative protein sources for human consumption in global concerns about food security and the growing population. New protein sources such as seaweed and algae, peas, reapedweed, and insects have a received level of interest over the past decade. While numerous R&D projects are ongoing, these sources should be critically evaluated for protein quality. More studies should be conducted on the metabolic behaviour, health effects, and cognitive functions of proteins (van Spiegel et. 2013, Karmaus, and Janes 2021). Further studies should consider safety aspects such as toxic chemistry, allergy, impurities and contaminants (ESA 2020).

In the light of the nutritional recommendations stated in the framework of HDRs by Willett et. (2019), the rest of this chapter will discuss the scientific advancements about several new protein sources (insects and microalgae) and innovative applications to reduce the sugar content of foods.
