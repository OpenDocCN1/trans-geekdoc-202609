The majority of studies, have been carried out in stirred tank bioreactors for a variety of adherent cell types (Hewitt et al. 2011, Chen et al. 2011, Badenes et al. 2015, Gupta et al. 2016, Rafiq et al. 2017, 2018, Hanga et al. 2020, 2021). Advantages of microcarrier based systems include the ability to hold adherent cells in suspension, promoting even distribution of media and gases through the mixing action of impeller and a straightforward scale up process. However, there is a difficult culture strategy for the carrier surface with many uncontrolled variables. Common challenges include uneven cell distribution on the carrier surface due to preferential adhesion during cell seeding (bead to bead), limited capacity of some types of cells to newly adhered and hydrodynamic forces acting on the carrier during aggregation. All the above can cause cell proliferation and negatively impact (Wu 1999) if not minimized through controlled approaches.

Bead-to-bead is a manufacturing approach that facilitates process intensification, resulting in increased cell yields and reduced process time. However, the choice of time for addition of microcarriers is critical to initiate successful bead-to-bead transfer at certain levels of aggregation (Hanga et al. 2021). The other limitation is hydrodynamic forces, which could be addressed by porous microcarriers as the cells grow mostly within the carrier rather than on the surface; however, harvesting of the cells from porous carriers is more challenging (Yang and Yang 2011).

For the production of cultivated meat products, any of the commercially available microcarriers could be used. However, there are several concerns, particularly regarding polystyrene-microcarriers, as validation of their removal during downstream processing is important to ensure the safety of these products. Ideally, the microcarriers to be used should be edible which the building blocks of the degraded microcarrier itself should also be food-grade substances with no need to eliminate them from the product. Tuneable degradation rates have been seen to enhance MSC spread and differentiation capacity due to the more dynamic microenvironment (Toh et al. 2012). It may be possible to use tunable degradation as a micropatterning technique, selecting and tuning degradation rates of certain regions on the microcarrier to form a topography that can guide cell adhesion, fusion and eventually muscle fibre formation (Zhao et al. 2009).

Another option could potentially enhance the nutritional and textural profile of the cultivated meat product. A similar type of microcarrier made of pectin (a compound found in fruits) has been previously commercialised, but now discontinued. The Corning dissolvable microcarrier has been used successfully to culture and harvest iPSCs (Rodriguez et al. 2018) and MSCs (Kalra et al. 2019) which are both possible starting sources for cultivated meat production.

## 4.2.3 Cell Encapsulation in Hydrogels

The suggested culture method is encapsulation in hydrogels. Hydrogels have their own intrinsic properties to be exploited and can be processed into different geometries, making this most versatile technique. Hydrogels can be categorised as either natural or synthetic; natural hydrogels favor more biocompatible, likely to have necessary RGD sequencing for accommodating cell adhesion and capable of being digested or remodelled by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), while synthetics have limited batch variability, are often hydrophobic, possess strong covalent bonds within their matrix improving their mechanical strength, service life and although are more easily modified for attachment, degradation, and stiffness requirements (Gyles et al. 2017).

However, natural hydrogels will have increased variability and less room for optimisation due to their plasticity in chemical and mechanical tuning, while synthetics will not be edited or for use in food products without rigorous purity checks. They may require external chemical degradation to allow cellular migration and prevent stalling of proliferation. Chemical conjugation and surface functionalisation of hydrogels can be expensive but may prove to be a valuable opportunity to generate versatile IP and an optimised process.

The encapsulation can be in a continuous, solid-state hydrogel acting as a scaffold to offer structure, which when degrades allows the cells grow or differentiate, or if they be in smaller spheroids, microporous, microcarrier systems. Large hydrogel scaffolds have provided advantageous for production of well-distributed muscle tissue due to their ability to form static tension with cell attachment points. These systems have been termed BAM (bio-artificial muscle) constructs (Gholova et al. 2018).

Novel strategies under development include the immobilisation of growth factors via embedding in the hydrogel scaffold (Silva et al. 2009) which micropatterned design could encourage differentiation different lines or the addition of acetylcholine to accelerate differentiation towards myotubes (Krause et al. 1995). Fibro-adipogenic progenitor and adipose stem cells have also been successfully encapsulated in hydrogels as alginate or fibrinogen which were then shaped into microfibers to enhance differentiation into fat tissues (Hsiao et al. 2015, Dohmen et al. 2022).

## 4.2.4 Culture on Scaffolds

Culture on scaffolds is another method applicable to cultured meat products. Scaffolds can be used during both the expansion and differentiation phases. In fibrous carriers which are either suspended in a stirred-tank reactor (STR) or packed in a packed-bed reactor (PBR), the expansion phase is typically low and limited to a few population doublings before the cells cover the surface area. They then have to be dissociated and re-seeded into a larger scaffold/surface area, unlike the microcarrier based systems, where cells can migrate on freely added carriers and several population doublings can be achieved without the need for passage (Hanga et al. 2021). Scaffolds are mainly useful for the differentiation stage and are indispensable for the production of cultured meat products (e.g., steaks, fillets, etc.).

Biomaterial scaffolds have been used for many years in the field of regenerative medicine and tissue engineering for transplantation purposes. Scaffolds provide internal structure and fall into two distinct categories: permanent or resorbable. Permanent scaffolds tend to be bioinert and remain in place, providing the structure to the growing cell mass. The material properties (stiffness and elasticity) can be tuned to direct differentiation of the cells to a specific lineage. Resorbable scaffolds, on the other hand, typically degrade as the cells differentiate, possibly even releasing bioactive molecules as it breaks down (Nikolov et al. 2019). The tissue formed at the endpoint would be in that case comprised only by the cells with no additional biomaterial and, more likely to be approved by the regulatory bodies as long as it can be demonstrated that the degradation components pose no risk.
