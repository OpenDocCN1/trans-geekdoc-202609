# 4. Considerations for Cultivated Meat Production

Animal meat, or skeletal muscle, has a complex structure comprising different types of cells with approximately 90% being muscle fibres, the remaining 10% being connective and fat cells (Listrat et al. 2016) with less than 1% blood (Warridus and Weiss 1977, Reiss et al. 2021). This composition can differ with spices and cut of meat. For cultivated products to be successful and accepted by consumers, the sensorial characteristics (texture, colour, taste, flavour) are of utmost importance. These characteristics are derived from its structure the different cells which determine the molecular characteristics such as content and type of proteins, presence of myosin and volatile compounds that contribute to flavour (Frae et al. 2020). The technological challenges in producing a cultivated meat that animal-derived meat are very much dependent on the type of cultivated meat product that is.

Cultivated meat can be split in two categories—structured or non-structured. Structured meat products includes steaks, fillets, chops which have a clearly defined organisation. Their production would require more complex processing to achieve such structure and to mimic the vivo microenvironment with the signalling pathways necessary to form muscle tissue. Non-structured products refer to ground or finely minced meat and involve a large heterogeneous cell composed of myocytes and adipocytes at varying differentiation states and ratios depending on the product. Non unstructured cultivated meat products can be commercialised under various forms such as meatballs, sausages and patties. The first proof-of-concept cultivated meat product was non-structured in the form of a beef patty (Post 2014), while the first approval for commercialised cultivated product was also non-structured in the form of chicken (BBC 2020). It is generally acknowledged that the production of non-structured cultivated meat is more feasible with the currently available technology (Hoquette 2016, Post and Hoquette 2017). The main challenge in production of thick cut cells is the absence of a complex blood vessel network throughout the construct to deliver nutrients and cells to gaps (Bhatt et al. 2010, Frae et al. 2020). In practice, this would translate to a perfusion system within the hole cut structure; however, achieving a vascular-like network integrated in the construct to overcome diffusion limitations would be the challenge.
