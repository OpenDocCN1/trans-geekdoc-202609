History has shown that civilizations and people who have gone through some deprivation at some point are those who have the greatest spectrum of potential foods they use in their diets. That is, civilizations peoples who have had a greater need to taste sources of food (Imathi 2020, Lange and Nakamura 2021).

One example is Mexico, which has many reports in the literature on edible insects, with over 500 species having been recorded. In the case of Brazil, despite limited disclosure to the public, around 100 species of insects are already used as food (Lesnik 2019). The production of insects for human consumption on a large scale is a relatively new concept in society, and one of the main challenges is popularizing this protein source (Patel et al. 2019). Various insects are already part of the cuisine in other countries and even in Brazil, such as the tenebro (flower beetle), whose larva is used for feeding reptiles and human consumption, in addition to clicks and some species of cockroaches that are produced for these purposes. In Brazil, despite the National Health Surveillance Agency (ANVISA) have not yet regulated the use of edible insects for human consumption, there are insect breeders who actively, use insects to produce animal food and make artisanal cookies and cookies for human consumption (Schardong et al. 2019).

The Resolution of the Collegiate Board (RDC) No. 14 of 2014 (ANVISA) establishes tolerable limits of ‘future meat’ in foods of edible insects to human health and/ of failures in applying good practices in the food and beverage production chain. The RDC lists considered part of the production process (food and beverages), establishing the limits. Insect fragments can only be considered failures in the production process, involving everything from the harvest of to the final product (Brazil 2014).

There are no regulatory barriers to their production, marketing, and consumption for countries where eating insects is traditional. Nevertheless, in western countries, regulations represent a significant barrier to the use of insects and food. European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has declared that all insects for human consumption will be considered as ‘novel food’ and must undergo Novel Food, with a 2-year transition (Dobermann et al. 2017).

In 2021, the EFSA issued the first positive opinion (EU Regulation 2015/2283) for mealworms (EFSA-Q-2018-00262) to be marketed for human consumption of food based on insects. Mealworm larvae can be used in products such as baked goods, energy bars, and pasta (ON-6343), with that the ingestion of the larvae can cause allergies in some people (EFSA 2021).

Currently, the EFSA is proceeding with eleven applications concerning insect species or certain products made, and the following are at risk assessment stage: Gryllides sigillatus (EFSA-Q-2018-0263), whole ground lesser mealworm (Alphobius diaperus) larvae products (EFSA-Q-2018-0028), Locust migratoria (EFSA-Q-2018-0513), Acheta domestica (EFSA-Q-2018-0543), Tenebrio molitor (mealworm) larvae (EFSA-Q-2019-01001), whole and ground grasshoppers (Locusta migratoria) (EFSA-Q-2019-00115), and whole and ground crickets (Acheta domestica) (EFSA-Q-2019-00121), defatled cricket (Acheta domesticus) powder (EFSA-Q-2019-0589), Tenebrio molitor (mealworm) flour (EFSA-Q-2019-00746), and edible Acheta (EFSA-Q-2020-00748) (EFSA 2021, Skitnick et al. 2021).

One of the great challenges in accepting edible insects is the aversion to insect-based foods (i.e., neophobia and disgust). Foodophobia is the fear of unfamiliar foods, and it is dependent on culture and social rules. Rejection of edible insects can be reduced by increasing familiarity, that by making insect-based foods more widely available.
