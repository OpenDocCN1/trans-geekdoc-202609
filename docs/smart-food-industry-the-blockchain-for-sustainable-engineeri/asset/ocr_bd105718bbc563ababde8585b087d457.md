# 2.2.1 Carbohydrates

Carbohydrate-based materials such as starch (Yang et al. 2018a, Liu et al. 2018, Maniglia et al. 2019, 2020), pectin (Derossi et al. 2018), and hydrocolloids (Kim et al. 2017) in which carbohydrate is used as a structuring ingredient have been studied for application in 3D printing.

Starch has been relied due to its rheological characteristics and its wide variety of natural and modified sources, making it a promising ingredient for 3D application. Some studies have been carried out using native starches as a gelling agent for 3D printing. Yang et al. (2018b) investigated the 3D printing of a lemon juice using native starch pore. The study revealed that the lemon gel using 15 g/100 g of starch from different sources presented better printability. Chen et al. (2019a) studied the rheological properties of pectin and found that high concentrations of starch extracted the material smoothly and those low concentrations (10-15 wt%), for maize starch, deformed resulting in poor printing resolution and sagging. Optimized conditions were dependent on the starch source.

Modified starches have also been utilized in 3D printing technology. Studies have already been carried out using olive in cassava (Maniglia et al. 2019, 2020), dry heat treatment (DHT) in cassava and (Maniglia et al. 2020) starches, and pulse electric field (PEF) in cassava starches and wheat (Maniglia et al. 2021). The use of modified starches, in special and ozone resulted in 3D products with well-defined geometry.

Fruit-based food ink has been developed for the development of 3D snacks designed for children (Derek et al. 2018). Pectin was added to the fruit-based food to enhance the rheological behavior of the ink and to prevent phase separation between water and fruit formula during. Vancauwenberg et al. (2019) studied fruit-based food ink for 3D printing of porous food simulators. The observed that food-based ink can be printed using different fruit-based food inks and that the concentration is the determinant for the firmness and strength of the printed objects. A higher concentration of sugar and pectin affected the build quality and increased the viscosity. The authors emphasized that future research should address the importation of ingredients to add color and flavor to fruit products.

The use of hydrocolloids such hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (MC) and others has been described for the production of 3D printed foods. Kim et al (2017a) established an underlying classification system to evaluate 3D printing using hydrocolloids as reference materials. MC was selected as reference material, as it presented better texture and viscosity results. Different MC concentrations can simulate the deformation behavior in a series of foods, being the most suitable for 3D printing of foods. # 2.2.2 Proteins

Proteins are composed of amino acids with functional groups bearing positive or negative charges, dependent on the pH. The distribution and the amount of each amino acid in the primary structure will dictate the isoelectric point of the protein. The printing of protein-based materials is highly influenced by protein aggregation, which in turn depends on the isoelectric point (IP) and the working pH (Portanguen et al. 2019).

There are few studies on the animal protein for 3D printing of food, as it is a great challenge to use meat products or an alternative mixture of proteins for the elaboration of meat products without the use of additives, and that presents a rheological property that guarantees a good printability and resolution of the final food. Liu et al. (2017) pointed out that accurate 3D structures can be better obtained by the addition of textures such as hydrocolloids or gelable proteins to the inks. Dick et al. (2019) produced 3D recombinant meats and meatizing tender meat products by using a multi-component ink.
