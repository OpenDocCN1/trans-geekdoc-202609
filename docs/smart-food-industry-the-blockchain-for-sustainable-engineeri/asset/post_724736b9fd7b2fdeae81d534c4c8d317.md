## 1. Introduction

The availability of freshwater resources for agricultural production is crucial to food security. Agricultural (food and nonfood) production consumes over 70% of freshwater water globally each year. Climate change adds to the uncertainty by altering rainfall patterns and increasing the frequency of extreme weather patterns like floods and droughts (Schewe et al. 2014). Increased worldwide water use in agriculture has resulted from growing populations and rapidly shifting diets, particularly increased consumption of animal source foods (ASFs) (Vörössmarty et al. 2000). Knowing the influence of food production and population-level dietary patterns on use is crucial for long-term water management, and understanding the impact of food production and population-level dietary patterns on use (Harris et al. 2020, Latham).

Globally, many people are transitioning to food consumption habits that include more animal products as part of a nutrition mission (Kearny 2010). Many parts of the world are witnessing enormous growth, which leads to higher buying power, resulting in increasing demand for food as well as a shift in the varieties consumed (Latham 2000). Changes in food production have increased demand for animal sources, meat, and eggs in recent decades, and meeting the requirements of a balanced diet has a particularly significant challenge (Henchon et al. 2021). Protein intake in developed nations is higher compared to developing countries, primarily due to the excessive consumption of ASFs. Meat and other animal products are often more expensive per capita consumption as it rises with the average per capita income (Gerbons-Leenes et al. 2010). If population increases in developing countries, especially when combined with economic growth, as is predicted in countries such as China and Brazil, demand for animal products is expected to increase in consumption. The consumption of 7 million tonnes of meat in 1975 has increased by nearly 13% reaching 86.5 million tonnes by 2018 turning it the world’s greatest meat consumption as shown in Fig. 1 (Chapagain and Hoekstra 2013).

Mapping and quantifying national water footprints (WFs) has been an evolving field of study to measure the environmental impacts of anthropogenic activities (Mekonnen and Hoekstra 2011b).

The notion of WF was proposed in 2002 to provide a consumption-based measure of water use in addition to the traditional production-based measures (Chapagen and Hoekstra 2013). Water consumption and pollution may be analyzed using the WF concept, which differentiates between a blue WF (consumption of surface and groundwater), a grey WF (amount of water required to dilute the pollution) and a green WF (consumption of rainfall). The WF related to agricultural production obtains the largest share of the total WF. Globally, agriculture contributes to 92% of the global WF; 29% of the water in agriculture is directly or indirectly used (Mendomken and Green-Lees 2020).

A high blue WF indicates considerable amounts of irrigation water used in agricultural production, which can be significant in regions where surface and groundwater sources are overexposed (Mendomken and Hoekstra 2016). A WF with a high concentration of green or total (green + blue) could suggest low yields or insufficient water consumption (Harris et al. 2020). A high blue and green WF indicates that rainwater has been used sustainably (Aleksandrowic et al. 2016).

Several studies have demonstrated the significance of virtual water in available water conservation and scarcity management, and it will effectively reduce the threat of water crisis for food production (Chapagen et al. 2006). This chapter aims to provide an overview of the water demand for food products and diets, as well as the WF of waste. Finally, the chapter highlights the global WF status of food and dietary habits along with a review of different methods of WF calculations. This also gives an insight into the WF of food waste and global policies highlighting essential measures toward food sustainability, highlighting the contributions of major countries and crops to the unsustainable WF.
