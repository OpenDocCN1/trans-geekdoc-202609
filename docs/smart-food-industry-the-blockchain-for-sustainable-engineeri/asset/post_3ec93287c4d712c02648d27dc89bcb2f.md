## 8. The Influence of Ethical Theory

In the previous sections, we have considered some positive and negative aspects of veggie alternatives alongside some positive and negative aspects of traditional meat. We have seen that the production and consumption of veggie meats appears to be friendlier than traditional meat, not too kindly. We have also seen that studies suggest that a flexible diet, predominantly vegetarian with the inclusion of meat, is possibly the most environmentally optimal. Furthermore, it would seem that the production and consumption of veggie alternatives avoids (direct) animal suffering and exploitation. One possible conclusion from all of this is a consequentialist ethics may propose, that producing, selling, and consuming veggie meat (and perhaps a limited quantity of meats that has had lives) is the right action because the action promotes as much as possible, and producing, selling, and consuming veggie meats meets as much good as possible.

An alternative ethics is aontology concerning what the right action is which expresses respect for all rational and autonomous beings; does not treat rational and autonomous beings as means but as ends in themselves. Since producing, selling, and consuming veggie meats do not violate the rights of rational and autonomous beings, then they are right actions. Furthermore, if we consider the issue of production and consumption of veggie meat in terms of deontic or consequential standards, veggie meats respect the rights of animals and humans and can be conducive to the greatest good for the greatest number. In this section, I would like to question such assumptions. The reason that such assumptions seem plausible, I argue, is due to the persuasive influence of certain ethical attitudes, such as the rights-view, neo-Kantianism, and utilitarianism. These ethical approaches may be referred to as act-centered theories because they are mainly concerned about right.

As mentioned earlier, virtually all ethical defenses of veganism are ultimately based, or rely heavily, on the concept of suffering. For example, the typical approach that utilitarian ethicists take is to claim that the goal of morality is to act in such ways as to maximize utility and minimize suffering. More specifically, we ought to act in such a way as produce the greatest overall good for the greatest possible number of sentient beings that benefit from it. The second step is to show that suffering, whether human or non-human, is bad. After all, non-human animals represent other species. If we value only our desires and wellbeing we will revive what some refer to speciesism, unjustified discrimination among species. Thus, all suffering must be minimized, and all preferences or happiness must be considered.

Therefore, the conclusion, according to utilitarian, we would be ought to avoid and shun food produced in such ways not because individual animals or people are special, or individuals are directly wrong, but because suffering is bad, and pleasure/happiness is good. Since animal-based food produces suffering, we ought to avoid animal-based food (this is the sort of argument that Singer (1975) and other vegetarians would produce). According to such a view, the production and consumption of veggie meets the test with fairly colors, and thus it is morally permissible (or at least not wrong) to producing, selling, and consuming veggie meets a lot more satisfaction of preference or happiness and a less suffering and dissatisfaction of preferences over animal-based food.

If the issue were framed in terms of a non-consequentialist argument, such as rights views or neo-Kantian defenses, the result would not change. Such approaches, unlike consequentialism, on concepts of right or duty. For example Tom Regan (1983) argues that all animals have a right to the same basic moral rights as humans. The point of such an argument is, as Regan says, and canonically all mammals such as cats, pigs, goats over a year of age possess the same basic moral rights as humans. Many animals are what Regan calls “subjects-of-a-life”, and they “subjects-of-a-life” precisely have inherent value, and it is immoral to use them for food. A neo-Kantian approach has been championed by Christine Korsgaard. In leisure Korsgaard (2004) argues that our autonomous nature is not the only source of duty, as Kant argues. In fact, we can say that we have duties towards non-human animals. The morals to ourselves arise from the fact that we experience pain and suffering, and while animals are not-legislative, rational creatures, like us, are creatures that experience pain and suffering. As Korsgaard puts it, “human ethics are simply the same as the other animals” (2004).
