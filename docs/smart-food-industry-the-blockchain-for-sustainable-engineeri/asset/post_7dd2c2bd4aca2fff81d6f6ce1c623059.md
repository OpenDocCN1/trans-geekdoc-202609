
Scaffolds investigated in the realm of cultivated meat include: decellularised tissue (Gershlak et al. 2017, Campuzano and Pelling 2019), fungal mycelium or chitin/chitosan, recombinant collagen or laminin (Campuzano and Pelling 2019) formed in a variety of topographies. Decellularised tissue maintains the animal free product selling point, would add insoluble fibre to meat products to aid digestion and enhance public health, and, being plentiful and easy to grow, could be incorporated into circular economies by utilizing plant-based food waste. Decellularisation with animal cells would make acquisition cheap and sustainable by cutting down energy for plant growth. Global food wastage is a large contributor to greenhouse gas emissions, responsible for 4.4 billion tonnes of CO2 equivalent annually (making up 8% of the total) (FAO 2015, Jwani et al. 2021), meaning utilisation of this waste is key to achieving a sustainable practice. However, decellularisation is an energy intensive process, requiring strong detergents and surfactants to strip the native cellular material (White et al. 2017). This would add layers of processing complexity and cost, it would force products to undergo toxicity analysis to ensure no traces of debris, and it would produce significant amounts of chemical waste which needs to be treated while consuming additional.

Fungal mycelium or chitin/chitosan are very similar in all respects but would require less land and less energy than green vegetation for decellularisation due to the lower light/nutrient requirements. Recombinant collagen or laminin would be the best option for differentiation and kickstarting morphogenesis due to their mimicry of physiological signalling pathways in vivo (Ahmad et al. 2020). However, it would relate to a much higher product price point at commercial sale due to expensive borrowings as are typically produced for medical applications meaning they need to be of a high purity which reflects their high costs.

## 4.3 Culture Medium

Cell medium is designed to support cell survival when they are removed from their natural environments and placed in vitro for expansion. Mammalian cells in vitro generally need a carbon source, usually glucose, nitrogen sources, i.e., amino acids, as well as vitamins, minerals and inorganic materials to maintain physiological osmolality. Growth factors are also essential as they activate signalling pathways and regulate behaviour. Animal-derived serum has been traditionally used as a medium in mammalian cell culture as it contains a mixture of proteins and smaller molecules for critical cell maintenance and viability. However, it becomes concerns because of how it is sourced. Additionally, it is a component subject to batch-to-batch variation and carrying a risk of viral, bacterial and endotoxin contamination (Gstrauth and der Valk, 2016, Fang et al. 2017). Meat production, ideally animal-derived serum should be replaced by non-animal and chemically defined alternatives of these because, but also as it is unsuitable for large scale production due to the volatility, which has appeared in the past few years (Kolmann et al. 2020). However, using an undefined component is problematic and with defined, recombinantly derived components is challenging and can only be achieved at high cost. Albumin.

pH buffers such as sodium bicarbonate or HEPES are often added to the medium formulation to negate the effects of acidic or basic products of cellular respiration (Arora 2013). Lastly, as an option, culture medium can also contain 1% antibiotic and/or antimicrobial to prevent bacterial or fungal contaminations. This approach is useful in the early-stage research where sterility is difficult to maintain, for example, post-cell isolation from tissue. However, using antibiotics in meat products presents more regulatory hurdles, with large markets such as the EU attempting to scale down their antibiotic use as per. From 2016 onwards, antimicrobial consumption in the EU in livestock populations has been lower than for humans (JIACRA III 2021). Avoiding antibiotics in medium prevents further spread of antibiotic resistance. There is also concern about the effect of antibiotics on gene expression, with a recent study identifying 209 PenStep-responsive genes, including transcription factors likely to regulate other genes (Ryu et al. 2017). Moreover, antibiotics were also found to have a detrimental effect on the growth of myoblastite cells with reduced proliferation by 26% over 6 months of culture (Kolkmann et al. 2020). The same effect was found when using adipose-derived MSCs (Skublas et al. 2017).

Serum-free media studies for expansion of myoblastite cells for cultivated medium production have been published to date (Kolkmann et al. 2020, Messamer et al. 2022). Kolmann et al. tested range of commercially serum-free media and two serum replacements for their ability to stabilize culture of bovine myoblasts. Serum-free media (SFM) tested Essential 8 Life (Technologies, Milenyen Biotech), StemPro SFM (Thermo Scientific), Stemmac HSC expansion (MesenCult Stem Cell Technologies), and TeSR E (Stem Cell Technologies), while the serum-replacements used Lipogo (RMBIO) and XerumFree (TNC Bio). When used with bovine myoblasts, they performed poorly probably because they were optimised for different species (i.e., human) and different cell types such as MSCs or PSCs. On the other, the Lipogo supplement was found to be successful as an FBS replacement for bovine myoblasts. However, it resulted in an adipocyte-like cell phenotype with fat droplets accumulation (Kolkmann et al. 2020).

Myogenic differentiation in serum-based cultures is typically induced by lowering the concentration of serum (serum starvation) (Messamer et al. 2022), while in a serum-culture, it would be induced by introducing ligands to receptors that are up-regulated during the initial stages of myogenic differentiation such as transferin, insulin, glucagon and lysophosphatidic acid (IPA). For adipogenic differentiation, typical cocktails include insulin, IMBX, hydrocortisone and dexamethasone (Hach Zachar 2021, Dafai et al. 2021, Dohman et al. 2023) used in combination or without serum.

Mammalian cells are not efficient in the way they metabolize nutrients such as carbon and nitrogen sources, producing a metabolic waste at certain concentrations (Schop et al. 2009, 2010, Post et al. 2020). Regular medium changes need to be applied, to remove the waste products and replenish the nutrients. To mitigate this, fed-batch or perfusion processes can be used to perform regular medium exchanges to remove the waste products and replenish the nutrients resulting in increasing cell yields (Europe et al. 2000) and a more effective cell medium, Additionally, medium composition can be optimized to reduce substrate fluctuations and more efficient consumption of nutrients. Strategies have been successfully used to optimize medium for cell lines in the production of biopharmaceuticals (Bell et al. 1995).

Cultured meat is a product that needs to have a price tag several orders of magnitude lower than biosimilars. Therefore, efforts of reducing the cost, increasing cell yields need to be investigated, stretching the current boundaries of cell culture methods applied to the production of biopharmaceuticals. Food grade or feed raw materials of low purity need to be explored for their performance in media formulations. Crude raw materials, such as protein hydrolyates from plant, alg or microbial sources are also potential candidates of nutrients to the cells. Hydrolysates can be produced with enzymatic or acid treatment of the alg, plant or microbial biomass and have proved effective in promoting cell growth (Ng et al. 2020, Ho et al. 2021). However, hydrolyates are also prone to variation and unless a high level or robustness in the production process is achieved, their use can result in significant differences in performance.

Recycling the culture medium is another strategy that can be applied to reduce costs and most efficiently use the raw materials. Recycling can be achieved through chemical or biological process.
