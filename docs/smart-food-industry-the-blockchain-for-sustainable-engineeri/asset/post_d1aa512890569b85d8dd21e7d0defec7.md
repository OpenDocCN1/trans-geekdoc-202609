## 2. A Typology of Low-carbon Food Across the Value Chain

With the increasing size of the global population, food production will need to double or triple by 2100 to meet the growing demand while maintaining the planet's environmental equilibrium to limit earth depletion (Crist et al. 2017). Enhancing food production, consumption, and waste management was pointed out among the diverse shifts that can take to avoid environmental consequences and transition to sustainability (Riple et al. 2017). Several innovative solutions across the value chain could join these steps, such as urban farming, genetic crop enhancement, unconventional alternatives, meal kits, dietary management, and food sharing applications. We present these low-carbon food innovations in a life-cycle-based typology, and we identify key acceptability factors among consumers.

### 2.1 Phase 1: Farming

Our typology identifies five life cycles: farming, processing, access and consumption, and waste management. To begin with, farming represents a sustainable toll on the environment through greenhouse gas emissions, chemicals residues and soil depletion (Aneja et al. 2008). To counter these effects, farmers have to resort to several solutions such as organic farming, local and urban farming, and genetically enhanced crops. Although these solutions hold potential for the environment, several issues arise at the time of commercialization to consumers. For instance, (Rana and Paul 2020), organic produce still have low market share and low customer demand relative to conventional food. The most recent findings in this regard reveal an existing intention-behavior gap from the side of the concerned consumers (Chekina et al. 2017, ElHhaffer 2020).

Another issue arises at the market: when consumers approach food labeled as sustainable, whether organic, or deriving from urban farming, they find it challenging to make sense of the food's environmental attribute. This intangibility is due to the distant nature of the ecological issues (Segev et al. 2015) and the individuals' lack of awareness towards environmental matters. For the genetic modification of crops, even when it has been approved by scientists and judged safe to consume, people are still hesitant to adopt food derived from these enhancements for the related unhealthiness and unnaturalness perceptions (Bryant and Barnett 2018).

### 2.2 Phase II: Production-Processing

At the production phase, agricultural, and natural resources are transformed, and packaged before exported to the markets. At this phase, sustainable energy can be used to run the machinery and production plants, such as solar thermal heat and clean energy solutions (Schnitzer et al. 2010). Still, companies stumble upon the intangibility challenge as this information is hard to communicate and explicit to consumers at the commercialization phase. Another low-carbon innovation in the production phase would include meat substitutes, as the production of beef meat is known for its relatively large carbon footprint (i.e., beef produces around 129 Kg of CO2 compared to 2 Kg for vegetable substitutes, See Nijdam et.. (2012) for a review). Insect-based meat, cultured meat and vegetable-based meat are an illustration of such alternatives.

But getting people to accept these alternatives is challenging. For example, consumers express disgust and repugnance when presented with edible insects, mainly because insects are associated with danger and diseases in Western cultures and less with food or health (Dagevos 2021). Likewise, intervention is less appealing to consumers than standard meat because of the perception of human health and taste. Hence, its unnaturalness provokes concern about its healthiness and safety. Taste and expectations also play a crucial role in consumers' acceptance of alternatives, as consumers recount that vegetable alternatives are often not tasty as meat (Boukid 2021). The same goes for cultured meat, as consumers expect it to be tasty, smell and look different than normal meat (Bryant and Barrett 2018, Hartmann and Siegrist 2017).

### 2.3 Phase III: Utilization (Distribution)

The next phase is acquisition and distribution, where the food product is distributed to retailers and merchandised for consumers. Here, sustainable innovations include eco-packaging, edible packaging, and eco-labeling. While eco-packaging offers a better alternative to conventional packaging in fast-moving food, consumers perceive it as less appealing, ineffective in protecting the content and less hygienic (2015). Edible packaging is still a lab-based innovation, which faces many challenges such as health, safety and toxicological effects (Jeiawan et al. 2020). To add to the contamination effects that influence product desirability and intention (Gunta and Coskun 2021). Ecolabeling, on the other hand, discloses its own acceptance challenges among consumers. Although the labeling system has been initiated as a medium to address consumers' distrust (Tonkin et al. 2015), research has revealed a back-firing consequence and evidence of distrust in the control (Nutavasih and Thogersen 2017).

### 2.4 Phase III and IV: Utilization (Consumption) and Waste Management

The final two stages of food's life cycle are consumption and waste management, whose main environmental contribution is to limit food waste and excessive consumption. Innovation in these phases addresses consumers' behavior change and often requires a technological component such as digital mobile applications for leftover sharing and restaurant. These innovations come with their own share of challenges. On one hand, the applications are not optimized for user experience and can be demeaning to use (Mu et al. 2019). On the other hand, food sharing and leftovers in a consumer-to-consumer sharing, without any seal or third-party guarantees, rises doubts about the safety and quality of the food (Falcone and Imbert 2017).

Furthermore, getting consumers to adopt digital apps requires an amount of effort and commitment from consumers and complicates their food choices. It implies changing their habits and regularly making deliberate choices, which is cognitively tiring. Overall, sustainable food choices are surrounded by a behavioral cost, which limits its attractiveness for consumers.
