Powdered milk has also been studied as a potential source of protein for the production of 3D printed food (Lille et al. 2018). A highly viscous ink was produced using 50 wt.% of skimmed milk powder, which hindered printing due to stickiness. # 2.2.3 Lipids

The structure and composition of triglycerides can modify the properties of inks and the functional properties of the 3D printed products, crystalline structure, and fat index (Godai et al. 2016).

Chocolate is the ingredient with the highest lipid composition used in 3D food printing (Lanaro et al. 2019, Manithal al. 2020). The reasons for choosing chocolate as an ingredient are its ability to be a viscous fluid that can easily solidifying controlling the demolding and packaging of the product after a cooling process (Hao et al. 2010). However, the use of high-quality chocolate for 3D printing is a challenge due to its softness and complex structure, which includes crystalline phases that must be controlled throughout the printing process to guarantee the quality of the final product.

Le Topic et. (2018) sought to understand the influence of the 3D printing process on the properties of processed cheeses. The results indicated that the melting and extrusion processing promoted by the 3D printing affected the properties of the cheese-melted and printed cheeses had hardness and higher melting compared to the untreated. It was assigned to control the microstructure lost due to food phase separation and the presence of fats.

Lipton et al. (2010) used bacon fat as a flavor enhancer to print turkey puree using tenderness as an advantage. The authors also used butterfat in a traditional pasta recipe to prevent the melting of the printed structures when tasted. Another interesting aspect related to the use of lipids for 3D printing is that they can act as a plastic or lubricant, which can help the extrusion, facilitating the flow of the ink, as observed by Lille et al. (2018).
