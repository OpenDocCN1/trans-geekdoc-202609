The culture of ESCs and IPSCs usually requires the use of expensive induction factors to guide the differentiation process into muscle tissue. Additionally, PSCs are prone to genetic instability due to the effect of extended cell culture and genetic manipulation (Yoshihara et al. 2017) which raises safety concerns and can complicate regulatory approval of cultivated meat products.

Other adult cells such as mesenchymal/stromal cells (MSCs) or fibrogenic-progenitors (FAPs) are alternative cell sources for cultivated meat. MSCs are versatile and can be isolated from muscle tissues including bone marrow, adipose, umbilical, cord and peripheral blood (Hill et al. 2019). Unlike MySCs and PSCs, MSCs do not require additional protein coatings or activation to enable their culture. ## 4.2 Scalable Culture Methods

To achieve economic success, the cultivated meat products need to be available and affordable. The availability of these products is directly linked to the scalability of the method used to produce them. Scalable cell culture methods include free-floating aggregates, microcarrier culture and encapsulation in hydrogels, whereas microcarrier-based bioreactor is typically used during the cell expansion phase for the generation of biomass, encapsulation in hydrogels is mostly used during the differentiation phase. Aggregate based cultures have been demonstrated as suitable for both the expansion and differentiation phases; microcarrier use can be extended to be used for differentiation and hydrogel encapsulation can also be conceivable for cell expansion as well (Torgan et al. 2000, Thakur et al. 2010, Park et al. 2014, Kim et al. 2019). All culture methods attempt to meet the environmental demands of adhered cell types in a scalable system.

### 4.2.1 Free-floating Aggregates

The first culture method that can be applied involves growing cells in "clumps" or aggregates (Mueller-Kieser 1987). This type of culture does not rely on cell adhesion to substrates, and it is only possible for specific cell types. When adherent cells are not provided with an attachment substrate, cells attach to one another and survive and proliferate through the activation of cadherin binding and self-production of extracellular matrix in situ (Laperle et al. 2015, Manibog et al. 2016). This approach of cell expansion, particularly when cultivated in scalable systems as bioreactors, is simplified and much advantageous from a downstream processing point of view. Cell-to-cell binding 100% purity of the output, allowing for higher cell densities and making simpler downstream process (harvesting and formulation) to lower the overall production costs and increase profit margins. However, there are certain concerns when growing cells as aggregates. For example, nutrient and gas diffusion become more difficult as the size of the aggregates increases, setting defined upper size limit for proliferation. If the size exceeds limit, necrosis will occur at the centre of the aggregate resulting in cell death, exposure to gradients that lead to spontaneous differentiation if the cells are potent, as well as heterogeneity in the cell population (van Winkle et al. 2012, Xie et al. 2017, Sart et al. 2017).

The successful culture as aggregates maintain a tight control over the size of the aggregates in order to avoid the possible issues. Different approaches have been reported to control the aggregate size including dextran sulphate (Lchitz et al 2018), adding heparin to the culture (Li et al. 2011), employing agitation to breakdown the aggregates (Takahashi et al. 2017) or encapsulating cells in hydrogel (Kim et al. 2019) will be further discussed more detail in one of the following sections of this chapter.

Linking back to the possible cell sources for production of cultivated meat, aggregate cultures are more common for pluripotent stem cells (ESCs and iPSCs) as they have inherent tendency to adhere to other rather than growing as single cells (van Winkle et al. 2012, Laperle et al. 2015, Sart et al. 2017, Lchitz et al. 2018, Noguira et al. 2020). A few studies were reported in literature looking at the possibility of producing myogenic cells as aggregates (Wei et al. 2011, Hosoyama et al. 2013, Boddeiu et al. 2020). However, doubling times of over 150 h were recorded that there was very limited proliferation. Another study performed on the C12 mouse myoblast line demonstrated that their culture as aggregates didn't result in proliferation and the cells were found to express markers of quiescent satellite cells (Aguano et al. 2019) is not suitable for the production of cultivated meat where cell proliferation is required. Similarly, a few studies demonstrated the ability to culture mesenchymal/stromal cells as aggregates in what are referred as 'mesenspheroids'. However it resulted in limited proliferation, but with an increased differentiation potential and maintenance of potency (Barniak and McDevitt 2012, Isern et al. 2013). This approach to their culture can be advantageous to clinical applications but might not be suitable for production of cultivated meat where extensive and fast proliferation is necessery.

### 4.2.2 Microcarriers

The second possible of scalable cell culture involves the use of scalable, substrates that are used in conjunction with bioreactors to provide a homogeneous and dynamic environment for the cells. These attachment substrates are known as microcarriers and are sized particles with different surface characteristics tuned to allow for cell attachment and proliferation.

The microcarrier is not new. It was first introduced 55 years ago when modified particles were used for the first-time to culture cells (van Wezel 1967). Then, a variety of microcarrier types with different sizes, different surface characteristics and porosities, with protein coatings and with without surface charges (Li et al. 2015, Tavassoli et al. 2018, Cher et al. 2020) have become commonly available. This variety of microcarriers and configurations were necessary to cover the wide range of applications and cell types. The core material and surface chemistry, as dictated by surface charges or inorganic coatings, are important factors that dictate attachment and proliferation. Their physico-chemical properties as density also play a role in the design of the bioreactor and cell harvesting step. Typically, the density of these microcarriers is slightly higher than water to allow for an easy suspension and fast sedimentation when required. Density influences the minimal agitation speed (Ns) is required for the suspension of microcarriers (Zwiestering 1958, Hewitt et al. 2011). For example, for denser microcarriers as Hillex II (SoloHill/Sartorius), the NS would be required to be higher to account for the increased density. On the opposite, the time required for sedimentation would be shorter for these microcarriers.
