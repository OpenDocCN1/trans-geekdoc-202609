
## Smart Food Industry: The Blockchain for Sustainable Engineering

One of the main challenges in Foodomics, along with food security, is to help the rational design and the development of novel foods for disease prevention and/or health improvement, and to be able to explain the molecular mechanisms with scientific proof. Extracting bioactive compounds is one of the first phases in the scheme of functional foods' design and, therefore, the design of green methods to concentrate or isolate them from natural sources is a very relevant research field in Foodomics. Obviously, the green preparation method when no sample is needed, but with samples related to Food Science, it's very far to be common. Green methods are eco-friendly alternative to chemical preparation methods; such as high-pressure techniques (pressurized liquid extraction (PLE), subcritical water extraction (SWE) and supercritical fluid extraction (SFE), and low-pressure techniques (microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), ultrasound extraction (UAE) and pulsed electric field (PEF)) are feasible for extracting target compounds from complex matrices (Demiran et al. 2017). These methodologies are based on the use of minimal quantities of solvents for extraction of target compounds, with selectivity. The use of pressure, temperature or other factor may enhance the efficiency and speed up the extraction processes. The use of one or other technique with a particular solvent depends, mainly, on the polarity of the target compound and/or the compressed fluid. Similia robust solvent also applies when dealing with compressed fluids. In temperature and/or the solvent can modify their physicochemical properties (density, diffusivity, viscosity, and dielectric constant), which also modify their selectivity and solving power, especially when dealing with supercritical fluid extraction. The selection of the method of sample preparation can be complicated, since it requires considering several parameters simultaneously. It is important to assess the chemical and physical properties of the compound of interest, including volatility, polarity, solubility and stability (thermal, oxidative).

Latest trends in the design of Green Foodomics processes for concentrating or extracting bioactive compounds emphasize not only the search for new, green-grade foods but also idea of multiple integrated processes, which can also be called biorefinery or downstream processing. The idea comes from chemical (petroleum) refining where different products are obtained from the same raw material in a single process. So, in biorefinery, they use this philosophy but from biological methods. In food science, one can be found in the process developed by Gilbert-López et al. (2015). They used supercritical fluid extraction to obtain carotenoids and the un-extracted material was sequentially treated with different solvents in polarity in order to extract as different things as possible, as can be seen in Fig. 4.

One of the best things of Green Chemistry is the possibility of showing "how green is a process"; in fact, there is a whole discipline called "green metrics" to quantify it. Nevertheless, despite green metrics being highly useful in analytical chemistry, they are not used very. Green metrics offer qualitative and quantitative data on the real environmental impact of analytical methods and help identifying "black points" of the analytical method under development. Several methods have been developed of varying complexity, namely, and analytical-namely Eco-Scale (Gahlusca et al. 2012), HPLC Environmental Assessment Tool (HPLC-AT) (Gaber et al. 2011), Green Analytical Procedure Index (API) and the Complex GAP software (Analytical Method Intensity (AMVI) (Hartman et al. 2021), among many others. These tools use different criteria from the Green Chemistry philosophy and the results are generally based on pictograms that picture the degree of agreement with evaluated criteria (López-Larente et al. 2022). But, probably the most powerful tool to quantify the environmental performance of chemical processes and, furthermore, analytical methods is LCA (Life Cycle Assessment). For LCA, analytical methods are considered independent processes, where inputs are samples, reagents, solvents, energy and, of course, instrumental methods. The boundaries of the LCA measurement can be done by step by step (i.e., clean up, derivatization, conservation, measurement, selection (or disposal) considering the method as a whole, the first way has the advantage of identifying "the blackest point" of method. LCA is a complex methodology, and we encourage those who learn about it to read the book of Horne et al. (2009) or Curran et al. (2016).
