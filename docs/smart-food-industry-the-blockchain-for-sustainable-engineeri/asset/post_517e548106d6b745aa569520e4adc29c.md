## 2.12 Onion Waste and By-products

The major waste and by-products discarded from industrial processing of onion are skin, the other few leaves and the top bottoms (Campone et al. 2018). Specifically, the major bioactive compounds found in onion skin are quercetin and quercetin glycosides having antimicrobial, antioxidant, cancer prevention, and anti-inflammatory effects. Pereira et al. (2017) studied on the utilization of onion waste for production of Pleurotus sajor-caju fruiting bodies and pectinases. They found a feasible substrate for *P. sajor-caju* cultivation and obtained pectinase, an interesting industrial characteristic of onion waste. There are many studies regarding the extraction of quercetin from onion wastes (Kim et al. 2019, Santiago et al. 2020, Wiesowska et al. 2021). It is reported that 400 mg quercetin/kg can be produced from onion wastes. Similarly, quercetin and derivatives in onion leaf were evaluated as natural food colorant in yogurt milk. The findings indicate that it is possible to use onion waste extracts as a natural colorant. Moreover, Chen et al. (2021) developed an alternative food source for honey bee by biconversion of onion waste into biogas. Biomedical and pharmaceutical applications have also been evaluated as a different valuation strategy for onion peel. As an example, Kumar et al. (2022) highlighted that onion peel extracts can exhibit health promoting effects such as antimicrobial, anticancer, (breast and liver cancer cells), diabetic, hypercholesterolemia and obesity, supported by in vitro.

## 3. Cereal and By-products

Cereals such as corn (*Zea mays L.'), wheat (*Triticum aestivum*), rice (*Oryza sativa*), sorghum (*Sorghum vulgare*), oat (*Avena sativa*), rye (*Secale cereale*), and barley (*Hordeum vulgare*) are among the basic foods of the agro-food industry, which have been harvested since ancient times (Osorio et al. 2021). Due to the changes in eating habits, cereal products such as bread, biscuits, and although have become highly preferred products in recent years, comprising up to 20% of the daily diet (Bel et al. 2019, Gutierrez 2019). According to FAO, worldwide cereal production increased from 2.49 to 2.91 billion tons between 2009 and 2018, with a growing rate of 16.87%. Maize (corn) is the most produced cereal with 1.15 billion tons, followed by wheat (766 million tons) and rice (755 million tons). These three cereals account for almost 90% of the worldwide cereal production. However, the production and transportation of cereals cause enormous solid waste formation as seeds, grain, germ, herm and waste streams including condensates, washing water and other solutions (Shehu et al. 2019). Overall, almost 19% of the production is lost during post-harvest and distribution stages (Ganesh et al. 2021).

### 3.1 Waste Fractions Formed During Processing

The increase in global food production and accordingly discarded waste has increased the relevant studies on various applications and evaluation of discarded food waste. Grain waste has a potential as a source of fibrous biomass due to its high carbohydrate and fiber content. Reducing cereal waste may provide practical advantages such as redefining the cereal production chain, increasing the efficiency and improving the economy gain. Bioconversion processes also have environmental advantages such as reducing emissions (in terms of carbon and chemical emissions) and ecological footprints (Grippo et al. 2019). At the same time, these processes enable raw materials to be used for the production of biodiesel, paper, animal feed, ethanol, and other contribute to the environmental sustainability (Gutierrez 2019). Due to these reasons, valorization attempts in academic and industrial fields are increasing by day with the aim of obtaining value-added products from cereal waste.

According to their properties, specific operations are required for the processing of cereals such as size reduction, dehusking, milling and sieving (Osorio et al. 2021). During the processing of cereals, some streams such as bran, hull, cob and straw are released as by-products (Fig. 4). On average, 9.6 million tons of maize corn, 120 million tons of rice, 730 million tons of rice, 59 million tons of wheat and 51.3 million tons of bran are generated globally (Deshal et al. 2021).

Corn (Zea Mays L.), which is the most produced grain in the world, is the primary food in many countries (Wahed et al. 2020a). 64% of the corn production is utilized for the animal feed industry, 19% for human consumption and 9% for the food processing industry, nearly 55% of the corn produced is waste (Kim and Dale 2004). Waste products such as corn cobs, leaves, shells, and roots are generated during harvesting and post-industrial distribution of corn (Fig. 5). The ratio of waste streams to the mass of corn is reported as 18.1% for stem, 12.9% for leaves, 8.8% for root (Deshal et al. 2021). Rice (*Oryza sativa L.*) is one of the most important grain products, which is a significant source for a quarter of the population (Alexandri et al. 2020). The process steps of milled rice, known as white rice, includes harvesting, cleaning, husking, and polishing (Fig. 7). During these steps, some waste products such as bran, germ and straw may be formed (Alexandri et al. 2020). The husk is the outer layer of the grain and is not edible, and is removed during the de-husking process. Rice bran is a wasted product discarded in milling, and is obtained from the outer layer of rice. On average, by-product fraction of rice is 20% rice bran, 2% germ and 8% bran. In addition, 0.4–1.4 kg of straw is released for every kilogram of milled rice (Alexandri et al. 2020).
