## 4. Pseudocereals: Abiotic and Biotic Stresses Factors

Yields of mainstream crops have plateaued during the past 30 years due to both abiotic and biotic factors of production. This trend casts doubts over the capacity of doubling production to meet the needs of a growing population by 2050. The situation is alarming in areas where average crop yields are reducing due to complex interaction between the current production practices, soil degradation, biotic stress and climate change and variabilities. Pseudocereals have been reported to be climate resilient albeit limited data for detailed analysis and narrow exposure of the superfoods to high intensity and extreme weather conditions. The production practices of pseudocereals need to be fine-tuned to tackle impending threats from climate change, soil degradation and biotic stresses.

### 4.1 Climate Change

In marginal areas, cereals include heat and drought stresses. Germplasm diversity in quinoa is a leading explanation why the crop has high tolerance to drought and other climatic conditions (Ruiz et al. 2014). Amaranth has also exhibited tolerance to a range of extreme weather conditions. It has salinity and drought tolerance, and this can be exploited to extend the geographic regions where food can be obtained. Relative to other crops, amaranth has been grown in conditions where cereals will perish due to climatic conditions (Olufolaji et al. 2010, Grundy et al. 2020, Ebert et al. 2011, Mlakar et al. 2009).

With regards to climate change, there are wide gaps which need attention to climate resilience of pseudocereals. A systematic review is required to explore the quality of available data and to explore its use in developing systems which could include pseudocereals. Climate resilience information is also important to define niche and mega environments in which most locations where cereals are hard by climate change, alternative crops which include pseudocereals are needed.

### 4.2 Soil Degradation

Pseudocereals harvested from natural stands and those from agricultural fields are affected by extensive production practices where limited inputs are added systematically and have net nutrient mining dominates. Coupled with low inherent soil fertility, nutrient mining is considered one of the major limiting factors in the smallholder farming systems. Similarly, an increasing limiting productivity of many groups is soil salt content because of salt water management or due to salt parent material. Reg salt affected soil, salinity stands out as the problem, followed by soil acidity and in soil is an impediment to production of crops.

#### Soil salinity

Majority of mainstream cereals, maize, and wheat are not tolerant to soil and water salinity. Pseudocereals show some tolerance to water and soil salinity and therefore the latter could be used in developing solutions to an increasing salt problem. More work is required in this area to determine the benefits from such interventions.

### 4.3 Biotic Stress

Biotic stress on major crops has reduced production by as much as 60% of the genetic potential. All three factors, i.e., diseases, pests and weeds have been found to cause major losses. The management of biotic stress is important for both cereals and pseudocereals. Data on improved production practices on pseudocereals is scarce owing to limited research and this situation hampers the discussions on the development of the crops. The similarities in the species range that infect the two groups of crops have not been widely published. More work is required in this area to characterize tolerance levels and remedial practices' effectiveness.

## 5. Value Chain Development

Agricultural systems' improvement in recent years have targeted efficiencies across all components of the value chain. a huge amount of effort will be required to improve value chain functionality. Several factors have been identified as breakers in improving amaranth, buckwheat, and quinoa value. These include, but are not limited to, first, input market weaknesses, i.e., limited high yielding seeds, seed formulations for improved productivity, and access to agrotechnicals. Second, production constraint which will include improved agronomic practices, low mechanization, and poor biotic stress management. The consumer demand for pseudocereals products is narrow and found in small pockets, market linkages are few and the unit price of produce is not supported by major distributors. From farmers, extension personnel, and researchers in both public and private sectors (Aderibge et al. 2014; Hall et al.). Pseudocereals' value chains have a huge capacity to contribute to the food systems' delivery and complement a handful mainstream crops which the world currently relies on. Current underutil is related to value chain bottlenecks, especially seed systems, distribution networks and the output market structures.

### 5.1 Seed Systems and Input Markets

Improved genetic performance through breeding has contributed to more than 60% of production capacity developed during the first green revolution era. Beyond the success of the 1960–1970s, several breeding objectives were followed resulting in variational development targeting a range of environments. Improved high yielding varieties introduced into the input market systems increased the production potential for crops-based starch, protein, vitamins, and minerals sources for both humans and livestock, however other supportive services that shape diversity and functional systems were not attended to until recently towards the end of the 20th century. For instance, farmers through private sector agro-dealer networks was not promoted in limited areas. Similarly, others that ensure sustainable production such as fertilizers and agrochemicals remained unavailable or too costly for most farmers, limiting utilization. With increased awareness of the importance of seed systems and related inputs, there is need for such provisions for pseudocereals in the 21st century if dietary diversity, food, and nutrition are to be achieved. Access to improve high yielding and climate smart pseudocereals is central to their contribution to current agriculture value.

Breeding for high yielding pseudocereals has not been supported widely because of deemed limited importance to pseudocereals and many other species outside the mainstream crops currently utilized for food, feed, and fiber. To advance the case of pseudocereals, breeding programs can take advantage of species diversity found in both domesticated cultivars and harvested from natural (in situ) stands in traditional communities. Modern and advanced breeding techniques, developed using mainstream crops, can be unleashed on potential. However, time and resources for successful breeding programs need proactive initiatives, policy support and the buy-in of value chain players with we use of utilizing pseudocereals to supplement the mainstream crops for improved food and nutrition security across the various socio-economic classes of society.

Current support for services for pseudocereals can be described as skewed towards a few interest-groups, dedicated foundations, in advanced research centers and universities to promote the marginalized communities with access to some of the species, the number of requests for pseudo cereals' accessions from gene banks and other formal sources show minimal traffic when compared to mainstream crops and legumes. Data on breeding effort and progress over time is rare and scattered for the whole range of pseudocereals, making systematic temporal analysis difficult at present. However, information on genetic variability in domesticated cultivar such as amaranth, buckwheat and quinoa seem to be widely available in literature. Similarly, there is evidence of whole or partial sequencing for a few cereals. Coker et al. (2022) reported the importance and participation of farmers' groups in community seed bank activities and building. Several seed systems' have been organized to support seeds of the main crops in the recent past and once of convincing the need for supporting pseudocereals and superfoods, the collaborative effort of farming communities, advanced research institutions, national breeding programs and private sector seed houses will be required.

A range of pseudocereals are important in developing improved agricultural practices for the crops. Most important is the provision of the suitable inputs for specific sites as guided by the interactions with biophysical factors of production. To improve efficiencies, there is need for mechanization across pseudocereals' value chains. Mechanization has been identified as the missing link in the mainstream crop value chains and its improvement was closely linked to both food and nutrition, and income security of producers.

At production centers and in communities where pseudocereals are currently produced, documented distribution systems are underdeveloped.
