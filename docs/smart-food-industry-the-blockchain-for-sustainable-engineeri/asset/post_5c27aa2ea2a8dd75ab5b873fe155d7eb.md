Perhaps the most surprising of the dietary paradoxes is the existence of obesity and malnutrition, very common in developing countries. Developing countries currently lack public resources to fight hunger and rapidly spreading diseases to gain weight. Policies to combat obesity include public education campaigns, promotion of healthy physical activity, implementation of mandatory labeling on food, taxing measures on foods high in fat, sugar, and salt, and restrictions on advertising aimed children and adolescents (Agostino et al. 2018). On the other hand, micronutrient deficiencies are often underestimated. Mineral deficiencies are common in low and middle-income countries and cause health problems including anemia, atrophy, and infant mortality (Tamanihardo et al. 2007).

When it comes to a healthy diet, options differ widely and many debates about what constitutes a healthy diet will likely continue. World Health Organization (WHO) is about updating its guidelines on fat and carbohydrate intake, which includes recommendations on dietary fiber as well as fruit and vegetables. WHO (2017) strongly recommends that all individuals should reduce their intake of free sugars to less than 10% and total energy intake to no more than 5%, and even advises to reduce salt intake in foods and switch from trans fat to polyunsaturated fat. However, what is clear that the increasing amounts of processed food and junk food consumed in recent years have contributed to an increased incidence of diabetes and cardiovascular disease.

According to the WHO, more than 1.9 billion adults, aged 18 and over, are overweight. Of these, more than 650 million are obese. Approximately 39 million children under 5 years of age were overweight or obese in 2020, and more than 340 million children and adolescents between 5 and 19 years were overweight or obese in 2016 (WHO 2021). Maternal and child obesity are increasingly contributing to obesity, diabetes, and non-communicable diseases in adults. These high prevalence rates of these conditions and the heavy burden of morbidity, disability, and death can pose a threat and create a devastating financial problem, straining a country's health services and harming its economic well-being. Therefore, action is needed to resolve this alarming problem (Agovino et al. 2018).

Certainly, access to nutritious food has been compromised by price volatility, conflict, and social inequalities are the main factors for food and nutrition insecurity. That's because more than 1 billion people live in extreme poverty and 870 million are undernourished (Mova-Patera et al. 2019).

In this sense, some partnerships have emerged to develop practices in the formation and offerings of healthier food. Some of these include the Global Alliance for Improved Nutrition (GAIN), a non-profit organization that fights malnutrition and increases access to nutritious food, especially for vulnerable communities. GAIN has supported initiatives like NutricRice, which makes rice as nutritious as possible, developed by DSM and Bühler Group. Examples of large companies include Danone's acquisition of a company specializing in organic food and beverages (WhiteWave) and Nestle, which acquired a vegetable protein manufacturing company (Sweet Earth). Nestle also recently announced new standards in its mixing (Fixing Earth).

Although, some countries are making progress in accessing and introducing healthy food at the national level. For example, Brazil has combined public policies and programs to combat the root causes of hunger (such as the Zero Hunger Program, Food Purchase Program, and the National School Feeding Program) (Henz and Porino 2017). France and Italy introduced legislation to prevent food waste and actions that prioritize the redistribution and donation of edible food by affiliated supermarkets. These initiatives dramatically reduced food waste that was later implemented by other countries such as Australia, Canada, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Slovenia, and the United States.

Undoubtedly, the most important practice today, in addition to fighting hunger, is balanced diet. The Food Sustainability Index (FSI) features several indicators on how to achieve sustainable performance (WHO 2021).

Finally, based on data from Global Health (2018) (Fig. 2), Japan and Cyprus have the best ranking. In contrast, Nigeria, Mozambique, and Sierra Leone are among the worst performers. Indeed, progress in ending all forms of malnutrition is limited and remains a concern for most regions and nations.

The good news is that food production is enough to feed everyone on the planet. However, although this is true, it will not be possible to end hunger or meet the global demand for food without addressing the high level of food loss and waste (Ortiz-Gonzalo et al. 2021).

Undoubtedly, leaving aside the issue of food loss and wastage is also missing chances to improve food security and environmental-prosperity, which are faces of the same coin. As for the environment, it is important to highlight that the way we produce and consume food today represents one of the greatest threats to the planet. That's because the loss and wastage of food also mean wasting natural resources. Food production has a huge impact on climate change and livelihoods. And since third of everything that we waste is wasted, a third of that is generated without reason (Li et al. 2022).

In North America, around 168 million tons of food are wasted each year. The loss and wastage of food per capita in Canada, the United States, and Mexico are approximately 396, 415, and 249 kg/person/year, respectively. Most of it is wasted at the consumer level (40%), followed by the pre-harvest stage (29%), processing (12%), post-harvest (10%), and distribution, retail, and service (9%). Some of the cases of food loss and wastage at these stages are:
- (i) inaccurate supply and demand forecasts
- (ii) classification standards for size and quality
- (iii) cold chain deficiencies
- (iv) inadequate infrastructure and machinery
- (v) damage during production and transportation
- (vi) remittances rejection
- (vii) strict management
- (viii) excess inventory
- (ix) inadequate handling and storage (CEC 2017, Chauhan et al. 2021).
