# Microalgae as Food Source

## 1. Introduction

Meeting the growing demand of the world population for nutritious diets is a global challenge this century. To address the risks of famine and food insecurity, contemporary food discourses center on the role of food systems in improving human nutrition (Chaudhary et al. 2018, Goden et al. 2021). Based on this understanding, microalgae-based products represent an opportunity for the global food system to nourish the world without exceeding planetary boundaries. As result, this market presents around 150 new companies each year (Dept et al. 2020). However, despite projections of promising growth, large-scale production is still questionable. These barriers are attributed to (i) economic viability, (ii) low production capacities, (iii) legislative and regulatory issues, and (iv) sensory characteristics (Lafarga 2019). Despite the numerous challenges to be overcome, the production of microalgae-based foods is a reality. Microalgae have been highlighted for their nutritional potential associated with the presence of bioactive specialties (Souza et al. 2019, Jacob-Lopez et al. 2019, Rahman 2020, Nörberg et al. 2022). Examples of microalgae-based products include plant-based foods, functional beverages, dry powders, capsules and tablets, which are marketed worldwide (Lafarga et al. 2019).

Based on the above, the purpose of this chapter was to provide an overview of microalgae-based products. Here, we cover topics related to cultivation, nutritional composition and bioavailability, products enriched with microalgae biomass, and finally, regulatory framework and legislation.

## 2. Cultivation Strategies for Microalgae Production

Historically, circular ponds and open raceway ponds are classic types of photobioreactors that have been practiced at large scale for the commercial production of microalgae (Fu et al. 2021, Jacob-Lopes and Franco 2010, 2018). These are characterized by their cost-effectiveness and simplicity of operation. However, the major bottlenecks are the external contaminants and the vulnerability to seasonal intervention, which directly inhibits their productivity (Mehariya et al. 2021).

Despite the numerous challenges to be overcome, raceway ponds are undeniably well established in the industrial production of microalgae such as Nannochloropsis sp., Chlorlla sp., Tetraselmis sp., Dunaliella linearina, Scenedesmus sp., Haematococcus pluvialis and Phaeodactylum tricornutum (Kumar et al. 2021). For example, raceway ponds present a promising model for industrial production of astaxanthin, found substantially in species such as Haematococcus pluvialis. Average astaxanthin yields are 10 mg dry wet in a 100 m² space (Zhang et al. 2009, Yu et al. 2022).

On the other hand, closed-loop photobioreactors provide a system with controlled conditions (Fu et al. 2021). In particular, tubular closed-loop photobioreactors have been highlighted for their industrial relevance in microalgae production systems (Lam et al. 2018, Yu et al. 2022).

As a result, tubular photobioreactors have been designed, including horizontal, vertical, conical, horizontal inclined types made of plastic or glass tube in which microalgae cultures were recirculated with either a pump or air technique (Kumar et al. 2021).

The improved design allows a controlled environment, high biomass production (up to 10 times more than the open pond), low evaporation rate, reduced risks of contaminants, and efficient transfer of nutrients (Kumar et al. 2021).

By comparison, studies Benidas et al. (2013) evaluated the biomass production by Phaeodactylum tricornutum in consolidated systems. The average closed-loop photobioreactors (1.0 g L⁻¹) values outperform raceway ponds (0.6 g L⁻¹). However, the critical point is the high capital and operating costs that limit the scaling of this system. Also, closed photobioreactors affect light quality and, consequently, trigger effects on microgal productivity (Yu et al. 2022).

Despite the photosynthetic nature of microalgae, some species have the potential to grow in the dark. Heterotrophic systems allow the use of conventional fermenters (i.e., stirred tank reactors), which operation and maintenance are considered more straightforward, reducing the production costs (Perez-Garcia et al. 2011, Hu et al. 2018). When using this cultivation method, the productive capacities are superior to the photautotrophic systems in biomass production (Lopes et al. 2020). The main interests with this condition are related to the substantial content of lipids. Especially the species Cryptochenium boomii has been grown in a heterotrophic environment for commercial production of long-chain unsaturated fatty acids (Chew et al. 2018, Lam et al. 2018).
