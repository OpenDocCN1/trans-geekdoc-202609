Other non-ible lignocellulosic residues, such as wheat straw and corn cob, can be used as feedstocks for production of biofuels (ethanol or butane), an indirect way to convert them to energy. Furthermore, non-edible wood or lignocellulosic feedbacks can be biochemically or thermochemically converted (e.g., via pyrolysis or gasification) to many different forms of energy. Some innovative options of this type are summarized by BRISK (Duarte et. 2020).

Food producing-by products are mixtures of substances of which might have specific functionalities when used as food ingredients by themselves, and are, therefore, valuable. Extracting these molecules from the wastes is the preferred waste valorization method since it adds more to a by-product than using it for energy (directly or indirectly), or as animal feed. There are processes that partially address this valorization approach (a review of which is provided, among others, by Moates et al. (2016)). For example, citrus fruit peels or apple pomace, both of which are utilized are found in Miraba et al. (2013), Dimou et al. (2019), Saini et al. (2019), Pires et al. (2021), Sharma et al. (2021) and many other research papers. These novel processes are developed in a lab scale and then scaled to industrial level using process tools, in order to perform a Technological Economic Analysis (TEA) and evaluate the processes and the feasibility of such a process. This includes chitosan production from shrimp waste (Gómez-Rodríguez et al. 2017), arabinoxyl and co-extraction of lipids and fats from fish skins (Kanyamakidis et al. 2015), production of xylitol, lactic acid, activated carbon and phenolic acids from sweet grain (Massa et al. 2013), production of plasticizer, lactic acid and animal food from waste (Kwan et al. 2015, 2018), biodiesel and ethanol co-production from lipid-producing sugarcane (Huang et al. 2016), anthocyanin extraction and ethanol production from purple and blue corn (Somavat et al. 2018), mango processing waste-integrated biorefinery (Arora et al. 2018), astaxanthin production from agro-industrial wastes (Duarte et al. 2020), integration of whey-products in cheese-making processes (Gomez et al. 2020), and many others.

In most of the above references (and many others not presented here), process simulation is used to assess the techno-economic feasibility of large scale production encompasses the proposed technologies. Waste may contain many valuable substances and the question which of one is technically and economically feasible and environmentally to extract does not have an obvious answer. At the same time, there may exist multiple alternatives for processing the same waste leading to different products or determining the optimal split of the waste throughput between the alternative processes is a frequent question that arises. For all these reasons, computer-aided process design is a valuable tool in assessing waste valorization processes.

In this chapter, two waste valorization cases are presented, simulated and analyzed: a pectin production from citrus fruits and an isobutanol production from corn stover. These processes were simulated with the SuperPro Designer v12 by Intelligent, Inc. (www.intelligent.com). SuperPro Designer can be used to efficiently model any processing mode (batch, semi-batch or continuous) in a wide variety of industries including food and food waste valorization processing.

The two case studies represent different operation modes (continuous for the pectin case, batch for isobutanol), different processing approaches (chemical for pectin, bio-chemical for isobutanol) and generate products of different nature and use (ingredient in the pectin case, bio-fuel in the isobutanol case).
