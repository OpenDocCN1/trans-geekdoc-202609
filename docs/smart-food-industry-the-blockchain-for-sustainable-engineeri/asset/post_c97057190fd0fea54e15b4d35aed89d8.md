
## Process-targeted Responsibilities

- Understanding the importance of food processing technologies for the production of safe, nutritious and organoleptically acceptable foods
- Applying 'Industry 4.0' technologies such as digitalisation, data analytics, robotisation and automation
- Use of more sustainable food processing technologies instead of traditional methods (e.g., green technologies as ultrasound, high-pressure process, green cloud point extraction, pulsed electric fields, green extractors, membrane processes and cold gas plasma)
- Choosing more energy-efficient methods for industrial processes and recommending efficient methods for food preparation at home
- Application of novel fermentation techniques to develop value-added ingredients and food products
- Optimising the parameters of processing techniques (temperature, time, pH, etc.) to reduce the loss of nutrients and bioactive compounds
- Applying processing techniques that consider the food matrix and the function of the constituents
- Carrying out R&D studies to produce nutritionally optimised and sensorially appealing ingredients and food products
- Customisation of food processing to consumer requirements (utilisation of the PAN concept - Preferences, Acceptance and Needs of the consumers)
- Increasing water/resource use efficiency and minimising waste

## Nutrition-targeted Responsibilities

- Use of sustainable and novel alternative protein sources (e.g., algal biomass, insects, and cultured biomass) to reduce the consumption of animal foods
- Modifying fat and simple sugar content in food formulations or replacing them with healthier options
- Enrichment with minerals, proteins, and bioactive compounds
- Reducing caloric value of foods by facilitating structural psycho-chemical changes in the food matrix
- Characterisation of accessibility and bioavailability of nutrients and phytochemicals in processed foods with consideration of food matrix effect
- Recovery of valuable components as bioactive compounds from food waste
- Adopting smaller serving sizes for packaged foods (El and Simsek 2012, Fasolin et al. 2019, August et al. 2020, Knorr and August 2021, 2022, Jagtrap et al. 2021)

Industrial-scale food processing enables the preservation and transformation of raw crops into edible, safe, healthy, sustainable and nutritious food products (Fasolin et al. 2019). However, negative perceptions about the food industry and processed foods, accompanied by a biased approach to the role of food processing in ensuring food safety and food diversity, still exist. This could be addressed by sharing more information with consumers on ultra-processed, minimally processed and unprocessed foods, as classified by the NOVA system (Monteiro et al. 2019). Bhrawra et al. (2021) developed a tool, Food Processing Knowledge (FoodProK) score, which determined consumers' knowledge about the healthiness of processed foods to different extents. Such tools can help improve processed food categorisation in NOVA systems.

## 4. Novel Protein Sources

With the limited protein sources we currently have, it is impossible to meet the protein requirement of the increasing population. The daily amount of protein (animal or vegetable) per capita is estimated at 52 g in 2030 and 54 g in 2050 (FAO 2017, FAO IAF UNICEF WPO 2020). Many national DGs recommend meeting protein requirements from various foods that support health and sustainability. For example, in the 2015–2020 Dietary Guidelines for Americans, the "protein foods" group consists of meat, poultry, seafood, beans and peas, soy processed products, nuts and seeds (USDA 2015).

The E-Lancet reiterates that humanity no longer consume more food than necessary (Willett et. 2019). The consumption of animal-based foods is more dominant in developed countries such as the USA than in developing countries. These countries must adopt more plant-based foods and novel protein sources to maintain adequate protein intake (Karmaus and Janes 2021). However, when making recommendations for developed and underprivileged countries, amino acid (AA) composition and digestibility of proteins must be considered (WHO 2007). This is because protein deficiency occurs when the diet provides little protein or lacks essential amino acids (EAAs). Health problems caused by protein deficiency, especially in the long-term, are severe with growth inhibition, impaired kidney and brain function, early aging, poor immunity, loss of muscle mass, and inadequate nutrient absorption (IU 2020).

Diet and environment have been identified as motivating factors in reducing meat consumption in European Union (EU) countries. However, this differs between consumers in northern and southern European countries with varying degrees. Europe, which consists of the richest countries with the largest share in economic and social sustainable development, the majority of consumers acknowledged their role in reducing meat as part of HDAS [sic*] (De Boer and Aiking 2022). A study in the UK investigated the consumption of plant-based alternative foods (vegetables, nuts and seeds) and compared it with meat and dairy reduction. The study reported that from 2008 to 2019 the consumption rate of plant-based foods, plant-based alternatives and plant-based milk increased (approximately three times) while meat intake was significantly reduced. Although plant-based foods are expected to be more popular and consumed more widely, ultra-processed forms of these products cannot contribute to a healthy diet and may lead to other health problems (Ala2022).

The food industry seeks alternative protein sources for human consumption in global concerns about food security and the growing population. New protein sources such as seaweed and algae, peas and insects have received interest over the past decade. While numerous R&D projects are ongoing, these sources should be critically evaluated for protein quality. More studies should be conducted on the metabolic behaviour, health effects, and cognitive functions (van Spiegel 2013, Karmaus and Janes 2021). Further studies should consider safety aspects such as toxicity, allergy and contaminants (ESA 2020).

In the light of the nutritional recommendations stated in the framework of HDRs by Willett et. (2019), the rest of this chapter will discuss scientific advancements about new protein sources (insects and microalgae) and innovative applications to reduce the sugar content of foods.

### 4.1 Insects

The most restrictive criterion for filtering foods is the high energy/protein ratio. In general, edible insects are accepted as sources of quality protein with high protein content and EAAs, also essential fatty acids, vitamins (A, D, thiamine, riboflavin, folic acid) and minerals (iron, calcium and zinc) (Van Huis 2013). In 2018, EFSA (European Food Safety Agency) approved the consumption and sale of insect foods in the EU states with the “New Food Regulation” (No 2015/2283) (European Commission 2015). It also issued an opinion on the safety of dried yellow mealworms (Turck et al. 2021), frozen and dried formulations from migratory locust (Turck et al. 2021), and frozen and dried formulations from house insects (Turck et al. 2021). After the EU Regulations, the consumption of insects as an alternative food source in member countries is expected to become increasingly widespread.

Edible insects such as crickets (Acheta domesticus), mealworms (Tenebrio molitor), mop wasps (Graphania bimella), and black soldier larvae (BSFL) (Hermetia illens), are considered good alternative protein sources with high protein content. They have a high feed conversion ratio, low environmental footprint and high economic value (Folin et al. 2019, Bessa et al. 2020, Math020).

There are more than 200 edible insect species, and their nutritional values vary widely between them. There is insufficient information about the nutritional composition of insects due to their diversity. The protein content of larvae, grasshopper, termite and bee vary between 35 and 77% of dry matter. However, insect digestibility differs widely due to chitin, a polysaccharide that is a major constituent of the exoskeleton. Some studies (Kim et al. 2019) showed in vitro protein digestibility of 7-8%, while 20% cricket flour was reported as 44% (Bas 2020). More studies are needed on the digestibility of insect protein and chitin in humans.

Even within the same species, the nutritional profile of the insects can differ depending on the growth conditions (environment, season and their diet). Köhler et al. (2019) reported that the Bombay locust (larva or adult), beetle, cricket and silkworm could be used as human food depending on their nutritional contents. AA scores range from 0.58 to 1.04 when the suggested pattern of FAO (2013) was used as a reference. Insects can be a good source of iron since it exists in a highly biologically available form. Buffalo mealworms were found to exhibit higher iron bioavailability over FeSO4 (Latitude-Daka 2016). Chitin and chitosan, abundant in the shells of insects, suppress pathogenic microorganisms in the intestines and as prebiotics (Stull et al. 2018, Montovska et al. 2012, Saadoun et al. 2022). These compounds also possess anti-nutritional effects such as binding minerals, dietary fats and fat-soluble vitamins and cause their excretion from the colon.

Baiano (2020) reviewed edible insects’ nutritional, microbiological, chemical properties and toxicological safety. They showed that insects could be biologically safe but might contain harmful residues such as heavy metals. Cross-reactions with allergens in some insects also need to be examined. More detailed studies on insects are required on their allergenicity, pesticide residue levels, bacterial contamination and heavy metal contents (such as arsenic, mercury and lead).

Using flour protein or extract as meals in food formations is a good strategy to increase consumer acceptance. Comprehensive data on their functional properties before being added to food is required. Their structural and functional properties that affect their technological and functional behaviours such as solubility, emulsification, oil and water binding capacity and gelling need to be researched (Gonzalez et al. 2021). During processing, these properties can cause changes in techno-functionality due to structural modifications affecting their nutritional properties positively or negatively.
