## 2. Key Factors of Carbon Footprint in the Food Supply Chains

Strengthening environmental responsibility, developing responsible methods and proactive communication between consumers and decision-makers are essential objectives of carbon footprint actors in food supply chains. However, to date, the main bottleneck for science involved is the information and tools they need to understand and influence footprint. Key issues such as how to effectively determine the environmental profile, especially carbon footprint. To this, market regulators follow the development and initiatives of actors in the processing chain, as guiding principles to identify potential hot spots in the processes, and as potential mere improvements.

As well known, the food supply chain is complex and nuanced as it progresses through each stage of the life-cycle. However, although there is oscillation of procedures, it is possible to consider a basic proceeding pattern that is commonly applied to the most different food products (Table 1).

As can be seen in Table 1, considering the large segments of the food industry, activity comes from agricultural practices. These, in turn, are mainly responsible for the natural resources; such as demand and contamination of water resources, and soil transformation, beyond the crops and soil fertilizations. Consequently, these processes result in direct impacts on the environment, mainly associated with environmental categories such as land use, greenhouse gases, nutrient depletion and demand for food resources and, therefore, considered demands in the contribution of the carbon footprint.

Among the food products affected by these categories are products of animal and vegetable origin, and the fertilization of crops and the soil, as well as the fertilization of animal feed, the main points associated with the impacts of eutrophication and acidification (terrestrial or aquatic). On the other hand, the steps considered downstream within the food industry differ according to the degree of processing of the raw material. For example, grain and cereal processing chains have the drying stage as a unit with greater environmental impact, due to high energy demand (Renzulli et al. 2015). The same profile can be attributed to the fruit and vegetable processing sectors.

On the other hand, the animal protein and meat products' sectors, as well as the dairy chain, present high-water consumption, release of effluents with a high content of organic components, and energy consumption as the main villains of the environment (De Borghi et al. 2021). Therefore, the stages of cooling carcasses and milk, and pasteurization are considered as the operations with the highest energy consumption.

Under these scenarios, it is important to emphasize that any activity that modifies or interferes with the environment plays an important role in terms of the carbon footprint. This is because even operations that do not directly involve carbon emissions, in their primary process, are also responsible for global carbonization. An easy-to-understand example is energy demand, which is the main factor in the food chain. Electric energy from coal processing is estimated to about 3.08 × 10⁻³ kg CO₂, kWh/Wh. On the other hand, alternative sources of renewable energy, in smaller partitions, also present carbon emissions with values of 1.05 × 10⁻⁵ and 2.50 × 10⁻⁶ kg CO₂, kWh/Wh for photovoltaic panels and wind energy, respectively (Depra et al. 2020).

Additionally, another variety present in the food chain is the soil fertilization process. Currently, nitrogen and phosphorus are estimated to be responsible for 4.94 and 1.88 kg CO₂, eq/kg. Therefore, being able to map the entire food chain enables an even deeper analysis.
