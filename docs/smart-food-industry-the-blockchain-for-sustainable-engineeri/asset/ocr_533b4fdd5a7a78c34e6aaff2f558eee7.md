# 1. What is Foodomics?

Before answering the question that this section touches, it is necessary to clarify some points dramatically and put it in context. Since the 1990s, chemical analysis of biological samples is turning dramatically. It was almost entirely focused on singles aspects of single molecules in each test. However, the evolution of analytical techniques and data science has made it possible to analyze thousands of molecules simultaneously, which has opened this way of work to study how these molecules interact with each other. Therefore, in order to easily identify this of work, the suffix -omics is added at the main.

Foodomics is a new global concept that includes all the working areas related to food and nutrition. The Foodomics concept was first cited in 2009 (Cifuentes 2009). In this sense, the Foodomics strategy is used to control safety, quality, traceability, and hygiene management of food and evaluate functional and nutritional factors in the field of food science (Valdés et al. 2021). This approach encompasses the use of multiple omics tools (see Fig. 1) as metabolomics, proteomics, transcriptomics, genomics and lipidomics, which allow comprehensive and high throughput information of quality, safety, composition and nutritional values of the studied complex (Bark et al. 2021).

In last years, techniques are increasingly gaining importance for validation and development in food technology, beside quality control of all the processes. A brief description of these tools is provided below.

Genomics is an interdisciplinary field of biology studying the structure, function, mapping, and editing of genomes (Kaur 2013). In this sense, the genome's complete set of DNA. The first genome was sequenced in 1997 by Frederick Sanger, who developed a sequencing technique for DNA to sequence the first complete genome, called phix174, which opened the doorway to the possibility in the field of genomics. In fact, his pioneer work in this field was awarded with the Nobel Prize in 1980 with Wally Gilbert and Paul Berg for pioneering DNA sequencing methods. Nowadays, genomics is segmented in two main areas: structural and functional genomics. The first one comprises the genomes of organisms, useful to detect evolutionary variations across genomes.
