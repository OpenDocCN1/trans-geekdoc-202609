# 2. Food Demand and Population

According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), one in nine people in the world (about 825 million people) do not have enough food to lead a healthy and active life, especially in China and India (FAO 2017, 2018, 2020, 2021a, Raheem et al. 2019). Food insecurity comes from the poorest classes not having access to the food needed for a healthy and balanced diet (Vilela and Purolnik 2015, Van Huis al. 2021). This problem has worsened with current variability and extreme weather conditions and spread of the COVID-19 pandemic, and it will worsen with conflicts and decline in some parts of the world, the current war between Russia and Ukraine.

It is estimated that the world population is expected to surpass 9.7 billion inhabitants by 2050, reaching 11 billion in 2100, and the demand for animal protein is expected to increase at astounding rates. Even with current trends in production growth over the next few decades, it will not be enough to meet the demands of a population that grows at the same time as it does in numbers (Meyer-Roch and Jun Jung 2020, FAO 2021b, Ojha et al. 2021); therefore, issues such as production capacity, demand, and resource depletion cannot be ignored (Mishna et al. 2021).

Nonetheless, population growth, the increase in per capita consumption, the expansion of cities in the coming decades, and the inability to meet the new human demands in terms of the content of proteins, especially those of animal origin, and restrictions on the productivity, primarily cultivate crops, exacerbate the issues regarding the supply of food in general (Saath and Fachinello 2018, Hunter 2021). In some countries, although there are areas available for agricultural expansion (Latin America and Sub-Saharan Africa), the demand must be met with increased productivity and/or crop replacement or even adjusting reassessing insect habits (Orsi et al. 2019).

Countries with the least agricultural land are likely to be the hardest by rising food demand. Populations that have already been struggling to find food will have an even worse situation in the coming decades, where 75% of their income will be used for food (Ojha et al. 2021). On the other hand, people in developed countries consume about 95 g of protein per day, of which almost 60% is of animal origin; this is a highly concerning piece of evidence, given that the protein in developing countries is only 45 g, 15% of which is insect (Abbas et al. 2016, Lange and Nakamura 2021).

The more significant use of alternative sources of protein, such as those of plant origin, has been suggested since these products promote human health. However, vegetables have several limitations, including anti-nutritional factors, amino imbalances, and high levels of non-starch polysaccharides (Mutungi et al. 2019, Longwane et al., Meyer-Roch and Jun Jung 2020). Food, in addition to nourish, provide health benefits, and these needs need to be met with the presence of bioactive compounds, which are present or incorporated into food, effectively all biological functions of the body. As alternative protein sources, insects are a potential substrate for obtaining these compounds, especially bioactive peptides (Matros and Casco 2021).

In the last 40 years, technological advancements and deforestation have allowed food production to grow in tandem with population growth (Saath and Fachinello 2018). However, the overexploitation of land, energy, and water will not only destabilize our ability to produce food but also the impact the food system has on the environment.
