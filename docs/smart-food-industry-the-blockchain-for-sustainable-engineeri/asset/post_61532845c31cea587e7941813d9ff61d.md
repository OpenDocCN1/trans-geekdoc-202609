## 1. Introduction 

The main objective of process design is the determination of the type and quantity of resources (process equipment, materials, utilities, consumables, labor and capital) required to fulfill the designed plant operational needs at the desired throughput. Especially in food processing, design must also account for food safety and quality and comply with hygienic regulations. Process design activities can be undertaken for greenfield plants or the expansion/retrofit of existing ones. Process synthesis is a process design activity aiming at selecting the “optimal” flowsheet when multiple alternative worksheets are possible. In food processing, the availability of alternative flows for producing the same product may be limited by the strict processing conditions and food sensitivity, safety and hygienic considerations (Maroulos and Saravacos 2003).

In the route to process or product commercialization, process design can be undertaken in stages of varying detail and accuracy from the conceptual design (which aims at estimating the technical and economic feasibility of a new process/product idea), all the way to the actual plant construction.

Process simulation is an enabling technology that aims at predicting the process outputs for given flows and process inputs (Foo et al. 2017). This is possible with the use of operation models that simulate the behavior of actual processes at given operating conditions. These mathematical models can be derived from first-principles (i.e., applying the laws of physics and chemistry that describe the underlying phenomena) or be represented by empirical equations that capture the process-output dependencies.

If the process is developed at a lab or pilot-plant level, process simulation facilitates translation to the manufacturing level.

With the above information available, it is possible to perform an economic analysis by considering the required capital investment and operating costs, expected revenues and the value of economic indices assessing the feasibility of the project. In the context of process synthesis and in an attempt to produce the optimal design solution, this process can be repeated for alternative flowsheets by varying the operating parameters or the structure of the firstly.

Process simulation can also be used for existing facilities for on-going optimization of the plant process, identification and elimination of bottlenecks, to facilitate decisions relating to in-house manufacturing versus for production planning and scheduling.

Sensitivity analysis are greatly facilitated by process simulation tools. The objective of these studies is to evaluate the critical process parameters on key performance indicators (KPIs) such as cycle times, plant throughput, and cost production.

Process simulation tools have been used for decades in the chemical and petrochemical industry, where small operational improvements translate to significant operating cost savings. However, low profit margins, short lead times, the ever-increasing palate of products, the demand for timely production and delivery due to the perishable nature of most foods are some of the factors that force the food industry into adopting more modern digital technologies in the spirit of Industry 4.0 initiative (Kouroulis et al. 2021). In order to increase product quality, minimize costs and ensure delivery of orders, it is necessary to optimize the food process both at the design and at the operational level; process simulation can play a major role towards achieving these objectives.

The implementation of process simulation in the food industry depends on the type of processing and product characteristics. According to Maroudis and Saravanan (2000), food processing plants can be divided into three types:
(1) Food Processing Plants that utilize raw materials and ingredient preservation,
(2) Food Ingredient Manufacturing Plants that utilize commodity products or by-products to produce food ingredients,
(3) Food Product Manufacturing Plants that produce a multitude of packaged food products ready for consumer use. By their these, food ingredient manufacturing plants are much closer to traditional chemical processing utilizing many unit operations and producing large quantities of bulk products; therefore, these types of industries are more apt to adopting process simulation tools. An ever-growing subset of ingredient manufacturing plants category is composed of the food waste valorization industries that utilize waste as inputs in order to produce a multitude of useful end products.

Food waste valorization is one of the most important areas in the food processing and production research. It is estimated that food production is responsible for about 26% of the global carbon dioxide equivalent emissions, according to about 14 billion tons per year. This consists of the emissions from the entire supply chain including deforestation, agriculture, cultivation, farming and the related energy requirements, as well as food products and food facilities. Livestock and fisheries, crop production, level and supply chains contribute 31%, 27%, 24% and 18% of the total greenhouse gas emissions, (Ritchie and Roser 2020).

In addition, a number of studies estimate that food waste is about one to third of the global food production (Gustavsson et al. 2013, Stenvark et al. 2016). This includes the edible and edible waste in all the supply chain elements such as residuals of agricultural production, post-harvest handling and storage, as well as food waste generated in the utilization elements such as food processing and packaging, distribution, retailers and consumption by the households. The developed countries typically weigh more in the later stages of the supply chain as in households and retailers, while the developing countries weigh more in the early stages of agriculture and post-harvest handling and storage. The percentage of food is also highly variable with respect to the food category or commodity group (Gustavsson et al 2013, Murugan et al. 2013). During the food processing stage, waste is between 12-45% (Elira et al. 2017). Moates et al. (2016) provide a list of the top 20 food waste streams, which include grains and other organic wastes from the production of alcoholic beverages, press cakes from vegetable oil processing and meat and dairy sides, such as whey-products and protein.

In order to reduce food waste in general, and during food processing in particular, broadly accepted management hierarchies such as the Lans can be added to determine the order of the most favorable actions. This is frequently referred to as Reduce, Reuse, Recycle (3R). After recycling, the preferred action is to produce energy, or if there are still considerable amounts, and finally, to dispose. This is achieved by extracting valuable components from the by-products. Following this, many options are established for by-products, such as animal feed. If there are still considerable amounts of by-products, then the focus is on how to produce energy with these.
