Analytical chemistry may also have side effects (e.g., energy usage, and wastes that could create risks for operators and damage the environment). One also the responsibility of method developers and users (Garrigues et al. 2010). Analytical chemistry offers the data necessary to make conclusions about human and environmental health. The main point of analytical chemistry is getting fast, precise, and accurate results; the green goal is to meet the informational requirements of chemists, industry, and society while lowering the human and environmental impact of the analysis. In this sense, the analysis has the same requirements as any other analytical chemistry. The natures of the matrix, the analytes, and the way signal is generated influence the creating of a green analytical method. In this sense, the methods that do not use pretreatment, less reagents, or with aqueous or easily biodegradable solvents have a green advantage. Therefore, there is a big path for improving analytical methods in the green way to achieve the ideal Green Analytical Chemistry.

In order to develop green chemistry, “12 Principles of Green Chemistry” to guide chemists were formulated by Anast and Warner (Garrigues et al. 2010). Unfortunately, principles did not explicitly include some important, greatly applicable to environmental impact, the need for life cycle assessment, the inherency of a product or process, or the process of heat recovery echoes reactions or heating. Few years later, Anast and Zimmerman (2003) formulated the “12 Principles of Green Analytical Chemistry”. In order to remember all these principles that direct the green approach to chemistry, some mnemonic bearer have been proposed to remember them using the words PRODUCTIVELY, IMPROVEMENTS and SIGNIFICANCE for Green Chemistry, (Green Engineering and Green Analytical Chemistry). Also, it must be kept in mind that Analytical Chemistry helps the society in general; in the sense that many decisions are made on the basis of analytical information. There is social responsibility under Analytical Chemistry (well, in every aspect of Science, but let us focus). Social responsibility in analytical chemistry denotes transferring information to the society in an undoubtedly, complete, and contextualized manner (Marcinkowska et al. 2019). Consequently, Green Analytical Chemistry can be considered the analytical arm of sustainable development, which requires the incorporation of all aspects of sustainability (economic, social and environmental) to transform into Sustainable Analytical Chemistry.

The progression of omnis is helping the other analytical areas to develop clean methods. The combination of modern analytical techniques with innovations in microelectronics and miniaturization drives the development of robust and effective analytical devices for successful control of processes and contamination. Combining miniaturization in analytical equipments with frequencies in geometrics is also of interest. The evolution of bioinformatics and forensics has reinforced expectation of solvent free methodologies based on material treatment of signals by direct measurements on untreated soil or liquid samples.

However, it must be also considered that those equipments are not affordable and the social dimension of Green Analytical Chemistry must link to such remarkable and new definitions as “Democratic Analytical Chemistry” or Equitable Analytical Chemistry”. Highlighting the significance of Green Analytical Chemistry lies in its capacity to make analytical procedures economically viable and accessible to countries, institutions, and individuals worldwide, thereby benefiting society as a whole.
