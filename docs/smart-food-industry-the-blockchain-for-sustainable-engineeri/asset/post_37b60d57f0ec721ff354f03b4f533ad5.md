## 3.2 Calculation of WF Related to Crops

The blue and green water consumption in terms of depth (mm) can be computed using CROPWAT0's stated as crop water requirements CWR (Chapagain and Hoekstra 2013). The green water (UG,m/ha) can be determined using the minimum CWR and effective rainfall (the amount of rainfall that is available in the soil for crop growth). The computation of blue water (UBWater, measured in cubic meters per hectare) involves multiplying the minimum irrigation water requirement and effective supply by the proportion of the total area of irrigated crops. Effective supply represents the stored soil moisture that is available for crop evaporation. The volume of fresh water needed to dilute pollutants to an acceptable level is referred to as grey (UWgrey, m/ha). It is derived using Eq. (1) and is based on the rate of pollution of various chemicals like insecticides, pesticides, and fertilizers.

```
WFgrey=(a * AB)/(Cmax- Cmin)γ
```

AB, (kg/ha) represents the pollutant application rate per unit area; a is the rate of leaching; Cmax(kg/m³) represents the maximum allowable concentration; Cmat and Ym(tonne/ha) represents the yield of crop. Due to a lack of local data, this analysis used nitrate-N as the pollutant, with a concentration of 100% (at a flat rate) as per the previous study (Chapagain et al., 2006), Cmat and Cmax as 11.5 mg/l under Nepal water guidelines. However, soil conditions (irrigation frequency, percolation rate, rainfall pattern, soil texture, and so on) and fertilizer application methods (rate, timing, agronomic practices, so so directly impact the amount of nitrogen leaching (Chapagain and Hoekstra 2010).

## 3.3 WF status Associated with Food Production

The overall volume of water used in agriculture is 7,980 Gm³/yr, with blue water accounting for one-third and green water accounting for the two-thirds (soil water) (Mekonnen and Hoekstra 2011). Wheat consumes the most of this total volume, accounting for 1,087 Gm each year (70 %, 19 %, 11%). The lowest proportion of green water to the total WF among the principal crops is date palm (43%) and cotton (64%). Large volumes of water are required and polluted in the production of meat, especially for the production of animal feed (Chapagain and Hoekstra 2013). The amount of water utilized in the production of a crop is calculated using the total volume of the crop and the crop's water content (WF). In the case of animals, the model from Eq. (2) takes into account the water content of feed as well as the amount of water consumed over the lifetime of an individual. The internal (water for products generated in that) and external (water for products imported into that) water consumption of a nation can be calculated using these calculations. With global averages of 907 m³/capita per year for production, the WF for US agricultural items was expected to be 1192 m³/capita per year for internal production and 267 m³/capita per year for external production. External WF are higher than in poor countries that heavily rely on imported foods mainly several European countries, such as the United Kingdom and Italy.

Animal products require high water input among pork and beef have the highest green WF of all animal products. Rice is the most-intensive crop among plant foods, using one 21% of the total water required by all field crops produced by wheat, cocoa, and olive oil were the second most-intensive crop. Coffee, cocoa and vegetables have lower WFs (MacDonald and Reitmeier 2017).

The overall animal category and in-country and production s (tonne/y) of animal production (2,422 Gm/y) accounts for 29% of the WF of total of agricultural production (18,863 Gm/y) globally. This was calculated as the global WF of crop production (7,040 Gm/y) (Mekonnen and Hoekstra 2011), the direct WF of livestock (46 Gm/y), and the WF of grazing (913 Gm/y). While examining total WF by animal group (Table 2), beef contributes the most (33%) to the WF of farm production, driven by dairy cattle (19%), pigs (19%), and broiler chickens (11). Over 97% of the WF related to feeding from grazing in the grazing system, and the WF is predominantly green (94%) in mixed and industrial systems, respectively. The blue WF in the grazing system accounts for 3.6% of the overall WF, with drinking water accounting around 33% (Mekonnen and Hoekstra 2011). Wheat consumes most, accounting for 1087 Gm per year (70% green, 19% blue, 11% grey). Rice (992 Gm/y) and maize (770 Gm/y) are the other crops with high total WF. Rainfed crops used 701 Gm³/y, whereas irrigated crops used 1070 Gm³/y, resulting in a global green WF connected to agricultural production of more than 80% of total WF. Among the major crops, date palm (43%) and cotton (64%) have the lowest proportion of green water to total WF. According to the consistent studies carried out, green water has a considerable impact on global food output. Most countries have a potential of self-sufficiency on green water-based and can produce their food requirement locally. There is a substantial possibility to increase water productivity in rain-fed agriculture by increasing production levels by up to fourfold within the existing water balance (Rockström 2003). This is a potential to boost water production in rain-fed agriculture by increasing the water productivity omitting the need to introduce new blue water (Critley et al. 1991). However, available precipitation in semi-arid dry regions is extremely limited, making agricultural productivity without the use of blue water impossible. Thus, global cereal crop output could be drastically reduced (Siebert and Doell 2010, Chaplain and Hoekstra 2013). As a result, in a world with finite freshwater supplies, to deal with the issue of increased water demand, a balanced green-blue water usage strategy would be required. Additional research is essential to identify the blue water availability by analysing the spatiotemporal variability, as the amount of blue water that may be utilised in a sustainable catchment without creating environmental damage.
