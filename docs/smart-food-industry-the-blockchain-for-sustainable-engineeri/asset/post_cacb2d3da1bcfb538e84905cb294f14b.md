The food conversion efficiency of insects is much higher than meat from animals (cattle, swine, and poultry). Because they are cold-blooded, they have a high feed conversion rate and are efficient in the transformation of matter into biomass (Orsi et al. 2019). The nutritional profile of different insect species is variable, with an insect body containing up to 80% protein, which is attributed to controlling the body temperature by the environment (Orsi et al. 2019, Kwiatkowski et al. 2021).

Furthermore, 1.7 kg of feed are needed to produce 10 kg of domestic insects with nutritional proportions superior to beef and with a production cost below required for conventional method, which demand 10, 5, and 2.5 kg of feed to produce beef, pork, and chicken, respectively. Compared to planting to grains, insect production is 50% higher per hectare (Minas et al. 2016, Raheem et al. 2019, Reis and Dias 2020). Regarding the water footprint, insect production is equivalent to 4,341 m³ per ton, especially for the larvae of Tenebrio molitor, while cattle, pigs, and chickens, the requirements are 145,115, 5,998, and 4,325 m³ per ton (Miglietta et al. 2015).

The area needed to produce the same amount of protein is about 1 ha for meals, 3.5 ha for pigs or chickens, and 10 ha to produce 1 kg of beef, emissions are equivalent to 14.8 kg of CO₂, and for pigs, 3.8 and 1.1 kg of CO₂, is necessary. Larvae, larvae, and grasshoppers emit 100 times less GHG and 10 times less ammonia than meat animals (cattle and pigs) (Dobermann et al. 2017, Reis et al. 2019, Imathiu 2020).

Roughly one insect species are known, although it is estimated that there are around 80, presenting different stages of development and consumption; quite different amino acid and fatty acid profiles (FAO 2019, 2020, 2021a, Reis and Dias 2020). The large group of edible insects are Coleoptera, followed by Hymenoptera, Orthoptera, and Lepidoptera. The most popular species for human consumption are beetles (Coleoptera, 31%), caterpillars (Lepidoptera, 18), bees, wasps, and ants (Hymenoptera, 14), grasshoppers and crickets (Orthoptera, 13), larvae, leafhoppers, mealyugs, and bugs (Hemoptera, 10), termites (Isoptera, 3), dragonflies (Odonata, 3), flies (Diptera, 2), and others (5%) (FAO 2021b). Of these species mentioned, 10% are from distinct parts of the world, and the rest are restricted to some geographic zones, of 12% are aquatic species and 77% tropical (Minas et al. 2016, Lange and Nakamura 2021, FAO 2021b).

Today, the most significant consumption of insects is in Africa, Asia, Australia, and Latin America, and they are a part of the human diet in some regions with culturally and gastronomically inappropriate or even taboo, in most European countries, the human consumption of insects is very low, and entomophagy is often considered distasteful (Garafalo et al. 2019, Kim et al. 2019). There is great potential for insect meals in animal feed, because they are an excellent source of protein and present an adequate profile of amino acids depending on the species, diet, and stage of development. The protein concentration of the insects used in animal feed varies from 46 to 65%, higher than the values found in beef (23.5%), lentils (26.7%), and soybeans (41.1%) (Reis and Dias 2020, Gravel and Doyle 2020). In addition to being a good source of protein of high biological value, insects are also good sources of food. However, the fat and energy content vary considerably between species and stages of development, ranging from 7 to 77% in dry matter, while energy can vary from 2930 to 7620 kcal per kg of dry matter. Crickets have about 13% lipids, while beetle larvae have 50% dry matter and low cholesterol compared to products of animal origin, having a favorable proportion of omega 6 and 3 fatty acids and polyunsaturated and saturated fatty acids. Fibers and minerals, such as copper, iron, magnesium, phosphorus, selenium, and zinc, are present in high concentrations in insect meals, although it is necessary to pay attention to the variation of minerals in different species (Reis and Dias 2020, Lange and Nakamura 2021).

Nutritional compositions vary with the species, diet, developmental stage, sex, and environments of insects, such as temperature, day, light intensity, and spectral composition (Zielinska et al. 2015, Barros et al. 2017). There are nutrient variations depending on the type of processing, if the insects are alive, dehydrated, cooked, etc., as shown in Table 1. Humans already consume insects as their diet, often realizing it. For instance, according to the US Food and Drug Administration, an average of 75 or more insect fragments per 50 g are allowed in wheat flour (AOAC 972.32). Studies show that the "meat" of insects contains substantial amounts of proteins and is rich in mineral salts, including iron, phosphorus, selenium, zinc, vitamins (A1, B2, B5, B6, B7, D, and K), carotenoids, and flavonoids (avigenin, luteolin, and rutin) and are easily digestible (Belluco et al. 2017, Castro et al. 2018, Hunter et al. 2021). Insects have nutritional value, an abundance of proteins, and may contain double or triple (>40%) compared to conventional sources of animal origin (Rumpold and Schlitzer 2013, Lucas et al. 2020). The protein content of chickens, silkworms, moth caterpillars, and palm weevil is higher than beef, chicken, and pork, as demonstrated in Table 2. The mopane cricket provides similar amounts of vitamin D as egg yolks. Silkworm larvae (Bombyx mori L.) fed mulberry leaves and lettuce, and when processed (and freeze-dried), can contain 10 mg of vitamin E in 10 g (Tong et al. 2011). Fat is the second-largest component of insects, and the fat profile depends on their diet and can range from 7 to 77 g of dry weight, which is higher in larvae than in adults (Kourmska and Adám 2016).

## 4. Consumption Safety

Not all insects are safe to eat, as certain vegetables are. When selecting insects, monitoring microbial contamination is the first concern; microbiological, toxicological, and allergic risks may be (Sun-Waterhouse et al. 2016, Mishna and Glumac 2021). The bioavailability and bioaccessibility of insect nutrients are poorly researched topics, and many studies have addressed nutrient characterization, however, as any other food, the type of preparation and processing significantly affects the nutrient content that will or not be bioavailable (Parada and Aguillera 2007, Mendez 2018).

The presence of anti-nutrients in some insects, such as tannins and phenolic acids, can hinder the bioavailability of some fractions of the compounds (Cardoso et al. 2015). In general, tannins in animal feeds are associated with reduced consumption, growth rate, efficiency, metabolizable energy, and protein digestibility, as they form complexes with protein, interfering with its bioavailability (Hammerman 2016, Mutungi et al. 2019). In animals, the bioaccessibility and bioavailability of compounds present in insects are variable; however, in the case of conventional foods, thermal processing can modify the flavor and increase the bioavailability of nutrients (Barba et al. 2017, Mandsitera et al. 2019).

There are natural variations in protein and amino acid profiles among different insect species, and the levels of these compounds are sensitive to processing methods, including cooking, boiling, frying, and drying (static, lyophilization, air circulation, vacuum, and microwaves). Minerals are less sensitive to processing conditions but can be affected by cooking style as they can be removed during drying (Zielinska et al. 2015, Mandsitera et al. 2019).

The digestibility of insect proteins is affected by the preparation and cooking style. For instance, some studies have reported that the roasting and drying of locusts reduces the digestibility of the protein, which does not occur when protein is applied to dehydrated termites. When boiled, Tenebro increases protein digestibility (Gravel and Djoyen 2020, Lifecaea). Some insects have reactive or toxic chemicals, others contain cross-reactive allergens that can induce allergic reactions to those associated with shellfish or mollusks (Barre et al. 2014, Lange and Nakamura 2021). The allergenicity of edible insects may resemble so-called arthropod and invertebrate allergy (caused by components such as the hemolymph, cuticle, and parts including exuvia, hair, bristles, and scales) (Broekman et al. 2015, Raheem et al. 2019).
