An easy-to-understand example is energy demand, which is the main factor in the food chain. Electric energy from coal processing is estimated to about 3.08 × 10⁻³ kg CO₂, kWh/Wh. On the other hand, alternative sources of renewable energy, in smaller partitions, also present carbon emissions with values of 1.05 × 10⁻⁵ and 2.50 × 10⁻⁶ kg CO₂, kWh/Wh for photovoltaic panels and wind energy, respectively (Depra et al. 2020).

Additionally, another variety present in the food chain is the soil fertilization process. Currently, nitrogen and phosphorus are estimated to be responsible for 4.94 and 1.88 kg CO₂, eq/kg. Therefore, being able to map the entire food chain enables an even more multifaceted process.

Therefore, when referring in terms, the representation of the environmental impact associated with these processes can vary greatly depending on the degree of the functional unit, the limits of the system evaluated, as well as the attributes considered (Shakbulatov et al. 2019), however data from the largest meta-analysis of the carbon footprint applied to food, published by Dore and Nemecek (2018), show even deeper insights into environmental performance. So, to help identify the main scientific question of the moment—where the carbon footprints of our foods come?—a qualitative illustration of the percentage average carbon footprint at different stages is shown in Fig. 1.

Although the steps the food supply chain can vary considerably, it is possible to segment the chain into several stages: land-use change, animal feed, feeding stage, transport, retail, and packaging (Fig. 1). From this perspective, for most foods and particularly the largest emitters—the carbon footprint result mostly from land-use change (in green) and farm-stages processes (buried yellow). Moreover, agricultural stages include processes such as fertilizer applications (organics or synthetics), and fermentation. Carbon- and farm-use emissions account for more than 80% of the food footprint.

Taking a closer look at animal products, now produced in terms of on the farm, carbon emissions represent 74% of the total emitted, while their protein alternative, tofu, have values less than 20%. However, when we evaluate the whole step, we find that the carbon emissions of beef cattle represent matters below 6%, while tofu presents values above 25%.
