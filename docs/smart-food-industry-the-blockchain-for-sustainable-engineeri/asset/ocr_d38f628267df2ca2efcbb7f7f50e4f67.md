To mention another example, after cattle domestication, some cattle herds must have developed some sort of lactose tolerance. Those who were not dependent on cattle, such as the Chinese, the Thai, or the American remained lactose-intolerant. Nevertheless, it is mere tolerance that people acquired—tolerance is not the same as beneficial. Grains and dates are by no means optimal for our health. In fact, millions of people—70 percent of the global population—are lactose-intolerant, many who regularly consume dairy products do not even know that they are lactose-intolerant (Physiological Committee for Responsible Medicine n.d.). They merely tolerate such products (Savaiano and Levitt 1987). What can be said about grain consumption. Many argue since the development of agriculture, humans have adapted to digesting grains. But it is evident that grains are not optimal for our digestive system. They would be virtually inedible if not produced by cooking. Second, grain products are generally conducive with the addition of refined salt, sugars, and other condiments. In fact, it is no accident that medical professionals warn people to consume in moderation or better, yet not consume breads, pasta, pizza, and other refine grain products, which are unhealthy.

Another point is that all living organisms consume nonprocessed food. No living organisms benefit from unprocessed food, and there is no reason to think that processed food benefits any organism. By processing food, humans can derive calories from substances that otherwise be indigible (grains and legumes for example). In a situation of food insecurity or scarcity, humans would benefit from processing substances in the sense that they would be able to survive and not starve of starvation. However it is here that I am referring when considered human food are optimal health and ultimately, flourishing. Furthermore, it seems implausible, and indeed suspicious, that only one out of thousands of species—the humans would benefit from processed food when all other species benefit from unprocessed substances. It would seem incorrect to argue that humans are supposed to be denied to consume processed food. Many individuals get by by consuming sub-optimal or non-optimal substances. Obviously, this shows humans ought not to consume processed food. I do not argue for moral obligations in the Kantian regime. Rather, I propose consuming substances that are not conducive to flourishing evinces an injustice. Moreover, having a diet that is deleterious or not and not conducive to our flourishing is tantamount to ignorant and deceiving (whether deliberately or not) our nature.

Consequently, since humans are the product of nature and natural selection—we are part of nature—like other species, we benefit from natural and unprocessed substances that grow in nature. Moreover, the human body is not endowed with the same features as carnivores, exceptional for speed, strong digestive system, etc., of which all humans are unequipped besides by weapons and fire to kill, process and consume animal flesh. Humans must use weapons and technologies to kill, process, consume, and consume animals. It follows that leafy greens and fruit constitute the optimal diet. As Dr. William C. Roberts puts it,

Most of us live as herbivores, in that we eat as well as vegetables and fruits, humans being have characteristics of herbivores or not. The teeth of carnivores are sharp; those of herbivores are flat (for grazing). The intestinal length of carnivores is short (3 times body length); that of herbivores is long (12 times body length). Body cooling of carnivores is done by panting; herbivores drink fluids by lapping; herbivores, by sipping. Carnivores produce their own vitamin C whereas herbivores obtain it from their diet. Thus, humans are not carnivores (2000).
