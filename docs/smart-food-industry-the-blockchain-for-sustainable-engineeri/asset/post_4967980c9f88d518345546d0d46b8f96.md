Fluidised bioreactors (FBBs) operate on a similar principle to PBBs with the difference that the cell/carrier bed is less packed, thus fluidised. This way, pH/nutrient gradients can be more easily avoided, however inevitably increasing the volume. The fluidised is needed, and, inevitably, the achieved densities are lower compared to the PBBs. However, in such a system similar to ST, continuous addition of free microcarriers or accumulation of cells in small scaffolds/beds can be applied to expand the culture. Usually, fluidisation at a constant or increasing rate is applied from the bottom of the column upwards and a filter membrane is used to prevent any cell loss. The medium is continuously pumped around the chamber, and can be aerated with the help of an external bubble or by the incorporation of a sparger in the culture column.

Hollow Fibre Bioreactors (HFBs) are another bioreactor type with potential for cultivated meat. HFBs are a type of hydraulic bioreactor meaning in such a system is achieved through the fluid flow similar to PBB (Reiss et al. 2021). HFBs have the greatest surface area to volume ratio among all bioretypes. HFBs are advantageous as they generate a low shear, which provide an increased availability to nutrients due to the perfusion operation mode resulting in high cell densities (Reiss et al. 2021). Although the medium perfusion provides a more efficient distribution of nutrients and gases to the cells when compared to PBBs, at scale, the development of gradients would be inevitable. The maximum perfusion distance allowed for the maintenance of a physiological tissue (i.e., how far a cell can be from a capillary and survive) is said to be 150–200 µm, which correlates with the maximum oxygen distance (Rowkema et al. 2009). HFBs have been previously used for the successful expansion of skeletal muscle cells (Battaglia et al. 2011, Yamamoto et al. 2012). HFBs are typically used in the biopharmaceutical industry for the production of proteins, but for cultivated meat where the biomass is the product, harvesting from such systems can be challenging particularly as increased cell harvest yields are required. However, it is conceivable that degradable and/or edible fibres can be used for a one-step expansion and differentiation of muscle and fat tissues in the same target.

The suitability of these types of bioreactors depend on the type of cultivated meat products which will be developed. As the industry grows and a diverse range of cultivated meat products will be developed, the continual development and optimisation of the bioreactor platforms for large scale production is. It is also important to acknowledge that novel bioreactor designs might also be beneficial for these products.

## 4.5 Co-culture

Co-culture is defined as two or more cell populations with some degree of cell contact between them, to investigate how one cell type affects the proliferative or differentiative capabilities of another cell type. Co-culture can be direct, with forming bonds or, indirect, throughout a transwell semi-permeable membrane, to gauge the effect of secreted factors (Goers et al. 2014). Examples of co-culture could prove beneficial in the production of cultivated meat include the addition of macrophages and fibroblasts, both used in muscle repair, where macrophages contribute an inflammatory response and fibroblasts a pro-migratory effect on the myoblasts (Venter and Nieler 2018). Increasing the myoblast potential presents an opportunity to enhance self-organisation and differentiation through mechanical stimuli via higher connectivity. Dendritic cells have also been shown to enhance the proliferative capacity of myocytes, but not to increase the myoblast number (Ladiskan et al. 2018). This may induce a delay in the induction or differentiation of further proliferative cycles, which could be useful in muscle cell banking if a two-stage 'proliferation-differentiation' process was to be developed. The further addition of hemoglobin, a protein naturally occurring in skeletal muscle and cardiac cells, has been shown to increase proliferation and metabolic activity of bovine satellite cells, with the addition of hemoglobin can improve colour of cultivated meat products (Simsa et al. 2019).

To obtain a tissue that resembles the structure and surface of livestock meat, co-culture of different cell types (fat, muscle and connective cells) will be necessary particularly for structured meat products. The co-culture of adipocytes by myotubes has been previously reported (Kovalik et al. 2011). Another co-culture model of muscle with cardiomyocytes has been developed by An et al. adipocytes were first mixed with a gel solution in an inner dish which was then solidified at 37°C. The cardiomyocytes were then seeded on the surface of the inner dish and the inner dish was placed in an outer dish containing growth medium (An et al. 2011). However, both of these examples did not facilitate scale up of the cell types nor were performed with humans. Moreover, these models did not support the survival of the two cells for more than 48 h which is not sufficient for achieving maturation. The co-culture needs to be three-dimensional, scalable and to sustain the growth of all cell types. Critical co-culture of cells in food-grade hydrogel microcapsules could enable a stable method for production of meat. ## 5. Conclusions

Cultivated meat products are highly complex and thus production is challenging. Several critical challenges need to be overcome to make these products affordable and available to the population. Bioprocess development for their production requires a very good understanding of cell requirements including media formula and environmental conditions. The development of a bioprocess for the scalable production of cultivated meat is even more complex as it requires knowledge and understanding of multiple cell types—a source for muscle and a source for cells as a minimum. In addition, their production has multiple stages—expansion, differentiation, and co-culture of multiple cell types targeting a structured product. It needs to be noted that at each of these stages, the culture requirements are different. Because of that, typical strategies involve separate processes being carried out in parallel and in multiple bioreactor vessels. Ideally, a simplified bioprocess where subsequent stages as expansion and differentiation would take place in the same bioreactor would be desirable. However, this one-step bioprocess is highly challenging as it requires flexibility and specific tools to address the requirements of differentiation and maturation. A particular challenge is formation of mature muscle fibres to the mechanical stimulus that needs to be incorporated in the bioprocess.
