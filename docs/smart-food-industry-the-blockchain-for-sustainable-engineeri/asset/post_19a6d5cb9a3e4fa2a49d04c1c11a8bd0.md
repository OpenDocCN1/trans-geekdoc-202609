## 5. Meat and Fish Waste and By-products

### 5.1 Meat Waste and By-products

Meat is a type of animal flesh that contains high biological value protein, essential amino acids, micronutrients, and vitamins that are required for a healthy lifestyle (Saeed et al. 2020). The term “meat” does not refer to a single or even a few species, but rather to a wide range of species ranging from poultry to cattle, sheep, goats, and thousands of fish species. The meat processing industry generates a large amount of waste. Therefore, economic and social benefits of recycling were recognized by meat, poultry, and fish over a century ago, and many organizations and foundations worked together to develop technologies for utilizing co-products (Castro-Muñoz and Ruby-Figueroa 2019). In terms of food waste composition, fruit and vegetables cover 85% of the total waste produced; on the other hand, as environmental impact, they contribute only 46% of the total carbon footprint. However, the facts show the opposite trend—meat accounts for only 3.5% of food waste, despite generating 29% of the carbon footprint (Mosna et al. 2021). As when, compared to all other food waste streams, meat waste has the greatest environmental impact in terms of greenhouse gas emissions. Furthermore, meat waste causes significant economic losses for the meat industry, affecting both global human and food security (Long and Mohan 2021). In modern technology, the production of meat waste has a significant environmental impact, resulting in increased levels of water, and soil pollution. This leads to undesired outcomes for humans and other living organisms by fostering the growth of harmful microorganisms, which contributes to the spread of diseases (Thanakakalan and Ganesh 2020).

The meat industry consists primarily of waste from slaughterhouses, fish, and poultry, the main characteristics of these wastes are that they are easily decomposed due to high microbial activity, having high water content (70-95%) (which will increase the cost of transport), are easily oxidized (due to high fat content), and undergoes changes due to enzyme activity (most of the enzymes are still active) (Shen et al. 2019). Recovering waste and by-products can help solve a number of problems. On a larger scale, the valorization of animal by-products may result in a reduction in carbon footprint because recovered-products benefit. Economically, the valorization of animal by-products would enable food companies to benefit, and improve their circular economy (Bechaux et al. 2019).

It is possible to classify animal-products as agricultural-products (meat bone, meal fertilizer, etc.), industrial-products (gelatin, adhesives, casings, etc.) and pharmaceutical (insulin, peptides, biochemicals, hormones, etc.). Proper use of these by-products in the meat industry needs to control pollution, provide better returns, provide highly nutritious animal food, establish recovery security and create jobs, and provide crop improvement (Irshad and Sharma 2015). Animal-products and wastes can be characterized as either edible or inedible, depending on whether they are or not used as food. Customs, religion, consumer acceptance, reception, product availability, economics, and hygiene play a role in determining edible food—which is edible in one region may be considered inedible in another. For instance, the use of blood edible in many countries where Islamic regulations (EC) 1069/2009 and EC 142/2011, outlining health rules concerning animal by-products and their derivatives that are not intended for human consumption (Holloway and Wu 2019). Additionally, the European law ec no. 174/2002 classified these by-products into three categories. Products in the first class are responsive to the transmission of contagious diseases in humans and animals while by-products in the second class are prohibited even for use in animal feeds. These products are derived from dead animals outside slaughterhouses or from animals with drug residues.

Here, they are all derived from healthy animals slaughtered in slaughterhouses and fitted for human consumption (Beauchaux et al. 2019). The yield of by-products from animals varies greatly depending on species, live weight, fat, and collection methods (Castro-Muhoz and Ruby-Figueroa 2019). Processes in the meat industry, such as slaughtering, cutting, and processing different by-products (fat, red and white offal, blood, bone, meat trimmings, hide, tissues, horns, fur, skin, etc.) can be valorized (Halloway and Wu 2019). Biologically, the majority of non-carcass material is edible if it is properly cleaned, handled, and processed. Red viscera, also known as “variety meat”, include the liver, heart, kidney, tongue, and other products that are frequently used as co-products. Blood and trimmings would also be included in white offal (intestines and stomach).

Hides, skins, pelt, hair, feathers, horns, fur, heads, bones, toenails, blood, organs, intestines, fatty tissues, and on the other, are inedible by-products. It has been noted that other meat and connective tissues account for approximately 50% of the live weight, 42% of pigs, 28% of cattle and 24% of turkey (Castro-Muhoz and Ruby-Figueroa 2019). Meat and processing products are high in protein and essential amino acids. Meat-products, in addition to protein, may be a good source of minerals (Ca, Fe, Mg, Cu, Se) or vitamins (niacin, B2, C). Also, iron, which is found primarily in meat, is more abundant than fresh meat (Mullen et al. 2017). It is possible to summarize the value-added and bioactive compounds obtained from the meat industry as fertilizer, animal food, lactic acid, and probiotics (S et al. 2022). Nevertheless, protein induced waste has gained popularity in the food industry for the production of by-products. Bioactive peptides are made of amino acids that range in size from 2 to 30 units (Saed et al. 2020). Hemoglobin is the most abundant compound in animal blood, which is a valuable by-product. It can be hydrolyzed to produce bioactive peptides, which possess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitory (antihypertensive), and opioid activities, with peptides receiving interest (Shen et al. 2019). Shen et al. (2020) investigated the anti-hypertension and antioxidant activity of meat-product hydrolys by enzymatic hydrolysis. According to the authors, liver hydrolys had higher activity. In addition, the ACE inhibitory activity of liver hydrolys increased significantly after hydrolysis, while the kidney hydrolys showed higher ABTS scavenging activity than liver. Bioactive peptide production is critical in the development of functional foods and a potential solution to address economic and environmental issues (Saed et al. 2020). In the animal body bones generally account for 20-30% of body weight, and are rich in a variety of minerals, such as zinc, phosphorus, calcium and iron (Shen et al. 2019). Animal wastes from the meat industry also contain significant amounts of insoluble structural proteins as anti-aging collagen, elastin, and keratin, which are major constituents of bones, organs, and tissues. These by-products are high in protein and can be utilized for the delivery of novel functional ingredients (Brandelli et al. 2015). Furthermore, they contain chondroitin phospholipids several times more than fresh meat, which is essential to the human brain (Shen et al. 2019). A specific example of the utilization of meat-products, Long and Mohan (2021) produced spray dried beef powder as a value-added and multipurpose food ingredient. Protein hydrolyates are also used in aquaculture as nutrients. The palatability of meat-product hydrolyates in feed can be increased by cleaving hydrophobic acids from peptides which cause bitterness. Also, keratin-containing by-products, such as hair, feather, and the outer layer of skin, can be used in food formulations after enzyme hydrolysis with keratinase (Halloway and Wu 2019). Another by-product obtained from animal bones is bone meal. Micro-bone meal is made of bone granules with an average diameter of 100-500 um.
