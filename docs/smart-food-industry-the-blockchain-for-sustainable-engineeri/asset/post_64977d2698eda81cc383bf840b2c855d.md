Here, the healthiness concern is primordial as it relates to the nutritional content of the food and the safety consequences of its consumption. ## 4. Behaviorally Informed Solutions

Consumers' acceptance of novel food is challenging for consumers, governments and policy makers; behavioral research suggests a plethora of solutions that can weigh considerably in their reconciliation. By deining, highlighting the personal benefits that consumers derive from their sustainable green consumption has shown potential in alleviating the green gap. This approach entails intertwining environmental benefits with communicating advertising the novel food. It is known as green bundling and has been extensively reviewed and exemplified by Delmas and Coligan (2018) in their book 'the green bundle'. For instance, emphasizing the long-term and short-term health benefits of adopting a plant-based diet (vs. meat-based) while also communicating the comparative carbon footprint of each style gives more reason to opt for sustainable meat alternatives.

Companies must also investigate and evaluate the behavioral costs or sludge that prevent consumers from adopting sustainable alternatives. These behavioral costs include, but are not limited to, price premium, store availability, notoriety, and product usability. Consumers who are not familiar with food kits or food sharing apps can benefit from awareness ads and 'how to' breaking the usability barrier.

Awareness plays a crucial role in influencing consumers; with the more existence of sustainable food solutions, realm marketing is a promising avenue (Johnstone and Lind 2018), and as it helps the commercialization and acceptance of sustainable food alternatives. It also implements cues and help alleviate the uncertainty and ambiguity surrounding the consumption of low-carbon food alternatives (White et al. 2019), especially for new and unfamiliar alternatives such as insect-meals.

As for intangible issues, it is best addressed through communication strategies targeting changing perceptions and attitudes. When communicating the sustainable attribute, advertising should address local and proximal environmental impacts of the products/behaviors in a way that makes the rewards more tangible by individuals (White et al. 2019). Also, implementing communities and gamification of the sustainable behavior into milestones can help minimize the abstractness of the ecological benefits (White et al. 2019).

Finally, and most importantly, companies' credibility is a primordial and pivotal pillar in gaining consumers' trust and encouraging them to undertake sustainable food choices. Trust influences behavior through their positive link to attitude towards the sustainable options, but also through its influence at attenuating concerns related to such alternatives (Ricci et al. 2018). Companies should develop an authentic brand, build a solid consumer-brand relationship, and inspire transparency to boost consumers' trust. Like in the farmers' market, short distribution circuit allows consumers to communicate directly with the producer, limit uncertainties, and strengthen transparency. Other strategies include introducing blockchain technologies to ensure traceability and will be discussed further in the following section.

## 5. Embedding Industry 4.0 into Society 5.0 for Ecosystem Transformation

The acceptability challenges that sustainable food innovations are present diverse, complex, and volatile, which entails addressing them a a convergent innovation approach (Dubé et al. 2014) rather than a ‘technical fix’ (Keogh et al. 2020). Interestingly, the vision of Society 5.0 merges both technological advances of Industry 4.0 as well as human-behavioral knowledge (Keogh et al. 2020), which would fit perfectly in the market, help overcome the challenges, and generate value through both technologies and behavioral creativity. In the following, we will discuss the potential of behavioral changes and industry 4.0 technologies in bridging the acceptability gap in a convergent innovation perspective on critical challenges.

Industry 4.0 has given rise to new developmental orientations in the era, and most importantly, it has emphasized the flexibility, decentralization, and individualization of product development and commercialization (Lasi et al. 2014). Human-behavioral interventions offer fertile soil for the resolution of such acceptability challenges, mainly the attitude-behavior gap and the bounded rationality; the nascent technological advances address the challenges relating to consumer trust and product quality.

For starters, the connectivity between the different supply chain nodes, and the real-time surveillance of food conditions made by the integration of industry 4.0 technologies, play a pivotal role in harnessing and resurrection of trust in sustainable food products. The internet of things, for instance, coupled with sensors and drones, allows for consistent monitoring of the weather conditions, health, and well... blockchain technology promotes food authentication and distribution transparency (Antonucci et al. 2019), and can restore sustainable foods’ credibility, especially but not exclusively, the labeled and processed foods.

Furthermore, technologies could tackle the product attributes from naturalness to health and taste perception. To illustrate, the potential of 3D printing in molding the internal structures of food products, their dimensions, shapes, appearances, and also personalized formulas (Keogh et al. 2020). As vegan or insect-based meat would have an appearing taste, and aspect to that of standard meat. Although this solution addresses the actual taste and not the taste perception, appropriate interventions and communication plan would resolve the attitude perception' discrepancy. ## 6. The Interdependence of Technological and Behavioral Advances in a Convergent Innovation Approach

In a world struggling to meet its sustainability goals, including environmental, economic, and social justice, there is a dire need to 'innovate the way we innovate' and to place the human at the center of the century's transformation (Dubé et al. 2014). While the technological advances offer substantial ecological alternatives and solutions to the food sector, there is still considerable effort to de-humanize the food sector, as demonstrated earlier, technological advances can be possible without human-centered knowledge. We found that the technological advances offered food alternatives were not enough to resolve the environmental problem. Instead, in this chapter, we explored the acceptability challenges as human-centered problems preventing the ecological transition from materializing. By addressing these challenges by adopting an integrated innovation approach, marrying both technological advances and deep behavioral insights, aiming to meet both the well-being of individuals and that of our planet.
