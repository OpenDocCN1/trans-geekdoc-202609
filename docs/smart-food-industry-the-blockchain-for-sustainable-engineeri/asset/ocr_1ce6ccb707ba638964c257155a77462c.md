Very high volumetric cell densities can be achieved this way. However, the system has gas and nutrient diffusion limitations, especially at very high-cell densities (Ellis et al. 2005). Fluid flow in packed beds can't exceed 3.0 cm⁻¹ without hydrodynamic forces affecting cell viability (Weber et al. 2010), while stresses 0.015 Pa have been shown to upregulate differentiation downstream osteogenic lineages in MSCs (Zhao et al. 2007). Scale up of PBBs proves challenging as the depth of the packed bed is limited without generating axial gradients of nutrient distribution and heterogeneity within the bed; there will be a critical depth below the fluids interface where solubility is zero (McB et al. 2007). However, once again, the adaptation of culture processes to the engineering of a complex, continuous process offers, particularly as energy, nutrient, and space requirements will be far relative to the physiological system a PBB is trying to manage.

Fluidised bioreactors (FBBs) operate on a similar principle to PBBs with the difference that the cell/carrier bed is less packed, thus fluidised. This way, pH/nutrient gradients can be more easily avoided, however inevitably increasing the volume. The fluidised is needed, and, inevitably, the achieved densities are lower compared to the PBBs. However, in such a system similar to ST, continuous addition of free microcarriers or accumulation of cells in small scaffolds/beds can be applied to expand the culture. Usually, fluidisation at a constant or increasing rate is applied from the bottom of the column upwards and a filter membrane is used to prevent any cell loss. The medium is continuously pumped around the chamber, and can be aerated with the help of an external bubble or by the incorporation of a sparger in the culture column.

Hollow Fibre Bioreactors (HFBs) are another bioreactor type with potential for cultivated meat. HFBs are a type of hydraulic bioreactor meaning in such a system is achieved through the fluid flow similar to PBB (Reiss et al. 2021). HFBs have the greatest surface area to volume ratio among all bioretypes. HFBs are advantageous as they generate a low shear, which provide an increased availability to nutrients due the perfusion operation mode resulting in high cell densities (Reiss et al. 2021). Although the medium perfusion provides a more efficient distribution of nutrients and gases to the cells when compared to PBBs, at scale, the development of gradients would be inevitable. The maximum perfusion distance allowed for the maintenance of a physiological tissue (i.e., how far a cell can be from a capillary and survive) is said to be 150–200 µm, which correlates with the maximum oxygen distance (Rowkema et al. 2009). HFBs have been previously used for the successful expansion of skeletal muscle cells (Battaglia et. 2011, Yamamoto et al. 2012). HFBs are typically used in the biopharmaceutical industry for the production of proteins, but for cultivated meat where the biomass is the product, harvesting from such systems can be challenging particularly as increased cell harvest yields are required. However, it is conceivable that degradable and/or edible fibres can be used for a one-step expansion and differentiation of muscle and fat tissues in the same target.

The suitability of these types of bioreactors depend on the type of cultivated meat products which will be developed, As the industry grows and a diverse range of cultivated meat products will be developed, the continual development and optimisation of the bioreactor platforms for large scale production is. It is also important to acknowledge that novel bioreactor designs might also be beneficial for these products.
