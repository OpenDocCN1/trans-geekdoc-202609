Despite not yet readily available, cultivated meat products are expected to have significant advantages over animal meat from multiple perspectives including resources, animal welfare, environmental impact and population health (Stehens 2018, Slejda et al. 2021). Initial projections showed that cultivated meat would require 45%-76% less greenhouse emissions (Tumiosto and Mattos 2021, Stephens et al. 2018). However, more recent life cycle analyses have shown that the benefits will vary across the different types of cultivated meat (e.g., beef, pork, poultry) (Mattick et al. 2015) and will be influenced by the method of energy production chosen in the manufacturing of these products (Smetaana et al. 2015). As it is very likely that these products would need to be produced in large scale bioreactors, there are concerns regarding the amounts of energy necessary for the production and the potential environmental impact in the long term due to the way that CO2 and CH4 could be produced. However, this issue could be minimised in the future by innovations in the energy field to achieve decarbonization of production (Bodiou et al. 2020).

This chapter will focus specifically on the manufacturing of cultivated meat products and will capture the different elements required for its production, from both an engineering and biological point of view.

# ## 3. State of the Cultivated Meat industry

The interest and the investment in the cultivated meat sector has increased significantly over the past couple of years. This growth profile of the industry is expected to continue, with the market size reaching 59 billion USD by 2030. The North American market is expected to capture over half of this due to their large market size and affinity for processed meats such as nuggets, meaning poultry is expected to be the most common cultivated meat product, followed by cultivated pork and beef (Cultured Meat Report 2019).

While the concept of cultivated meat was envisaged nearly a century ago (Churchill 1931, Arshad et al. 2017), the first proof-of-concept cultivated beef burger was only showcased to the public in 2013 by Mark Post, the founder of start-up Mosaic Meat (Netherlands). The burger weighted approximately 85g and required cultivation of 10 billion individual muscle cells to achieve the necessary number and cost approximately€250,000 (Post 2014). This breakthrough in technology stands a valid proof of concept that worked to generate interest and over $96 USD in investment in the concept over the years. Through industrial scale and the development of new processes and technologies, Mosaic Meat now prices a cultivated meat burger at around $9 (Clean Techica 2019). Since then, multiple start-ups have the world showcased their proof-of-concept products. For example, in 2016, the US-based start-up, Memphis Me showcased their cell-based meat. In 2019, Shiok Steak (UK) showcased beyond beef while securing significant funding, over 200 million USD since being founded in 2015, Upside Foods (US). They focus on the cultivation of poultry and have recently announced the development of a “cell feed” that is entirely animal component free (ACF) addressing one of the current challenges that this industry is facing (Upside Foods 2021).

Currently, Just, Inc., a US-based start-up is the first company to have achieved a major milestone in the field to obtain the first regulatory approval for their cultivated chicken nuggets to be commercialised in Singapore (BBC 2020).
