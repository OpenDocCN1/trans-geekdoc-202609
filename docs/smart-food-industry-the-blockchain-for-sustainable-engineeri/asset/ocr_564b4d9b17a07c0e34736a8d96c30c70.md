Legal and institutional responses to the scope of agricultural sustainability have so been quite limited. For example, no instruments legally linked to combating drought and desertification have apparently been developed (UN 2018). Building Instruments and Soft Law Instruments have contributed to the development of rights and innovative means of conflict resolution. Greenhouse emissions, through the 2015 Paris Agreement, the water footprint through the Global Food Security Index (GFSI), rights of workers linked to the United Nations (UN), are also widely discussed in international agreements and treaties.

In any case, agriculture and food are at the heart of Agenda 2030 not only for their systemic nature but also to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG). According to studies carried out by Car et al. (2018), the question of why the combination of food and agricultural systems are essential to advancing the 2030 agenda can be answered considering several factors in common: (i) food shortages and rural poverty are causes of the political problems—instability, conflict, violence, and migration; (ii) the agricultural sector is one of the most responsible for the use of existing energy resources; (iii) agricultural practices are closely linked to the environment, health, and climate change; and (iv) the agricultural sector is the source of income for the poor and vulnerable people. According to Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO 2019), agriculture is closely linked to poverty reduction, where more than 30% of the world's workers are employed in agricultural and rural sectors.

Given the complexity of food and agricultural supply chains, a collaboration between these sectors is key to success in promoting sustainable practices. For example through joint actions, governments, industry, non-governmental organizations, and research centers can achieve concrete measures to eradicate hunger while preserving the environment (UN 2019). While companies pursue sustainability strategies through external partners (e.g., Carrefour with the International Foundation for Human Rights), support farmers and traditional agricultural practices, increasing coverage cropping, different rotation patterns, and no-till, improve soil fertility, retain water and achieve food yields that provide nutrition and market increase. In this case, Danone, for example, provide a Livelihood Fund for agriculture and invest in low-carbon agriculture projects to produce higher quality products through traditional practices. TechnoServe uses no-till approaches to promote better yields and prevent land depletion and deforestation, while many governments are currently providing support to low-income farmers through training programs (Fixing Food 2018).

On the other hand, technology is also enabling significant progress and precision farming and new digital tools can help increasing the efficiency and sustainability while improving yields (Belhuova-Uzunova and Danche). Initiatives such as ClimateSmart Agriculture focused on farmer resilience, access to finance, the supply chain, and agricultural-related deforestation, work through a partnership with governments, research centers, universities, and other institutional partners. The Connecterra monitors and Indigo Agriculture's database also provide traceability and are able to predict the best investment times in the industry. Finally, as venture capital and impact investing play a role in promoting and facilitating the financing of technologies and tools to make sustainable agriculture possible in most parts of the world (Fixing Food 2018).
