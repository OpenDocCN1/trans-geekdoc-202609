The direct/progressive reduction of sugar depends on legal restrictions, consumer preference and the recipe to be used (Di Monaco et al. 2018). Gradual sugar reduction is a slow-progressing method; consumers become accustomed to sugar concentrations without noticing any change (Oliveira et al. 2016). Bulk agents such as polyextrose, insulin, resistant starch and fructoolaccharides are also used to compensate for the undesirable structural changes caused by reduced sugar content (Auchauch Dockman 2012).

The use of artificial sweeteners instead of reducing sugar content could cause metabolic diseases; therefore, the sugar perception thresholds should be lowered by gradually reducing the sugar content and sweetness intensity, in processed foods (Deliza et al. 2021). The study emphasised that policymakers provide responsive regulatory approaches to encourage the food to action and that food scientists play a crucial role in promoting new approaches.

The use of flavoring agents (such as vanilla or fruit extracts) are complementary to (or enhancing) the flavour profile of the original product taste-area interaction (Stieger and van Velde 2013, Di Monaco et al. 2018, Caporizzi et al. 2021). Model systems containing sucrose (placed between hydrocolloids and layers of mixed air-in-oil gelatin gel) have also been reported as an effective approach for increasing the perception of sweetness. The results showed that it was possible to reduce the sucrose content by 20% without causing any reduction in flavour intensity (Mosca et al. 2010).

These approaches can bring in some technological difficulties; employing more than one approach may increase its acceptability. Some new methods that can overcome these technological problems are promising. For example, modifying the microstructure of the food can compensate for the loss in the perception of sweet taste due to reduced sugar levels. This relies on the fact that food structure and taste perception during oral digestion are closely related. The reduction of sugar content content can be accomplished by the following strategies:

+   - Ensuring the homogeneous distribution of sugar in the food structure
- Modification of the mechanical properties of food
- Increasing the release of liquid from the food matrix
- Modification of bolus rheology (Stieger and van Velde 2013, Kuo and Lee 2014, Busch et al. 2013, Ilyasoglu and El 2021).

The inhomogeneous spatial distribution of sucrose is a new approach to increase sweetness. For the non-homogeneous distribution of sucrose in semi-liquid foods, air bubbles, layered gels that get in sucrose distribution, and oil-in-water (O/W) or water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion systems have been used (Hutchings et al. 2018, Ilyasoglu and El 2021). Using double or multiple emulsion systems allow sugar to be dispersed non-homogeneously in the food structure. This is achieved by keeping the sugar content in the product constant and using sugar-free ingredients (e.g., air, oil, etc.) to occupy some volume of the product. This enables the perception of higher sweetness by the taste receptors to compare the food with the same total sugar content and the same volume. The oil phase in the emulsion is the part that contributes to the bulk of the food does not contain sugar, resulting in a higher sugar concentration in the water phase and a more intense perception. The outermost layer in the water-in-oil (W/O) multiple emulsions interacts with the tongue surface and affects taste perception (flavour and aroma). Therefore, an increased flavour intensity can be achieved by adding sucrose only to the outer water phase (Ilyasoglu and El 2021).

Inhomogeneous sucrose distribution was also studied in cake formulations, where it was demonstrated that inhomogeneous distribution could allow the reduction of sucrose content without changing the perceived sweetness (Dadali and Elmaci 2021).
