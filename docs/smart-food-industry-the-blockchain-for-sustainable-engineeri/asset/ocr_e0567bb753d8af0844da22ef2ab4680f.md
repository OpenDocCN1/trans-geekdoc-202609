# 2.8 3D Printing

3D printing or additive manufacturing has been around for some years, the technology is now gaining momentum. It is a new technology that has the potential to alter and revolutionize a variety of industries, including manufacturing, construction, aviation, and healthcare, as well as the food industry (Wieczek 2017). 3D printing refers to methods that include joining or solidification of material(s) under control in order to construct a three-dimensional item. Through the successful accumulation of layers of materials, computer-aided software makes this approach, which creates three-dimensional slices of intended and desired objects (Jiang et al. 2019). Additionally, 3D printing enables the layer-by-layer printing of intended and desired objects (Portaugeau et al.). For example, binder jetting, material jetting, directed energy deposition, powder fusion/binding, vat photopolymerization, material lamination and extrusion are all techniques associated with 3D printing technology.

From a sustainability perspective, 3D printing establishes itself as a viable technique of preparing and presenting food in the modern day (Raj 2017). In turn, could increase consumer engagement and satisfaction and contribute to developing competitive advantage. 3D printing technology has shown to be effective in reducing the complexity of the food supply chain by displacing old production techniques (Chen 2016), and fabricating customized food (Sun et al. 2015). Implementing this technology will enhance the closeness of production operations to customers/users, resulting in a reduction in transit volume and associated packaging, distribution, and shipping costs. As a result, inventory management for food producers would become more cost-effective and simple, as they can make products on demand. Additionally, less transportation within the food chain may result in less damage to the environment due to reduced traffic from shorter vehicle journey.
