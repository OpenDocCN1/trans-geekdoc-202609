Other non-edible lignocellulosic residues, such as wheat straw and corn cob, can be used as feedstocks for production of biofuels (ethanol or butane), an indirect way to convert them to energy. Food producing-by products are mixtures of substances of which might have specific functionalities when used as food ingredients by themselves, and are, therefore, valuable. Extracting these molecules from the wastes is the preferred waste valorization method since it adds more to a by-product than using it for energy (directly or indirectly), or as animal feed. There are processes that partially address this valorization approach (a review of which is provided, among others, by Moates et al. (2016)). For example, citrus fruit peels or apple pomace, both of which are utilized are found in Miraba et al. (2013), Dimou et al. (2019), Saini et al. (2019), Pires et al. (2021), Sharma et al. (2021) and many other research papers. These novel processes are developed in a lab scale and then scaled to industrial level using process tools, in order to perform a Technological Economic Analysis (TEA) and evaluate the processes and the feasibility of such a process. This includes chitosan production from shrimp waste (Gómez-Rodríguez et al. 2017), arabinoxyl and co-extraction of lipids and fats from fish skins (Kanyamakidis et al. 2015), production of xylitol, lactic acid, activated carbon and phenolic acids from sweet grain (Massa et al. 2013), production of plasticizer, lactic acid and animal food from waste (Kwan et al. 2015, 2018), biodiesel and ethanol co-production from lipid-producing sugarcane (Huang et al. 2016), anthocyanin extraction and ethanol production from purple and blue corn (Somavat et al. 2018), mango processing waste-integrated biorefinery (Arora et al. 2018), astaxanthin production from agro-industrial wastes (Duarte et al. 2020), integration of whey-products in cheese-making processes (Gomez et al. 2020), and many others.

In most of the above references (and many others not presented here), process simulation is used to assess the techno-economic feasibility of large scale production encompasses the proposed technologies. Waste may contain many valuable substances and the question which of one is technically and economically feasible and environmentally to extract does not have an obvious answer. At the same time, there may exist multiple alternatives for processing the same waste leading to different products or determining the optimal split of the waste throughput between the alternative processes is a frequent question that arises. For all these reasons, computer-aided process design is a valuable tool in assessing waste valorization processes.

In this chapter, two waste valorization cases are presented, simulated and analyzed: a pectin production from citrus fruits and an isobutanol production from corn stover. These processes were simulated with the SuperPro Designer v12 by Intelligent, Inc. (www.intelligent.com). SuperPro Designer can be used to efficiently model any processing mode (batch, semi-batch or continuous) in a wide variety of industries including food and food waste valorization processing.

The two case studies represent different operation modes (continuous for the pectin case, batch for isobutanol), different processing approaches (chemical for pectin, bio-chemical for isobutanol) and generate products of different nature and use (ingredient in the pectin case, bio-fuel in the isobutanol case).

## 2. Process Simulation Case Studies

### 2.1 Pectin from Citrus Peels

This study demonstrates the development and analysis of a simulation model for the production of pectin from citrus peels. Pectin substances are complex mixtures of polysaccharides polymers found in most plants in varying concentrations. They behave like stabilizing gels and contribute to the adhesion between the cells and to the mechanical strength of the cell wall. Typical molecular weights of pectins are within a range of 50-150 kDa. Commercial pectin is typically extracted from citrus fruit peels or from apple pomace (Van Buren 1993).

Pectin have found many applications in the food industry mainly due to their rheological properties; they are used primarily as gelling agents, and as texturizers, emulsifiers, thickeners, and stabilizers. When pectin is dissolved in a hot aqueous mixture of sugars and then cooled down the gelling temperature, a gel will form. The strength of the gel depends on parameters such as the type of pectin, the content and types of sugars, the pH of the solution, and the presence and concentration of divalent solutes, especially calcium. The basic categorization of pectins depends on whether they are highly esterified (high methoxyl or HM pectins) or not (low methoxyl or LM pectins) (Hoofeer 1991).

In the food industry, HM pectin is used as ingredient in jellies and jams, with LM pectin used in dairy. Pectin are also used as and/or stabilizers in dairy products, soft drinks, and pharmaceuticals. For instance, pectin stabilizes acidic drinks as sugary, and improve the mouthfeel and pulp stability in juices. Pectin also acts as a fat substitute in baked goods. In the pharmaceutical industry, it is used to reduce blood cholesterol and gastrointestinal disorders. Other applications of pectin include edible films, paper substitutes, and plasticizers.

The global market demand for pectin and other thickening agents is expanding. This trend is mainly due to the evolution of eating habits globally as people in developed and developing countries prefer processed foods and particular textures. The global pectin market in 2015 was estimated at about $964 million, and it is expected to grow at 7.1% compound annual growth rate (CAGR) over the next nine years. The food and beverage industry is the largest consumer pectin in the USA (https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/pectin-market, accessed Nov. 2021).

The global production of all citrus fruits is more than 100 million tons per year. The juice uses more than 30% of the fruit production, from which it wastes about 50% as peels, seed members and generates about 10-25 million tons per year. Citrus peels have a moisture content of about 80%. The remaining solid is composed by fibers, which are primarily pectins but also hemicellulose, cellulose and lignin, soluble sugars, proteins, and others. The peels also contain about 0.5% of essential oils, of which 90% is D-Limonene. The current industrial valorization uses a considerable part of citrus peels to extract and produce pectin, as well as essential oils, while the rest end as animal feed and disperse in landfills. Novel uses and processing of citrus peels include the extraction of cellulose, sugars, flavonoids, phenols and other antioxidant compounds by physical and chemical methods (Patsal et al. 2020), or thermochemical processing for the production of bio-oil and biochar by synergy or hydrogen, via pyrolysis (Kwon et al. 2019, Abdel et al. 2020) and gasification (Chiodo et al. 2016).

The sections that follow describe and analyze a pectin production process from orange peels modeled in SuperPro Designer. The development of the model was based on data available in the patent and technical literature and the authors’ experience. The modeled plant runs in continuous mode, processes 35000 kg/h of peels and produces around 645 kg/h of pectin.
