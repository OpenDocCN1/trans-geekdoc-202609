## 9. Circular Economy

According to the European Union Parliament, the circular economy is a model of production and consumption which consists of sharing, leasing, reusing, repairing, refurbishing, and recycling existing materials and products. In this way, the product life cycle is extended to minimum. (European Parliament 2015), the circular economy implies reducing waste to When a product is at the end of its life, the product can be reused as much as possible to provide added value. The circular economy is a regenerative system that minimizes the uses of resources, waste, emissions, and excess energy by slowing, closing, and narrowing the energy and material cycles (Geissdoerfer et.. 2017).

The circular economy is important to maintain the availability of raw materials for industry in the midst of the increasing human population, where the raw materials available from nature are limited. In addition, the circular economy can also reduce environmental impacts due to the use of raw materials, able reduce carbon dioxide emissions, and benefits the economic growth (Hysa et al. 2020).

The bioeconomy has similarities with the circular economy. Some of the similarities are increase resources with the higher co-efficiency and lower Green House Gas (GHG) effects. In addition, they also reduce the demand for fossil carbon and lead to the valorisation of waste and side streams (Carus and Dummer 2018).

However, the circular economy is a broader cross-sectoral concept compared to the bioeconomy. The circular economy strengthens the eco-efficiency processes and uses recycled carbon to reduce the use of additional fossil carbon. Bioeconomy replaces fossil carbon with biobased carbon from biomass.
