## 2.2 Waste and By-products

Date is continually and economically valuable fruit, having an important role in human diet especially in the Middle East and North Africa (Ahmed et al. 2021). Dates are consumed freshly or processed into a broad range of food products, including date bars, syrup, juice concentrate, jam, candies, and wine. Processing of dates can involve fumigation, washing, drying, sorting, and packaging. Generally, processing steps of dates involve higher durability and lower transfer costs. Large amounts of waste and by-products are generated during these processing steps. Date seeds (11–18%) and date cake (17–28%) are the major processing wastes of date fruit (Siddiq et al. 2023). Seeds are mostly discarded during de-peding, while date cake is discarded from the syrup or juice extraction process. Besides, date studies have revealed that date seeds contain carbon-rich minerals, including lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, and proteins (Oladeip et al. 2021, Rambau et al. 2021). Also, studies have reported some fatty acids such as oleic acid, linoleic acid, respectively, 21%, and it was predicted that 0.90 million tons of date seeds contain 9% of oil was processed all over the world (Maser et al. 2020, Ahmed et al. 2021). Thus, date seeds are potential resources for several areas, i., bio-fuel (Oladipo et al. 2021), biochar (Samer et al. 2021), bioethanol (Bouazid et al. 2022), hydrogen (Rambau et al. 2021), and activated carbon (Heidinaraje et al. 2018). Date cake also has a high amount of dietary fiber, phenolic compounds, and antioxidant that can be considered a source of antioxidants. Considering the production rate and the nutritional value of these wastes, Heidinaraje et al. (2018) investigated the conversion of date cake as KOH-activated carbon. They observed high surface area (263 m³) and microporous texture in activated carbon prepared from date cake. The usage of these valuable wastes not only address their problems related to waste management but also make functionality to industries. Accordingly, plum has a potential for the production of oil and for pharmaceutical industries. More recently, the production of gallic acid from tannin-rich biowaste (black plum seed) was investigated by Saeed et al. (2020). This research proved a solution for the disposal of industrial processing waste of plum, and also ensured the production of cost-effective and high yield gastric acid at industrial scale.

## 2.3 Apple Waste and By-products

Apple wastes are collectively known as apple pomace, which contain the skin, pulp and seeds derived from the processing of apple juice, jam, and sweets (Dhillon et al. 2013). It is predicted that 20–40% of the total production of apple is processed annually, which generates 6 to 12 million tons of apple pomace during the processing of apple juice (Awashi et al. 2021). The apple pomace is mainly composed of dietary fibers (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, pectin, and carbohydrates), proteins, minerals and vitamins. Apple pectin and oligosaccharides, procyanidin B₂, kaempferol, ramnetin, cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, phlorizin, and anthocyanin, p-coumaric acid, p-coumaroyl-quinic, ferulic and chlorogenic acids, which are the major bioactive compounds in apple waste, especially apple pomace-based bio-refineries (biopower, biochemical, biopolymer, etc.) Considering the biopowerery of apple wastes, apple pomace could have an alternative for environmental, economic, and social areas through waste management and sustainability. Furthermore, apple pomace is one of significant sources of commercial pectin production.
