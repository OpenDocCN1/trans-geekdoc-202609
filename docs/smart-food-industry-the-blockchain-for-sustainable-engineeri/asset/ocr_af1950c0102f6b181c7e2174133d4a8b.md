# 2.2 Big Data Analytics

In recent years, big data analytics (BDA) approaches have arisen as an enabling tool for organizations to gain a competitive advantage (Fosso Wamba et al. 2015, 2018, Tan et al.). BDA gained considerable interest from academics, practitioners, and decision-makers (Dubey et al. 2016). Big data consists of diversified (variety), voluminous data that is generated at a high rate of speed (velocity). These attributes are often referred to as the 3Vs. It was later developed by adding value and became the 5V concept (Queroz and Telles 2018). Big data advancements have the potential to provide several advantages to sustainable logistics, such as improvements in tracking, operational performance, inventory management, distribution, and transportation management. Food logistics generates a considerable quantity of data due to the sheer volume of food products exchanged along the value chain. These characteristics may be linked to their provenance (origin), ownership, physical conditions (weight, size), quality (form), environmental circumstances (e.g., temperature, moisture, microbial activity), and freshness (Jagat and Dau 2019).

From a sustainability perspective, BDA could usher in the development of data-driven food supply chains with actionable insights for decision making. BDA could facilitate coordination among supply chain partners and improve supply responsiveness to market demand resulting in better performance (Yu et al. 2018). BDA can aid in the optimization of truck delivery routes by analyzing data to identify or adjust delivery routes in real-time road accidents or traffic congestion, hence avoiding food waste after delays in transit. In this, it aids in the optimization of traffic, and reduces energy consumption as greenhouse gas emissions and other pollutants. The data could also be used for new product and services development. Integrating with cutting-edge technologies, the information retrieved from BDA could contribute significantly to scheduling by aggregating resources, capacity, and demand in real-time, enabling supply chain actors to make informed decisions and perform preventive actions which lead to increased food safety and food waste mitigation (Parvin et al. 2019). An area requiring further exploration is the optimization of tracking capacity and their utilization to reduce the volume of empty cargo trucks on returns. This phenomena is often referred to ‘empty miles’.

# 2.3 Digital Twin and Simulation

A digital twin could be defined as a virtual representation that simulates the integration and behavior of cyber-physical systems. These digital tools simulate physical environments and may contribute to the learning of manufacturing and supply chain times, resulting in a leaner, more adaptable, and intelligent manufacturing and supply chain environment (Abideen et al. 2021). Moreover, a digital twin is a digital/cyber reflection of various forms of data (e.g., operational data), and processes which could include physical features, machinery or tools. It evolves in lockstep with the real system and is capable of predicting the outcome of adapted solutions in real-world systems (e.g., optimizing physical operations) (Bosch and Roses 2016). A digital twin ensures over time, technology-based simulation may be utilized in a ‘one-off’ manner to plan and construct exploratory models for effective decision-making, and to develop and simulate complex production systems’ activities (Coelho et al. 2021).
