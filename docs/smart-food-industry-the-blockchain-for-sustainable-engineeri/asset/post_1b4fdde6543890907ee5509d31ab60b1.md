## 1. Introduction

The evaluation of food waste by converting into value-added products has been among major focus of attention in recent years, due to many advantages in economic, social and ecological terms. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), food waste is defined as “losses in quality and quantity through the supply chain process occurring at the production, postharvest, and processing phases” (Sharma et al. 2020). Spoilage, damage, poor forecasting, overproduction, surplus stocks, inefficient management, exogenous factors (seasonal effects), supply and demand, etc.), contamination, and packaging issues are the reasons of food waste formation (Lemaire and Limbourg 2019). FAO estimates that 1.3 billion tons of food is wasted annually around the world, accounting for one-third of all food produced (Sharma et al. 2020). The rapid increase in world population in recent years has resulted in a massive increase in food demand. By 2050, the world population is expected to exceed 9 billion, necessitating a 60–70% increase in food production (Laso et al. 2016). Generally, wastage results in significant losses of other resources such as water, land, labor, and energy (Sharma et al. 2020). In order to avoid the environmental problems and to maintain the sustainability of the resources, waste streams must be properly managed. The consequences of food waste include not only economic and social concerns, but also environmental concerns that could jeopardize the food chain (Mosna et al. 2021). Food waste is estimated to contribute to greenhouse emissions by releasing approximately 3.3 billion tons of CO2 into the atmosphere each year (Paritosh et al. 2017). According to a recent report of the World Bank, the current average generation rate of municipal solid waste in urban areas is approximately 1.2 kg per person per day, and by 2025, this value is expected to increase to 1.42 kg/person/day (2.2 billion tons of waste per day on a global scale) (Stino et al. 2016). Solid waste is generally incinerated or buried in an open area, which can result in serious health and environmental challenges. The processes associated with thermal recycling are central approaches for waste management. All procedures implement waste oxidation, which occurs as a result of incineration, gasification, or waste decomposition (e.g., thermal decomposition). Thermal disposal can be used for a variety of waste types, including municipal waste, industrial waste, hazardous waste, and sludge disposal. For this reason, several countries restrict or prohibit strategies like incineration and landfilling. The ability to convert waste to a safe material, a significant reduction in the weight and volume of waste and the ability to recover amounts of heat are many advantages of this approach. However, the incineration of food waste with a high moisture content results in the release of dioxins (Bulk 2019), which can cause a number of environmental issues. Furthermore, incineration reduces the value of the substrate by destroyed substrate (Paritosh et al. 2017). Composting is a valuable way of waste treatment that contributes to reducing organic waste for landfill or incineration, which can reduce costs and environmental impact due to collection, transportation, and treatment. However, the main problems with composting are the development of potential odors and the presence of insects and rodents (Storino et al. 2016). One of the issues for food and environmental scientists is to provide practical solutions for the disposal of food-by-products and wastes, which actively contribute to pollution. Decantation, dissolved air filtration, de-emulsification, flocculation, adsorption, centrifugation, coagulation, oxidation technologies, bioreactors, oxygenation, enzymatic treatments, and membrane technology are all used for the treatment of by-products and wastes (Castro-Múñoz and Ruby-Figueroa 2019). As a result, proper methods for managing waste are required. Food waste has gained prominence in the European political debate as a result of the Circular Economy (CE) package (EC 2015a). Indeed, CE Action Plan (EC 2015b) is defined food waste as one of the "priority areas," or areas that should be carefully considered in order to strengthen the circularity of the European economy (Cristóbal et al. 2018). In line with this, current chapter aims to provide a detailed overview of food waste valorization options, with particular emphasis on each major waste stream discarded during processing.

## 2. Fruit and Vegetable Waste and By-products

Fruit and vegetables are the most utilized and preferred commodities among consumers, and have a crucial role in human diet and lifestyle (Ganesh et al. 2022). On the other hand, considering the rate of food waste and by-products, fruit and vegetable wastes constitute the largest share by the ratio of 44% (Vilarño et al. 2017, Ganesh et al. 2022). Especially, juice processing industry has led to a huge amount of fruit and vegetable waste and by-products. The juice processing steps for various fruits involve occured waste and amounts for each process.

Global production and waste rate of fruits and vegetables with major countries are illustrated in Table 1, and their composition in waste/by-product are given in Table 2. Most of the fruits and vegetables include a considerable amount of peel, skin, and seed (Cheok et al. 2019). Recently, researchers have focused on the recovery of these wastes to overcome the adverse environmental effects and to convert them into biofuels (Ng et al. 2020), edible films and coatings (Torres-León et al. 2018), enzyme processing (Srivastava et al. 2021), microbiological media (Vieira et al. 2021), food additives (Mahmoudi et al. 2020), and green nanoparticles (Bambu et al. 2021). Besides, non-use of these wastes causes the production of biochar, adsorbent, biosorbent, carbon quantum dots, biogas, and others. 

The variety of potential biorefinery systems leads to the valorization of fruit and vegetable wastes and by-products is highly broad as seen in Fig. 2. Fruit and vegetable wastes are mostly known as having high moisture content and rich organic compounds including carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins. Additionally, highly biodegradable lignocellulosic biopolymers (cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin) are also found. These properties have adverse effects on conventional waste disposal systems due to high operating costs in the stages of transport, greenhouse gas emission and leachate discharge in landfills (Edidges et al. 2018). Depending on the chemical composition of fruit and vegetable wastes, there are various valorization options such as extraction of bioactive compounds, enzyme production, and synthesis of bio-based products (bioplastics, biochar, biogas, biofuels, and degradable biofilms) (Esparza et al. 2020).

### 2.1 Plum Waste and By-products

Plum (Prunus domestica L.) originates from Asia, and globally has a 12.6 million ton of production rate annually (Liandska et al. 2020). During the past 25 years, plum harvest has doubled worldwide, reaching 12.6 million tons in 2018 (Dolzynska et al. 2020). Plum has various bioactive compounds such as dietary fibers, phenolic compounds and vitamins (Savi and Sajic 2020). These bioactive compounds have diverse benefits for human health, mainly prevention of cancer, diabetes, and obesity. The industrial processing of plum generates a large amount of skin, pomace, and seed.
