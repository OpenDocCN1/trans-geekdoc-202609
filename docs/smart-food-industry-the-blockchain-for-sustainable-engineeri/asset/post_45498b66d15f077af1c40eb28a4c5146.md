Fast-food joints, such as McDonald's, Wendy's, Burger King, and White Castle, have been offering veggie meat alternatives in addition to their meat-based products.

Although veggie meat products were originally created for vegans, today they are manufactured and enjoyed by many non-vegans; some consume them for ethical reasons, others consume them simply because they taste good, and others consume them in the hope of losing weight or to obtain health benefits. One thing is that, since their inception, the popularity and demand for veggie meat alternatives has been growing stronger and stronger. Some may even argue that veggie meat alternatives are a possible solution to many ethical and environmental problems, such as the sustainability of the livestock sector, animal suffering, food insecurity and shortage, the problem of human overpopulation, environmental degradation, the negative health-related aspects of unplanned diets, and climate change. In this chapter, I discuss such questions and argue that, on a practical level the production and consumption of veggie meat alternatives may seem to contribute to such problems, which we should not stop at these considerations. In fact, if we reflect on the morality of veggie meat alternatives from a virtue-ethical perspective considering human excellence and flourishing, we may discover previously unexplored reasons for not promoting the promotion, sale, and consumption of veggie meat alternatives.

## 2. The Origin and Purpose of Veggie Meats

Let me begin by saying something about veggie meat alternatives and their evolution. Believe it or not, they have a long history. A popular example is a gluten product that nowadays goes by the name of "seitan". Seitan dates back to century-old China. Buddhist monks accidentally discovered seitan by letting dough soak in water, which caused the starch to dissolve, obtaining pure wheat gluten (Shortruff et al. 2014). Most of gluten obtained is a chewy and edible substance that is shaped and cooked just as it were a piece of traditional meat. Seitan is possibly the simplest of all veggie meats. Today, most grocery stores sell even wheat flour to make home-made seitan.

Also, veggie meat can be made from soy, legumes, seeds, and fungi (Bonny et al. 2015, Asgar et al. 2010). Another popular product is tofu, which was consumed as early as 965 CE. Other products include yuba and tempeh, also used for decades by some people (Bohr 2019, Shurtle and Ayoyagi 2014). These products are still used in these many days when making veggie dishes emulate meat-based dishes. In the West, the Seventh-day Adventist company Loma Foods started in the late 1800s making the earliest examples of soy- and wheat-based meats (Land 2005). In the sixties, British scientists discovered the microorganism *Fusarium venenatum*, a high-protein substance that was used (Wiebe 2002).

Thus, veggie meat has a long history. Since their inception, the market has been growing exponentially as a result of increasing social demands. The growth is due to many factors, the most important of which is that veggie meat products have become more and more appetizing. In fact, veggie meat are not only consumed by vegans and vegetarians. Even traditional meat eaters enjoy veggie meat. Not only do people enjoy veggie meats, but also all sorts of plant-based analogues, such as plant-based eggs, milk, cheese, and veggie. Veggie meat has gained popularity such that the market will be increasing over $21.2 billion USD per year (Bohr 2019, Shurtle and Ayoyagi 2014).

## Veggie Meat Alternatives: A Virtue-Ethical View

Quorn, Gardenburger, and Boca Burger started a variety of products that had better taste, texture, and appearance than the previous generation of veggie meat alternatives. Still, at that stage, veggie alternatives never appealed to anyone other than some adventurous vegans or vegetarians. It would seem safe to assume that meat alternatives did not intend to create products that would appeal to meat eaters. In my experience, early veggie meats looked like some synthetic food out of a sci-fi movie, and while their taste was not revolting, they weren't delicious. Meat alternatives in those days tasted like meat, not vegetables. They had a hybrid food feel; I remember that they were unappealing, artificial to taste, many as tasting like meat; they had a strange color and no odd texture.

By the 2000s, the taste, texture, and physical aspect of these products underwent considerable improvement. However, they were still made specifically for vegans and vegetarians. They were not marketed as meat alternatives that would please, including meat eaters. They were proposed as a sort of special plant-based food that offered something new and spicy to the typical vegetarian or vegan dish of tofu and lettuce. Not only meat eaters, but even the vegetarians and vegans did not find these products to be exciting. As plant-based company Sweet Earth's CEO Kelly Sweet remarked, "When we launched in 2002, most vegetarian products looked like hospital food" (Vox Creative 2019). This would soon change.

Since the 2000s, veggie meats changed again. With like Sweet Earth, Beyond, Garden, and Impossible to make (just a few) starting plant-based alternatives that boasted uncanny taste, smell, and behavior indistinguishable from traditional meat. Not to mention the variety of plant-based choices that could melt and stretch to all those things that dairy cheese does, eggs replaces, vegan, and more. Veggie meat producers nowadays are manufacturing products that can satisfy a wider audience, especially meat-eating lifestyles and diets. As observed, vegans, the question is whether meat eaters will eventually give up meat alternatives and consume veggie meats. With such good products might be, and as even as they could be in the future, I cannot rule off the possibility that meat eaters will become vegetarian and that meat could be substituted by veggie meats. So far, it does not look that way. According to National Dietary Panel (PAN), "U.S. consumers have not gone off beef burgers but are willing to mix things every now and then" (Seifer 2019). Surprisingly, in 2019, approximately 95% of people who purchased meatless burgers were meat eaters, and 228 million servings of plant-based burgers and sandwiches were purchased at traditional fast food restaurants. But the consumption of meat is not decreasing. In fact, it is growing exponentially (Saiidi 2019, Linnane 2019, Cooper 2021).
