For instance, biodiesel produced from animal fat has been reported to reduce the fossil CO2 emissions, by 80%, a better rate than to soybean oil (30%) (Toldra-Reegi 2020).

Thermochemical processing of meat and bone, such as pyrolysis, combustion, or gasification, is used to produce high phosphate ashes as fertilizers, and the gases from these processes are used to generate energy. Animal-by-product incineration produces excellent mineral fertilizers while providing an efficient source of heat (Holloway and Wu 2019). Conventional organic fertilizers are credited to increased atmospheric methane emission; however, organic fertilizers are a good alternative to improve crop yield with reduction in methane emission (Sharma et al. 2020).

The continuous flow of poultry industry increases amounts of solid waste (visera, bones, etc.) generated by both production and processing facilities. These materials are currently rendered into meat and bone meat, meat blood, and fat/oils (Brandelli et al. 2015). In contrast to the meat components, feathers, hoof, and hair are processed to create the meat (Hicks and Verbek 2016). Heads, gizzard and lard have traditionally been used in meat production, and feet, skin, intestines, and glands can also be a source of poultry fat (Brandelli et al. 2015). The abdominal and gizzard fat that remain inside the carcass, or other hand, are a good source of fat due to high content of unsaturated fatty acids and vitamin A (Peña-Saldarriaga al. 2022). Feather meat is a by-product of poultry processing and for approximately 8% of weight containing approximately 90% protein. Feather meat has high energy due to its lipid content, which ranges from 18% to 12%, and can be converted to biodiesel (Seidav. 2019). Furthermore, feather hydrolyates could be converted into methane gas and fuels for heating, as well to produce hydrogen (Brandelli et al. 2015). The main protein in feathers, is nearly useless. To improve digestibility, feathers are rendered in a batch or continuous hydrolyzer, inoculated with a feather-degrading bacteria (Bacillus lichen), produces a powerful enzyme capable of hydrolyzing collagen, elastin, and feather (Hicks and Verbek 2016). Poultry hides can be used to produce elastin, which is commonly used to make gelatinous feeds due to its antioxidant properties (Sharma et al. 2019). Dry pet foods typically contain high amounts of protein and fat, with low ash in content. Animal proteins, poultry proteins (e.g., mechanically separated chicken) are widely used in both dry and wet pet foods (Donatelli et al. 2018). Traditionally, the protein sources have been derived from animal by-products in the meat industry during rendering. Additionally, thermal rendering has shown to be effective in destroying protozoa, bacteria, and viruses, including RNA viruses like avian influenza (Seidav et al. 2019). Keratin-rich materials, such as nails and beaks, can be deacetylated by keratinolytic microorganisms, and are frequently used in the production of animal feed in conjunction with viscera or blood (Brandelli et al. 2015).

# 5.2 Fish Waste and By-products

Seafood losses are related to fishing, post-catch, and to the processing, distribution and consumption of fish. The processing stage, accounts for 5% of fish losses due to the generation of by-products that are edible for human consumption (Laso et al. 2016). A typical fish-processing operation includes stunning, grading, size removal, deheading, washing, scaling, gutting, cutting, bone removal, and fillet preparation (Hicks and Verbek 2016). The fish-producing products are approximately 57% (w/w) of the total catch, which is not directly used for consumption (Lapeña et al. 2018). Also, by-products produced during the fish production process, accounting for more than 30% of the fish weight and, in some cases, up to 70% (depending on fish type or processing). Among these by-products, muscle pieces, viscera, heads, skin, fins, and scales are the most highlighted ones among others (Marti-Quijal et al. 2020).
