Further evidence has been found in the Altamura caves in northern Spain, dating back to 9000-3000 BC (Hunter 2021). Julien Lesnik found termites that were included in the diet of Proto-Pleistocene hominins (Waterhouse et al. 2016, Lesnik 2019). In Biblical writings, historical records have cited the 'manna' consumed by the Hebrews during the exodus, insects responsible for feeding a nation; manna consisted of the secretion of the milky triatrubina natura (Santos and Florencio 2013). Minas et al. (2016) reported that according to the culinary journey, back to Biblical times, John the Baptist locust and honey comb.

Records of the Aztec culture have 91 insect species in their diet, which were cooked roasted, fried, in spices, boiled, or as a condiment; some were stored for dry use. With the arrival of the Spanish conquistadors, cultural overlap occurred, and many of the insects used by the Aztecs were well qualified and thus forgotten or deprecated (Santos-Elorday and Pino 1996, Santos and Florêncio 2013, Soagri et al. 2019). The Romans and Greeks used to dine on locust and larvae; Aristotle, a Greek scientist and philosopher, wrote about collecting cicadas and their use in food, demonstrating that entomophagy or orthopterophagy is a historical practice (Minas et al. 2016, Gukovushu 2019).

Although less in the western world, mostly without prejudice, insect consumption is common in Asian and African cultures, to be considered as a food or as the main constituent of the diet of different people in numerous regions of the world, including indigenous people (Kelas 2015, Gere et al. 2017). In some countries, local people have developed an intricate knowledge of the main species consumed, which is reflected in how insects are prepared, from collection to preparation as food. The taste of some species depends only on taste and value but also on customs, ethnic preferences, religion, or beliefs (e.g., vegan, kosher, and halal) and are an indispensable part of the diet of some ethnic groups (Kourska and Adamková 2016, Patel et al. 2019).

In Mexico, it consists of using some types of bug as a condiment, to replace unpleasant or repulsive odorless compounds. Bed bugs are sold alive and consumed roasted and ground, and sometimes, they are used together with pepper to season dishes. On the other hand, the Thai transform the praying mant into a paste, which flavor resembles that of shrimp with mushrooms; in China, it is believed that the consumption of ants has rejuvenating effects (Santos and Florencio 2013, Grassi 2014). In central Australia, the aboriginal culture of the Anunta tribe represents six groups of insects, which they represent as food, and feeding on one of these insects is the same as eating one's ancestor. The only member who can eat insects is the head of each group (Grassi 2014).

In Latin America, insect consumption is widespread among indigenous peoples, such as the Tukano Indians who inhabit the Colombian Amazon, consuming ants and termites as their only food of animal (Lesnik 2019). The culture of eating insects in Brazil has an indigenous origin, persisting the Suruí Indians of Pará State, who consumed beetle larvae of the species Pachymercus duro, Caryobruchus sp., Rhynchophorus palmarum, and Rhina barhibstris (Morandes 2018). The population of Popará uses locally to treat tucum oil; the juice is extracted from the inside of the tucum palm. In general, in the Brazilian states that have the habit of eating insects, four main types of insects are tanajara (female ants, genus Atta), the taguar (larva of the Morphius butterfly), larvae (the beetle Rhynchophorus palmarum and Rhina barhibstris), and the coconut worm (larva of the beetle Pachymyrus nucleorum). These species have great nutritional value, and analysis of fried larvae of R. palmarum has shown 54.3% protein, 21.1%, and 12.7% moisture (Minas et al. 2016). In Pernambuco state (northeastern Brazil), rural inhabitants enjoy the ovate abdomens of tanajaras with rice and beans, replacing the meat of domestic and wild animals with insects.
