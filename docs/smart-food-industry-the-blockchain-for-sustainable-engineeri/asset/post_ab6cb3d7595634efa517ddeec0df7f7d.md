## 5.2 Fish Waste and By-products

Seafood losses are related to fishing, post-catch, and to the processing, distribution and consumption of fish. The processing stage, accounts for 5% of fish losses due to the generation of by-products that are edible for human consumption (Laso et al. 2016). A typical fish-processing operation includes stunning, grading, size removal, deheading, washing, scaling, gutting, cutting, bone removal, and fillet preparation (Hicks and Verbek 2016). The fish-producing products are approximately 57% (w/w) of the total catch, which is not directly used for consumption (Lapeña et al. 2018). Also, by-products produced during the fish production process, accounting for more than 30% of the fish weight and, in some cases, up to 70% (depending on fish type or processing). Among these by-products, muscle pieces, viscera, heads, skin, fins, and scales are the most highlighted ones among others (Marti-Quijal et al. 2020). The disposal or non-valuation of these by-products will pollute the environment, and may endanger the ecological health. Water soluble minerals, peptides, collagen, gelatin, enzymes, oligosaccharides, and fatty acids are all present in large quantities in the aquatic products (Shen et al. 2019). Therefore, all such resources can be utilized to produce a variety of high-valued goods such as bioplastics, lactic acid, or preservative compounds, which are very useful in food, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics industry (Davy and Routledge 2018, Marti-Quijal et al. 2020). For instance, in a study, antioxidant compounds were produced through the fermentation of fish-products by bacteria isolated from sea bass (Marti-Quijal et al. 2020). As a result, authors concluded that the lactic acid bacteria from sea bass can be promoted and synthesize peptides with strong antioxidant capacity.

There is an increasing worldwide awareness and interest in the valuing of available resources, particularly the nutrients and by-products, to produce a circular economy in aquaculture. Therefore, The European Commission promoted “Blue Growth” in 2012, a green economy focused on marine and coastal sectors (EC (2012)) (Campanari et al. 2021). To recover the bioactive components from aquatic waste, three major techniques, namely chemical/physical, enzymatic and microbial methods, are used. The enzymatic method is regarded as one of the best techniques for all types of fish among the three (Ashraf et al. 2022). The crude protein content of fish products varies from 8% to 35%. Several methods for recovering protein and peptides from fish by have been developed, including acid and alkaline hydrolysis, and enzymatic hydrolysis (Zamora-Siller et al. 2018). As the valuable components, fish collagen and fish gelatin (partially hydrolyzed collagen) can be extracted from heads (92.3–100), fish (0.8–8.0), skin (2.0–12.6), scales (0.8–6.0), bones (9.0–19.0), and fish bodies (Zarubin et al. 2021). In a study, collagen was extracted from the skin of two species of chondrocytes using peppsin, with yields ranging from 14.2% to 61.2% (Bianco et al. 2017). The main components of fish scales are hydroxyapatite (30%), and protein (70%) consisting primarily of collagen and keratin (Shen et al. 2019).
