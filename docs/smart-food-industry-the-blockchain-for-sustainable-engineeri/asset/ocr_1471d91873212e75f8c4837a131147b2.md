As mentioned earlier, virtually all ethical defenses of veganism are ultimately based, or rely heavily, on the concept of suffering. For example, the typical approach that utilitarian ethicists take is to claim that the goal of morality is to act in such ways as to maximize utility and minimize suffering. More specifically, we ought to act in such a way as produce the greatest overall good for the greatest possible number of sentient beings that benefit from it. The second step is to show that suffering, whether human or non-human, is bad. After all, non-human animals represent other species. If we value only our desires and wellbeing we will revive what some refer to speciesism, unjustified discrimination among species. Thus, all suffering must be minimized, and all preferences or happiness must be considered.

Therefore, the conclusion, according to utilitarian, we would be ought to avoid and shun food produced in such ways not because individual animals or people are special, or individuals are directly wrong, but because suffering is bad, and pleasure/happiness is good. Since animal-based food produces suffering, we ought to avoid animal-based food (this is the sort of argument that Singer (1975) and other vegetarians would produce). According to such a view, the production and consumption of veggie meets the test with fairly colors, and thus it is morally permissible (or at least not wrong) to producing, selling, and consuming veggie meets a lot more satisfaction of preference or happiness and a less suffering and dissatisfaction of preferences over animal-based food.

If the issue were framed in terms of a non-consequentialist argument, such as rights views or neo-Kantian defenses, the result would not change. Such approaches, unlike consequentialism, on concepts of right or duty. For example Tom Regan (1983) argues that all animals have a right to the same basic moral rights as humans. The point of such an argument is, as Regan says, and canonically all mammals such as cats, pigs, goats over a year of age possess the same basic moral rights as humans. Many animals are what Regan calls “subjects-of-a-life”, and they “subjects-of-a-life” precisely have inherent value, and it is immoral to use them for food. A neo-Kantian approach has been championed by Christine Korsgaard. In leisure Korsgaard (2004) argues that our autonomous nature is not the only source of duty, as Kant argues. In fact, we can say that we have duties towards non-autonomous animals. The morals to ourselves arise from the fact that we experience pain and suffering, and while animals are not-legislative, rational creatures, like us, are creatures that can experience pain and suffering. As Korsgaard puts it, “human ethics are simply the same as the other animals” (2004). In other, Korsgaard’s neo-Kantian approach, too, relies upon the notion that animals are capable of suffering.

Now the first point is to make that the aforementioned arguments are by, not only the defenses of veganism. Second, it is not meant to argue to give an in-depth critique of such arguments. Third, these theories are the most influential both among academics and laypeople. For reason mentioning them it is to draw attention to these arguments' lack of ethical competence. Factors, they offer a narrow approach to morality; according to consequentialist ethics, the main focus is the result of our actions, and the total (or average, depending on the particular theory) of happiness. Thus, so long as our actions can lead to the maximization of utility, we ought to perform such actions. Since the production, sale, and consumption of veggie meat alternatives lead to the greatest good for the greatest number (let's assume so), then we ought to promote the production, sale, and consumption of veggie meat alternatives.

Similarly, non-consequentialist theories may conclude that veggie meat alternatives should be immoral practices. They are not immoral because they do not violate any basic rights, especially animals’ rights, because we do not undermine in any way our moral duties. In fact, one may argue, the production and consumption of veggie meat alternatives would favor the fulfillment of our duties to non-human animals.
