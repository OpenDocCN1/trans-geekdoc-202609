# Part III Social, Economic, and Environmental Aspects in Food Industries

## Smart Policy as a Response to New Sustainable Food System Agenda

### 1. Introduction

Intensive agriculture and poor food environments have led to severe ecological damages, climate change and contributed to a growing burden of non-communicable diseases. In response, there is an increasing call for a reorientation of food system policies from a neo-liberal and top-down approach to an inclusive governance approach towards sustainable development (2021).

The nature and urgency of a food system transition requires a simultaneous implementation of short-term policies to reduce the negative effects of the current regimes and long-term to promote transformative changes in society by supporting sustainable niches (Geels 2002). In parallel, disruptive technologies, such as AI and blockchain in general, are increasingly reshaping governance structures and pave the way for making organisational and governance processes more efficiently (Awan et al. 2020).

As a response to the growing complexity and need for system change, “smart policy” is gaining a foundation as a governance heuristic for transiting the food system. Although the concept of smart policy is not well conceptualised, it emerges as a response to new paradigms and as a governance tool to solve wicked problems at systemic level (Christensen 2009).

We argue that capacity-building around the food system—both at the socio-technological and governance level—will contribute to a shift towards a sustainable food system. As a result, this chapter aims to contribute to an understanding of smart policies as a governance heuristic for improving policy design and implementation. Based on a snapshot of the literature on “smart policy”, we aim to highlight that it has essentially applied as a governance approach for dealing with complex problems and induce policy change.

Lastly, we present a conceptual framework based on the seminal work of Christensen (2009) on smart policy as we aim to illustrate capacities for governing a food system that can contribute to security while ensuring prosperity and equity of natural, social and human capital. By doing so, we highlight a need for further capacity-building at both governance and the broader system level for a transition towards a sustainable food system.

## 2. A Snapshot of “Smart Policy” from the Literature

The concept of “smart policy” is not new in the literature, but it has in recent years attracted attention as a policy design and management tool for dealing with complex policy problems. The notion of smart policy has its roots in the seminal work of Christensen (2009), who conceptualised smart policy from an instrumental-technical perspective to implement the “new public administration approach”. Since then, it has been increasingly applied in a diversity of studies focusing mainly on the linkages between one hand, policy design, organisation, governance, and on the other hand, effective and outcomes (Kugelberg et al. 2021). Since Christensen’s seminal work are 85 papers on Scopus, with a significant increase in the last decade. None of these papers belong to social science literature. Still, the smart policy has been found in other subject areas, including environmental studies, computers, economics, engineering, energy, and health studies.

One of the first articles on policy focuses on technological innovation in the health care system and the introduction of Smart Cards in the health sector (Alpert 1993). The paper show how governance and management structures address tensions between principles of dignity and privacy to be applied in health care settings. In the following years, O’Keefe (2000) proposes a smart policy approach to face climate change, while Sherman (2001) a smart policy network to reduce violence from a public health perspective. These authors focus on the effective use of science in policy-making to reduce uncertainties in decision-making. This can be ensured by making scientific results available for policy-making and increasing access to non-academic scientific literature.

Fort et al. (2006) claim that smart policy is conducive to support innovative business solutions for urban development. The authors describe smart policy both as evidence-based approach (i.e., multicriteria analysis based on sustainable indicators) and as a new governance structure (the integration of the private and public sector) as a way to face complex and unsettled issues in urban planning. The proposed smart policy represents a way to integrate the private and public sectors and support the adoption of shared strategies.

Leow et al. (2014) conceptualised smart policy as a coherence framework for intervention among several sectors to include education, nutrition, and health in public schools. Despite high ambition from governments, the authors observe that smart policies have not resulted in new policy but rather legitimisation of the existing ones. Similar approaches to policy talk about Smart City Policy. Cagaglio and Del Bo (2016) have noted that policies are more likely to be implemented with high human and social capital, in areas with well-established ICT infrastructure, and finally, where the policy exercises in decision making are in place.
