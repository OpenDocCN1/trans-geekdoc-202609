## 4.4 Bioreactors

To scale-up of production, the use of bioreactors is a must (Steps et al. 2018, Allen et al. 2019). The bioreactor's role is to ensure a controlled environment suitable for cell culture. There are multiple categories of bioreactors depending on the method used to achieve mixing and environmental homogeneity, and thus to add nutrient and gas to the cells. Bioreactors can also provide additional stimuli to support cell growth, differentiation and even cell maturation. Regardless of the type of bioreactor, a dynamic environment is achieved with a portfolio of environmental parameters that can be monitored and controlled which includes temperature, pH and gases (O2, CO2). This level of control is not possible to be achieved in planar vessels. Bioreactors also allow for sterility which is critical for growing mammalian cells. Moreover, the resulting product would be sterile which could result in longer shelf lives for cultivated meat products due to the lack of bacteriological loads obtained through slaughter.

Stirred-tank bioreactors (STRs) are a type of bioreactor that use mechanical means to achieve homogeneity of contents. STRs are equipped with impellers (agitators) that can be of various shapes and sizes and generate either an axial, radial or mixed flow. For cultivated meat production, mammalian cells will most likely be used for typical impellers include pitted-blade or marine that generate a common flow. This type of bioreactor is a common choice for the production of high value products due to its scalability to over 20,000 L, as well as established ordering of operating systems (Zhong 2011). To achieve the high cell numbers required for meat (> 2 ×¹⁶ cells) (Allan et al. 2019), scalability is critical and as such, STRs are the most promising type of bioreactor. Moreover, despite being optimised for suspension cell types like bacteria or CHO cells used for production of biology, this type of bioreactor has been successfully adapted to the culture of adherent-dependent cells, and as, offers a level of flexibility in regards to the cell method to be chosen as it can be used with free-floating aggregates (Serra et al. 2012, Kropp et al. 2016, Abasis et al. 2017), microcarriers (deSource et al. 2016, Gupta et al. 2016, Takashi et al. 2017, Lawson et al. 2017, Rafiq et al. 2018, Hanga et al. 2020, Bodieou et al. 2020, Hanga et al. 2021) or hydrogel capsules containing cells (Miring et al. 2010, Toesto et al. 2010, Jin et al. 2010). Stirred-tank systems are optimal for cell cultures and experimental work due to easily controlled parameters (impeller RPM, number of impellers, vessel dimensions), their commercial availability at various scales up to >1000 L, the efficient gas transfer for cultivated cells and mixing of the medium. Moreover, they are also associated with high power consumption and introduction of high hydrodynamic forces, especially at the proximity of the impeller and the bioreactor (Garcia-Ochoa et al. 2011).

While STRs have been extensively and successfully used for the expansion stage, they are less likely for the tissue formation phase of structured meat products, especially when conventional scaffolds with anchor points need to be used in that process. Instead, a different type of mechanical bioreactor could be useful for muscle tissue formation. Dynamic enrichment could stimulate muscle maturation and alignment (Meinert et al. 2017).

The differentiation stage is an integral part of the production of cultivated meat products. The adipogenic differentiation is easier to achieve as it can be induced by small molecules in the media (Scott et al. 2011, Chen et al. 2017). However, myogenic differentiation very specific requirements for nutrients and environment (typically a mechanical stimulus). To satisfy these requirements, the myogenic differentiation is performed in monolayer using a hydrogel with anchor points to simulate tension, creating a passive tension and the minimal mechanical stimulus required for differentiation and maturation of tissue (Vandenberg and Karlisch 1989, Morgan et al. 2003). This is not a scalable approach. To achieve the mechanical stimulus required in stirred tank bioreactors, several approaches have been tested: Torgan et al. expressed and differentiated satellite cells on microcarriers in a microgravity bioreactor. However, the cells expressed in “normal gravity” stirred tank bioreactors (Torgan et al. 2000). They have reported that the shear stress generated by the stirring alone in stirred tanks used with microcarriers can promote differentiation; however, the shear stress needs to be controlled (Naskar et al.). For example, high shear stress within the ranges of 5-10 Pa was found optimal, while a shear stress of 16 MPa resulted in a higher expression of myogenic markers and longer myotubes, 42 MPa determined a better alignment (Naskar et al. 2017).
