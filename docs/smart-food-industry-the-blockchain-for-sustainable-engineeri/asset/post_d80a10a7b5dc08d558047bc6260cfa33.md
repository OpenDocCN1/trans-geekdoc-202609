## 2.1 Sustainable Agriculture

Discussions about the environmental and social aspects of conventional agriculture, in the mid-1980s, global environmental issues (destruction of forests, acid rain, environmental accidents, greenhouse effect), leaving the economic environment and institutions reaching the consumers. Concerned about the quality of the products they are getting and the environmental damage caused by the conventional agricultural models, consumers started to interfere in the production system, through the demand for healthy products, which were produced respecting the environment and the health of workers. The term "sustainable agriculture" can quite fit in context, the Brundtland Report was essential for the concept of sustainability, previously restricted to other branches of the economy, to be extended to agriculture. Also entitled "Our Common Future", was created in October 1987 by the World Commission on Environment and Development (WCED) and noted the incompatibility between sustainable development and current production and consumption patterns (UN 1988, Purvis et al. 2019).

In general, the concept of sustainable agriculture can be described as a system of agricultural practices, based on scientific approaches through which it is possible to produce healthy food with respect to land, air, water, as well as the health and rights of farmers. The goal of sustainable agriculture is to satisfy humanity's need for food, improve the quality of the environment, maintain the natural resource base, use non-renewable and agricultural resources more effectively, implement biological cycles, and sustainably support the rural economy, development and quality of life of farmers (FAO 1988). Therefore, sustainability is more than ensuring the protection of the natural resource. To be sustainable, agriculture must meet the needs of present and future generations for its products and services, ensuring profitability, the environment, and social and economic equity (EOS 2019).

The various approaches to agricultural sustainability must take into account a number of factors, constraints, and opportunities that are determined by the availability of agricultural resources, as well meeting the needs of individuals in communities. The configuration of agriculture and the means to stimulate sustainability processes will require continuous adjustment, innovation, and improvement in strategies, policies, and technologies to maximize productivity and social, economic, and environmental aspects. All processes reflect the evolution of society's values and accumulated knowledge, which have a major impact on how sustainability goals are defined in practice. Within this complex system, specific constraints and natural and socioeconomic boundaries will define what fits into the sustainable operational space (Holling 2006, Rostström et al. 2009, Movilla-Pairo et al. 2019). A growing challenge for sustainability is identifying and balancing interactions, benefits, and tradeoffs that result from different agricultural configurations. Furthermore, the institutions that govern agricultural (which determine production, type of technologies and practices, and level returns obtained) are fundamental levers for regulating the type and distribution of products and services that can be derived from agriculture (FAO 2019).

Legal and institutional responses to the scope of agricultural sustainability have so been quite limited. For example, no instruments legally linked to combating drought and desertification have apparently been developed (UN 2018). Building Instruments and Soft Law Instruments have contributed to the development of rights and innovative means of conflict resolution. Greenhouse emissions, through the 2015 Paris Agreement, the water footprint through the Global Food Security Index (GFSI), rights of workers linked to the United Nations (UN), are also widely discussed in international agreements and treaties.

In any case, agriculture and food are at the heart of Agenda 2030 not only for their systemic nature but also to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG). According to studies carried out by Car et al. (2018), the question of why the combination of food and agricultural systems are essential to advancing the 2030 agenda can be answered considering several factors in common:
- (i) food shortages and rural poverty are causes of the political problems—instability, conflict, violence, and migration;
- (ii) the agricultural sector is one of the most responsible for the use of existing energy resources;
- (iii) agricultural practices are closely linked to the environment, health, and climate change;
- (iv) the agricultural sector is the source of income for the poor and vulnerable people. According to Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO 2019), agriculture is closely linked to poverty reduction, where more than 30% of the world's workers are employed in agricultural and rural sectors.

Given the complexity of food and agricultural supply chains, a collaboration between these sectors is key to success in promoting sustainable practices. For example through joint actions, governments, industry, non-governmental organizations, and research centers can achieve concrete measures to eradicate hunger while preserving the environment (UN 2019). While companies pursue sustainability strategies through external partners (e.g., Carrefour with the International Foundation for Human Rights), support farmers and traditional agricultural practices, increasing coverage cropping, different rotation patterns, and no-till, improve soil fertility, retain water and achieve food yields that provide nutrition and market increase. In this case, Danone, for example, provide a Livelihood Fund for agriculture and invest in low-carbon agriculture projects to produce higher quality products through traditional practices. TechnoServe uses no-till approaches to promote better yields and prevent land depletion and deforestation, while many governments are currently providing support to low-income farmers through training programs (Fixing Food 2018).

On the other hand, technology is also enabling significant progress and precision farming and new digital tools can help increasing the efficiency and sustainability while improving yields (Belhuova-Uzunova and Danche). Initiatives such as ClimateSmart Agriculture focused on farmer resilience, access to finance, the supply chain, and agricultural-related deforestation, work through a partnership with governments, research centers, universities, and other institutional partners. The Connecterra monitors and Indigo Agriculture's database also provide traceability and are able to predict the best investment times in the industry. Finally, as venture capital and impact investing play a role in promoting and facilitating the financing of technologies and tools to make sustainable agriculture possible in most parts of the world (Fixing Food 2018).
