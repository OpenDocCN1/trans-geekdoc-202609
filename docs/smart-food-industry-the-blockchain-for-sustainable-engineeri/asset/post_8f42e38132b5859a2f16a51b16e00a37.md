
# 3. Isobutanol from Corn Stover

The production of biofuels from crop biomass has attracted enormous global attention in the last two decades, with bioethanol (produced from sugar or starch-based feedbacks) and biodiesel being the most prominent (produced from vegetable oils by transesterification processes or cracking). While bioethanol is a well-established biofuel, there are several drawbacks with its use including a low energy content and environmental regulation limit on the amount that can be blended with gasoline. On the other hand, biodiesel has been associated with extensive changes in land use which does not only have a negative effect on CO2 emissions but also impacts food availability. As a response to the concerns, the EU in 2016 to phase out first-generation biofuels by 2030.

As a result of the although, the global interest has shifted to second-generation biofuels that can be produced from various types of non-edible biomass. One of the proposed alternatives is butanol. Butanol exhibits similar advantages as ethanol but possesses several superior characteristics including higher energy content and vapor pressure which facilitates blending with gasoline. As a result, US specifications allow butanol to be blended in gasoline up to 16% whereas the relative limit for ethanol is 10%.

Regarding various candidate alcohols, isobutanol has a higher octane number and is less toxic than n-butanol. Furthermore, it is mainly produced with ethanol and acetone in a process called ABB fermentation (Tao et al. 2014), isobutanol has a wide range of industrial applications: as a precursor, it is used in the production of various industrial esters, chemicals, and automotive paint color additives. According to an Allied Market report (2021), the isobutanol market size was valued at $1.0 billion in 2020, and is projected to reach $1.9 billion by 2030, growing at a compound annual growth rate of 6.3% from 2021 to 2030.

The production cost of biodiesel is considerably affected by the feedstock price which accounts for much as 60-65% of the total cost (Mansur et al. 2019). At present, biofuel production mainly relies on sugar and starch-based feedbacks such as sugarcane, corn, or wheat. While these feedbacks are globally available, they are also used as food sources or food ingredients for humans and animals. Therefore, alternative feedstocks with similar availability which are not food-needed to be utilized. A promising approach is the use of lignocellulosic biomass from agricultural residues (corn stover, sugarcane, wheat straw and rice), forest residues (sawdust, timber) and energy crops (switchgrass and miscanthus). These materials are available in greater abundance and at a lower cost than the traditional edible feedbacks.

Renewable lignocellulosic biomass from agricultural and forest residues is a promising alternative to the traditional sugar and starch-based feedstocks for the production of biofuel. Besides the fact that it is not used as food ingredient, lignocellulosic biomass at a lower cost is a very important aspect considering that the cost of materials accounts for 60% of biofuel production cost (Mansur et al. 2019). However, the lignocellulosic biomass requires an additional process to convert the cellulose and hemicellulose into fermentable monosaccharides that can be utilized by the biofuel-producing microorganisms. This hydrolysis involves either a chemical (acidic) or biochemical (enzymatic) agent that acts as a catalyst. On the two alternatives, enzymatic hydrolysis is considered a more environmentally friendly option, but on the other hand, requires large amounts of enzymes (cellulases) which substantially increase the overall process cost (Kolesinka et al. 2019). The hydrolysis of cellulose generates glucose which can be utilized by a variety of microorganisms to produce biobutanol. On the other hand, the hydrolysis of hemicellulose in a variety of sugars, mainly pentoses, which can be used by certain microorganisms, although with potentially lower conversion percentages. To improve the efficiency of the enzymatic hydrolysis, the lignocellulosic biomass is treated by a pretreatment method that increases the surface area so that enzymes can penetrate more effectively. Common pretreatment methods include wet, liquid hot water steam explosion, ammonia fiber explosion and supercritical CO2 explosion (Shirirkav et al. 2016).

A known limitation of the fermentation production of butanol is product inhibition. Butanol is toxic to microorganisms at concentrations as low as 13 g/L for n-butanol and 8 g/L for isbutanol (Mariano et al. 2011; Philippou and Liao 2009). To address this problem, the concentration of substrate during fermentation is typically limited to less than 60 g/L (Green Al-Shorgani et al. 2018). This pretreatment results in reduced water consumption and lowbutanol titers after fermentation which negatively affect both the heat requirement and the amount of wastewater generated in downstream processing. As it is clear from the above, the feasibility of the process depends, to a large extent, on the fermentation titer (Stephopoulos 2007).

There are two approaches to overcome the toxicity limit genetic engineering and process modification. A number of researchers (e.g., Asumi et al. 2018, Smith and Liao 2011, Jang et al. 2013) have attempted to improve the fermentation yield of butanol by using engineered toxicity-resistant microorganisms that are able to operate at increased product concentrations. The fermentation performance can be achieved by the implementation of (in situ) product recovery during fermentation. The objective of this approach is to maintain butanol concentration in the fermentor below levels, enabling the use of denser substrate solutions and increased product titers. Recovery technologies include adsorption, liquid-liquid extraction, permeation, reverse osmosis, vacuum fermentation, vacuum stripping (flash generation) and gas stripping (Roussis et al. 2019).

This case study analyzes a process that produces isbutanol from corn stover. The term "corn stover" encompasses material left in the field (leaves, stalks, cobs) after harvesting corn. Isobutanol can be produced by engineered E. coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Zeeji 2014). The technical challenges that must be overcome in order to render the production of isbutanol economically viable can be summarized in two main objectives: the reduction of cost of raw materials through the utilization of alternative feedstocks and the improvement of fermentation yield, titer and productivity by genetic engineering and application of new technologies (Chen 2016; Hahn et al. 2018). The following detailed study can be regarded as a "what-if" analysis on the hypothetical isbutanol fermentation titers and can be used as a reference for the simulation and comparison of different scenarios based on more realistic or optimistic data.

## 3.1 Process Description

This case study presents a conceptual design of a process producing isbutanol from corn stover using an engineered E. coli that can theoretically achieve an isbutanol titer of 65 g/L during fermentation in a vacuum stripping system. The operation is batch with a batch size of 247 metric tons (MT) of corn stover produced around 40.8 MT of isbutanol per batch or 40000 MT per year (assuming an operation time of 48 weeks). The process is divided into four sections: pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis, fermentation, purification, and a utilities section steaming and extraction from the lignin-rich residue of the pretreatment.
