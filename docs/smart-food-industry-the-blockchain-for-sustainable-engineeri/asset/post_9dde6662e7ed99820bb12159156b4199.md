# Insects as Food: A Sustainable Foodways

### 1. Introduction

Since the beginning of mankind, in the search for food for survival, insects have been part of the animal (entomophagy) and human (anthropophagy) diet due to their abundance, availability, and ease of collection. Insects have been used as food throughout history and are one of the food sources humans relied upon. Despite the absence of insects in the conventional western diet, they remain popular. Every primate is, to some degree, insectivorous (Macrew 2014, Soagri et al. 2019).

Human evolution has caused mankind to go through adversity in the search for survival; so, as hunters, they began to collect edible, available or cultivated insects and realized that they could be a great source of food (Minas et al. 2016). Eating insects can be considered taboo in some countries due to social, religious, or beliefs (Cho and Hong 2018). Eggs, pupae, and adult insects have been used by humans, and this trend has continued into modern times. Man was omnivorous in early development and ate insects extensively before humans had hunting or agriculture; insects constituted a critical component of the human diet. Most people mostly lived in warm regions where different types of insects were available, especially in the absence of vertebrate meat (Kourska and Adamková 2016).

The appetite for insects seems to be the main driver of ancestors' discovery. Our closest primate relatives, chimpanzees, are famous for their ability to use tools, and many of them have used insects efficiently (Koops et al. 2015). There is evidence from early human sites that bone tools were used for insect extraction. In caves in the USA and Mexico, evidence of insect consumption in human history has been found by analyzing coprolites. For example, at the Federal University of Santa Maria, coprolites include ants, beetle larvae, ticks.

Further evidence has been found in Altamura, northern Spain, dating back to 9000 BC (Waterhouse et al. 2016, Lesnik 2019). In Biblical writings, the 'manna' consumed by the Hebrews during the exodus was responsible for feeding a nation (Santos and Florencio 2013, Minas et al. 2016). Back to Biblical times, John the Baptist ate locust and honey comb. The Romans and Greeks used to dine on locust and larvae; Aristotle, a Greek scientist and philosopher, wrote about collecting cicadas and their use in food, demonstrating that entomophagy or orthopterophagy is a historical practice (Gukovushu 2019).

Although less in the western world, without prejudice, insect consumption is common in Asian and African cultures to be considered as food or as the main constituent of the diet in numerous regions of the world (Kelas 2015, Gere et al. 2017). In some countries, local people have developed an intricate knowledge of the main species consumed, which is reflected in how insects are prepared, from collection to food. The taste of some species depends not on taste but also on customs, ethnic preferences, religion, or beliefs (e.g., vegan, kosher, and halal) and are an indispensable part of the diet of some ethnic groups (Patel et al. 2019).

In Latin America, insect consumption is widespread among indigenous peoples, such as the Tukano Indians who inhabit the Colombian Amazon, consuming ants and termites as their only food of animal (Lesnik 2019). The culture of eating insects in Brazil has an indigenous origin, persisting the Suruí Indians of Pará State, who consumed beetle larvae of the species Pachymercus duro, Caryobruchus sp., Rhynchophorus palmarum, and Rhina barhibstris (Morandes 2018). The population of Popará uses locally to treat tucum oil; the juice is extracted from the inside of the tucum palm. In general, in the Brazilian states that have the habit of eating insects, four main types of insects are tanajara (female ants, genus Atta), the taguar (larva of the Morphius butterfly), larvae (the beetle Rhynchophorus palmarum and Rhina barhibstris), and the coconut worm (larva of the beetle Pachymyrus nucleorum). These species have great nutritional value, and analysis of fried larvae of R. palmarum has shown 54.3% protein, 21.1%, and 12.7% moisture (Minas et al. 2016). In Pernambuco state (northeastern Brazil), rural inhabitants enjoy the ovate abdomens of tanajaras with rice and beans, replacing the meat of domestic and wild animals with insects.
