# 4.2.3 Cell Encapsulation in Hydrogels

The suggested culture method is encapsulation in hydrogels. Hydrogels have their own intrinsic properties to be exploited and can be processed into different geometries, making this most versatile technique. Hydrogels can be categorised as either natural or synthetic; hydrogels, usually being cheaper, favor, more biocompatible, are likely to have necessary RGD sequencing for accommodating cell adhesion and capable of being digested or remodelled by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), while synthetics have limited batch variability, are often hydrophobic, possess strong covalent bonds within their matrix improving their mechanical strength, service life and although, and are more easily modified for attachment, degradation, and stiffness requirements (Gyles et al. 2017). However, natural hydrogels will have increased variability and less room for optimisation due to their plasticity in chemical and mechanical tuning, while synthetics will not be edited or for use in food products without rigorous purity checks. They may require external chemical degradation to allow cellular migration and prevent stalling of proliferation. Chemical conjugation and surface functionalisation of hydrogels can be expensive but may prove to be a valuable opportunity to generate versatile IP and an optimised process.

The encapsulation can be in a continuous, solid-state hydrogel acting as a scaffold to offer structure, which when degrades allows the cells grow or differentiate, or if they be in smaller spheroids, microporous, microcarrier systems. Large hydrogel scaffolds have provided advantageous for production of well-distributed muscle tissue due to their ability to unfirmi static tension with cell attachment points. These systems have been termed BAM (bio-artificial muscle) constructs (Gholova et al. 2018).

Novel strategies under development include the immobilisation of growth factors via embedding in the hydrogel scaffold (Silva et al. 2009) which micropatterned design could encourage differentiation different lines in regions or the addition of acetylcholine to encourage myotubes towards to accelerate differentiation (Krause et al. 1995). Fibro-adipogenic progenitor and adipose stem cells have also been successfully encapsulated in hydrogels as alginate or collagen or fibrinogen which were then shaped into microfibers, to enhance differentiation into fat tissues (Hsiao et al. 2015, Dohmen et al. 2022).

# 4.2.4 Culture on Scaffolds

Culture on scaffolds is another method applicable to cultured meat products. Scaffolds can be used during both the expansion and differentiation phases. In fibrous carriers which are either suspended in a stirred-tank reactor (STR) or packed in a packed-bed reactor (PBR). However, the expansion phase is typically low and limited to a few population doubles before the cells cover the surface area. They then have to be dissociated and re-seeded into a larger scaffold/surface area, unlike the microcarrier based systems, where cells can migrate on freely added carriers and several population doublings can be achieved without the need for passage (Hanga et al. 2021). Scaffolds are mainly useful for the differentiation stage and are indispensable for the production of structured cultured meat products (e.g., steaks, fillets, etc.).

Biomaterial scaffolds have been used for many years in the field of regenerative medicine and tissue engineering for transplantation purposes. Scaffolds provide internal structure and fall into two distinct categories: permanent or resorbable. Permanent scaffolds tend to be bioinert and remain in place, providing the structure to the growing cell mass. The material properties (stiffness and elasticity) can be tuned to direct differentiation of the cells to a specific lineage. Resorbable scaffolds, on the other hand, typically degrade as the cells differentiate, possibly even releasing bioactive molecules as it breaks down (Nikolov et al. 2019). The tissue formed at the endpoint would be in that case comprised only by the cells with no additional biomaterial and, more likely to be approved by the regulatory bodies as long as it can be demonstrated that the degradation components pose no risk.
