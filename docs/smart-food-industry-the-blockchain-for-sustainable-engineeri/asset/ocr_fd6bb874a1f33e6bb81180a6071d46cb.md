# 3.1 Challenge 1: The Attitude-behavior Gap

When it comes to save the planet, consumers are good intention. If you ask them about the
environment cause, they do not hesitate to convey their concern and their willingness to act in
sustainable ways. Nevertheless, when in the marketplace, these good intentions fail to translate
to real behavior. This inconsistency between consumers' declared values, attitudes, and intentions
on one hand, and their real purchase behavior on the other, is well documented in the literature as
the green-attitude-behavior gap (ElHaaffer al. 2020). This gap applies to sustainable
food products, as well as other product categories such as personal care products, electronics,
apparel, and it can be traced at both the purchase phase (at the purchase), and at the disposal phase
(recycling, upcycling, reusing).

To approach the attitude-behavior gap as a primordial challenge in promoting sustainable food
innovations requires a thorough understanding of this phenomenon. The gap entails that for some
sustainable innovations, the problem is beyond attitude acceptance of the innovation, but
rather a behavioral reluctance when it comes to purchasing and integrating the innovation
in consumers' diets. Some reasons could explain this hesitation in the innovation

To begin with, consumers face a difficult choice when it comes to switching to eco-friendly
food alternatives. If the decision was based on values, then action would be guaranteed,
but consumers often assess the costs associated with a behavior before undertaking it
(Delmas and Colgan 2018). For instance the choice of buying organic fruits. If this solely based
on the environmental factors of the organic fruits (soil is protected from bioaccumulation
of chemical substances, groundwater is protected from contamination), consumers would buy
or organic alternative in a heartbeat. But this specific example comes with substantial costs. Consumers
would probably pay considerably higher price. Moreover, switching costs are encountered as
consumers would have to visit specific points such as farmers' market, and more time and
offort invested in the research. This situation creates a conflict of interest for consumers
bween their altruistic values (the environment) and economic values (their own) following
with lower costs and most personal gains (Delmas and Colgan 2018).
Thus, consumers are not able to prioritize the environment over their own beliefs in consumption
choices (ElHaaffer al. 2020), when the costs are larger than the
perceived benefits (Delmas and Colgan 2018).

Another reason for the attitude gap can be found in the defense and coping mechanisms
developed by consumers to protect their mental wellbeing. Indeed, when there is an inconsistency
between their values and their actual behavior, consumers experience a moral discomfort and
accumulated feelings of guilt and shame (ElHaaffer al. 2020). To alleviate these negative emotions,
consumers tend to justify their unsustainable behavior through neutralization techniques (Atkinson
and Kim 2015, Gruber and Schlegelmelich 2014). These techniques include, among others, the denial
of responsibility and the defense of the necessity (Gruber and Schlegelmelich 2014). For instance,
when consumers don't go the extra mile to purchase bulk instead of canned food, they would blame
the in of these options and their need to buy and consume this type of food.
This is a serious problem, as it can easily turn into patterns of justification, and ultimately turn into
ifference about sustainable behavior, establishing and maintaining the inconsistent behavior over
time (Gruber and Schlegelmelich 2014).
