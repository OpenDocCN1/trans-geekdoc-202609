I love this stuff - bitcoin, Ethereum, blockchain technology - and what the future holds.

ABIGAIL JOHNSON

We’ve all heard of the blockchain, mostly in connection with Bitcoin. But do you know what it is? How it works? Why do we even need it? Not many people do, but you obviously want to know, given that you’ve picked up this book.

Blockchain technology is classed as one of the world’s greatest innovations. Many proponents claim that it can cause massive disruption to just about every industry and will impact all our lives in one way or another within the next decade or so.

Is this true? Is it the greatest innovation the world has ever seen? Or is it just another hype that will go the same way as many others? Are we seeing another bubble that will burst as spectacularly as the dot com bubble burst in the 80s?

Perhaps the more important question is, will this technology lead financial firms and governments to change how they store and process our data, or will they continue as they are? Many people are unhappy with the rising number of data leaks and hacks we’ve seen in recent times, and the blockchain could be just what we’ve been looking for to keep our data secure.

This book will answer these questions and more. From what a blockchain is to its advantages and disadvantages, we’ll look at everything. We’ll even take a technical look at how a blockchain works, its use cases, now and potential, and how this technology will impact all of us in the years ahead.

What it won’t do is convert you into believing that the blockchain is the be-all and end-all of everything, that it is the answer to your prayers, to all the problems the world faces. You will have to make your own mind up about that once you have finished reading it. I will provide you with everything you need to understand the technology, both good and bad, and talk about some of the hype.

This is for those of you who know little about the blockchain but want to understand it. While it is technical in places, I have tried to keep it simple to ensure every reader can understand it. I hope you enjoy it and learn all you need to know about blockchain technology.

Blockchain is the biggest opportunity set we can think of over the next decade or so.

BOB GREIFELD, NASDAQ CHIEF EXECUTIVE

That simple quote should have grabbed your attention; it should make you sit up and wonder exactly what this opportunity is, why it’s so great and why there’s so much hype around it.

This chapter will describe what a blockchain is, comparing it to the current transactions we do daily, whether financial or otherwise. We’ll also discuss trust issues and the primary differences between the blockchain and Bitcoin, although we’ll delve into this more deeply later in the book.

So, what is a blockchain?

Perhaps the simplest definition is that the blockchain is a database, a method by which we store transactions and records of value.

That isn’t very interesting, is it! It doesn’t make you widen your eyes and shout, “wow!” In fact, it probably makes you think, “huh? All that fuss and hype for a simple database?”

That would be a bit unfair, though. Saying the blockchain is just a database is like saying that email is the new postal service. Yes, the blockchain is undeniably a database, but that doesn’t tell you anything about the sheer genius behind it, and it stores your transactions and records of values.

Right now, whenever a transaction or record of value is stored in a database, we rely heavily on governments, banks, or other companies to ensure this information is recorded accurately. We trust that the banks won’t steal the money we deposit with them because the government regulates them. We trust that, should our bank fail, the government will make sure our money is safe.

When we pay for something using a credit or debit card or transfer money from our bank account to another person’s, we trust that the banks and credit card companies will take the right amount from our bank account and put it in the seller’s account. The seller trust that they will receive the money from the bank or credit card company. They trust that the bank or company will handle any disputes or fraudulent activity relating to that payment.

If we go to a store and pay cash for something, the seller trust that this piece of paper we give them can be used to purchase goods in another store. After all, the government backs that piece of paper, and we trust that they will honor that backing. The sellers’ trust that they can turn that piece of paper into a digital balance at their own bank. They trust that they can use that digital balance to purchase other goods or services online or by card.

We trust these third parties to look after our data and money. We trust the banks and other financial institutions to ensure our information is kept secure and safe. We trust that they maintain databases containing transactions and balances and that they are accurate. The banks trust that the governments maintain their own records and databases of every note they issue.

This trust goes way beyond the financial and into almost every area of our lives. Let’s say you have joined a library. That library maintains multiple databases containing the following information:

  • Every book they have
  • Every library member
  • Every book that has been borrowed and who has it
  • The date borrowed books are due to be returned
  • Overdue books

They also have databases containing your personal information, including your home address. Should you borrow a book and not return it, they can send fines and even institute legal proceedings against you for theft.

All this information is centralized, and each library maintains its own individual systems and records, and we trust them to keep our details private.

There is another common theme – we don’t trust one another.

Imagine the scenarios laid out above taking place without a trusted third party in the mix.

Let’s say you have your own business, a store of some kind. A customer comes in and purchases goods totaling $50. They hand you a signed piece of paper that says, “I owe you $50,” telling you that you can use it in another store to purchase goods.

Would you trust that person?

Of course you wouldn’t.

But that’s what you do every day when you pay for something using paper cash. That $50 bill in your wallet is nothing more than a piece of paper that says, “I owe you $50,” signed by the government. You accept these pieces of paper for what they are, and you trust that you can use them in any store. Those stores trust that they can deposit this paper in their bank accounts or use it to purchase goods in another store. And so it goes on.

All of that amounts to an awful lot of trust.

So, how does the blockchain fit in? Quite simply, the blockchain offers huge potential for those countries whose citizens don’t trust their government, banks, financial institutions, currency exchanges, and so on.

The financial system in the USA is one of the most regulated in the world, but that didn’t stop major banks and financial institutions from crashing during the 2008 financial crisis, one of the worst to hit the world in decades. These were companies that had been in existence for hundreds of years. Companies that went bust overnight, taking every penny of people’s hard-earned money with them.

Greece is a developed country in Europe, which took on the Euro as its currency. In 2015, the banks froze all bank account deposits, and people were only allowed to withdraw the Euro equivalent of $70 per day from an ATM.

These people believed in these banks, believed they were trustworthy. What else were they meant to do with their money? Hide it under their mattress? As soon as someone finds out that’s where your money is, you become a target for theft. If your house goes up in smoke, so does your money.

If the government can freeze withdrawals from the banks and banks can disappear overnight in countries like the USA, Greece, and other developed countries, how can anyone in a less developed country with little regulation trust their governments and banks?

The short answer is, they can’t.

TRUST AND THE BLOCKCHAIN

Around the world, billions of people live in countries whose governments are nothing more than dictatorships. These governments own the banks, and people cannot even use their local currency in the stores. There are high levels of crime and a distinct lack of legal systems to help protect people. This is happening right now in many countries. Even if you could trust that the banks wouldn’t go bust or steal your money, in some countries, the government monitors every deposit and can use your transactions to arrest you, put you in prison or even execute you.

Going back to our library example, let’s say you borrow a book, but the government doesn’t approve of the subject matter – something like 1984 by George Orwell, for example. In this case, the government, which is monitoring everything, might see your reading habits as a red flag. That could lead to them investigating your life, arresting you, or worse, in some countries.

Clearly, transactions are difficult and full of risk in countries with no trust in the governments or banks. If you place your money in a bank, you risk losing it to the bank or the government. If you buy a house or make some other large purchase, you might be forced to save for this in cash, gems, gold, or other materials, which brings its own risks – you could lose your savings in fire or theft, for example. Even if the worst doesn’t happen, and you manage to save the money you need, you risk it in other ways. For example, the person you are buying a house from may take your savings and not transfer the house ownership to you. Because the transaction took place in cash, there is no electronic proof or any other evidence of it taking place.

The blockchain is based on trust, it is the core concept, and the blockchain is, in itself, a trust system, allowing transactions to be made between people without needing a centralized third party involved. You can carry out any transactions of value on a blockchain. While the most common is cryptocurrency, in recent years, that has been extended to digital files, property, money, shares, pretty much anything you want to sell to or buy from someone else.

BITCOIN AND THE BLOCKCHAIN – WHAT’S THE DIFFERENCE?

The blockchain first came to our attention when it was mentioned in conjunction with Bitcoin. When Bitcoin was created, the first blockchain was created, but we’ll be covering that in more detail in chapter 3.

The blockchain is one of Bitcoin’s underlying technologies, and I say ONE because many people think it is the only one. However, when Bitcoin was created, it was done with multiple cryptocurrency technologies, not just the blockchain.

Systems based on the blockchain are currently in use in many applications in multiple industries, including social networks, cloud storage, digital identity, decentralized applications, voting, and many more that we’ll discuss later. It would appear that the blockchain has an endless stream of possibilities, whereas Bitcoin has one use and one use only – as a digital payment system.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

The key takeaways from this chapter are:

  • The blockchain is similar to a database and is used to store transactions and records of value. You can record just about anything on the blockchain.
  • Most of the transactions we carry out today require a third party to ensure security and trust and ensure the transaction takes place. Those third parties include governments, banks, credit card companies, and so on.
  • The blockchain eliminates the need for that third party and lets people carry out their transactions with one another.
  • Billions of people reside in countries where the governments, banks, legal systems, and other third parties cannot be trusted to keep accurate records or carry out transactions. In these cases, the blockchain is particularly useful to provide the assurance and trust people need when they transact with one another.
  • While Bitcoin is based on the blockchain, the blockchain is not based on Bitcoin
  • Bitcoin is a digital payment system, while systems based on blockchains can be used for just about any transfer of value.

“The blockchain does one thing: It replaces third-party trust with mathematical proof that something happened.”

ADAM DRAPER

So, now you know that the blockchain is more than just a simple database, you must want to know how it all works, right? In a non-technical way, this chapter will explain how a blockchain works – a more technical explanation can be later in the book when we delve deeper into things. We’ll talk about how the blockchain got its name (quite simple, really), changing things once they’ve been added (clue – you can’t), double-spending, and how value is exchanged on the blockchain. We’ll also delve into distributed consensus, mining, and proof of work.

In the previous chapter, we learned what the blockchain is, and we looked briefly at how it is used to replace third parties in a transaction. Now we’ll look at some examples that explain how it all works.

We’ll use our library example again. We know that a library is a third party that keeps centralized databases of every person who borrows a book from them. They also hold a database showing every book they have and how many copies of each. Let’s say you want to borrow a specific book, but there’s only one copy of it, and someone else has it. You can ask the library to let you know when the book is back in the library, but you can’t ask them to tell you who has the book – they cannot reveal personal information about their members. The person who has borrowed the book might be your next-door neighbor, but you can’t knock on their door and ask them to give the book to you. The library has its own central database, and they will not share any of its information with you.

Now let’s take things one step further and say that we have a shared library. Everyone contributes books, and other people can borrow them. You probably have books that others would like to read, and no doubt others have books you want to read. Anyone can join this library, and when they borrow a book, they can pass it on to someone else without taking it back to the library first.

The big question is this – how do you record all the necessary information? You need to know:

  • What books were available
  • Who borrowed which book
  • Who originally owned the book

These records are not just for your own books but all the books in this shared library. You need to know everything – who owns the book, who borrowed it, who did they lend it to, and so on. Sure, you could have one member of the shared library keeping all the records, but that’s no different from how it’s done now – it would just be a centralized database with an intermediary maintaining it.

Now, you are probably asking yourself, what’s the point of this? Surely I should just get a Kindle and get these books on it.

This is one scenario where blockchain technology comes into its own, with benefits far outweighing the traditional database system. The blockchain is a decentralized database that holds the records of every book in the shared library. With a database like this, all library members can access the records. They can see what books are available, who owns them, who has them at the moment, and if that person has lent them to someone else.

Whenever a book is “borrowed,” every record relating to that book is updated so every member can see it. There isn’t a centralized database and no third party maintaining and controlling the records because every library member helps maintain the entire database.

WHERE DID THE NAME “BLOCKCHAIN” COME FROM?

Continuing with our library example, a transaction is created when someone borrows a book. Multiple transactions occur simultaneously, so they are grouped, creating a block. This block is then placed atop the last block with reference to the previous block that links them. For example:

With all the blocks linked together, a chain is created, and this is where the name “blockchain” comes from. Each block is linked to the previous one by referencing it. The previous one refers to the block before that, and so on, right back to the first block in the chain.

Anyone can see the last block in the chain, seeing who borrowed what books. They could then go to the previous block to see its transactions and right back to the start. This blockchain does not have any regulating body or centralized database. If someone claims to be the original book owner, ownership can be traced right back to the first block, commonly called the “genesis block.”

CHANGING BLOCKS AND TRANSACTIONS

Once a block has been permanently added to the blockchain, it cannot be changed by anyone who added a transaction, nor can it be tampered with by anyone else. Because every block refers back to the previous one, anyone intending to change a transaction and commit fraud would need to change every block that comes before the one they want to change.

We use the term “confirmation” to refer to the number of blocks on top of any transaction. Some people wait until six confirmations have been added before they will accept a payment – this is their way of ensuring that the transaction cannot be changed.

DOUBLE-SPENDING

The blockchain also solves the problem of double-spending. Let’s say that someone wants to steal books from the shared library. Whenever someone borrows a book, it creates a pending transaction. This transaction goes to all the people on the network so it can be validated and added to the chain – everyone does this validation. Whoever adds the transaction to the group of other pending transactions and then adds a validated transaction block to the chain is given a reward. When the new block is added, all the databases are updated with the new transaction record.

Every person on the network can see everything- who has borrowed a book and who from. Because everyone knows who currently has every book, everyone can see when a book is not returned. In that way, double-spending or borrowing a book already with another person is avoided. In terms of other blockchains, it means that a transaction cannot be done twice using the same currency.

We need to consider another factor. The borrower uses a token to pay the lender whenever they borrow a book. This token is known as a “bookcoin.”

Let’s assume that you want to make a profit. When you lend a book to someone, you are paid one bookcoin. When you borrow a book, you pay the lender one bookcoin. To make a profit, you need to lend more books out than you borrow. Sneaky Pete is a new member of the shared library. He joined the library despite suspicions from other members that he was going to do something, well, sneaky.

Sneaky Pete contributes a book to the library. Someone borrows it, and he is paid one bookcoin. Because he is somewhat sneaky, he develops a plan to borrow a lot more books than his bookcoin account allows for. He borrows a book from member A and then immediately borrows another book from member B. These result in two transactions being created. The first goes to the entire network for validation and approval, and Sneaky Pete pays member A one bookcoin. Because the new transaction is validated, it is added to a new block which is then added to the blockchain as:

  • Sneaky Pete borrows “a book” from member A
  • Sneaky Pete pays member A one bookcoin.

Once this transaction has gone through, a new one is sent to the network for validation:

  • Sneaky Pete borrows “another book” from member B
  • Sneaky Pete pays member B one bookcoin.

The network looks at Sneaky Pete’s balance – he only had one bookcoin, and he spent that on the first book he borrowed. Sneaky Pete is trying to copy his coin and trick the entire network. However, because the blockchain is open, everyone can see every transaction that ever took place and can trace them right back to the start. They can see that Sneaky Pete received one bookcoin for lending his book and that his balance was just one bookcoin. He doesn’t have the two that he was trying to spend, and all network members can see this. Most of the network agrees that the second transaction is not valid and will not allow him to have the second book. The payment is invalidated, the transaction refused, and not placed on the blockchain.

DISTRIBUTED CONSENSUS

Whenever a transaction is sent to the network to validate, most people on the network must agree for the transaction to be considered valid. This is called distributed consensus. It doesn’t require 100% agreement because there may be people on the network who are trying to cheat the system by validating fake transactions and double-spending.

Most blockchains have a consensus threshold of more than 50%. Therefore, if at least 50% of those on the network agree on a transaction, it is validated.

This is the essence of a decentralized blockchain and is how they work to validate transactions. Rather than one centralized party agreeing on everything, the entire network shares the responsibility. Everyone connected to the network gets to have a say in whether a transaction should be validated and added to a block, which then goes on the blockchain.

MINING

If you are familiar with cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin, you have likely heard the word “mining” being used. When a transaction is created, it goes to all people on the network. Each computer is given a puzzle to solve for this to happen, which is connected to the next block. Whoever solves it first adds the transaction to the block and adds the block to the chain. For this, they are given a reward, usually in the form of the token or cryptocurrency used in the network. This is called mining because it is similar to mining small bits of something valuable from a large block.

PROOF-OF-WORK

The reward a miner receives isn’t just for solving a puzzle and adding a block to the network. It is also a kind of compensation for contributing electricity, computer power, and resources to keep the network running efficiently. It’s similar to a combination lock. For a new block to be added to the chain and the miner to receive their reward, they have to solve the puzzle that gives them the combination lock. This can only be solved by guessing the numbers, and every person on the network will make random guesses. The fastest person to solve it gets the reward and the honor of adding the block onto the blockchain. Once the combination has been solved, all other people on the network can use those numbers to make sure they open the lock.

Solving the puzzle is proof that electricity, computer power, resources, and time were contributed, and the miner is rewarded for keeping the blockchain running.

This chapter looked at how blockchains work in the case of a shared library. However, the books in this library can easily be replaced by anything of value. For example, if we used property title ownership instead, the example would show how the blockchain can be used to transfer property ownership and manage the ownership titles. When a property is transferred from one owner to another, every person on the network is notified of the transfer. Most of the network will approve the transaction, and it then gets added to the blockchain as a public record.

If the property owner attempted to sell the title to two people, the whole network would see it as a duplicate transfer, and one would be rejected.

As we already mentioned, these blockchains are most useful in countries where the citizens do not trust their government, banks, or the legal system, and record systems are unreliable and have to be done manually. The blockchain can replace a cumbersome, untrustworthy centralized system with a network maintained by everyone. This can have huge benefits, not just for property titles but for anything of value.

We took a general look at how a blockchain works in this chapter, with a few examples to help you understand. Later, we’ll drill down into the blockchain and look at how it works more technically.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

The key takeaways from this chapter are:

  • To process a transaction and validate it, it must be added to a group of similar transactions and added to a block
  • The new block is added at the end of the chain. Each block references the previous block, thus linking them in a chain, which is how the name came about
  • The blocks are chained right back to the block that started the chain, which is called the “genesis block”
  • The network validates every transaction block on a decentralized blockchain, and they are all given information about every transaction. No central party or database controls it.
  • A transaction block is tough to reverse once it has been added to the chain. Each block added is called a confirmation block, confirming that no reversals will happen. The more blocks there are, the harder it is to reverse a transaction until, at some point, it is no longer feasible. The Bitcoin network allows 6 confirmation blocks to confirm a transaction will not be reversed.
  • Distributed consensus requires that a majority of network computers agree on a transaction to validate it before it can be added to the blockchain
  • Double-spending is when someone tries to do the same transaction more than once. The transactions are sent several times but only will typically be accepted and added to the blockchain – the rest are rejected
  • A double-spending attack can happen when one user has control of over half the computers on the network, allowing the user to control the transactions that are accepted and added to the blockchain
  • Mining is a process whereby transactions are validated, and new blocks are added to the chain. Each miner is given a small reward when they add a new block to the blockchain
  • Proof-of-work requires a puzzle to be solved before a block is added to the chain. These puzzles are not easy to solve but can easily be proved, much like a combination lock. This proof is evidence that the resources, electricity, and computing power contributed to the network.

“Blockchain technology could change our world more than people imagine. Bitcoin, however, could be a bubble.”

JACK MA

We first saw the blockchain formally mentioned in Bitcoin’s original code but, while the blockchain and Bitcoin are now separated, their history is irrevocably intertwined, and that’s what we’ll be discussing n this chapter.

The technology that underlies every blockchain is cryptography which, in itself, has a very long and varied history dating back many thousands of years. Cryptography has long been used to protect messages and secrets, and perhaps one of the most famous examples is the Caesar Cipher. Julius Caesar used this cipher when he wanted to send communications with sensitive information in them.

The Caesar Cipher is really quite simple – each letter in a message is replaced with another alphabetical letter a certain number of letters away from the original. For example, all the letters could be moved five letters forward. So, A would become F, B would become G, C would become H, and so on until all the message’s letters were replaced with new ones. Apart from the message writer, the only other person who would know the code would be the recipient, who would then be able to decipher the message.

At that time, literacy wasn’t high, and there were hundreds of languages being spoken globally. That meant even if an enemy intercepted the message, the chances of them being able to read the message were very low, and many simply assumed they were written in foreign languages. Today, it would be easy to decipher a message like this, but in those days, it was an effective encryption method that made it difficult to intercept and understand messages.

Modern cryptography has come on in leaps and bounds, but the foundations remain the same. Data or messages are changed by replacing the numbers and letters with others, ensuring the only person who can read it is one who knows the code and can decrypt it.

So, how does this fit with the blockchain’s underlying cryptographic technology?

THE FIRST DIGITAL CURRENCY

In the 1980s and 1990s, several papers were published that proposed cryptography could be used to secure data while, at the same time, that data would be securely linked in chains. These proposals were made along with proposals for digital currencies.

David Chaum wrote and published a paper, “Blind Signatures For Untraceable Payments,” in 1982. This paper led to him being accredited with inventing blind signatures and digital cash. A blind signature obscures a message’s content before it gets signed. While the contents stay hidden, the digital signature can be compared to the original, one of the earliest cryptographic signatures for cryptocurrencies.

This paper, and subsequent ones published by David Chaum, proposed that digital currency could be obtained and spent in a way that banks or other institutions couldn’t trace it. Chaum, Mont Naor, and Amos Fiat also proposed systems of offline transactions that could potentially solve the double-spending problem by detecting if the currency had been previously spent.

Chaum founded DigiCash in 199o, thus using the ideas he proposed to create a digital currency. In 1994, the very first DigiCash payment was sent electronically, and the DigiCash press release started with:

“World’s first electronic cash payment over computer networks. (Release Date: May 27, 1994}

Electronic cash has the privacy of paper cash while achieving the high security required for electronic network environments exclusively through innovations in public key cryptography.”

That press release came out 14 years before Bitcoin was created, but it could easily be a Bitcoin press release if you were to use the word “Bitcoin” instead of “electronic cash” in that release.

So, DigiCash was responsible for the first untraceable electronic cash system, ensuring payments could be made with the governments, banks, or any other institution knowing about it. Cryptography was the underlying technology, a system of public and private keys and blind signatures that is still used by today’s cryptocurrencies.

In 1994, most people weren’t even aware of what the Internet was, so, perhaps, DigiCash was a bit ahead of its time. In 1998, the company declared bankruptcy, and eCash Technologies, another digital currency company, purchased the assets.

EMAIL SPAM AND THE PROOF-OF-WORK ALGORITHM

When the Internet was still in its infancy, a problem raised its ugly head and proved very difficult to resolve – email spam. In 1997, a system was proposed to limit spam with a denial-of-service (DoS) using hashcash, which was a proof-of-work algorithm.

This algorithm required the system from which the email was sent to solve a puzzle. The answer would then need to be inserted into the email header. This meant the email sender would need to use resources and computing power to send that email, which made sending spam emails in bulk difficult. The puzzle wasn’t easy for the sender to solve, but the receiver could easily verify the right answer, thus filtering out any email that didn’t have the proof-of-work attached.

THE FIRST DIGITAL CURRENCIES

In 1998, “bit gold” was proposed by Nick Szabo. This was a decentralized digital currency, and Szabo proposed that each person on a network would provide computing resources to solve cryptographic puzzles. Before a new puzzle could be solved, most of the network would need to agree on a valid answer for the current one. Once the puzzle was solved, and the network accepted it, it would then become a part of the next one. Every puzzle had a time stamp on it. Because each puzzle was linked to the next one by its solution, it became a chain.

Szabo stated that, unless control was passed to a centralized authority or bank, digital currencies were still subject to the problem of double-spending – they could easily be copied and pasted. The work he did on Bit Gold was his attempt at combining a decentralized digital currency with the solution to double-spending.

Bit Gold was never meant to be a real currency, purely existing in theory. However, it is accredited as the first digital currency to lay the framework for blockchain technology and Bitcoin to be built on in later years.

Another paper was published in 1998 by Wei Dai, titled “B-Money – An Anonymous Distributed Electronic Cash System.” This paper proposed foundations for all cryptocurrencies and is referred to in the Bitcoin white paper issued by Satoshi Nakamoto. Wei Dai states that electronic cash systems must have the following to function correctly:

  • Computational work and proof-of-work
  • Rewards for the completed computational work
  • A ledger that all members verify and update
  • Fund transfers completed on the group ledgers and cryptographic hashes used to verify them
  • Digital signatures to sign every transaction. These must use public key cryptography, and the network must verify them

Finally, in 2000, a paper was published by Stefan Konst, providing solutions for the proper implementation of chains secured by cryptography.

THE BITCOIN SCENE IS SET

All of this, everything that happened between 1980 and 2000, together with the many published academic papers, led us to the blockchain and Bitcoin.

In 2008, that infamous white paper was published on the Internet. Satoshi Nakamoto, a name thought to be a pseudonym, published the paper called “Bitcoin: A Peer-To-Peer Electronic Cash System,” in which he discussed a digital currency called Bitcoin and chains made of transaction blocks linked together. It is worth noting that, when he discussed this, he never referred to it as a “blockchain.”

In 2009, the idea of Bitcoin became a reality. Satoshi Nakamoto took his academic idea and turned it into the Bitcoin network, creating the very first blockchain. In Bitcoin’s original source code, the word “blockchain” was actually referred to as two words – “block chain.”

This blockchain was one of Bitcoin’s core features, designed to solve the double-spending problem, and was a distributed public ledger for every Bitcoin transaction.

Nakamoto is also given credit for mining the “genesis” block, the first-ever block on the Bitcoin blockchain. He left the following message in this block:

“The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks.”

Nakamoto may have left this message as proof that the block was created on or after January 3rd and to comment on the failures of the currency market and banking structure. The headline came from a UK newspaper so Nakamoto may have resided in the UK when he did this.

As mentioned earlier, the word “blockchain” was originally two words when used in conjunction with Bitcoin, and this remained the case even when Bitcoin gained more awareness. It took years for the words to be joined into what we know and use today.

The first blockchain used with Bitcoin was not perfect, and it suffered problems, like any business venture or major technology does. In August 2010, the first big issues were discovered with the Bitcoin protocols when it was discovered that some transactions had been altered between being submitted and being recorded on the blockchain. Thus, the official transactions had been tampered with. It was determined that people could bypass the restrictions built into Bitcoin and change original transactions to read more than they should. The extra was then skimmed off.

This system vulnerability was exploited, and one single transaction managed to create more than 184 billion Bitcoins, sending them to just two network addresses. The transaction was spotted quickly and was removed from the blockchain. This resulted in the Bitcoin network being overhauled and updated. To this day, the same thing has not happened again.

THE SILK ROAD

In 2011, a drugs marketplace was launched. It was called Silk Road and it was a drugs version of eBay, where people could sell and buy drugs online. The main payment form used was Bitcoin and while this resulted in more Bitcoin being used, it also served to associate the cryptocurrency with illegal activities.

Bitcoin continues to grow in both public awareness and popularity and, in 2013, it reached a high of $1,000. Despite coming under heavy criticism from governments and law enforcement, it seemed that Bitcoin could not be stopped.

However, the FBI closed Silk Road down in 2013, seizing all their assets and arresting their creator, who, it seemed, would face life imprisonment. At the same time, Mt. Gox, the biggest of the Bitcoin exchanges handling around 70% of Bitcoin transactions, was fined by US governmental agencies, facing regulation issues. By the end of the year, the exchange had stopped withdrawals into USD, and, early in 2014, they declared bankruptcy.

The Bitcoin source code provided ample opportunity to create other cryptocurrencies, and many began to appear. However, they used different blockchains. The Bitcoin blockchain was split into a fork, and Litecoin was created as a separate digital currency with its own blockchain. However, it offered some benefits over Bitcoin, not least in the time taken to add blocks onto the blockchain. Where Bitcoin takes around 20 minutes for a block to be added, Litecoin reduced that to just two and a half minutes.

Once Silk Road and Mt. Gox closed down, the Bitcoin price plummeted to $200. New cryptocurrencies were being created, and many people declared publicly that Bitcoin was done.

However, this couldn’t have been further from the truth. When Silk Road was closed, Bitcoin’s association with illegal activities and drugs began to fade away, and interest began to grow in Bitcoin’s underlying technology.

Sadly, as much as people couldn’t forget the incredibly public failure of Mt. Gox, large businesses, financial companies, and banks struggled to accept Bitcoin as a serious currency and, although faded, there was still that association with drugs and hitmen paid for by Bitcoin. Even if we excluded the criminal associations, people considered Bitcoin to be fake, a scam, or nothing but a bubble that would soon burst. While Bitcoin was still associated with crime, the word “blockchain” became fashionable and respectable. That one word separated Bitcoin and its technology, allowing the blockchain technology to stand on its own. Financial institutions and other businesses had no interest in Bitcoin but blockchain technology was a completely different matter.

ON THE UP

In 2014, Bitcoin’s price was quite low, as was interest in the currency. However, blockchain interest was fast gaining momentum. Rather than being used for cryptocurrency, blockchain technology began revolutionizing databases and distributed ledgers, with many businesses turning to it to replace outdated ledger systems to record their data entries.

In 2015, the Ethereum blockchain went live, taking the blockchain technology’s potential up a notch or two. The network is designed for decentralized applications, or D’Apps as they are commonly known, to run on the Ethereum blockchain in conjunction with smart contracts. Both D’Apps and smart contracts are considered the future of blockchain technology, which is often called blockchain 2.0.

These days, most major global finance companies and banks are developing new systems based on the blockchain to replace their existing networks and database systems. blockchain technology is now open to virtually all industries, primarily because it is easy to access and offers functionality in smart contracts and decentralized apps. Home programmers can also build blockchain-based software without having to create blockchains of their own.

One statement that alludes to the direction blockchain technology is heading in came from Hazard Bynessdrirr in 2017, when he said that the blockchain had the potential to create new social and economic system foundations. This is somewhat reminiscent of when the Internet was new, with its infinite potential that is only just being realized today. Today, venture capitalists, big businesses, startups, programmers, and governments are all working on their own blockchain-based database systems and decentralized applications.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

The key takeaways from this chapter are:

  • The blockchain’s underlying technology is cryptography, which can be dated back many thousands of years when codes were used to obscure messages, stopping enemies from reading them.
  • During the 198s and 1990s, a number of academic papers were published, offering theories on combining cryptography with secure data chains and digital currency creation.

1982 – “Blind Signatures for Untraceable Payments” was written and published by David Chaum, who is credited with inventing blind signatures and digital cash.

1990 – Chaum founded DigiCash, using cryptography, signatures, and public and private keys to create the first untraceable digital currency. In 1998, the company declared bankruptcy and sold its assets to eCash Technologies.

1997 – A proof-of-work algorithm was created by Adam Back. Called hashcash, it was designed to cut down on spam email. The system worked by requiring the email sender to solve a puzzle and embed the answer in the email header. This ensured resources and computing power were required, making spam emails more expensive to send.

1998 – Bit Gold, a decentralized digital currency, was proposed by Nick Szabo, incorporating proof-of-work. A computer network would need to validate the proof-of-work and link it to the next puzzle using a timestamp. However, Bit Gold was only ever a theoretical currency, never a real one.

1998 – In the same year, a paper called “B-Money: An Anonymous Distributed Electronic Cash System” was published by Wei Dai. It discussed the foundations that cryptocurrency should be based on and was referred to in Nakamoto’s white paper on Bitcoin.

  • These papers and other work done between 1980 and 2000 provided the groundwork for the blockchain and Bitcoin. From here, more papers were released.

2008 – Satoshi Nakamoto released his Bitcoin whitepaper, called “Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System.” It outlined Bitcoin as a currency and transaction blocks linked into chains. The word “blockchain” was never used; instead, it was always referred to as “block chain” – two words instead of one.

2009 – Bitcoin became a reality when Nakamoto took his idea and created the network and the first blockchain. Again, the original source code used two words to mention the blockchain.

  • The first blockchain was one of the main features of Bitcoin, designed to solve double-spending and worked as a distributed public ledger for all Bitcoin transactions.
  • Nakamoto was given credit for mining the first Bitcoin network block, called the “genesis block.” He left a message on it, possibly referring to when the block was created and to the state of the currency markets and banking structure at the time.
  • To this day, no one knows who Satoshi Nakamoto is. Some have long suspected that Wei Dai or Nick Szabo were responsible, but both have continually denied it.
  • The Ethereum blockchain was launched in 2015, allowing D’Apps and smart contracts to run on the technology. This blockchain is commonly known as blockchain 2.0.

You should now understand what the blockchain is and how it came about. In the next chapter, we’ll look at its distinct advantages.

“There’s a way to do it better – find it.”

THOMAS A. EDISON

Up to now, we’ve looked at what a blockchain is and how it works. We also looked at a brief history of the blockchain and Bitcoin. Now, after all this, you must still be wondering whether the blockchain has any real advantages in everyday life, and the answer to that is, yes, it does.

This chapter will discuss those benefits in detail.

TRANSPARENCY

This is one of the biggest advantages the blockchain offers over the traditional methods of ledgers and record-keeping. Whenever a change is made in the ledger, all blockchain users can see it, and there can be no alterations to any transaction entered onto the blockchain, nor can they be deleted.

In traditional record-keeping systems, anyone with access could potentially change things on the database and keep those changes hidden from others. We are constantly hearing of massive fraud cases that were not detected until too late because of the lack of transparency in the ledgers. This lack of transparency meant people could change entries or data in secrecy, and the only time it would be detected was when, indeed, if the fraud were detected.

With blockchain technology, every person can see every entry and transaction – complete transparency. Most of the computers on the blockchain network must approve every transaction or change, thus ensuring that nothing can be manipulated or hidden from view.

Every change is made in almost real-time, occurring as each transaction or change is approved and then added onto the blockchain. This ensures that a common scenario of one person in a company being able to skim money or hide major company losses by changing ledger entries is highly unlikely to occur where everything is stored on a blockchain.

Transparency doesn’t just apply to financial transactions; it works with just about anything of value stored on the blockchain. Later, we’ll be looking at how the financial and other industries use the blockchain to provide their customers with transparency.

REMOVING THE THIRD-PARTIES

As we mentioned at the start of this book, most of today’s transactions require a third party or intermediary, such as a credit card company, bank, government, etc., to provide security and trust.

One advantage that blockchain technology provides over traditional systems is the removal of this third party. This way, transactions can take place directly between the interested parties without involving anyone else.

This offers huge benefits to billions of people living in countries where third parties, such as banks, governments, other financial parties, etc., cannot be trusted due to corruption, poor regulation, high levels of crime, limited options in pursuing legal issues, manual transaction recording, and so on. The blockchain is particularly helpful in these scenarios, especially where it is just as difficult to do direct transactions between two or more people face-to-face or via online means.

It all comes back to the transparency mentioned earlier and provides trust while cutting the risks inherent in traditional transactions and removing that intermediary.

DECENTRALIZATION

This advantage is one of the key reasons we can remove the third parties from the equation while increasing trust and transparency. Each blockchain is considered as a single ledger and is maintained as such. This is in complete contrast to the current system of multiple ledgers managed privately by more than one institution. When the blockchain is used, neither companies nor individuals need to relinquish any control to a third party, ensuring that collaboration and transactions are much faster and can easily be managed.

Let’s use an example of banks that transfer their assets between one another. In the systems we currently use, every bank would have their own ledgers and their own transaction records and would be responsible for maintaining them. If they used the blockchain, all they would need to do is ensure their individual transactions were reconciled to the shared ledger, and each bank would be able to access it and agree on the transaction record.

The decentralized nature of the blockchain really pays dividends for companies in competition with one another, but that work as part of a consortium or group. Many companies are not happy to hand over information or work together on databases owned by one of their competitors, understandably so. Where competitors collaborate but only one has ownership of the data, there’s the need for involved legal contracts and NDAs (non-disclosure agreements) to ensure the access and privacy of the data is protected. However, where the blockchain is concerned, competitors can collaborate on shared databases that they all have access to and control over.

As we are all aware, centralized databases can easily be hacked and are prone to corruption and data loss. With the blockchain, that risk is eliminated because there is no central point of failure, no central point that can be manipulated, and data cannot be lost. Every computer on each blockchain network retains a copy of the blockchain, ensuring the risk of data loss is minimal. Data manipulation would require more than 50% of the network’s computers to be hacked at exactly the same time, and that is pretty much impossible to do.

While traditional transaction methods require all parties to place trust in a third party for the transaction to be completed, that is not an issue with the blockchain. The third party is removed, but security and trust between all parties are retained. That’s because the trust goes to the blockchain and not the third party.

While the decentralized nature of the blockchain, its transparency, and no need for a third party are all important features, the most important non-tangible benefit is the significant increase in trust between parties.

SECURITY

When data is entered onto a blockchain, it becomes immutable. This means there is no way to alter or change it in any way. Each block in a blockchain is linked and can be traced right back to the beginning, to the ‘genesis block.’

This creates an audit trail that can easily be followed and includes every transaction that has ever been recorded on the blockchain.

There have been many thousands of cases of fraud documented throughout history. When fraud is committed, the trail that leads to it is usually altered in some way, thus muddying the waters and making it hard to investigate. In some cases, the data trail may have been changed so much that the fraudulent transactions cannot be traced.

There is no way to change any transactions already entered on the blockchain, which means the audit trail is clear and can easily be followed. And, as mentioned earlier, changing any previous transactions would require someone to have control of more than 50% of the network simultaneously, which is nigh on impossible. If this did happen, the rest of the network computers would see it straight away and be able to put a stop to it.

That said, blockchain security is not without its flaws, but most existing blockchain-based systems have been shown to solve most of the issues we face with traditional systems. It is likely that fraud will never be completely eliminated, but the blockchain does let us have an audit trail that can easily be followed, thus allowing the network to see almost instantly if any attempt at fraud is made.

PLENTY OF POTENTIAL USE CASES

The blockchain isn’t just about recording financial transactions. It can be used to record pretty much anything of value. We have used an example of books, but you could use the blockchain to record ownership rights, digital identities, digital files, copyright licenses, or anything else that you can record in a database today.

Let’s take copyright licenses as an example. These are considered assets of value, but they are nothing more than numbers or data added to a database. Their value comes from protecting the ownership of something and the income derived from that ownership.

Copyright licenses are traditionally stored in centralized databases and controlled and managed by certain associations and organizations. However, as assets of value, and because they are not physical assets, they can easily be stored on the blockchain, eliminating the need for third-party organizations to have control over them. You can also store other assets of value on the blockchain, such as digital assets and cryptocurrencies. These can exist purely as blockchain assets, which ensures they can be managed much easier than the current ownership records.

ACCESSIBLE TECHNOLOGY

Blockchain technology is one of the most easily accessible technologies, especially now we have the Ethereum platform, smart contracts, and other recent innovations. These innovations ensure that anyone can develop applications based on blockchain technology.

The blockchain can potentially change just about any industry, and the projects currently being developed indicate the significant impact this technology could have on the real world. Today, many companies are already developing their own blockchain-based systems, and in a later chapter, we will discuss some of their uses and some projects already in the pipeline.

REDUCED COSTS

For now, it’s enough to know that the technology could reap significant savings for many companies simply by removing the third parties needed in transactions, recording assets, and transferring them. Whenever a third party is involved in a transaction, it adds significantly to the costs. In the systems we use today, transferring and recording assets requires multiple databases and ledgers to be maintained by each organization involved in the transaction. With the distributed ledger, all parties can transfer and record assets on one ledger, thus reducing the time and cost involved.

It isn’t cheap to maintain databases and ledgers, and, more often than not, it is a manual process involving many people to keep a check on each ledger’s integrity. With the distributed ledger, all these ledgers are replaced with one, ensuring transaction settlement and auditing is done in real-time by all parties involved.

FASTER TRANSACTIONS

Not only does the blockchain reduce transaction costs, but it also increases the speed. Because there is no third party involved and all transactions are done on one shared ledger, transactions can be completed almost immediately.

Let’s say you want to transfer some money from one bank account to another. While the money gets taken from your account straight away, it may take several days to appear in the other account. The same applies to purchases made using a credit card. While the store owner provides you with the goods or services immediately, they may not receive their payment for several days until the credit card company has completed the transaction.

blockchain systems are currently being developed to speed up these types of transactions, but these are only two of many such examples. Systems are being developed to speed up any transaction type or any transfer of value. Later, we’ll talk about the companies who are developing systems to do this, not just in the financial industry but also in many others.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

The key takeaways from this chapter are:

  • Transparency – the blockchain provides transparency that most traditional database, record-keeping, and ledger systems don’t have.
  • No Third Parties – third parties are removed from the equation, removing the need to trust someone else in a transaction or transfer. Instead, that trust goes to the blockchain.
  • Decentralized Nature – blockchain systems run on a network of computers that is decentralized, ensuring a significant reduction in downtime, hacking, and data loss.
  • Trust – blockchain-based systems ensure trust between parties in a transfer or transaction is increased via transparency, decentralization, and removing the third parties. Trust is the largest nontangible benefit of the blockchain.
  • Security – transactions and data become immutable once recorded on the blockchain. This prevents data manipulation and fraud because all transactions can be clearly audited to the first block on the chain, the “genesis” block.
  • Plenty of Potential Use Cases – the blockchain is not purely for financial transactions. Pretty much anything of value can be stored on the blockchain, and lots of companies are already developing their own systems.
  • The Technology Is Easily Accessible – besides having many use cases, the technology ensures people can easily create applications to work on the blockchain without needing to sink a ton of time and money into infrastructure. This is because of recent innovations, such as the Ethereum network and smart contracts.
  • Lower Costs – because there are no third parties, the cost of transactions on the blockchain is significantly lower than on traditional systems. One traditional transaction may require multiple databases and ledgers, but it would require a single shared ledger on the blockchain. This reduces validation, confirmation, and auditing costs.
  • Faster Transaction Speeds – again, because there are no third parties and no requirement for multiple ledgers, transactions can be carried out much quicker than traditional systems.

“The first rule of any technology used in a business is that automation applied to an efficient operation will magnify the efficiency. The second is that automation applied to an inefficient operation will magnify the inefficiency.”

BILL GATES

Is blockchain technology really that good? Can it save the world from itself?

In many circles, the blockchain is the only technology that can solve every problem in every industry and the whole world. That might sound like a dramatic statement, but some do believe this, and, every day, new cryptocurrencies and blockchain startups are launching, with promises to disrupt the financial sector and much more, right up to eliminating poverty across the world.

Many of these claims are similar to those we heard about the internet when it was first developed. Yes, the internet has changed the world in many ways, but many of the claims made were exaggerated, with unrealistic timeframes, and many of the startups people thought would see success soon filed for bankruptcy.

This chapter will delve into the disadvantages of blockchain technology and some of the issues surrounding it.

NO PRIVACY

One of the biggest issues surrounding a decentralized blockchain is the lack of privacy, making complete acceptance of the technology hard. This lack of privacy surrounding the data is bad enough, but perhaps worse is that this can be accessed instantly by any person on the blockchain network.

Take the Bitcoin blockchain, for example. Once payment has been received, it isn’t too difficult to work out the identity of the account it came from. Let’s say you go to a shop and make a payment using the blockchain. The store owner can see that transaction, and the information would detail which wallet the payment came from. The store owner could check that account and see what money is in the account and what transactions have been made in and out of the wallet.

One thing that many people find quite worrying is the fact that whenever a transaction is made to a public network, the decentralized blockchain publishes it. This is even more worrying where in-store purchases are made, and the person’s identity and their account can be linked.

In terms of privacy, or the lack thereof, some find it concerning that many of the computers running the largest blockchain networks are based in China and Russia. Both countries are well-known for their computer crime and hacking, and there is a very real fear that personal and private information may be used against those who live in these countries or travel there.

Some decentralized blockchains do provide extra privacy for transactions and may even restrict who can access the information. However, many of the largest cryptocurrency blockchains, such as Bitcoin and Ethereum, don’t do this and don’t plan to change how they work. In other words, they do not intend to add any more privacy around accounts or transactions.

SECURITY ISSUES

Any asset stored on the blockchain is akin to cold, hard cash. If you keep cash in your wallet and the wallet is stolen, or the money taken from it, it’s gone for good. Advanced cryptography is used in most blockchain-based systems, along with encryption that is far more secure than what we use on the internet today. However, sometimes less is more, and that is true in this case – too much security can lead to more problems.

Scour the internet, and you will find tons of examples, especially with cryptocurrencies, where a private key has been lost or forgotten, and the person cannot get their money. Your private key is just that – private. Share it with someone else, and you risk them taking all your money. Lose or forget it, and there is no way to retrieve it – you are locked out and cannot get your money. In either case, there is nothing to be done. There is no password reset system and no way of getting back currency or money that someone else has stolen from you.

This often happens more when a person purchases a cryptocurrency at a low price and then forgets all about it. Later, they find it has shot up in price and that what they might only have paid a few dollars more is now worth many times more than that. However, they can’t remember their password when trying to get to their currency to sell it. If you purchased Bitcoin for $100 in 2009 and left it alone, it would now be worth in the region of $4 million. But, because you cannot remember your password, those Bitcoins and that money is gone forever. One case that really stands out occurred in the UK in 2013 when James Howell mistakenly threw out a laptop containing the private key to access 7500 Bitcoins. Today, they would have been worth around $66 million, but, despite extensive searches, he has been unable to retrieve the laptop.

The blockchain’s transparency means that a public key allows people to see their balance and see how much it is worth, but it doesn’t give you access to it. This is much the same as your bank telling you your bank balance but not letting you have the money. When you lose your internet banking password or forget your account numbers, you can go into a physical bank and provide identification to get access again. This doesn’t happen with cryptocurrencies based on the blockchain. A lack of security, scams, and hacking have resulted in the theft or loss of billions of dollars’ worth of cryptocurrencies over the years, and nothing can be done about it.

If someone stole your credit card and PIN and withdrew money from your account, the bank could intervene and cancel your card. That way, no more money could be fraudulently withdrawn. It’s also likely that the bank has fraud protection in place; not only can they trace where your card is being used and find the perpetrator, but they can reverse any withdrawals made, so you don’t lose any money.

Now let’s say your transactions are done over a blockchain-based system. These cannot be changed or reversed, and if fraud does occur, you don’t have that third party to intervene. If you accidentally sent funds or cryptocurrency to the wrong blockchain wallet, that’s too bad. Your funds are gone, the original recipient is out of funds, and it’s someone else’s gain. If someone gets hold of your private key, they can take all your money – you can’t reverse it, and you can’t claim compensation like you could with a bank or credit card company.

Go on any blockchain forum or the FAQ section of any blockchain-based system, and one of the most common questions asked is, “If I lose or forget my password, how do I reset it?” Simply put, you cannot. Perhaps the worst advice of all is to write your private key down when you set it up. With all the encryption and security the blockchain is meant to have, people are reduced to doing the one thing everyone tells them not to – write their password down or store it on their computer. That results in less security than a traditional method.

As you can see, what should make blockchain-based systems more secure in mainstream implementation is likely to make adoption less likely. One of the most popular types of blockchain wallets is the web-based type, where cryptocurrencies are stored with third parties. However, this means they lose the benefits of the blockchain’s security, preferring to set up a traditional password system instead, knowing it can be reset if they lose it. However, that means they are also back to relying on an intermediary, the one thing the blockchain was designed to remove.

LACK OF CENTRALIZED CONTROL

Robert Sams, the founder, and chief executive of Clearmatics in London, once said,

“In financial markets, there is always a mechanism to correct an attack. In a blockchain, there isn’t – you have to accept it.”

ROBERT SAMS

Earlier, we learned that systems based on the blockchain remove third parties from the equation, ensuring control and responsibility go back to the parties in the transactions. The control goes to the majority share of the computers and the people who operate them on the blockchain network, resulting in control issues.

Because the blockchain is decentralized, the majority of the network must agree on transactions, and they decide the direction the blockchain goes in. In traditional software and networks, where the organization wants something changed, they get approval from certain departments before doing it. In a decentralized blockchain, a set majority of the network must agree to the changes, usually more than 50% but sometimes 70-80%.

An example of this was seen with the divisions caused in the Bitcoin network regarding the implementation of SegWit (Segregated Witness) or Bitcoin Unlimited. Large numbers of the network members support different Bitcoin network changes and, so far, the majority needed by Bitcoin Unlimited or SegWit has been unattainable.

This has led to other blockchains overtaking Bitcoin in technological changes, and the network disagreement has led to stagnation on the Bitcoin network. Transaction times are much slower, as are confirmation times, and the network suffers from scalability problems.

Software and other technologies change constantly, but this can cause problems with decentralized blockchain networks. A lack of a majority vote can stop changes from happening, but even if a majority were reached and the changes went ahead, the network would still have plenty of people on it who were not happy with them.

This has led to many organizations seeing the blockchain as a risk they are unwilling to take. Companies can end up building entire businesses and software solutions around a network only to find they cannot control changes that could dramatically impact how they work.

THE 51% ATTACK

As a continuation of the control issue, if someone had control of more than 50% of all the computers on a network, they would have complete control of every blockchain transaction. When a malicious user gains that control, it is called a 51% attack. By leveraging this over, say, a cryptocurrency network, in theory, they could block any new transactions from being confirmed, reverse existing transactions, and make way for double-spending, one of the issues the blockchain is designed to prevent.

51% attacks are largely theoretical because gaining that much control over a network would be next to impossible. That said, in Russia, China, and other parts of the world, you can find massive mining farms controlling huge amounts of a blockchain network’s computing power. If these farms were to get together, they have a massive potential to take over entire networks and manipulate them how they want to.

Even without a 51% control, they still have the potential to control and manipulate networks. They can do this by ensuring computing power is allocated in such a way that the network’s future development comes under undue influence. We have already seen this with the division in the Bitcoin network mentioned earlier.

UNPROVEN TECHNOLOGY

Blockchains and systems based on them are an as yet unproven technology, mostly used for cryptocurrencies. Right now, there are few real-world applications, and those in existence haven’t been used enough to prove how effective the technology really is.

It is a new technology, and, yes, it does have plenty of potential, but most of that potential is theoretical right now. There’s an old saying

“Build a better mousetrap, and the world will beat a path to your door.”

This is a common error in business reasoning – yes, the technology may triumph over some existing systems, but it doesn’t mean everyone will choose to use it.

Another blockchain benefit, the removal of third parties, can also have a negative effect because many people aren’t prepared to take the risk of connecting to this otherwise unproven technology and sending money. They also find it difficult to set up private keys, and keeping them safe is a whole other matter. Because of that, some people prefer to use wallets or software where their private keys are given to third parties, thus eliminating that blockchain benefit.

COST

Most blockchain networks use a proof-of-work algorithm, which proves that resources and computing power were contributed to the network before a transaction block could be added to the blockchain. This proof is provided as the solution to a puzzle linked to the block; the network has to solve this puzzle to confirm the block is correct and doing so takes an awful lot of electricity and computing power.

According to calculations made by Professor J Quiggin from the University of Queensland, the Bitcoin network uses the same amount of electricity in 30 minutes as an average household in the USA does in one year. That household probably uses an average of 10,000 to 12,000 kWh of power per year, and that same amount is required to generate just four Bitcoins.

Because the electricity cost for running the computers using the proof-of-work algorithm on the blockchain network is so high, the only real advantages come for countries with cheaper electricity or where organizations can strike good deals with their energy providers.

These puzzles will become more difficult as time goes on, which relates to an increase in electricity consumption. In turn, that equates to higher costs, and it will become so resource-intensive that it will be difficult to run a blockchain using the proof-of-work algorithm on large scales.

NO SCALABILITY

The previous point about costs leads to scalability issues. For companies like MasterCard and Visa, both of whom process large numbers of transactions daily, the current energy consumption will make it too expensive to use the blockchain. This has a serious effect on blockchain scalability.

One Bitcoin block is added to the blockchain every ten minutes. Each block contains in the region of 2,000 transactions, which means the network processes approximately three transactions per second.

Because there are limits on the size of each block, the Bitcoin network only has the capacity to handle around seven transactions per second at the most. In tests conducted between Visa and IBM, it was determined that the Visa network could handle in excess of 20,000 transactions per second. That in itself ensures that the blockchain is not really suitable in its current format for massive companies like Visa.

Another issue revolves around a lack of funds. If you don’t have enough money on your credit card to pay for something, the transaction will be rejected immediately. On the Bitcoin blockchain, that won’t happen because there is nothing in place to make it happen.

Bear in mind that transaction blocks take around ten minutes to be added. Some companies may not accept a transaction until a few more blocks have been added, thus ensuring that the transaction cannot be reversed.

So let’s compare these methods. If you purchased goods in a store and paid with a credit card, cash, or debit card, the store owner gets their money quickly. If you paid using Bitcoin, the store owner might need to wait until more blocks have been added to confirm the transaction before they get paid. You can see why some people might not be too keen on taking a risk on blockchain technology, at least right now.

Some blockchain networks are quicker than the Bitcoin blockchain, but none are as popular, and none are as acceptable as a payment form as Bitcoin is. Even those cryptocurrencies and blockchains with faster transaction times cannot scale to meet the demands of MasterCard, Visa, and other major financial networks.

These scalability issues mean that mainstream, large-scale blockchain implementation is seen as nothing more than a time-stamped ledger of information.

UNDERSTANDING, REPUTATION, AND TRUST

At the moment, there is still a lot to learn about blockchain technology, as too few people truly understand how it all works. Add to that the bad rep the technology picked up with its Bitcoin connection and Silk Road, and it’s easy to see why people don’t really trust it yet.

We know that Bitcoin is the most popular and best-known use of the blockchain, but too many people associate it with criminal activities. While it is slowly becoming accepted as a legitimate method of making payments, computer crime and terrorist activities linked to the cryptocurrency are constantly being dragged back into the news, forever keeping that link alive.

One example is when a computer virus locked the National Health Service (NHS) computers in the UK. Nobody could access those computers unless a ransom demand in Bitcoin were met. This pushed Bitcoin firmly into the UK news headlines and not in a good way; it resulted in the cryptocurrency being irrevocably linked with hackers, anonymous computer crime, and terrorist activities. Hospitals were locked out of patient records, potentially causing a threat to those needing urgent medical care.

Blockchain technology comes with a claim of creating trust between transactional parties without the need for a third party. However, we still need to be able to trust the blockchain network, which is difficult when we know that a system of anonymous computers runs it. How do you get people to place their trust in a system knowingly used by criminals? How do we trust a system when we know that many of the computers on the network are located in foreign countries where there is a lack of regulation? Or where governments control the citizens to the extent where there is no trust?

Criminal incidents linked to Bitcoin are just one reason organizations currently developing their own blockchain systems try to maintain a significant distance from the Bitcoin connection. More and more, we are hearing the term “distributed ledger” these days, further expanding the gap between Bitcoin and new technologies and systems based on the blockchain.

Many people simply don’t understand the benefits of blockchain technology. We know that some use third parties to store their private keys and standard passwords to log into websites, thus skirting around the technology’s main benefits. Most people don’t want others to see their transactions or balances or any other aspects of an open system and prefer to stick to existing ones.

If the technology is to be accepted in the mainstream, people need to understand and trust it, and their perception of it being connected to crime must change. This is likely to take a long time and puts a metaphorical spanner in the works of blockchain technology going mainstream anytime soon.

REGULATION AND INTEGRATION

Assets based on the blockchain are currently facing long processes in regards to integrating them with existing systems and regulating them. Banks, financial institutions, governments, even legal organizations are somewhat resistant to making changes, mostly because of the enormous cost and sheer scale of replacing their current systems.

It’s down to blockchain-based systems to prove that switching to them can provide long-term savings and benefits before organizations agree to change their systems. Unless they can do that, governments and banks are unlikely to use the blockchain in the near future.

The Estonian government is currently running tests on blockchain systems, but they only have a population of just over 1.3 million. China, the US, and other countries have individual cities with more people than this. While the blockchain may work on smaller scales, integrating them on a larger scale for international banks or governments of much larger countries won’t be easy.

Two examples of distributed or blockchain-based ledgers currently being integrated with global financial companies are Ripple and the R3 Consortium. On the other hand, some financial institutions are waiting, holding back on blockchain integration because so far, the blockchain has only been tested on a small scale, not enough for them to see the real benefits in changing their current systems.

If lots of the larger, trusted financial institutions moved their operations to untested technology and issues come to light, it could prove risky to their customers and the financial markets.

The FSOC (Financial Stability Oversight Counsel) has also raised concerns that some systems could be more open to fraud, something we can’t fully understand on the small-scale testing done so far.

Another issue raised is regulation, especially where many financial organizations work on a shared blockchain system or distributed ledger. In theory, a single system based on the blockchain can cover multiple regulatory jurisdictions and boundaries, thus blurring the lines between the regulators and where a transaction should be handled.

Major financial companies are likely to be wary about using systems with little to no government or central regulation. In terms of business and finances, the risks are too high should there be no clear regulation about the handling of blockchain-based assets. Uptake in blockchain technology by governments and large financial companies is likely to be slow for three primary reasons – integration cost, regulatory concerns, and the fact that tests have thus far been on a small scale only.

HYPE

It would be easy to say that much of what we read about blockchain technology could be considered hype. We constantly read of claims that the technology will disrupt governments and every other major industry in the world. We read that it will end world poverty, get rid of the banks and credit card companies, and change the world in so many other ways.

Getting caught up in hype is easy, especially when it surrounds technology with the serious potential to change the world as we know it. The internet was the same, a technology slated to revolutionize the world, and, to a certain extent, it has done just that. However, many of the predictions about the internet were never realized, and the same is set to be true of blockchain technology.

More often than not, timeframes surrounding how new technology will have an impact are significantly underestimated. We know that digital cash existed long before Bitcoin, and technologies based on cryptography have been around for decades. Their predictions about marker adoption were seriously underestimated.

Even if there are some accurate predictions regarding the use of blockchain technology, it will be years before their mainstream societal impact becomes known. Startup companies responsible for pioneering technology may not make it far enough to see their dreams come to fruition.

Because many people who use blockchain technology and Bitcoin prefer to use traditional methods, such as traditional passwords and third parties, the need for the blockchain is effectively eliminated. After all, the blockchain is designed to remove passwords and third parties, and if people still want to use them, there is little point to the blockchain.

At the end of the day, many people see the blockchain as nothing more than a way of storing data and managing it. It can’t change the world, and it certainly cannot change world problems, so there is no point in getting caught up in all the hype surrounding it.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

The key takeaways from this chapter are:

  • No Privacy – not all decentralized blockchains are private, and anyone on a network can see every transaction and account balance.
  • Security Issues – blockchain assets are considered similar to cash – if your cash is stolen from your wallet, it’s gone, and if your assets are stolen from the blockchain, they too are gone. Many people are choosing to write their private keys down on paper, so they don’t forget what they are, which goes against the secure nature of the blockchain. If your private key is stolen, there’s nothing you can do about it, and some of the security methods used on the blockchain are likely to make wide-scale adoption more difficult.
  • Lack of Centralized Control – on decentralized systems, transactions and changes must be agreed upon by more than 50% of the network. Nobody has control over any changes, which makes them a risk to organizations as they will lose control over changes to their systems.
  • 51% Attack – many of the computers on major blockchain networks are based in countries where there is historic evidence of widespread crime, no regulation, no trust in governments, and lax legal systems. In some of these countries, computer costs and electricity are cheap, enabling massive mining farms to be set up. Should these choose to collaborate, the networks could be hit with a 51% attack, where blockchain control is lost, allowing double-spending, reversed transactions, blocked transactions, and so on.
  • Unproven Technology – blockchain technology is relatively new and hasn’t yet been proven. So far, its main use has been linked to cryptocurrencies, and testing has been too small-scale for most organizations to determine if it will work on a larger scale.
  • Cost – blockchain networks require vast amounts of energy, which is costly in terms of electricity. The estimated cost of the blockchain is that, in just 30 minutes, it uses the same amount of power that a standard family in the USA uses in 12 months. This calculation is based on a yearly consumption of approximately 10-12,0000 kWh per year, roughly the same amount needed to mine four Bitcoin blocks.
  • Scalability – because testing has only been small-scale, there is no proof that they can effectively work on a larger scale, especially compared to existing systems. For example, the Visa network can handle more than 20,0000 transactions per second, while Bitcoin can only handle seven.
  • Trust and Reputation – blockchain technology is commonly associated with Bitcoin, which, in turn, is connected to criminal activities and terrorism. We need to have more trust in a system we are using, especially if that system takes the place of a trusted system, and many people will not trust networks with a criminal association.
  • Lack of Understanding – not many people truly understand how the blockchain works and cannot understand the benefits associated with the technology. There are also valid concerns about transactions and balances being so open to others, leaving most people preferring to trust in existing systems.
  • Integration and Regulation – in terms of integrating existing systems with the blockchain, there are considerable costs in financial and time terms and regulatory issues to face. Most banks and governments resist change because of scaling issues and the cost of moving to a blockchain-based system.
  • Hype – blockchain technology is subject to a significant amount of hype, especially in terms of what it can do. We need to remember that a blockchain is nothing more than a way of storing and managing assets; it isn’t a magic wand that will solve all the world’s problems and is largely unproven, especially outside of cryptocurrency use.

As you can see, the blockchain isn’t all roses and sparkly lights. Despite its many benefits, it has significant disadvantages, especially in terms of large organizational use. The next chapter will look at how the blockchain works with the financial industry.

OTHER FINANCIAL INDUSTRY BENEFITS

The finance industry will benefit from blockchain-based technology in many ways because they know that currency can be transformed securely and reliably. There are two main benefits to the blockchain:

  2. Immutability – blockchains provide accurate transactional histories, with all transactions in chronological order. No transaction or block can be changed once added to the blockchain, nor can unverified information be added. The ability to do this would require a highly coordinated hacking attack on more than half the network’s computers simultaneously, which is unlikely ever to happen.

These features offer tons of benefits for the financial industry, and we’ve already seen how the industry is working on blockchain technology and using it to streamline its own business. Now we can look at some of the obvious ways the industry can harness the blockchain’s power:

MONEY TRANSFERS

Consumers can also use the blockchain to transfer money electronically using mobile devices, saving them the hassle of visiting the bank or a money transfer business and paying for the privilege of sending their money to someone else.

CHEAPER DIRECT PAYMENTS

  • Lower Fees – If customers use credit cards to pay for something, the merchant has to pay a processing fee, reducing their profit. With blockchain payments, fees are eliminated or at least reduced.
  • Less Chance of Insufficient Funds – some consumers pay for goods with a bad cheque, leading to the merchants losing money and paying fees. They may also have to go down a legal route to get their money back. With the blockchain, merchants have the peace of mind that transactions will go through in a very short time.

For the individual, the benefits are:

  • Smaller Chance of Scams – online scams are a huge concern, but payments are much quicker and less expensive than traditional banking services with the blockchain. Also, if fraud is committed, the payment can quickly be reversed.
  • Less Money and Time – wire transfers, cash, and cashier’s checks are safe payment methods, but wire transfers take too much time, cash cannot be traced, and cashier’s cheques are easily forged. The blockchain removes all these issues, giving individuals more confidence in their payments.

TRANSACTION DETAILS CAN BE TRACKED

The blockchain won’t just revolutionize money transfers. It is also an excellent way to track every transaction and ensure that the information is secure and accurate. This includes:

  • Smart Contracts – most transactions are time-consuming, complex, and expensive, but with the blockchain comes an automation opportunity. With smart contracts, buyer payments can be tracked, as can deliveries, and any issues that arise throughout the transaction can easily be addressed. The result is less human error, and they work 24/7.

A REDUCTION IN FRAUD

All information stored on a blockchain is stored on a ledger in blocks. Those blocks contain a unique hash referring to the block before them. All people on a blockchain network are sent a copy of every transaction. All this means that the technology is less likely to become a target of DOS (Denial-of-Service) attacks, malicious hackers, and any other type of fraud you can think of. By eliminating these threats, the expenses associated with business are reduced, ensuring everyone saves time and money.

DIGITAL IDENTITIES AND IDENTITY MANAGEMENT

Using the blockchain for identity management purposes is an important innovation that can potentially open the way for other industries to benefit from security. When you know you can trust a person is who they say they are, identity and trust can be connected to multiple applications.

Blockchain technology can solve multiple existing problems related to digital identities. When the blockchain is used to build identification systems, cryptography is used to provide digital signatures. These signatures are unique to the person, completely secure, irrefutable, and virtually impossible to access or duplicate without the right authorization.

Digital identities on blockchain-based solutions are a very real possibility, and the Estonian government is already developing its own solution, along with ShoCard and other companies. HEALTHCARE AND MEDICAL RECORDS

Blockchains work on distributed ledgers where every copy of the ledger is updated whenever a change is made on one. This way, all network members have an up-to-date ledger at all times.

This offers huge potential in the healthcare industry. Most of us have visited more than one hospital or doctor. Whenever we visit a new one, we have to complete tons of paperwork regarding our medical histories, any allergies, and all manner of other medical-based questions you’ve had to answer in multiple other locations.

If all this data could be stored on a shared database, every doctor, surgeon, hospital, Medicare facility, health professional, and nurse would be able to access our details whenever we attended a new place. This would save a lot of time and would make sure they could make better decisions for a patient’s healthcare.

In cases where someone is rushed into surgery, this system could potentially be a life-saver. With all the patient’s records to hand, doctors and surgeons would know straight away about any underlying health issues, allergies, blood type, medication they are currently on, emergency contacts, etc.

A patient’s previous medical history could also help doctors determine what might be causing a current illness. You may visit the doctor for something that wouldn’t sound alarm bells, and you visit again or go to another doctor or hospital with a different complaint that appears unrelated. If all this were on a shared ledger, it could tell the doctor that an undiagnosed problem may be causing the complaints. While each doctor only has one part of the puzzle under traditional systems, they can see it all with the blockchain, allowing for a much more accurate diagnosis.

Health insurance companies could benefit from time and money savings by accessing this ledger. If you apply for insurance, you need to answer many questions and perhaps undergo invasive medical tests. With access to your records on the blockchain, an insurer can see your complete medical history and make a decision based on whether to accept your application and what your premiums might be.

Several companies are already working on blockchain solutions for storing healthcare records, including Phillips Healthcare, Tieroim, and Gem, and once again, Estonia is at the forefront. Using the blockchain to store medical records doesn’t just benefit medical professionals; it can also benefit the individual. They will get a transparent view of their records, knowing that no one can change the information without them and the entire network knowing about it.

Estonia has developed a patient portal, allowing its citizens to access their medical history, referrals records, prescriptions, and health insurance information. They can also declare whether they are organ donors and make their own decisions about treatment during surgery.

THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN ETHEREUM AND BITCOIN

Although Bitcoin and Ethereum are both cryptocurrencies, they do have some differences. The biggest is that the Bitcoin blockchain is primarily a distributed ledger used for financial transactions. By contrast, Ethereum is a distributed computing platform designed to run decentralized apps.

We can use Bitcoin to buy goods and services wherever they are accepted as a payment form, but We cannot use Ether to do the same. Ether is purely for use as a payment form by developers who need computing power to tune their decentralized applications.

Both have their own digital currency, but they have different purposes. Ether was never designed as an alternative payment; it was only ever meant to encourage developers to create decentralized applications and run them on the Ethereum network.

In simple terms, Bitcoin is a financial transaction currency, and Ethereum is multi-faceted. It doesn’t just have a cryptocurrency; it also uses the blockchain to run a computing platform.

THE BENEFITS OF ETHEREUM

Like the Bitcoin blockchain, the Ethereum blockchain is run by a network of computers, thousands of them spread across the world. That allows applications to run on the network, using the computing power from all these computers.

The biggest issue with the Bitcoin network is that it has far more power than all the supercomputers in the world, but all that power goes to waste because it is only used to generate random numbers and add blocks.

Ethereum makes far better use of the computers on their massive network by allowing developers to run their apps on the blockchain. There is no need for developers to create their own blockchains; they simply use Ether to pay for some of the computing power already available on the network.

But it doesn’t stop there. Ethereum also has a programming language called Solidity and the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM). Solidity is used to create smart contracts or decentralized applications, which the EVM then compiles and runs on the blockchain.

DECENTRALIZED APPLICATIONS – D’APPS

Decentralized applications, often termed d’Apps, are open-source applications that no one entity or person controls. They run on a network of computers or distributed blockchain. These applications don’t have a central server. Instead, peer-to-peer connections are used to connect users to one another.

With the applications we use today, a single entity is in control, and a centralized server is used to run them. These servers are prone to being hacked, breaking down, or going offline, causing no end of problems for the application user.

Decentralized apps don’t rely on any of this, instead using the computer network, and all users on the network decide the changes. Because there isn’t a centralized server, it can’t break down or be hacked. If one computer on the network goes down, the application is not affected because there are so many others on the network that are running the application simultaneously. If a computer on the network is hacked, it can’t be used to make unauthorized changes because the vast majority of the network users have to agree to any changes.

SMART CONTRACTS

A smart contract is written in the Solidity computer programming language and works on a distributed ledger or blockchain. The contract is automatically verified, executed, and enforced based on the contract terms specified in the code. Smart contracts can be fully or partially executing and are self-enforcing.

They can be used for exchanges of anything of value and, for many industries, when they adopt blockchain technology, they will be using the smart contract system.

When they run on the blockchain, smart contracts automatically operate. Provided the contract terms in the code are met, the value or payments are exchanged as specified. In the same way, if the conditions are not met, the payment or exchange can be withheld, depending on how the contract was written.

Because smart contracts run exactly as they are programmed, the risks surrounding fraud, unauthorized changes, server failure, or non-compliance with the contract terms are removed. And because the contracts automatically execute, there is no need for a third party, such as a lawyer or a court of law.

An entry made on a blockchain is timestamped, and it cannot be changed. That’s part of what makes this the ideal contracts platform, as all changes are timestamped, and previous versions stay on the blockchain.

We can store contracts and create new versions while retaining previous copies. Each copy has its own timestamp, thus providing us with an accurate overview of how the process took place and ensuring honesty between transaction parties because there is no way to change the ledger. Blockchain technology eliminates the need to have the contracts managed by a third party.

Smart Contract Uses

The sender could decide not to send what you ordered or claim the payment didn’t arrive. Because a third party is not required for transactions on the Bitcoin blockchain, there is no way to dispute a transaction, ask for your money back, or use an intermediary to get your money back for you. When you sign up for a Bitcoin wallet, know that they too are anonymous, so you cannot know who the payment goes to. If you do send it to the wrong Bitcoin address, it’s tough luck, the money is gone, and there is nothing you can do about it.

Smart contracts solve many of the problems associated with doing transactions on a blockchain. You can use them for anything of value, and many companies are already developing decentralized applications to use these smart contracts. The situation above, where you risk not receiving the goods you paid for, cannot happen with a smart contract. When the contract is set up, the payee sends the correct payment, which is then placed into a kind of escrow account and held until the goods are sent and received. That way, the payee knows they are getting what they paid for, and the sender knows their money is safe.

HOW THE BLOCKCHAIN WORKS

We will look at the Bitcoin blockchain because it was the first one, and every other blockchain is based on it. As such, they all work in much the same way.

The algorithm used on the Bitcoin blockchain is SHA-256. This generates a 25-bit hash, unique and fixed in size. But, what is a hash? It’s much like a secret code, using encryption to hide the data, preventing it from being decrypted without the correct authorization.

The generated hash is always fixed in length, and each one is the same length. It doesn’t matter whether a single word or an entire library is entered as data; the hash will always be the same length. If a single letter is changed, the hash changes entirely.

At first glance, a hash would appear to be completely random, unconnected to the data. Unless you have a private key or know the original message, there is no way to work the message out from the hash.

The word “blockchain” hashed, with a lower case b:

The word “block chain” hashed, with the word s” block” and “chain” separated by a space:

The Shakespeare play “Romeo and Juliet,” in its entirety, with more than 20,0000 words:

As you can see in these examples, it isn’t possible to determine the input data by looking at the generated hash. You can also see that making a minor change, such as adding a space or changing an upper case letter to lower case, results in a significantly different generated hash.

BLOCKCHAIN CONFIRMATIONS

Every 10 minutes, a new block gets added to Bitcoin’s blockchain, but other blockchains are much faster at adding blocks than this.

We already know that changing a transaction results in every block needing to be mined again with new hashes, but this has to be done faster than blocks are added to the rest of the network. While this could possibly be done for some of the recent blocks, it is accepted that block transactions cannot be changed once six blocks have been added on top of the block containing the transaction to be altered.

When a block is added on top of a block, it confirms the validity of the previous block, basically a kind of guarantee that the previous block’s transactions will not change. If six blocks were added, that is six confirmations of validity and guarantee, providing businesses with sufficient confidence that previous transactions won’t be changed in any way.

When the number has been found, it is added to the transaction data by all other computers on the network and validated as correct. Because it is a random number, it is not easy to find but, once found, verifying it is simple.

Once validated, the block is added to the chain, and all the network computers update their own systems with the newest blockchain version that includes the new block.

The valid block is added to the blockchain, and then all the computers on the network update their system with the latest version of the blockchain, including the new block.

CAN NETWORK DIFFICULTY BE INCREASED?

Finding the right number to generate a valid hash is nothing more than a game of chance, like rolling a dice. One of the biggest factors is processing speed. The faster a computer guesses the right number, the faster the correct answer is found.

We know that the Bitcoin blockchain adds a block every 10 minutes. The more computers there are on the network, the more processing power the network has. That means more guesses are being made at the correct number for the block. To keep the block addition time at 10 minutes, a change is made to the difficulty after every 2016 blocks, thus changing the minimum and maximum numbers needed for a block to be validated.

Using our dice example, if the network difficulty is 3, a block can only be validated if a number lower than 3 is rolled. If only one person is rolling the dice, there is a 2 in 6 chance of rolling a 1 or 2 and validating the block to add it to the blockchain. This could take around 10 minutes, for example, which means the target of adding a block every 10 minutes is met. However, if a second person is added and they are also rolling the dice, it may only take 5 minutes to roll a number lower than 3. Thus, the time taken to add the block is also only 5 minutes, half the time set by the network.

When the number of people on the network increases, an adjustment needs to be made to maintain the network target of adding a block every 10 minutes. As such, the number would be reduced from 3 to 2, ensuring the block can only be added if a number below 2 is rolled.

With our original example of just one person on the network trying to roll below a 3, there was a 2 in 6 chance of rolling a valid number. If we double the number of people on the network and reduce the network target, that reduces to a 1 in 6 chance. This adjusts the time to add a block back to the target of 10 minutes.

The Bitcoin blockchain works in much the same way. The more computers get added to the network, the more adjustment is made to the target. That means fewer valid hashes are accepted, with more numbers in the range to be guessed to get a valid hash.

PROOF-OF-WORK ISSUES

The method by which the right number is calculated to validate a hash is called “proof-of-work.” It is termed as such because it indicates the resources and computing power contributed in adding a validated block.

The reward the “winning” miner receives is to compensate for their resources, computing power, and electricity and is in the form of a payment for every block successfully added. This is called the block reward, and the successful miner also receives the transaction fees attached to each block they add – this is why they go for transactions that have higher fees.

Proof-of-work takes an awful lot of electricity and computing power. We know that the Bitcoin network is more than 10,000 times as powerful as the top 500 supercomputers in the world combined. However, much of that power is wasted in generating random numbers.

The primary issue with proof-of-work is the waste of resources used in doing something that seems pointless and isn’t really needed to run the blockchain network.

Just stop and think about that for a minute. One network of computers is far more powerful than the combined power of the most powerful supercomputers. But instead of trying to provide solutions to major world problems, all it is doing is generating some random numbers. Doesn’t that seem a tad ridiculous? This is why many people criticize the proof-of-work method that the Bitcoin blockchain uses.

Other methods can be used in place of proof-of-work. They include proof-of-activity, proof-of-capacity, and proof-of-burn. You should understand that other blockchains use different methods, although we won’t be covering them in detail here.

Ethereum uses its network for decentralized apps, which is a far better use for computing power. The platform is also moving towards using the proof-of-stake method rather than proof-of-work.

BLOCKCHAIN NETWORK SECURITY

Decentralized blockchains all have many security features, but one of the biggest is the access that all network members have and that every transaction is updated on every network member’s computer. This goes towards ensuring that there isn’t a centralized database and that no single person or organization can manipulate the blockchain to commit fraud.

Blocks can be added to the blockchain by anyone, but each block must be accepted as valid by the majority of users on the network. Once a block has been validated and added to the blockchain, every copy of the blockchain on the network is updated with the new version, and then a new block is added.

As you learned earlier, when someone tries to change a transaction, a new hash must be generated, which means it won’t match the other network copies and won’t be validated.

51% ATTACK AND BLOCKCHAIN FORKS

We mentioned the 51% attack earlier in this book. Theoretically, a 51% attack cannot happen because it would require a user to have control of more than 50% of the network. If someone did have that control, they could decide which blocks and transactions were rejected or added, and every copy of the blockchain on the network is updated with falsified blockchain.

Forks are where a large number of the network’s users don’t agree with network changes, such as a change in the network functionality or with a block or transaction that was added. This kind of disagreement will result in a fork in the blockchain. Some users will split away from the main network and use their computing power and resources on another separate blockchain from the original one.

There have been several major forks in blockchains, including Ethereum. The original Ethereum blockchain split after a disagreement, resulting in some users allocating their resources to a different blockchain, thus creating two blockchains – Ether and Ether Classic.
