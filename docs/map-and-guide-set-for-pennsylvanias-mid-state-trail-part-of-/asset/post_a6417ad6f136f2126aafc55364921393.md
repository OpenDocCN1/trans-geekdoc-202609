If you are navigating using a Global Positioning System (GPS) device, you can probably set it to read your position directly in UTM. If you do this sure the units of length are not any other units, and that the datum is NAD 1983 not, say, NAD 1927.

If you are navigating using a compass after the batteries in your GPS unit go dead, you should have prepared first by finding out your magnetic declination relative to the UTM grid moves every year and no magnetic declination is shown on the MSTA maps. Remember the MSTA map north is not true but UTM grid north – hence you need to add the local grid and the magnetic declination to find the declination you will use for your hike. Topographic contour lines at 50 or 25 meter intervals relate to mean sea level are shown on the maps. Reading contour line topographic maps is an art that can be easily learned from numerous sources. When you compare MSTA maps to other topographic maps, be aware that 50 meters is a much greater interval than most other maps you will see. In other words, when the contour lines get close together on an MSTA map, the terrain is truly steep – but if the contour lines are far apart, that doesn’t mean the terrain is flat.

Color shading on the maps represents different land ownership – each land owner has different rules as stated elsewhere in the Guide. Light green on the MSTA maps does NOT represent what it does on a USGS topographic map. A light green color on the MSTA map represents State Forest Land, which may or may not be wooded. Light yellow represents Game Lands where camping is not allowed – again, these tracts may or not be wooded. White areas on the map typically represent private lands -- again, these may or not be wooded. Other colors may appear as included in the legend to each map. The land ownership shown on the maps is derived from various sources but is not accurate as a survey. If in doubt, look closely for land owner-boundaries. In PA along MST, public land boundaries are typically marked by white blotches on trees. State Game Land boundaries are typically better marked as State Forests.

# ELEVATION PROFILES

This edition of the trail guide is the first to include elevation profiles for each section of the trail. These profiles are displayed in a horizontal orientation on the page immediately following the general description of the section. Using these profiles will help in visualizing the difficulty of hiking this particular section of the trail by visually representing the elevation changes found along the trail. The vertical exaggeration for these profiles is 5:1. This means that for every meter plotted on the x-axis (distance), a corresponding meter on the y-axis (elevation) is plotted at five meters. This vertical exaggeration is constant on all elevation profiles for each section.

One additional note regarding the elevation profiles: you may notice a discrepancy between the distances displayed on the elevation profiles versus listed in the guide text. The distances on the elevation profiles will seem to be shorter. This is because the elevation profiles are generated from GPS data whereas the guide text indicate the distance measured on the trail using a surveyor's wheel. Distances calculated using a GPS are typically shorter because they do not take into account changes in elevation and also because of the interpretation that between data points. Always refer to the distance reported in the guide text as the more accurate data when quoting distance on the Mid State Trail.

# TRAIL OVERSEERS

Routine maintenance of the Mid State Trail is performed by volunteer overseers. Their tasks include removing blowdowns, cutting back encroaching brush, improving the footway and repainting the blazes. An individual overseer is assigned a section about 3 km long but organization may be assigned that a longer section of trail. The overseer system is responsible for the generally good condition of the Mid State Trail. An overseer is expected to make a minimum of three trips per year over his or her section. Individual overseers’ efforts are currently coordinated by volunteer Regional Managers based in the areas of Everett, State College, Woolrich, and Tioga. The “Manual for Maintenance of Mid State Trail” and the “DCNR Trail Marking Guidelines” are posted to the MSTA web site.

As with any group of volunteers there is a high turnover and sections become available when overseers move away from the area. The extensions have created many opportunities for new overseers. Some overseers come all the way from Pittsburgh, Philadelphia, and even Virginia to help maintain the Mid State Trail while others live within walking distance. If you would like to adopt a section of the MST, visit the Mid State Trail Association website at www.hike-mst.org for more information.

# GREAT EASTERN TRAIL

Mid State Trail is part of the Great Eastern Trail (GET) network of footpaths. The Great Eastern Trail provides a premier hiking experience on a series of existing trails that are becoming linked to each other into a long-distance footpath in the Appalachian Mountains stretching ultimately from Alabama to the Finger Lakes Trail in New York.

In southern Pennsylvania and Maryland, the Great Eastern Trail divides into an east route and a west route.

GET's west route from the Potomac River traverses the Chesapeake & Ohio Canal National Historic Park to near Little Orleans, MD whence it passes through Maryland's Green Ridge State Forest to a junction with MST at the Mason-Dixon Line. GET's west route continues on MST to Detweiler Run in Huntington County.

The east route uses Tuscarora Trail north from Hancock, MD to a junction with Standing Stone Trail near Cowans Gap State Park in Fulton County, PA; hence north to Greenwood Furnace State Park where it follows MST's spur north to Detweiler Run Natural Area.

Great Eastern Trail follows MST exclusively through northcentral Pennsylvania to the New York border where it meets Crystal Hills Trail, a branch of NY's Finger Lakes Trail crossing eastern Steuben County.

# METRICATION

The MST was the first hiking trail in the United States to use metric. The second edition of the guide (1973) was completely metric. Metrication is a patriotic measure designed to help end our cultural isolation and ease our chronic balance of payments problems. All trail distances, with the exception of a few side trails, have been measured with a Rolata measuring wheel of two meter circumference, or by GPS when wheel data are not available. Side trails measured with a homemade wheel are only given to 0.1 km. By pure serendipity the initials of the Mid State Trail taken from state planning maps of the late '60s also stand for Metric System.

# USE OF THE MAIL DROP & FOOD STORES

The MST is so long that it would be extremely difficult to carry enough food to hike the entire trail without any resupply. There are several small post offices, grocery stores, park offices and other facilities within hiking range of the MST. A mail drop of nonishable food should be addressed to:

Your Name
c/o General Delivery
Post Office Name, PA Zip Code

and marked:
HOLD FOR MID STATE TRAIL HIKER

Allow plenty of time for your mail drop to arrive before you do. It wouldn't hurt to write or call the postmaster involved ahead of time. REMEMBER: POST OFFICES ARE CLOSED ON SUNDAYS AND FREQUENTLY ON SATURDAYS AS WELL. AS OF 2012, MANY RURAL POST OFFICES HAVE REDUCED THEIR HOURS TO AS LITTLE AS 30 MINUTES PER DAY. Please give a park office advance notice if you are going to send them a food drop. Park offices are generally open on weekends in summer. Even if a park office is closed you can wait for the park ranger to show up and retrieve your package. Please visit www.hike-mst.org for a comprehensive and up-to-date list of mail drops, food stores, and other services along the Mid State Trail.
