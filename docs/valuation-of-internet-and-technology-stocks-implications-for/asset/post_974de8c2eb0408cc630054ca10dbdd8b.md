
| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
|  | modern programming. In 1979, the US Department of Defense named a programming language ‘Ada’ in her honour. |
| 1837 | American inventor and artist Samuel Morse invents the magnetic telegraph and Morse code. |
| 1847–1931 | Life of Thomas Alva Edison, who came up with more than 1000 inventions, including the electric light bulb, the electric generator, the stock ticker and the phonograph. |
| 1854 | English mathematician George Boole develops Boolean, or binary, algebra, which is instrumental in the development of computer systems. |
| 1876 | Alexander Graham Bell, who began his career as a teacher for the deaf, invented the first workable telephone and microphone. |
| 1888 | George Eastman patents the box camera, the first to use roll film. It sells for $25. |
| 1895 | Guglielmo Marconi created wireless telegraphy when he sent a signal a few kilometres. In 1901, he sent a radio signal from Cornwall, England to St John’s, Newfoundland; he won the Nobel Prize for physics in 1909. |
| 1898 | Danish Engineer Valdemar Poulsen invents the magnetic tape recorder. |
| 1905 | E = mc², concludes physicist Albert Einstein. The theoretician of relativity wins the Nobel Prize for physics in 1921. |
| 1923 | First transatlantic telephone call connects New York and Britain. |
| 1925 | Phototelegraphy is invented at Bell Laboratories. |
| 1936 | British mathematician Alan Mathison Turing wrote a paper ‘On the Theory of Computable Numbers’, devising theoretical plans for a machine capable of performing any mathematical calculation. His work was important in later development of the digital computer. He laid the foundation for artificial intelligence, developing the Turing Test to determine whether a computer could ‘learn’ or ‘think’. |
| 1938 | Chester Carlson invents xerography. He tried unsuccessfully to sell the process to more than 20 companies. The Haloid Co. purchases the rights in 1947 and changes its name to Xerox. |
| 1939 | Stanford engineers William Hewlett and David Packard start a company on $500, and set up shop in a garage in Palo Alto, California. A coin flip determines whose name goes first. |
| 1940 | Radio Detection and Ranging, or Radar, is developed by Scottish physicist Robert Watson-Watt. Konrad Zuse of Berlin invents the first programmable calculator, called the Z1. |
| 1941 | Iowa State College professor John Vincent Atanasoff and graduate student Clifford Berry develop the first digital computer, which they called the ABC. John W. Mauchly and colleague J. Presper Eckert later patent a digital-computer design, forming a company that later becomes Sperry-Rand Corp. |
| 1943 | Harvard scientist Howard Aiken develops the first program-controlled computer, the Mark 1, independently of Zuse. It is 15.3 metres long and weighs 4.5 metric tons. The US navy uses it to calculate missile trajectories. Eckert and Mauchly develop the ENIAC, or electronic numerical integrator and computer. It weighs 27 tons and has 18000 vacuum tubes. The US army buys it to calculate trajectories and to develop the hydrogen bomb. |
| 1947 | Bell Labs makes the first cellular telephone. |
| 1948 | The transistor is invented at Bell Labs by William Bradford Shockley, Walter Houser Brattain and John Bardeen, for which they win the 1956 Nobel Prize for physics. |
| 1950 | Eckert and Mauchly develop the first commercially available computer, the UNIVAC-1. This is the first general-purpose computer, capable of using both numbers and text. |
| 1952 | Grace Murray Hopper, a navy programmer working in Howard Aiken's Harvard lab, devises the first compiler, which translates English-like programming code into the ones and zeros of machine language. In 1957 she develops the Common Business-Oriented Language, or Cobol. She also coined computer terms: Investigating a problem with a Mark 1 vacuum tube, she pulled out a moth and, legend has it, exclaimed, 'It's a bug!'. |
| 1954–58 | Formula Translation, or Fortran, the first high-level programming language, is developed by John Backus and others at IBM. |
| 1956 | The first transistorized computer is developed at MIT. |
| 1957 | Light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation, known as laser, springs from the research of American Gordon Gould.<br>Sputnik, the first satellite, was launched by the Soviet Union on 4 October. It spurred a revolution of science education in the US. |
| 1959 | Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce of Texas Instruments develop the integrated circuit. Noyce goes on to found Intel Corp. |
| 1963 | The first minicomputer, the PDP-8, is developed at Digital Equipment Corp.<br>Douglas Engelbart of Stanford patents the mouse; he also develops the word processor. |
| 1968 | UNIX is developed at Bell Laboratories by Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie, ushering in the era of computing languages that aren’t tied to a single computer brand. |
| 1969 | ARPANET, a four-computer network, is developed by the US Department of Defense, and forms the basis of what is now the Internet. |
| 1971 | Intel introduces the 4004 chip, the first microprocessor. |
| 1972 | Bill Gates drops out of Harvard. With Paul Allen he starts Traf-O-Data, which makes traffic-counting systems. The company later becomes Microsoft Corp.<br>Ray Tomlinson modifies an e-mail program he created in 1971 for ARPANET where it becomes a big hit. The @ sign was chosen from the punctuation keys on Tomlinson’s Model 33 teletype for its ‘at’ meaning.<br>Atari releases ‘Pong’, the first video game. |
| 1973 | Vinton Cerf and Bob Kahn present the basic Internet ideas at the University of Sussex. |
| 1974 | The Altair, the first popular personal computer, is developed by Edward Roberts of Micro Instrumentation Telemetry Systems and sold as a kit. |
| 1975 | IBM introduces its Mercury 5100 portable computer. The machine costs $9000 and weighs 25 kilograms. |
| 1976 | Wang unveils the first fully computerized word-processing machine. Price tag: $30 000. Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak found Apple Computer Inc., working out of Jobs's garage. |
| 1979 | Dan Bricklin and Bob Frankston develop VisiCalc, the first spreadsheet software. They later sell the rights to Lotus Development Corp. CompuServe starts a bulletin-board service for computer enthusiasts. |
| 1980 | IBM asks Microsoft for an operating system for its upcoming PC. Gates and Allen don't have one, so they license the rights to Q-DOS (which stands for Quick and Dirty Operating System) from Timothy Patterson for less than $100 000. Microsoft drops the Q and changes the name to Disk Operating System (DOS). |
| 1981 | IBM rolls out the IBM PC. The first graphic user interface, Star, is developed at Xerox Palo Alto Research Centre. |
| 1983 | Novell introduces NetWare, its network operating system. |
| 1984 | Hewlett Packard introduces the Laser Jet printer. The break-up of AT&T in the US gives way to competition and ultimately innovation in the world-wide telecom market. Apple unveils the Macintosh. The company runs just one ad, but news and talk shows replay it. |
| 1985 | Microsoft introduces Windows. IBM isn't interested, so Microsoft sells the system independently. Windows doesn't become popular until the release of version 3.0 in 1990. First addresses ending in .com launched. |
| 1988 | The US National Science Foundation Network, in collaboration with IBM, MCI and the University of Michigan, develops NSFNET. The system boosts the Internet's capacity and allows the transfer of graphics. |
| 1989 | The World Wide Web comes to be, developed by English computer scientist Timothy Berners Lee for international researchers at the European Organisation for Nuclear Research in Geneva. |
| 1991 | The first text-only Web browser is released to the public. The Internet backbone, the fastest part of the whole system, runs at 44 Mbps. |
| 1992 | The term ‘surfing the net’ is coined by the writer Jean Armour Polly.<br>WWW software is released in 1992. |
| 1993 | Intel releases the Pentium processor.<br>The first web browser, Mosaic, is released by the National Centre for Supercomputing Applications at the University of Illinois in Champaign. |
| 1994 | The Web is growing at an annual rate of 341 634%.<br>Two Arizona-based lawyers, Lawrence Canter and Martha Siegel, send an advert to 6000 newsgroups – the first spam. |
| 1995 | Microsoft launches Windows 95.<br>Lycos and Altavista are launched. |
| 1997 | Deep Blue, an IBM supercomputer, beats world chess champion Garry Kasparov. |
| 1998 | A US court bans the practice of buying domain names of famous companies and then selling them for exorbitant prices. |
| 1999 | Microsoft used its monopoly in PC operating systems to hurt consumers and competitors, says a US judge’s findings of fact. |
| 2000 | There are 21.1 million websites worldwide. |

Sources: Collier’s Encyclopaedia, Computer Chronicles, Microsoft Encarta, World Almanac
