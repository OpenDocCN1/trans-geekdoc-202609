# Preface

# The collapse of Internet and technology stocks

It was a bubble, and it did burst. About that most people agree. The history books will record 10 March 2000 as one of the most tumultuous days ever to engulf the stock market. In a matter of hours billions of dollars were wiped from stock markets all over the world, as the technology bubble finally burst.

America’s Nasdaq index, made up largely of technology firms, collapsed into what many believe to be the biggest stock market crash of all time. The Nasdaq Stock Market smoothly rose six-fold to a record high over the five years ending 10 March 2000, before collapsing. This was not the first time stock prices have been driven by waves of ‘irrational exuberance’ rising to dizzy heights and falling back accordingly.

‘Stock prices have reached what looks like a permanently high plateau,’ declared the eminent Yale economist Irving Fisher in the autumn of 1929. A few weeks after this oracular pronouncement, the Dow Jones Industrial Average had declined by more than a third. The worst was still to come. On 8 July 1932, the Dow Jones closed at 41.88, a drop of nearly 90% from its 1929 peak. The stock market chart of these years resembles a precipice rather than a plateau.

Why did Professor Fisher get things so wrong? The answer is that he had fallen for the decade’s most alluring idea, a thesis which underpinned the great bull market of the late 1920s: he believed that America had entered a new era of limitless prosperity.
