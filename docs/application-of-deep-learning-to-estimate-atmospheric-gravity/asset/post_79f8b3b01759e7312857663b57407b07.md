We also evaluated the horizontal wavenumber spectrum of gravity wave momentum fluxes. The momentum flux spectra were calculated by Re[U*W*(k)] and Re[V*W*(k)], where k is the horizontal wavenumber; U, V, and W(k) are the Fourier components of u', v', and w'; and an asterisk (*) denotes their complex conjugate (e.g., Sato et al., 2017). The characteristic horizontal wavenumber was estimated as a wavenumber with the largest spectral density, which is also a key parameter of gravity waves. In the zonal direction, both estimated characteristic wave numbers were m⁻¹ for Re[U*(k)] and 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ rad m⁻¹ for Re[V*(k)] and 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ rad m⁻¹ for Re[W*(k)]. Finally, for the momentum flux in the target area we estimated for each month. Figures 4–6 depict the distribution of maximum momentum flux magnitude |u'|, |v'|, and |w'|, as well as maximum |J| for the ground truth and the estimates. All three months show that the estimates tend to be larger in winter and smaller in summer. This is attributable to seasonal movement of the jet stream that is more frequent during these periods, which means the cloud base to the surface is much stronger triggering the organic gravity waves. This seasonal change was observed for a limited period of years. In April gravity wave activity is high. In the December, when the momentum fluxes were strongest, the estimated values of |u'| and |v'| were 0.23 and 0.14 m/s, respectively, while their values were lower than those of the ground truth. This could be attributed to the misalignment of the phase of the estimated u', v', and w' in the atmosphere above. Furthermore, the estimation errors (mean square errors (RMSEs)) of the momentum flux magnitude |u'|, |v'|, |w'|, maximum |J| and maximum |J| are shown in Figures 4–6, respectively. The RMSE was large in winter and small in summer. The large RMSEs for the maximum momentum fluxes are observed for December: The month, third quarter, and maximum RMSE for the maximum momentum flux magnitude |u'|, |v'|, |w'|, maximum |J| and maximum |J| were approximately 0.32, 0.30, and 0.70 m/s.

Figures 4–g show the true and estimated values of the zonal and meridional wave numbers involved in the momentum flux for each month. The true values do not vary much throughout the year. The values are -8 × 10⁻¹ to -1 × 10⁻¹. The estimated values agree well with the ground truth in winter. Figures 4–k show the estimation errors (RMSEs) in the characteristic horizontal wavenumber for each month. The mean error in the zonal wavenumber is approximately 1.1 × 10⁻⁵, which is around 10% of the ground truth while in summer, the mean error is approximately 0.3 × 10⁻⁵ to 5.0 × 10⁻⁵, which is around 30% of the ground truth. From the results of these analyses, it can be concluded that the proposed method adequately estimates the momentum flux associated with strong gravity waves, especially in winter.

## 4. Conclusions

This study demonstrated the performance of the deep convolutional neural network (U-Net) to estimate the parameters of organic gravity waves. Low-resolution data (u, v, t and n) at levels of 850, 700 and 300 hPa in the troposphere as well as high-resolution topography data were correctly identified by 3D wave functions (u', v', w') in the lower atmosphere. Wave parameters such as wavenumber and well as amplitude of the momentum flux, could be properly estimated using the U-Nets. The estimates are particularly suitable in winter when gravity wave activity is high.

Generally, the learning techniques vary greatly in the quality of the results that they produce depending on the neural network layer selection, the choice of the training data, and the definition of the error function. As in the supporting information (Figure 53), the estimation accuracy varies depending on the difference in the input data (physical quantity and level).
