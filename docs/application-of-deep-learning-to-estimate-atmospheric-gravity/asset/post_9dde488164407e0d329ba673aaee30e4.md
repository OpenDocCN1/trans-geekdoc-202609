**Abstract** Gravity waves play an essential role in driving and maintaining global circulation. To understand their contribution in the atmosphere, the accurate reproduction of their distribution is important. A deep learning approach for the estimation of gravity wave momentum fluxes was proposed, and performance at 100 hPa was tested using data from low resolution and meridional winds, temperature, and specificity in Hokkaido region (Japan). This convolutional neural network was trained on 29-year reanalysis data (JRA-55 and ERA-5) and the final 5-year data were evaluated. The results showed that the fine-scale distribution of gravity waves could be estimated at a reasonable computational cost. In particular, where gravity waves are stronger, the mean root square error (RMSE) of the maximum flux was significantly better than that of the low resolution method.

## 1. Introduction

Atmospheric gravity waves are small-scale waves that originate from various sources, including high mountains, jet-front systems, and convection. They propagate momentum vertically and play an important role in driving and maintaining the global circulation in the atmosphere (Fris & Alexander, 2003). They possess a potentially significant amount of momentum deposit. Since the 1980s, a wide range of studies to gravity waves have been detected, and theoretical advancements in their understanding have been achieved (Achiers et al., 1995; Chun & Baik, 1998; Erb et al., 2014; Guo et al., 2017; Ploughberg & Zhang, 2014; Sato, 2014; Sato, 2009; Tsuda, 1992; Wright et al., 2017).

The scales of gravity waves have been parameterized on the computational grid as the key in several atmospheric models, such as sub-grid scale waves as momentum flux, wavelength, and frequencies (Alexander et al., 2010; Palmer et al., 1996). Most parameterizations assume that gravity waves only propagate vertically, but they are also affected by horizontal topography and conditions (Dunkerton, 1984; Sato et al., 2009). Watanabe et al. (2008) used an atmospheric circulation model with a resolution to show the vertical and horizontal propagation of gravity waves with a fine resolution. Although such a high-resolution method can successfully simulate the generation, propagation, and dissipation of gravity waves, it is computationally expensive and unsuitable for practical problems with reasonable computational costs would be beneficial for further development of gravity wave parameterization.

Recently, image pattern recognition using deep learning has attracted a lot of attention in various fields (Krizhevsky et al., 2012). Deep learning, which uses multiple neural networks to statistically learn features from large amounts of data, has demonstrated high performance in various tasks, including image transformation, where an input image is transformed into another. In the field of atmospheric science, deep learning has also been introduced in troposphere and atmospheric wave detection (Earth et al., 2018), satellite image retrieval (Matsuno et al., 2019), and wave reconstruction (Tao et al., 2016). Rasp (2018) first applied a deep neural network to capture front detection. Subsequently, deep learning has been used for parameterizing moisture convection (O'Gorman & Dwyer, 2018), ozone deposition velocity at the planetary boundary layer (Yang et al., 2019). Furthermore, Gaje et al. (2020) proposed deep-learning-stochastic parameterization which has the advantage that an estimation model based on the training data set, the computational cost associated with running the model is much lower than directly solving the sub-problem.

The aim of this study was to apply the deep learning to estimate the key parameters for surface gravity waves that propagate for the further development of gravity wave parameterizations. In order to achieve a reasonable computational cost, we used lower-resolution data in the stratosphere as the input values to learn the conversion process to fine-scale wind fluxes in the lower stratosphere. This method is particularly effective for describing non-organic gravity waves whose sources are generally complex. However, as the first step, we tested organic gravity waves which are relatively easy to handle, over the Hokkaido region of Japan.

## 2. Data and Methods

### 2.1. Data Analysis and Training Data Preparation

Our model learned the relationship between coarse-resolution atmospheric fields and high-resolution wind fluxes. As input, the Japanese 55-year Reanalysis (JRA-55; Harada et al., 2016; Kobayashi et al., 2013), which is a global reanalysis data, was used, and for output (ground truth), the resolution scaling data (DSRA-55) from the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) was used. JRA-55 and DSRA-55 have horizontal resolutions of 60 and 5 km, respectively. The ground truth may not be perfect because DSRA-55 is based on information from forty-six JRA-55 runs which are related with initial conditions with the JRA's operational model.

The lower-resolution (60 km) physical quantities used as input were the zonal (u), meridional (v), and vertical (w) winds, air temperature (T), specific humidity (q), and pressure (p) at levels of 100, 300, 700, and 850 hPa which were used for the generation and propagation of gravity waves. These are shown in Figures 1. Since organic gravity waves are radiated from the mountains, we used winds near the terrain (i.e., 850 and 700 hPa for high mountains). Temperature is necessary because the static stability of the atmosphere is a factor influencing the structure of gravity waves. In the case of gravity waves, the condensation of relative humidity is important: in the case of gravity waves, the levels in the troposphere (i.e., 300 and 700 hPa) needs to be included. Thus, for future studies, u, v, T, and q at horizontal wavelengths are required to cover the multiple sources of gravity waves in the deep learning system, only organic gravity waves are the focus of this study. The fine-resolution (5 km) topography of DSRA-55 was also used as input; the total number of channels was 15.

The fine-scale ground truth used to reconstruct the input data, 3D fluctuations (u', v', and w') were obtained as the residuals of the original data from the gravity waves which are spatially simple moving averages of 20 grid squares (1,00 km-squares). The pressure level of the ground truth should be higher than that of the source of gravity waves. Here, we used 100 hPa, which is the top level of DSRA-55, as a tentative level in method development. The momentum flux at may be changed for a parameterization with better performance in the future.
