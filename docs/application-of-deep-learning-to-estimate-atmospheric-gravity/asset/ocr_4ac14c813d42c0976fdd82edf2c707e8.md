**Abstract** Gravity waves play an essential role in driving and maintaining global circulation. To understand their contribution in the atmosphere, the accurate reproduction of their distribution is important. A deep learning approach for the estimation of gravity wave momentum fluxes was proposed, and performance at 100 hPa was tested using data from low resolution and meridional winds, temperature, and specificity in Hokkaido region (Japan). This convolutional neural network was trained on 29-year reanalysis data (JRA-55 and ERA-5) and the final 5-year data were evaluated. The results showed that the fine-scale distribution of gravity waves could be estimated at a reasonable computational cost. In particular, where gravity waves are stronger, the mean root square error (RMSE) of the maximum flux was significantly better than that of the low resolution method.

# # 1. Introduction

Atmospheric gravity waves are small-scale waves that originate from various sources, including high mountains, jet-front systems, and convection. They propagate momentum vertically and play an important role in driving and maintaining the global circulation in the atmosphere (Fris & Alexander, 2003). They possess a potentially significant amount of momentum deposit. Since the 1980s, a wide range of studies to gravity waves have been detected, and theoretical advancements in their understanding have been achieved (Achiers et al., 1995; Chun & Baik, 1998; Erb et al., 2014; Guo et al., 2017; Ploughberg & Zhang, 2014; Sato, 2014; Sato, 2009; Tsuda, 1992; Wright et al., 2017).

The scales of gravity waves have been parameterized on the computational grid as the key in several atmospheric models, such as sub-grid scale waves as momentum flux, wavelength, and frequencies (Alexander et al., 2010; Palmer et al., 1996). Most parameterizations assume that gravity waves only propagate vertically, but they are also affected by horizontal topography and conditions (Dunkerton, 1984; Sato et al., 2009). Watanabe et al. (2008) used an atmospheric circulation model with a resolution to show the vertical and horizontal propagation of gravity waves with a fine resolution. Although such a high-resolution method can successfully simulate the generation, propagation, and dissipation of gravity waves, it is computationally expensive and unsuitable for practical problems.
