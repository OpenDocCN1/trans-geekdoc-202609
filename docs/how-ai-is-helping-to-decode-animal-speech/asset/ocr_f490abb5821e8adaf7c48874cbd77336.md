These results challenge ideas about what makes human language special – and even how 'language' should be defined. Understanding complex vocal communication in other species could help people to talk back. By Rachel Fieldhouse

Deep in the rainforests of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Mélissa Berthet found bonobos doing something thought to be uniquely human. During the six months that Berthet observed the primates, they combined calls in several ways to make complex phrases. In one example, bonobos (Pan paniscus) that were building nests together added a yelp, meaning 'let's do this', to a grunt that says 'look at me'. It's really a way to say: 'Look at what I'm doing, and let's do this all together,' says Berthet, who studies primates and linguistics at the University of Rennes, France.
