Despite the widespread applications of machine learning force fields (MLFFs) in solids and small molecules, there is a notable gap in applying MLFFs to simulate liquid electrolytes—a critical component of commercial lithium-ion batteries. We introduce ByteDance Artificial Intelligence Molecular simulation Booster BAMBOO, a predictive framework for molecular dynamics, with a demonstration of its capability in the context of liquid electrolytes for lithium batteries. We design a physics-inspired graph equivariant transformer architecture as the backbone of BAMBO to learn from quantum mechanical simulations. Additionally, we introduce an ensemble knowledge distillation approach and apply it to MLFFs to reduce the fluctuation of observations from molecular dynamics simulations. Finally, we propose a density alignment algorithm alongside BAMBOO which demonstrates state-of-the-art accuracy in predicting key electrolyte properties such as density, viscosity and ionic conductivity across various solvents and salts. The current model, trained on more than 15 chemical species, achieves an average density of 0.01 g cm⁻³ on various.

Liquid electrolyte is an indispensable component of the current commercial lithium-ion batteries¹. The existing commercial electrolytes are mostly carbonate-based, and composed of five to ten different materials. Experimentally exploring molecular interactions for the rational design of electrolytes is expensive, time consuming and heavily reliant on chemists' intuition and experience. Limitations poses challenges in transitioning from proof-concept experiments to products markets, particularly due to the increasing complexity in optimizing the properties of multiplicity liquids.

Atomistic simulation is an accessible and feasible alternative to exhaustive experimentation. However, to achieve reliable requirements, such as high accuracy and sufficient timescale and lengthscale. Quantum mechanical simulations offer high accuracy in describing electronic properties, yet they are computationally intensive and impractical for large-scale and complex systems like liquid electrolytes. While classical methods, although efficient, often sacrifice accuracy in capturing molecular structures and behavior of electrolytes, there presents a need for a balanced and general approach that can reconcile the difference between and in modeling liquids with electrolytes: multiple solvents and varying types of ions.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
(1) We present a ML model that integrates a graph equivariant transformer (GET) architecture to capture both semi-local and direct electrostatic interactions.
(2) We demonstrate the application of an ensemble knowledge distillation algorithm to reduce the fluctuation of results obtained from ML-based MD simulations.
(3) We propose a physics-informed data algorithm aligning MLFF-MD simulations with experimental data, which ensures physical consistency and demonstrates considerable limitations to liquids not included in the alignment process. As a result, BAMBOO shows state-of-the-art accuracy in predicting the density, viscosity and ionic conductivity of various liquids.

In recent years, there has been a growing utilization of machine learning force fields (MLFFs) to perform molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. This trend is primarily attributed to the ability of MLFF to deliver results faster than initio quantum mechanical simulations, as well as chemical quality data with comparable accuracy. The development of MLFFs has been limited by the lack of high-quality data for diverse fields of matter. This field has also seen a shift from descriptor-based rotation-invariant MLFFs$^{1-16}$ towards graph neural networks (GNNs) with rotation-equivariant representations$^{17-20}$ and transformers. On the other, progress has been made by incorporating constraints derived from general physical consistency into MLFFs. This includes electrostatic interactions$^{21-24}$ and spin-spin interactions.

# Results

Advances in the architecture of ML models and the concept of ‘universal’ ML, which allows a single model to simulate a wide range of systems, have gained increasing attention within the field of solid-state materials$^{25-30}$ and bio-organic molecules$^{31-32}$. However, when it comes to electrolytes, which are complex mixtures, the ability of ML to predict multiple properties across various solvents and salts is still lacking. This limitation may arise from the complex challenges inherent in electrolytes, such as the coexistence of different species and the formation of ion pairs and clusters (AGG). As a result, utilizing to simulate aqueous systems$^{33-34}$, molecular liquids and ionic liquids$^{35}$ remains a notable research difficulty focused on MLFF for electrolytes.

We illustrate the workflow of BAMBOO. In Ia., we simulate local environments within lithium electrolyte as gas clusters and subsequently use density functional theory (DFT) to compute their energies, structures, and properties. In Ib., we demonstrate the broad accuracy of BAMBOO on various molecules and salts in the DFT dataset (Fig. 1). In particular, our focus encompasses compounds commonly found in electrolytes utilizing lithium ions, such as carbonates. In Ic., we incorporate two most frequently used organic liquids—ethanol (EO) and acetone (AC)—to show the generalizability of the trained model. The quantities calculated using DFT are used to train a GNN to generate the MLFF. The MLFF is then used to perform MD simulations. These independently trained GNNs are then compared with measured data, specifically density in this context, to evaluate their performance. Details of methodology, distillation and alignment algorithm are provided in the following sections.

Prior to this work, several efforts have been made to simulate electrolytes using MLFFs: Ref29 utilized deep potential to calculate the density and solution structure of LiTFSI in water; Ref36 used a charge reactive neural network to predict the ionic conductivity of electrolytes in solvents. Despite achieving some success, these studies lack clear evidence regarding the generalizability of their findings across a wide range of electrolytes. For instance, Ref30 used a charge reactive neural network to achieve high accuracy only for specific salt systems.

In addition to the scarcity of MLFFs for electrolytes, there are two major challenges encountered with MLFFs: we have encountered issues of data scarcity and fluctuation of results, which stem from the inherent nature of ‘machine-learning’ and the partial usability of MLFFs. Most deep learning-based models are solely relying on quantum mechanical simulations, which lack the flexibility to match experimental data of diverse systems across different scales.

Despite the conceptual challenge of universal molecular fields$^{37-39}$, deep learning-based MLFFs are available parameters are limited by optimized experimental results.

In this work, we introduce the ByteDance Artificial Intelligence Molecular Booster (BAMBOO) workflow, designed for constructing MLFFs for the MD simulations of organic liquids.

In II, we describe the setup of semi-local, electrostatic and direct interactions in BAMBOO. In gallery, we explicitly compute the interactions between corresponding types of atoms and the two types of atoms to calculate the total energy. (1) The semi-local interaction is generated by a GNN with message layers to decode the data in the following section. The GET takes the atom types $\{i\}$ and the relative coordinates $r_{ij} = |r_i - r_j|$ as input, and outputs the energy $E_{ij}$ to encode the local environment. We further input $\{i\}$ to a multilayer perceptron to output the predicted predicted energy $E_i^{sc}$. (2) The electrostatic energy is computed by summing all $E_{ij}$. The representations of $\{i\}$ are fed into another neural network to output the predicted electrostatic energy $E_{ij}^{el}$. The total electrostatic energy $E^{el}$ is based on the predicted charge distribution under the framework of charge equilibrium$^{40}$. (3) Dispersion energy is directly computed on DFT-B3LYP correction$^{41}$. Finally, we sum the dispersion energy $E^{disp}$ to obtain the total energy $E$ of the system.
