### 3.5.4 Flooz

![](Images/image00205.gif)

Flooz.com was a poster child for the late-nineties tech boom, which petered out by 2001. Flooz was compared to coupons or airline miles that could be accumulated through promotional campaigns or purchased directly from Flooz's central platform as a digital tool for merchants accepting payment on the still relatively young Internet. Flooz raised up to $35 million in venture capital over the course of its three years in business, betting on merchant demand for new and exciting payment mechanisms. Flooz spent no money on its advertising campaign, even enlisting the help of comedienne Whoopi Goldberg for a series of TV commercials. Despite the momentum, Flooz reportedly ended in failure to attract a large number of users and merchants, and the company's platform's criminal activities would ultimately prove too much for it. By August 2001, the company's demise had been hastened by rapidly declining investor support as well as allegations of criminal activity on its platform. According to a 2001 New York Times report, Russian criminal groups are suspected of using stolen credit cards to buy online currency and launder illicit funds, prompting a federal investigation. An approximate $300,000 was stolen, according to a Flooz source. Shortly after the fraud allegations surfaced, Flooz shut down after failing to find a buyer.

### 3.5.5 DigiCash

![How To Get Your DigiCash Card? - YouTube](Images/image00206.jpeg)

DigiCash, a digital currency company founded in the 1990s, came close to becoming a global success. DigiCash, on the other hand, failed due to a combination of internal strife and a lack of ignition, which prevented it from becoming a widely used online payment system. David Chaum, the creator of the ecash system, is a pioneer in cryptographic protocols. DigiCash, which was formed in 1990, supported cyberbucks, a digital currency that provided anonymity to users and security to merchants who accepted it. Libertarians and others backed an international online currency that could transcend government control backed DigiCash, according to a 2003 report by The Guardian. DigiCash was notable for allowing a diverse range of payment sizes, particularly micropayments. For currency trading, the system used an email mailing system, and reports suggest that traders engaged in off-market transactions. According to reports from the time, a lack of cash flow, as well as friction between the company's employees and leadership, were among the reasons for DigiCash's demise. Despite promising high-level talks with major banks and credit cards, the venture was unable to secure a major deal that would ensure its survival in an increasingly digitized financial sector. Citibank, for example, has been in long-running talks about an integration project, but the bank has since shifted its focus to other ventures. Chaum left the company in 1996, and two years later, it filed for bankruptcy.

### 3.5.6 B-Money

Wei Dai came up with B-money as an early proposal for an "anonymous, distributed electronic cash system." Satoshi Nakamoto made a reference to B-money when he created the world-famous Bitcoin. B-money was first offered in 1998 by Wei Dai, a graduate of Washington University. His plan was to create a free online financial system. So that no one can impose taxes or compel people to do anything, few people will remember B-Money, which was first introduced to the public in 1998, more than ten years before bitcoins. B-Money, on the other hand, is very different from bitcoins, despite the fact that the creator of the first, Wei Da (Wei Da), also wanted to create an unnamed and distributed method of issuing coins. Technical documents (link) (the so-called "white paper") containing the same basic principles as modern cryptocurrency were published to speed up the implementation of B-money. The "digital aliases" are expected to be able to send as well as receive money through a decentralized network, as well as ensure the execution of contracts between themselves without the involvement of a third party. The use of a proof of work function as a means of creating money is proposed in the first protocol. Dai's B-Money was proposed in the context of cypherpunks mailing-list discussions about Hashcash, the first symmetric proof-of-work function, which was also printed on the same mailing list the previous year - in May 1997. (Bitcoin uses the hashcash cost function as the proof-of-work during coin minting, just like the B-money proposal.) Money is transferred in B-Money by transmitting the transaction to all participants, who all keep track of each other's accounts. Contracts with the possibility of reparation in the event of default can be made with a third party agreeing to act as arbitrator. If no agreement is reached, each party broadcasts arguments or evidence in its favor, and each participant calculates the reparations/fines in his own account. The accounts can be kept only by a subset of participants, the "servers". These accounts are necessary to be published, and the participants who do exchanges verify their balances by asking a large number of them in the second protocol. The participants also make sure the money supply isn't inflated. To become a server, you must post a bond, which is forfeited if the server is found to be dishonest. An alternative method of creating money is proposed, involving an auction in which participants bid on the solution of known-complexity computational problems. Regretfully, the B-Money project has remained stuck since the white paper was implemented.
