What Is a Blockchain?

The blockchain is a digital, open ledger that stores transactions in code. In practice, it's similar to a checkbook that's spread among thousands of computers all over the globe. Transactions are stored in "blocks," then connected to previous bitcoin transactions in a "chain." Imagine a diary wherein you write it down whatever you spend the money on etc. Each page is a block, and the whole book, or collection of web pages, is a blockchain.

Each transaction is validated using two basic validation mechanisms to avoid fraud: proof of labor or proof of stake.

Proof of Work vs. Proof of Stake

Proof-of-work and proof-of-stake are alternative verification approaches that award verifiers with extra bitcoin before transactions are included in a blockchain. Most cryptocurrencies employ either proof of labor or proof of stake to verify transactions.

Proof of Stake

Some cryptocurrencies utilize a proof-of-stake verification approach to decrease the amount of electricity required to review transactions. The amount of bitcoin each individual is prepared to "stake" or temporarily start locking up in a common safe for the opportunity to engage in the system limits the number of operations each person may verify. It's almost like collateral. Each individual who invests in cryptocurrency can verify payments, but the likelihood of being picked increases as the amount invested grows. 

Proof of stake is far more efficient than proof of work since it eliminates energy-intensive equation solving, allowing for shorter confirmation times of operations. When the owner of a stake, also known as a validator, is selected to validate a fresh batch of transactions, they will be paid in bitcoin, perhaps in the quantity of the block's aggregate transaction fees. You lose a portion of your investment to deter fraud if you are picked and verify incorrect transactions.

"Consensus protocol" is a way of confirming activities on a blockchain whereby an algorithm generates a mathematical puzzle for computers to solve. Every participating machine, known as a "miner," solves a mathematical challenge that aids in verifying a set of transactions known as a block, which is subsequently added to the blockchain principle. The first system to do so effectively gets awarded with a modest sum of bitcoin for its efforts.

Bitcoin Transactions and Cryptography

Miners are users who examine a transaction to see if it is genuine or not. The transaction, along with numerous others, is then uploaded to the network, where the data can no longer be modified.

### 1.2.1 Barter System

The barter system is the oldest system with regard to a medium of exchange. Mesopotamians are thought to have introduced it around 6,000 BC. Services and goods were exchanged in this system. As a medium, there was no separate tool. Rather, goods and services served as the instruments. Despite the fact that this system was quite straightforward, it had a number of flaws. To begin with, the barter system necessitated locating a party with products of equal value, which was not always possible. Second, obtaining an exchange rate for all of the commodities was nearly impossible. Third, it was unable to develop the economy due to the scarcity of products. Fourth, because the comparable value of products was not always available, this system was virtually impossible to follow. Because of these flaws, the barter system fell out of favor, and the concept of currency was born.

### 2.2.1 History of Fiat Money

In the 10th century, Fiat money emerged from China, primarily in the dynasties of the Yuan, Tang, Song, and Ming. In the 18th Century, the West began using paper currency. The American colonies, the Continental Congress and France, continued to issue bills of credit that were used to make payments. Money was created for social purposes

Money was created for religious purposes

### 3.5.8 Bit Gold

![](Images/image00208.jpeg)

One of the first attempts at developing decentralized virtual money was Bit Gold, which was conceived by blockchain pioneer Nick Szabo in 1998. Despite the fact that Szabo's bit-gold project was never realized, it is widely considered as Satoshi Nakamoto's bitcoin protocol's precursor. In fact, the bit-gold and bitcoin protocols are so similar that some have suggested that Szabo is Satoshi Nakamoto, the anonymous bitcoin developer, but Szabo has denied this. Bit gold achieves decentralization by combining cryptography and mining techniques. These elements include time-stamped blocks that are maintained in a title registry and created using proof-of-work (PoW) strings. According to Szabo, he proposed a decentralized PoW function that could be "securely kept, transmitted, and assayed with minimum trust."

Understanding Bit Gold

Bitcoin and bit-gold have a lot in common, especially when it comes to the technologies that process transactions and keep the decentralized network safe. A user will have to employ processing power to solve a cryptographic challenge in the bit gold structure. All solved puzzles are communicated across a Byzantine Fault Tolerant (BFT) peer-to-peer network, and the problem solver's public key is allocated to them. The details of the transaction are kept in a title register. In the consensus system, it is analogous to a blockchain in that it offers an immutable record of and order for transactions that have occurred). The bit gold scheme proposed by Szabo is non-fungible. This implies that various quantities of bit gold must be merged to produce a single transaction. Rather than a centralized authority managing its levers, the bit gold relies on a decentralized and distributed system of trust amongst the numerous nodes—or participating computers—that make up its network.

 

How is Bit Gold different from Bitcoin? 

Despite the parallels between bit-gold and bitcoin, the two initiatives have several fundamental distinctions. According to Szabo, the difficulty of mining bit gold will alter over time. It wouldn't inherently get easier or harder, but there would be peaks and troughs in the quantity of bit gold that could be produced at certain periods. Bitcoin, on the other hand, is designed to increase the difficulty of mining bitcoin over time. (Bitcoins have a finite quantity as well.) More significantly, unlike bitcoin, bit gold was never intended to be utilized as a medium of exchange. Bit gold was designed as a reserve currency for a different sort of electronic money. In the days before fiat money, bit gold was intended to serve as tangible reserves.

Goals of Bit Gold 

Szabo claims that bit gold was invented to alleviate inefficiencies in the existing banking system. According to Szabo, parties must invest a large level of confidence in order for transactions to take effect in the traditional financial system. When customers and financial institutions trade through trust-based systems, they are unfortunately prone to fraud and theft. In reality, the banking system's history of repeated losses, as well as the large cost of fraudulent activity and walled design, motivated Szabo to propose bit gold, a more trustless approach for trading.

### 3.5.10 Hash-Cash

![](Images/image00210.jpeg)

Hash-cash was a forerunner to digital money. It was Satoshi's inspiration for Bitcoin's proof-of-work implementation. We must first deal with Hash-cash in order to comprehend Bitcoin's consensus mechanism and proof-of-work. Adam Back created hash-cash in 1997 as a way of preventing email spam. Email spam was an annoyance to anyone with an email account, even in 1997. This was due to a fundamental problem, according to Adam Back: all you had to do to email someone was send a few packets to a mail server. They made the decision to charge for emails. Sending an email was so cheap that it was practically free. Even though honest users only send a few emails per day, spammers can send tens of millions at little or no cost. Consider how much it would cost to send an email for a penny. This is fine for a regular user who has a few pennies to spare each day. Spammers, on the other hand, would have to pay millions of pennies, effectively ending their spam campaigns. You'll notice that this is a little backward: charging a penny per email presupposes the existence of digital pennies with which to pay. But there was no such solution, which is why we needed Bitcoin. So, how could Hashcash make spammers pay a virtual penny? The answer lies in Hashcash's core concept, which is now also present in Bitcoin: proof-of-work.

Hashcash's proof-of-work

The proof-of-work in Hashcash is pretty straightforward. The mail server first designates a difficulty level, which is a number that indicates how difficult the PoW must be. The default value is twenty. In Hashcash, the proof of work is to generate a message whose SHA-1 hash digest begins with 20 zeros in binary. How do you make a hash digest with a prefix that you specify? Remember that you shouldn't really be able to predict the output of a cryptographic hash function any better than chance. You don't have a choice but to force it. To solve this PoW, simply try different inputs till one of them has a sufficient number of 0s in front of it.
