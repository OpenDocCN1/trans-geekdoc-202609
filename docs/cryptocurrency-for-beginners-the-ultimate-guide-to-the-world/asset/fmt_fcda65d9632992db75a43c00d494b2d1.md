### 1.2.6 Bimetallic standard of the US

In 1792, the United States government maintained its gold legacy by creating a bimetallic standard. Every monetary unit in the United States had to be supported by either gold or silver, according to the bimetallic standard. For example, one dollar was worth 24.75 grains of gold at the time. This meant that the coins used as money were just representations of the central bank's gold (or silver) holdings. This gold standard, however, did not continue indefinitely. Several important events in the early 1900s contributed to gold's demise from the monetary system. The issue of promissory notes began in 1913, following the formation of the Federal Reserve (the present-day mirror copy of paper money). On-demand, these promissory notes could be easily redeemed in gold. The United States government received the right to all gold coins in circulation as a result of the Gold Reserve Act of 1934, allowing it to stop minting new gold coins. In a nutshell, this legislation established the notion that gold or gold coins were no longer required to serve as money. As a result, the United States abandoned the gold standard in 1971. It was at this time that its currency lost its gold backing.

### 1.2.7 Modern economy and gold

Although gold is no longer used to underpin the US dollar (or any other global currency), it still has value in today's culture. It is crucial to the global economy at all times. By focusing just on the balance sheets of central banks and other financial institutions, such as the International Monetary Fund, we may readily verify this argument. Approximately one-fifth of the world's supply of above-ground gold is held by these organizations as of today. Moreover, some central banks have prudently increased their gold reserves. This underscores their fears about the global economy's long-term viability.

### 1.2.8 Gold helps to preserve wealth

The primary reason for gold's relevance in the modern economy is that it has demonstrated exceptional performance in the successful preservation of wealth over a lengthy period of time spanning many generations. Paper-denominated currencies, on the other hand, cannot make the same claim. Let's look at an example to assist us to comprehend what we're talking about: In the early 1970s, one ounce of gold was worth $35. Assume you had the option of keeping either an ounce of gold or merely the $35 at the moment. Both gold and the dollar, in fact, would purchase you the same items, such as a brand new tailor-made business suit or a sporty bicycle. In contrast, if you had an ounce of gold today and converted it to today's pricing, you'd be able to buy a whole new suit with it, but the same cannot be said for the $35. In reality, if you had elected to keep the $35 instead of the one ounce of gold, you would have lost a significant sum of money. This is mostly due to the fact that the value of gold increased while the value of the dollar decreased due to inflation.

### 1.2.9 Gold is secure

As seen by the unabating tensions in the Middle East, Africa, South Asia, and elsewhere, the current world's economic climate is becoming increasingly dependent on and determined by regional and political stability. It is for this reason that investors have a natural affinity for gold and regard it as a secure investment, particularly when political and economic instability are prevalent. An examination of history indicates that it has been polluted by the demise of dynasties, military and political coups, and the resulting currency collapse. During these times, gold investors were successful in safeguarding their riches and, in some cases, using the commodity to flee the economic and political chaos. As a result, whenever there are news events that hint at global economic turmoil, investors turn to gold as a safe haven.

### 1.2.10 Use of gold for diversifying investment

In general, gold is seen as a great way to diversify one's portfolio. Regardless of whether your concerns are connected to inflation, wealth protection, or a sinking US currency, gold has shown to be a profitable investment alternative that may add a diverse component to your investment portfolio. Gold, on the other hand, is unrelated to stocks, bonds, or real estate if your goal is just diversity.

### 1.2.11 Evolution of gold

Gold has evolved over time as a consequence of three primary transitions: first, it became the global standard as a medium of exchange; second, an international banking system was built around it, with paper money fully convertible into gold; and third, convertibility into gold ceased in 1971, and gold was replaced by a pure fiat money system. The benefits of gold, on the other hand, have not altered in the 40 years since the world switched to a fiat money system. Governments discourage the use of gold as money for their citizens, but they trade in it through their central banks and treasuries, according to the financial world. There are confirmed claims that the US Treasury holds 261 mm ounces of gold. In 2012, the Fed's assets were projected to reach 13.5 million ounces. India and China, through their central banks, have been buying gold at an alarming rate in recent months. As a result, in less than a year, they have boosted their gold holdings from 156 metric tons to more than 400 metric tons. This is a significant departure from the government's long-term dumping of gold equities during the last four or five decades. Even the International Monetary Fund (IMF) has lately overturned a long-standing policy of gold liquidation by purchasing 44 metric tons. Of course, gold ownership is a worldwide phenomenon. As a result, gold prices are influenced not just by the Federal Reserve of the United States but also by the actions of other central banks across the world. The ECB is widely expected to have to "print" as a last resort in order to rescue the euro. If this is the case, the impact of E.U. printing will exacerbate the current currency wars, as no country wants a strong currency. Until now, the European Union has been wary about printing money. If they become a major player in the currency wars, they will just add gasoline to the fire by speeding up global money issuance and lowering gold's put value. Gold, unlike many other assets, does not produce income or even promises to provide money at any point in the future. As a result, any profit from gold is solely based on its price fluctuation.
