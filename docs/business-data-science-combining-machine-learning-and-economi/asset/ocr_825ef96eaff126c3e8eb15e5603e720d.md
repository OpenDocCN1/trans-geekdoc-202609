$$\prod_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{p}\left(y_{i} \mid x_{i}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{N}\left(y_{i} ; \mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime} \boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^{2}\right)=\left(2 \pi \sigma^{2}\right)^{-\frac{n}{2}} \exp \left[\frac{-1}{2 \sigma^{2}} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime} \boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{2}\right]. \tag{2.20}$$

Using Equation 2.17, this leads to the following deviance:

$$\operatorname{dev}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\right) \propto \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime} \boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{2} . \tag{2.21}$$

Here, the $\infty$ symbol denotes “proportional to”\u2014in other words, ignoring multiplicative and additive terms that do not depend upon $\boldsymbol{\beta}$.7

Looking at Equation 2.21, we see that to minimize the deviance we need to minimize the *sum of squared errors*. The deviance minimizing estimates for $\boldsymbol{\beta}$\u2014the MLE\u2014are exactly the *least-squares* estimates. Thus, “standard” or normal linear regression is a synonym for the common procedure of ordinary least-squares (OLS) regression.8

Given that deviance minimization for a linear regression is the same as least squares regression, why bother to introduce the extra concepts of likelihood and deviance? It's because these concepts apply also to alternative probability models\u2014like the model behind logistic regression. For binary response with probabilities $p_{i}=\mathrm{p}(y_{i}=1)$, the likelihood is

$$\operatorname{lhd}=\prod_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{p}\left(y_{i} \mid \mathbf{x}_{i}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n} p_{i}^{y_{i}}\left(1-p_{i}\right)^{1-y_{i}} . \tag{2.22}$$

Using our logistic regression equation for $p_{i}$, this becomes

$$\operatorname{lhd}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\exp \left[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime} \boldsymbol{\beta}\right]}{1+\exp \left[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime} \boldsymbol{\beta}\right]}\right)^{y_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{1+\exp \left[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime} \boldsymbol{\beta}\right]}\right)^{1-y_{i}} . \tag{2.23}$$
