$\mathrm{d} \mathrm{ev}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=-2 \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i} \log \left(p_{i}\right)+\left(1-y_{i}\right) \log \left(1-p_{i}\right)\right)$
$\begin{gathered} \cong \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\log \left(1+e^{\chi_{i}^{\prime} \beta}\right)-y_{i} . \chi_{i}^{\prime} \beta\right] . \end{gathered}$

The *residual deviance*—say, $D=\mathrm{dev}\left(\hat{\beta}^{\prime}\right)$—is the deviance for the fitted model as in Equation 2.21 or 2.24. It is what glm has fit $\hat{\beta}^{\prime}$ to minimize. The *null deviance*—say, $D_{0}=\operatorname{dev}(\beta=0)$—is the deviance for a “null” or basic model, ${ }^{9}$ where all of the $\beta_{j}=0$. That is, the null deviance corresponds to the model where you don’t use $\chi$ and instead just set $\hat{\rho}_{i}=\bar{\rho}$. 

+   - $D_{0}=\Sigma\left(y_{i}-\bar{\rho}\right)_{2}$ in linear regression;
- $D_{0}=-2 \Sigma\left[y_{i} \log (\bar{\rho})+\left(1-y_{i}\right) \log \left(1-\bar{\rho}\right)\right]$ in logistic regression.

The difference between $D$ and $D_{0}$ is due to information contained in the covariates. An important metric of how tightly your regression fits is the *proportion of deviance explained by $\chi$*, commonly labeled the $R^{2}$:

$\begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} R^{2} & =\frac{D_{0}-D}{D_{0}}=1-\frac{D}{D_{0}} . \end{aligned} \end{equation*}$

This measures how much response variability you are able to model as a function of the regression inputs. In these two examples,

+   - Spammy: \$\begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} R^{2} & =1-\frac{1549}{6170}=0.75 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}$ and
- ojreg: $\begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} R^{2} & =1-\frac{13975}{30079}=0.54 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}$,
