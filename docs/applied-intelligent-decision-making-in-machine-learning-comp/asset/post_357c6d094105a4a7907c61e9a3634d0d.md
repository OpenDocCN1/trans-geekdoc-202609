## 9.2A Section of DT model.

Sections of the validated DT model are shown in Figures 9.2a and 9.2b. Negligible MSEs can be observed at each internal and leaf node, and it is suitable to compete with the other three ML models. The variables $X_0$ to $X$, within the internal nodes tally with the feature input dataset; the first feature “private sector” is $X_0$, “GDP” is $X_1$, “physical investment” is $X_2$, “population” is $X_3$, “trade” is $X_4$, “urbanization” is $X_5$, “energy use” is $X_6$, and “FDI” is $X_7$.

The RF model with the parameter combination of a maximum tree depth of 9, with minimum samples for a leaf node as 1, and with the number of estimators/trees at 250 had the highest MCV score of 0.984858, attained after evaluating all 1250 RF models from the grid search with a ten-fold cross-validation process. Corroborating the findings from the DT model, higher tree depths are inversely related to the minimum samples required for a leaf node. For instance, in Figure 9.3, the maximum number of tree depth 9 corresponds to a minimum sample of 1 required a leaf node.

## 9.3.4 DEVELOPMENT AND VALIDATION OF SVR MODEL

After performing the grid search with ten-fold cross-validation, 1260 SVR models were developed. With hyperparameters of a C of 50.0, a gamma of 0.001, and an RBF kernel reached the highest MCV score of 0.967804. Considering the illustrations of more than three features in 3D chart, we show separately for each kernel the MCV scores in association other hyperparameters (Figure 9.7). During the grid-search process, it can be observed that an increase in the gamma does not have a significant influence on the linear and polynomial SVR models.

On the other hand, a significant inverse relationship is seen between the gamma and the MCV score of the RBF models. This is because the gamma regulates the influence of features on the decision boundary. Hence, a large gamma could result in overfitting, and no amount of C could avoid it.

## 9.3.5 Performance Evaluation of Model

We compared the amount of variance that each validated model explained (Figure 9.9). The DT model explained almost 100% of the observed variance, putting its R² at 99.71%, and having its data points almost perfectly fitted on the regression line. The RF model followed closely with 99.14. The XGBoost model also accounted for 98.88% of the observed variance around its mean. The SVR model had the lowest R² in this study, its ability to explain 97.42% of the observed variance leaves less than 3% of the variance accounted for the model.

In terms of accuracy, the tree-based models had the lowest MAPE and RMSE, as shown in Table 9.2. In all, the DT model produced the most accurate predictions. On the other hand, the kernel-based model, RBF-SVR, had comparatively higher errors. This could be observed in Figure 9.8 as the SVR model only passed through/near a few of the test data points.
