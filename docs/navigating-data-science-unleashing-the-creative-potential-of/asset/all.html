Abstract

Exploring the game-changing potential of the data science and artificial intelligence (AI) in the healthcare sector, this chapter specifically looks into the use cases to develop practical guidelines for the industry to implement the technology at a higher level. By analysing the use cases of data science and AI in the healthcare industry, the chapter identifies key areas where the combined use of these technologies is bringing superior benefits to the stakeholders. These cases then become a base for the development of practical guidelines that can be adopted by the healthcare industry to adopt, integrate and intensify the use of technology in relevant areas of business performance. The chapter concludes with the fact that adoption of the holistic approach is required for the long-term adoption of the data science and AI in the healthcare sector. The study also emphasises close collaboration between industry and policymakers so that the process of technology is adoption can be sustained for a longer period of time.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence (AI); collaboration; data science; healthcare sector; policymakers; stakeholders

1. Introduction

The transformative potential of data science and artificial intelligence (AI) has just begun to find mention across the academic world and corporate sectors. Technology is enabling businesses to make sweeping changes in their products and services and helping them to deliver superior value to the target market. From the automobile industry to the FMCG sector and the consumer durable industry to exports, the use of data science and AI is opening new avenues of growth and opportunities for the industry players. Against this specific background, this chapter explores the transformative potential of data science and AI for the healthcare industry. The healthcare sector is very important not only in terms of its contribution to the economy but also in ensuring that the masses continue to receive quality healthcare services at affordable prices. There is no doubt about the role of technology in guaranteeing that quality healthcare services can be offered to people without charging very high prices. Data science and AI has a great potential that can be realised by the healthcare sector, and by integrating the technology in its functional department as well as operational mechanisms, the sector can utilise the technology to achieve new heights of growth and profitability. Another important facet of data science and AI is its crucial role in catalysing innovation for the industry. Technology can be used to come up with different kinds of products and services that can help the entire healthcare sector to come up with new methods and mechanisms for delivering superior healthcare services to patients. Such is the large scope of applications related to generative intelligence in the healthcare sector that businesses across the verticals are now investing in data science and AI to come up with products specifically related to healthcare management (Aggarwal et al., 2020). The chapter is organised into the following sections: the use cases related to data science and AI in the healthcare sector have been discussed that is followed by the development of the conceptual framework that can help the industry holders to use the technology in healthcare practices. The Development of the Framework is then followed by the Conclusion and Discussion while the authors also detail the important implications related to the use of data science and AI in the healthcare industry. The study has important implications for all the stakeholders in the healthcare ecosystem, and by focusing on the specific benefits related to the value chain of the industry, this chapter will set the tone for future research (Bisam, 2024). Researchers and academicians are likely to benefit greatly from the study and can utilise the chapter in order to come up with innovative practices for making sure that they deliver superior patient care services to the masses. The research will also contribute to the cause of making superior healthcare services available to the masses and by implementing and integrating data science and AI in the day-to-day working of the healthcare industry; the significant benefits can be reaped by the concerned stakeholders.

2. Data Science and AI in Healthcare: Use Cases

2.1 Screening Patients

Data Science and AI is increasingly finding used in screening of the patients in hospitals. The technology is proving very successful and instrumental in reducing the load of the practitioners so that they can strategically invest their time in taking care of the patients. With the help of generative AI, the various images related to the medical records of the patients can be analysed quickly, and accordingly, the data can be forwarded to the doctors for further analysis and treatment procedures (Bughin et al., 2017). The fast completion of the preliminary process is of critical importance as it can help the doctors and practices to deliver the patient care services without any kind of unnecessary delay. It is also important to understand that the inclusion of the technology has also made the process more cost-efficient which means the vision of delivering affordable healthcare services to all can be realised quickly and efficiently. Another significant benefit of using technology in screening patients is the fast processing as unlike human beings that technology do not require any kind of a break or rest period. This means patients can be continuously screened by the technology, and accordingly, analysis is offered to the doctors for taking the remedial actions. There are many machines to which the integration of data science and AI can be done, and satisfactory results on the Diagnostic front can be achieved. The potential of making errors also happens to be quite low in the case of data science and AI, and by ensuring that the right kind of data is fed into the machine, the optimum results can be obtained from the technology (Dhiman et al., 2018). More and more hospitals are now investing in data science and AI to come up with the right kind of a diagnostic techniques and mechanisms, and it is just a matter of time before we start witnessing that technology becomes a means of operations related to patient diagnostics and treatment procedures. By simultaneously increasing the efficiency and effectiveness of diagnostic procedures, the use of data science and AI can also play a crucial role in furthering the concept of affordable healthcare in the world. Especially when it comes to the developing and under-developed economies, the role of technology in providing good healthcare services is very crucial. These countries require a proper investment in the healthcare infrastructure, and by using the technology, the cost efficiency related to the healthcare management practices, healthcare services and allied issues can also be tackled effectively. The role of policymakers becomes also very important in encouraging the use of technology in the healthcare industry. By promoting new investment in the sector and encouraging technology developers, entrepreneurs and allied players in the ecosystem, renewed progress can be achieved in the healthcare industry (Dorn, 2024).

2.2 Vaccine Discovery

The large number of iterations are required before formerly a vaccine is introduced in the market. These trials are very costly and by making use of the generative AI, pharmaceutical companies and researchers can save a lot of money and resources. The technology uses offered them the opportunity to conduct these iterations in a cost-effective and efficient manner. The insightful information offered by the data science and AI can also help in trials and by correlating the results of different kinds of vaccine trials the technology can help further bring down the cost factor. The industry has already started using data science and AI in the vaccine discovery process, and while using the technology in different kinds of procedures, the overall time and cost related to drug discovery will definitely going to be shorter than before (Floreano & Wood, 2015). The machine learning aspect of data science and AI is very helpful in the vaccine discovery phase, and training the machines on identifying the ideal candidates for the trials of the vaccines and then correlating the results of how the trials have brought a positive change is very important and significant for the vaccine discovery process. The technology also very useful in managing the impact of the new vaccines, the data and analysing its impacts, the data science and AI can be widely used for preserving the data management related to the important vaccine discovery for the future too. The process related to cleaning the data and generation of the various queries related to the possible impact of the new vaccines on health can also be preserved with the help of generative AI. Especially when it comes to the decoding process related to various biomedical processes, the usability of the data science and AI is very crucial. In fact, the utility of data science and AI in discovering new drugs and vaccines is holistic and encompassing. From identifying the molecules related to the targeted diseases to finding the right candidate for administration of the vaccine under the trial and tracking the results with the purpose of preserving the information for the years to come, all things can be done very efficiently with the help of the generative AI. The primary benefit related to the use of technology in drug and vaccine Discovery is a reduction in the cost as many research reports have predicted that the then use of data science and AI can bring down the cost to one-tenth of the total amount required as an investment to come up with a new vaccine (Fox, 2024).

2.3 Automating Administrative Processes

In comparison to the other sectors, the automation enabled by the data science and AI in healthcare is very critical for improving the overall delivery experience to the patients. Speed of processing the requirements to refer the patient to the doctor can become very critical and even decide the fate of the entire treatment procedure. It also makes sense as the focus of the healthcare management services must be on delivering superior patient care rather than focusing on completing files, managing bills and other associated aspects related to the healthcare sector. By achieving better efficiency in the administrative processes, data science and AI can play a crucial role in enhancing the overall diagnostic and treatment procedures for patients (Ghosh, 2024). The automated process can also be related to gathering the data and information of patients, and by combining it with the analysis of their present conditions, a complete record related to particular patient can be made for reference purposes. The sharing of the report among the participating stakeholders in the healthcare ecosystem will then lead to the creation of a dynamic and vibrant healthcare sector. The need or to meet processes and treatment procedures is also being felt to divert the critical human resources on other important parameters of the healthcare system (Hurley & Adebayo, 2017). The utilisation of the manpower in the hospital must be done in a judicious manner, and by automating healthcare features and systems with minimum human intervention, the enhancement in the efficiency of the system can be achieved in a significant manner. The automation of data collection, analyses and processing is another significant benefit of generative AI. Of specially, the ability of the technology to extrapolate the data for to predicting future business scenarios is quite commendable. The current crunch of manpower that the World Health Care ecosystem is experiencing today is very acute. According to the leading authority in the field of healthcare ecosystem, it is estimated that around 30 million people are required to fill management positions in hospitals, dispensaries and infirmaries in order to provide satisfactory healthcare services to the targeted people. This number is bound to increase in the coming times with the population expected to rise and reach the level of 9 billion by the end of 2050. In order to offer quality and affordable healthcare services to such a number of people across the globe, technology adoption is a prerequisite and primary criterion to focus upon by all players in the healthcare ecosystem (Kopalle et al., 2020). The use of data science and AI can also be done to issue different kinds of statements required by both regulatory bodies and customers along with filling out records of the patients in relation to their medical conditions. These are things in addition to the enormous utility of data science and AI in digitising the records that can be used for a whole lot of purposes by both industry players and regulatory organisations.

2.4 Remote Operations

The use of data science and AI is increasingly finding favour among stakeholders, thanks to its capability to offer remote assistance to patients. This utility is specifically significant for elderly people who find it difficult to commute to the hospitals for their appointments with healthcare professionals. Also, people who are suffering from acute diseases which leave them almost in still positions can also utilise the technology to get remote assistance from the doctors and physicians. With the help of data science and AI and IoT, the dream of receiving quality healthcare services is fast becoming a reality (Olsen, 2024). This aspect of getting assistance remotely is also proving beneficial for countries in developing and underdeveloped economies. The small towns and villages in these countries do not have quality healthcare services, and by using advanced technology such as telemedicine applications, the patients get a chance to have treatment from the best doctors in the world. This also saves time for the practitioners by allowing them to offer treatment services to a large number of people without actually needing to visit them in person. By combining the generative aspect of AI with natural language processing (NLP) models, desirable progress can be achieved in delivering quality healthcare services to target patients. The primary advantage of these telemedicine applications is their round the clock availability, which is a big plus for patients (Pal & Shalender, 2021). The best thing about remote assistance is that not only physicians or doctors offer medical services but people associated with the medical fraternity are also able to offer these services to patients. Nurses, junior doctors and attendants can use the telemedicine services to offer assistance to patients worldwide. The concept of virtual nurses is fast gaining prominence across the world is also natural extension of the generative AI. The critical role of the data scientists in making the processes more efficient and effective is also recognised across the globe. The data science has proved instrumental in bringing the AI revolution in the healthcare sector, and by offering the collection, analysis and evaluation of the relevant data, the healthcare sector is becoming increasingly effective in treating patients cost-effectively and affordably. The significance here lies in integrating data science with data science and AI as the combination is the one that has the transformative capability to change the very face of the global healthcare sector (Pelley, 2024). The cost associated with travelling and bringing the patients in person for the treatment process can also be significantly decreased with the help of data science and AI technologies. Another important point to note is that all these revolutions are catalysed by the availability of high-speed internet, and by ensuring that the connectivity remains available across the globe, policymakers can ensure that the healthcare sector will continue to benefit from the combined capabilities of data science and AI.

3. Development of Holistic Framework

6. Imperative Analysis

International reports published by the survey conducting agencies help measure the nation's effectiveness of the S&T policy by widely accepted concepts, guidelines, and surveys globally. Though the Indian STI reforms' effectiveness can be analyzed by the R&D, patents, and publications output, these conventional indicator matrices record innovations from the formalized sectors only. The Indian survey conducted at the identified first or second-tiered institutions and agencies fails to cover the innovations and advancements in the informal sectors (Charmes et al., 2016). Sizeable hidden indicators do not make it to the surface as most of the Indian workforce is still engaged in the informal economy (Kraemer-Mbula & Wunsch-Vincent, 2016), contributing to a significant chunk of the nation's GDP. In addition, many non-quantifiable aspects do not get addressed and absorbed in these conventional measurements. Knowledge and sector-specific best practices at an operational level are aspects of innovation that are not noticed in any survey and traditional matrices (Bonnet et al., 2019). Lack of identifying such innovations, measures for standard connect, and official medium to nurture these skills make it challenging to capture the workers' in-house knowledge, informal networks, and ability to adapt or imitate formal network knowledge as per their requirements (Basole, 2014). Though the National Innovation Foundation (NIF) identified more than 300,000 innovations, many such best practices are yet to be unearthed. On the other side, the institutions exposed to the procedures showed many patents, publications, and innovations with very little quotients of practical utility. Several instances brought out that the Indian S&T policy prioritized R&D for developing innovative solutions. However, the innovations from rural areas and unorganized sectors were ignored, thus ignoring the prevailing innovations and inherited knowledge.

On the one side, the Indian Government promotes grassroots innovations, frugal innovations called Jugaad, generally belonging to the informal sector (Sharma & Kumar, 2019; Sheikh, 2014), and the lack of where-with-all to formalize these innovations for registration (Bhaduri & Kumar, 2011; Jain & Verloop, 2012). Since these innovations are constrained to specific problems and realized with local resources, they lack patenting novelty with immediate sophisticated utility. The meager incentive structure and payoff of such innovations do not extend motivation to innovate further. Hence, conventional S&T indicators do not record these innovations. Integrating frugal innovations into formal S&T indicators encounters a significant hurdle. As the patenting procedure is absent in the informal sector, difficulties are being observed in verifying the frugal innovations for their applicability and authenticity. There exists a challenge to ascertain their application on a larger scale. Indian IPR agencies and conventional registration methods have not reached the informal sectors and thus have missed the count of all such innovations, failing to acknowledge them. Cohesively, the traditional innovation metrics leave out the genuine count of the innovations (Shekar & Paily, 2019). The Indian industry-specific investment figures do not translate to the befitting outputs in S&T indicators. Though the government focus remains in the sectors, i.e. defence, mining, fuels and engineering, the private sector investments are relatively low. The private players emphasized prioritizing pharmaceutical, FMCG, biotechnology, agriculture, etc. sectors where government share is negligible, and the IPR statistics fail to cover any strong inferences. Although the public and private partnerships have helped informal innovations to be on board, “Indian Jugaad” – ground level – innovations are not reaching into formal sectors. It is highly recommended to build “Innovation Hubs” in rural areas to promote and streamline the IPR processes. Making amendments to existing government policies would definitely help advocate, govern and align the informal sectors with the formal sectors of innovations.

7. Conclusion

The STIP indicators are largely limited to R&D support, i.e. government and private player investments in R&D and measuring the quantifiable outcomes in patents, trademarks and research publications. Innovation must be considered beyond the enterprise-level incentives. It certainly impacts the increased revenues and market share. The entire issue of the S&T assessment highlights a potential that needs to be unearthed. The latest programs run by the Indian Government, i.e., “Make in India” Atma Nirbhar Bharat, are improving the innovative capacity. These programs have attracted sizeable traction among innovators, SMEs, private players and institutions. Hence, there is a need to identify indicators that can map the latest innovation policy, which can directly measure policy investment, newly formulated policies, their effectiveness, resulting impacts and economic returns. In addition, it is essential to add a facet to the conventional metrics that can cover the non-quantifiable, unexplored aspects to reflect a correct picture of innovativeness. Since the S&T activities tend to cluster around established or leading actors and geographical locations, conventional methods are not effective in analyzing the changing landscape in STI. India needs to devise an indicator to map and track innovative clusters in the subcontinent. The comprehensive revamp of the entire system demands de-novo look to promote innovativeness. The very basis of the education system must promote research with complete re-orientation of the youth. The higher education system, colleges and academic institutions must form an ecosystem that facilitates a smooth transition from students to researchers and provides platforms to reach out to the NIF to obtain IPR for innovations. There is a strong need to provide conducive environments with good infrastructure, extend appropriate incentives, and nurture and keep the talent within the country. Measures to groom students and scholars require a relook, which can increase potential national multifold. There is a requirement to explore innovativeness and make it reach the utility sector by fusing academia, private sectors, and government agencies' potential to explore new heights in science, technology, and innovations. An ecosystem at the central level can synchronize the objectives, harmonize the efforts of the stakeholders, and bridge the gaps for speedier growth. The market's response to these choices has implications for the worker's ego, identity, and finances. Gig workers face the challenge of balancing different work identities and dealing with the absence of a distinct work identity. This can be particularly difficult as gig workers often have to manage multiple jobs simultaneously (Ashford et al., 2018). The impact of gig work on worker autonomy and adaptability is not inherent, but rather a political construct shaped by socio-economic circumstances and technological platforms. While certain employees appreciate these advantages, others encounter severe working conditions as a result of their positionalities (Anwar & Graham, 2021). Work-related stress factors, such as conflicting roles, uncertainty, and excessive workload, can greatly contribute to the occurrence of work–family conflict (WFC). These stressors have the potential to extend into an individual's family life, creating challenges in separating oneself from work-related worries. Research has demonstrated that elevated levels of role overload, conflict, and ambiguity are linked to a rise in WFC. When employees are burdened by work-related responsibilities, they may require assistance in fully engaging with their family members, participating in family activities, or fulfilling their caregiving duties. Role conflict in the workplace can greatly affect employees' WFCs. The correlation between WFC and employees' psychological distress has been thoroughly examined in diverse industry sectors. Extended exposure to WFC can result in burnout, emotional fatigue, cognitive fatigue, depersonalization, and diminished personal achievement. Experiencing chronic stress due to the demands of juggling work and family obligations can result in feelings of being overwhelmed, anxious, and tense, which can contribute to psychological distress (Abdou et al., 2024). Gig workers firmly believe that they possess a high degree of work flexibility. Work flexibility encompasses both temporal flexibility and the extent to which employees have the freedom to select their work location and methods. These choices can significantly influence various work and life-related factors, such as work-family balance and emotional exhaustion (Ma, 2018). Therefore, the presence of multiple role identities and excessive work demands can give rise to WFC, ultimately resulting in psychological distress. However, it is worth noting that work flexibility can provide some degree of assistance in this regard. The implementation of additional employment strategies aimed at enhancing individuals' well-being may foster trust in AI.

Gig work's on-demand nature frequently leads to inconsistent employment opportunities, which complicates the ability of workers to establish a consistent income.

An optimal workweek enables employees to effectively manage their work–life balance, resulting in advantages for both their well-being and their family dynamics. Furthermore, employees who can adjust their work schedule and location can work for extended periods without facing difficulties, highlighting the significance of flexibility in attaining a balance between work and family responsibilities (Hill et al., 2001). Low-skilled workers are being impacted by the changing landscape of employment, as they face challenges in attaining a sufficient income and job stability. The enhanced power imbalance resulting from the increased flexibility in the gig economy heightens the vulnerability to exploitation. The economically disadvantaged individuals, who have a preference for full-time employment but are compelled to work multiple part-time jobs, are becoming progressively more susceptible to vulnerability. Sustainable management aims for greater functionality with fewer resources, opening new markets, and enforcing social good regulations. AI can enhance ecological resilience by optimizing resource use, reducing waste, and promoting sustainable practices throughout the product lifecycle. It can identify inefficiencies, improve recycling processes, design sustainable products, and facilitate the transition from linear to circular economy models. AI can also monitor environmental conditions and make informed decisions to protect and enhance ecological resilience (Lavinia, 2023).

5.5 Trust in AI

Trust is an essential and fundamental concept when it comes to the utilization of technology, especially in the realm of AI. Vulnerability is the state of being willing to expose oneself to the actions of another party, regardless of one's ability to observe or regulate that party. Trust plays a crucial role in human–AI relationships because of the perceived risk associated with them, stemming from the intricate and unpredictable nature of AI behaviors. AI is widely regarded as gradually replacing various human occupations and fundamentally reshaping organizational frameworks. The susceptibility of low-skilled and low-cost employees to AI-driven replacement is uncertain in comparison to knowledge workers and top-level managers. Although AI is capable of performing certain human tasks, there is currently no occupation where every single task can be completely replaced by AI. Trust is a fluid concept that can be influenced by the actions of the individual or entity being trusted (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). The complexity of machine learning models makes trust a vital element in user-AI interactions. The growing interest in explainable AI (XAI) arises from the necessity to uphold user confidence. When AI is developed to gain user trust, it can be securely integrated into society (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). The technology acceptance model (TAM) is a conceptual framework that elucidates the behavior of technology adoption among diverse populations. The study initially proposed that perceived usefulness and ease of use are factors that determine one's intention to use a product or service in the future. Subsequent studies have integrated external variables, such as trust, which plays a vital role in the adoption of emerging technologies. Trust is formed through the combination of characteristics, intentions, and behaviors, which fosters mutual reliance and interconnection between individuals or groups. The TAM expands upon existing research on organizational trust and trust in technology as discussed by Choung et al. (2022). The objective of our research is to examine the role of trust in AI as a mediator and the ethical application of AI concerning the psychological well-being of gig workers in food delivery platforms. This is because trust in AI may lead to a more positive ethical implementation of AI for gig work.

5.6 Ethical Approaches in Food Delivery Services

The ethical frameworks for conducting business are based upon principles such as rights, justice, the common good, virtue, and utilitarianism. Rights are centered around the principle of treating individuals as autonomous beings whereas justice pertains to the equitable treatment of all individuals. The concept of the common good centers around the welfare of society, particularly for individuals who are less capable of taking care of themselves. Virtue prioritizes universal principles such as truthfulness and moral uprightness. Utilitarianism is a moral theory that prioritizes the maximization of positive outcomes and the minimization of negative consequences. Within corporate environments, it provides a convenient method for assessing ethical choices, but it may diminish the importance of other stakeholders' concerns and overlook intangible expenses (Arogyaswamy, 2020). Digital technologies, such as AI, are frequently advertised as being difficult to understand because of their ability to make decisions independently. Detractors contend that holding individuals accountable for the outcomes is no longer feasible, while automated decision-making yields fairer and just results. Nevertheless, the driving force behind these technologies is not primarily focused on design but rather on the influence of corporations. Researchers have devised techniques to evaluate and record ethical considerations, indicating that incorporating AI is a deliberate decision (D'Cruz et al., 2022).

5.7 Psychological Well-being of Gig Workers

Well-being in the workplace has expanded to management science and organizational behavior, focusing on employee well-being. It includes psychological and physical conditions like job satisfaction, burnout, and emotional exhaustion. However, some argue it doesn't consider the emotional aspect of work. Maintaining workplace well-being is crucial for personal and professional growth despite challenges like the gig economy and mobile internet usage. According to Wu and Zhou (2023), improving well-being can be achieved by acknowledging the worth of each individual and increasing job autonomy. However, progress in this regard can be impeded by factors such as economic insecurity, uncertainty about the future, and a lack of stable interpersonal support.

Gig workers consistently face distinct challenges in comparison to employees of traditional organizations. Gig workers encounter six challenges: viability, organizational, identity, relational, emotional, and career-path uncertainty (Caza et al., 2022). Research in human resources management emphasizes the significance of security in the well-being of gig workers although there is a lack of consistency in the effects. Advocates contend that greater autonomy in gig work can result in enhanced well-being as conventional employment provides stability but also entails substantial compromises in terms of flexibility and remuneration. Gig workers, such as Uber drivers, value the ability to have a flexible work schedule and the ability to control fluctuations in their income. Reducing barriers to entry and implementing lower tax rates for gig-work platforms can have a positive impact on overall well-being, as suggested by Felix et al. (2023). An individual's quality of life, including work performance and productivity, is greatly influenced by their psychological well-being. It is shaped by emotional experiences, cognitive function, and job evaluation. Psychological well-being is associated with positive emotions, life and work satisfaction, and the absence of negative feelings. Satisfaction and happiness contribute to the enhancement of psychological well-being, which is indicative of an individual's interaction with social structures and conditions (Kim et al., 2023). The psychological well-being of gig workers is the main objective of our research study. Workers' mental health may be significantly affected by the introduction of AI as they may experience fear and a lack of knowledge when using smartphones and this technology. Consequently, we aim to incorporate all ethical best practices for the use of AI in gig work.

6. Conclusion

The ethical use of AI in the gig economy is complex. The task includes data privacy, algorithmic fairness, optimal employment practices, and ecological resilience. Data privacy helps gig workers feel safe and respected, encouraging them to use AI-powered platforms. No matter their background or demographics, algorithmic fairness eliminates biases and treats all workers equally. Gig workers' rights and welfare depend on fair compensation, manageable work schedules, and resources and perks. Ecological resilience is essential to the gig economy. By promoting green practices with AI, gig platforms can contribute to societal goals. This approach builds trust and purpose in employees. AI's ethical use and trust connect predictors to outcome variables. Transparent, equitable, and accountable gig worker rights and welfare practices build trust in AI. This framework aims to improve gig workers' psychological well-being. Gig workers can improve job satisfaction, and overcome stress, by prioritizing ethical AI practices and building trust through transparency, fairness, and respect. This holistic approach improves individual and collective gig economy results.

Ecological resilience is also included in ethical AI discourse to consider AI-driven platforms' environmental impacts. Psychological well-being is a key outcome variable in the framework, shifting the focus from economic or operational metrics to AI's human-centered effects in the gig economy. The framework helps policymakers and industry leaders implement ethical AI strategies. Focusing on AI as a mediator advances trust theory in technology adoption. Abstract

The aim of this study is to examine the ethical conundrums that arise when artificial intelligence (AI) is used to drive decision-making processes in various industries, with a particular emphasis on the ethical implications of employing Big data for decision-making, and also to identify and analyze the ethical issues that arise in various industries at various steps of the algorithm development process due to the usage of AI and Big data in decision-making. Analysis at the data collection stage led to ethical grey areas in the development of machine learning algorithms using biased datasets. Multidisciplinary research and case studies from a range of industries, including healthcare, finance, marketing, and education, were employed in the study process. Some of the main subjects addressed were invasions of privacy, impartiality and inherent bias in decision-making algorithms, the openness and explainability of AI systems, accountability in automated decision-making and the ethical implications of data ownership and control and their effect on data subjects. This research adds to a better understanding of the ethical environment surrounding Big data and AI applications by combining insights from many businesses. It emphasizes how crucial it is to take proactive ethical considerations into account when designing, implementing and governing AI-driven technologies in order to guarantee their responsible, advantageous and most crucially, consensual integration across industries. The study concludes with the consequences and implications of entrusting AI with human lives directly and indirectly and the ultimate question regarding the future of algorithms which govern humans.

Keywords: Big data; automated decision-making; ethical concerns; AI-based technologies; biasedness

1. Introduction

In this decade, machine learning algorithms have advanced at a breakneck speed, and organizations have been trained using vast amounts of available data. Artificial intelligence (AI) has taken over the world, and now the scenario has shifted towards outcomes and predictions that these algorithms are making using Big data. Big data is not only the use of large amounts of gathered data but rather the interactions between various datasets. It comprises technology to handle the data, analyzing data to make claims and the myth that Big data can provide insights with exceptional accuracy. With the increasing relevance and popularity of Big data, it is necessary to analyze whether it is a phenomenon that has the potential to aid rapid, efficient advancements or if the seemingly dystopian future it entails will outweigh its benefits. One of the most anticipated discussions is the role of ethics in Big data. A case where supposedly anonymous data from Facebook were released and de-anonymized, raised questions about the use of public data without consent. Big data can be used by corporations to make decisions that do not take into account the privacy of the individuals whose personal data is being exploited (Herschel & Miori, 2017). Organizations such as Institutional Review Boards are set up as a response, prompting researchers to think critically about the ethical implications of their studies on stakeholders across the board. Questions about informed consent and accountability of data usage are raised. With AI being the buzzword around, organizations are joining the bandwagon but what are the odds of using the data ethically? This study raises striking questions that are not necessarily considered when having discourse regarding Big data usage by organizations. Perhaps the most important concern for the general public is the question of privacy, consent for their data being used and safety in terms of data breaches. So, the three mandatory aspects of data privacy, consent for usage and biases in AI systems are been addressed in this study.

2. Literature Review

2.1 AI and Big Data

The technical field of AI studies how computers exhibit intelligence through perception, synthesis and inference of information, which is different from the intelligence exhibited by people and non-human animals (Ram & Verma, 2023). It entails using algorithms inspired by human intelligence to simulate human-like methods to problem-solving, culminating in the creation of biomimetic robots with lifelike features and actions (Alkurd et al., 2020). Talking about Big data, it is the term used to describe the enormous volume of data that is gathered, saved and examined in order to derive insightful information and make defensible decisions in a variety of industries (Rawat & Yadav, 2021). Big data's exponential expansion has transformed decision-making processes and allowed businesses to use a variety of data sources for operational and strategic planning (Arena & Pau, 2020). Studies reveal a notable surge in Big data publications, emphasizing the multidisciplinary nature of the field and the changing trends from technological challenges to methodological problems and the advancement of AI (Tagay et al., 2023). Big data and AI are entwined in today's technological innovations, with the potential to revolutionize a multitude of fields. Large datasets may be accurately and efficiently analyzed thanks to the combination of Big data analytics with AI approaches like machine learning and evolutionary algorithms (Gomathi Meena & Dharani, 2023). Innovation is sparked and decision-making processes are improved by this integration's ability to extract insightful knowledge and valuable insights from intricate data structures (Gandomi et al., 2023). In addition, the application of AI and Big data to product design methodology transforms conventional methods by fusing real-time data with consumer preferences and market demands to provide customized and intelligent designs (Quan et al., 2023; Rikap & Lundvall, 2021). The convergence of AI and Big data not only boosts productivity but also tackles issues like security, governance and regulatory compliance in the dynamic field of intelligent systems and technologies (Quan et al., 2023).

2.2 Ethical Considerations in AI

The Greek word ‘ethos’, which means ‘character’, is where the word ethics first appeared. Ethics is the branch of philosophy that studies how people behave in social situations, offering logical defences of moral conclusions, identifying what is morally acceptable or unacceptable and differentiating between what is morally just and what is morally unjust (Pan, 2024). The study of reasonable arguments, ethically good or bad, and just or unjust, for the responsible development and application of AI technology is known as AI ethics (Gao et al., 2024). The utilization of Big data by AI raises a number of complex ethical questions that cut across several fields (Schultz & Seele, 2023; Siau & Wang, 2020). Concerns about data privacy, manipulation and misuse are brought up by Big data-driven AI applications, which put people and society at danger (Agbese et al., 2023; Christoforaki & Beyan, 2022). The convergence of AI and Big data in social science research brings up issues with risk management, subject protection, methodological biases and social harms (Hermansyah et al., 2023; Mensah, n.d.; Zohny et al., 2023). The ethical implications of AI in social systems, like social computing, also highlight the necessity of designing with society's interests, vulnerable populations and responsible data collecting and processing in mind (Bleher & Braun, 2023; Choung et al., 2023; Díaz-Rodríguez et al., 2023). These discussions highlight how crucial it is to take ethical issues into account when developing and implementing AI systems that use Big data in order to guarantee their benefits for both people and society at large.

3. Research Methodology

The research methodology used for the purpose is case methodology to comprehend why and how companies extract Big data and use it in decision-making with the help of AI in an ethical way. The ethical questions regarding bias, transparency and accountability of the decision-making processes used to harvest data and make predictions using models were interrogated. It also delved to assess how this can operate in sync with rest of the organizational strategies. For the purpose, mixed methodology was used by considering personal interviews of the top managers, surveying the clients of the company and assessing the impact of AI for decision making and the ethical dilemmas surrounding it. Case studies from different industries like healthcare, finance, marketing and education were referred. A thorough analysis of steps taken by various organizations, industries and governments to address these ethical issues for public purview was conducted through blogs and research articles available. The issues like invasions of privacy, impartiality and inherent bias in decision-making algorithms, the openness and explainability of AI systems, accountability in automated decision-making, the ethical implications of data ownership etc. are analyzed. The information about companies taken as case in point is also synthesized to harness the pertinent ethical issues related to the topic of use of AI in decision-making.

4. Theoretical Underpinning

4.1 Information and Computer Ethics (ICE)

The farfetched concept of information and computing ethics (ICE) was appended by Floridi (2006) with the details of philosophy of information (PI) and correlative information ethics (IE). Digital landscape demands for interrogation of interactions between human and devices with innovative methods. The PI implied the comprehension of digital ecosystem, with ethical contexts, information required and the involved organization for deciphering changed civic interactions and behaviour, whereas the IE denoted the ethical perspective while creating, using or sharing information in the digital ecosystem. IE is crucial for ethical behaviour, data privacy and ethical usage of information in all the decision-making processes (Estelle, 2019).This framework reports the role of AI in defining ethical considerations within information societies, propagating consideration of global ethical issues rather than specific applications in the infosphere (Bruneault & Laflamme, 2021). This framework make basis for most of the studies pertaining the ethical concerns while using AI-based decision-making in a variety of context as discussed in the next section.

4.2 AI for Decision-Making

The presence of biases in AI algorithms is a matter of serious concern because AI algorithms are increasingly being used worldwide for decision-making purposes. Even though AI may be efficient in aiding the process of decision-making, the transparency of the process is debatable. The AI's algorithm can be a black box, and it is not clear how it self-learns, hence lacking in transparency. It can be further proved by the fact that the most popular AI chatbots like ChatGPT and Gemini are closed-sourced and being used in various fields. One person alone can train them on a dataset without the intervention of anyone else. The understanding of process will lay foundation for further discussions. There are multiple stages involved in making policies using AI.

4.2.1 Setting Priorities

In the first stage, it can help the decision-makers identify the most urgent tasks by analyzing large datasets and data collected through various means. It can detect patterns that can guide the decisions. For example, the Victorian government uses AI for advanced text analysis of anonymized data to form health-related policies. Similarly, AI is also being used in France to aid the health sector (Wirjo & Calizo, 2024).

4.2.2 Making Policies for Decisions

In the second stage, AI can directly lead to decision-making. AI's ability to quickly assess large amounts of data and predict future outcomes can help in various spheres. For example, in the economic sphere, the AI framework ‘AI Economist’, created by Salesforce, has been shown to formulate economic policies by predicting the costs and benefits of the possible policies and properly recognizing the target audience. The United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) has also created an AI to predict the implications of trade policies on global trade (Wirjo & Calizo, 2024).

4.2.3 Streamlining Decisions

In the third stage, AI can improve the efficiency of the decision-making process. It can simulate, test and forecast the possible effects of policies. This will help in speeding up the decision-making process. For making informed decisions with the help of AI, it is crucial to understand the sources of Big data and how these data are extracted for analysis purpose as these are the responsible variables which induce the inherent biases and makes the foundation of ethical issues.

5. Harvesting Big Data for Algorithms

Informed decisions are based on actual data that are harvested from a vast audience. Quantitative data from applications such as Instagram, Twitter and transaction services are collected in the form of likes, comments, tweets and other transactions. Qualitative data take the form of surveys, online forums, blogs etc. It is put through various methods of qualitative analysis to yield a quantifiable result. Appropriate data are then extracted and organized into the requisite structure. Now, a machine learning algorithm can be trained to process these data and recognize all possible patterns. These patterns once developed can be utilized to predict a ‘target attribute’. The validity and correctness of the algorithm in target prediction is thoroughly analyzed and tested before market release. Throughout this process, there arise multiple questions regarding the data subject's consent and privacy at every step. More often than not, there is no active information regarding consensual usage of personal information that an individual may wish to keep private. These vast amounts of data have a large market.

5.1 Inherent Biases of Big Data

Big data enables to draw conclusions based on vast amounts of privately or publicly available data. It shifts how one learns and gains knowledge by instigating approaches such as data driven decision-making etc. Currently, various AI frameworks are using them as resource and apply them to specific decision-making problems (Zhuang et al., 2017). The question of inaccuracy and misinterpretation is one of the first concerns that the usage of Big data embodies. It is not true that large data samples are representative enough to be used to make important decisions that affect human lives. The sheer size of Big data can also lead analysts to confuse it with the statistical ideal of a ‘population’, when it is actually a biased sample (McFarland & McFarland, 2015). The fairness of Big data is the first concern in ethical decision-making.

5.2 Big Data Is Not Simultaneous to Better Data as Methodological Issues Remain Relevant Even With Big Data

Using Twitter as an example, it is illustrated how Big data presents challenges related to sample representation and accountability. It also advocates for recognizing the value of ‘small data’. Small data is a strategy that has been remarkably successful, enabling various disciplines to advance in leaps and bounds (Kitchin & Lauriault, 2015). Why, then, must we turn towards Big data entirely? The next focus is on the importance of context while using Big data. To analyze connections between messages and accounts on social media platforms, using data from only publicly accessible accounts is a matter of investigation. In contrast, historical methods used for collecting data about people's relationships through instruments such as surveys, interviews, etc. emphasize the value of understanding personal networks.

5.3 Limited Access to Big Data

It is crucial to understand that access regarding Big data creates a digital divide. Access to proprietary datasets is restricted, with large corporations being the sole authority dictating how their data can be accessed. The skill sets are required to work with Big data, restricted to those with a computational background. The use of Big data induces critical reflection on the implications for future research and technology built on it.

5.4 Big Data–Based Decisions and AI Biases

Organizations utilizing the user's data to make decisions by powering machine learning/AI algorithms has become a significant concern in recent years. The AI can assist in and directly lead to decision-making in various spheres including, but not limited to, the economic, educational, healthcare, tax and social spheres. Within these spheres, it can create decisions for optimization, welfare, fraud detection, personalization, etc. It is common practices of various countries to harvest user data for various purposes, to make predictions and decisions which impact users' lives. The methods used for data collection vary from traditional census surveys to the use of advanced surveillance technologies such as monitoring online activities, tracking mobile phone locations and even using facial recognition as large datasets. The algorithms are employed in various domains, including healthcare, law enforcement and social services, However, using user data to power AI algorithms raises concerns about the potential for biases to be amplified and perpetuated. The algorithms rely on the data they are trained on, and if these data are biased or incomplete, the outcomes produced by the algorithms may also be biased. AI algorithms have been found to exhibit biases related to gender, race, ethnicity and criminal record, among other factors. These biases can have far-reaching implications for users, whom algorithmic decisions may unfairly impact. One example of racial and ethnic biases is AI algorithms used in law enforcement, which have come under scrutiny for disproportionately targeting minority communities. The use of predictive policing algorithms is considered in the case of facial recognition technology. In a study assessing such disparities, it was shown that the maximum error rate for lighter-skinned males is 0.8%; however, the error rate for dark-skinned females can go up to 34.7% (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018). And the datasets are overwhelmingly composed of lighter-skinned subjects. This shows that facial recognition algorithms exhibit higher error rates when identifying individuals with darker skin tones, leading to disproportionate misidentifications and false positives among people of colour. This has significant implications for law enforcement and surveillance practices as misidentifications can result in wrongful arrests and infringements on civil liberties. Another significant example of racial and ethnic biases in AI algorithms pertains to immigration policies and border control. Governments employ AI-driven systems to analyze vast amounts of data to determine immigration eligibility. However, these systems have been found to exhibit biases against individuals from specific racial or ethnic backgrounds, leading to discriminatory treatment in visa approvals, border screenings and immigration enforcement. Minority communities, particularly people of colour, often face heightened scrutiny and profiling based on algorithms that prioritize specific demographic characteristics or perceived risk factors. This in-depth analysis indicates that datasets being used to train AI algorithms are already biased, and the results represent these biases in the form of ethnic, gender and racial disparities. Since data collection and initial test runs are the first step towards releasing AI-based policies, services or products, it can be assumed that the foundations of these algorithms (that will ultimately affect users) are already treacherous.

6. AI in Governance: Ethical Concerns

While AI doesn't directly write legislation, it's increasingly used to analyze data and suggest strategies that shape policies with significant real-world consequences for citizens. It can automate tasks, leading to faster processing of applications, reduced bureaucracy and potentially improved services. The analysis of the policymaking process using AI raises some obvious questions: What are the policies/structures that employ AI algorithms in the global scenario, and how are these policies transforming and affecting citizen lives? Let's explore three current and upcoming policy areas where AI is playing a major role, examining the impacts and public reception. In the criminal justice system, AI algorithms are used for tasks like predicting recidivism rates to determine bail amounts and parole eligibility. Proponents argue for efficiency and reduced incarceration costs. However, critics point out inherent biases in the data used to train these algorithms. For instance, if past arrest data informs the model, it could perpetuate racial profiling, leading to harsher sentences for minorities. This has sparked outrage, with movements like ‘Black Lives Matter’ calling for fairer justice systems untainted by AI bias (Lageson, 2023).

AI-powered ‘smart cities’ initiatives use cameras and sensors to manage traffic flow, optimize resource allocation and even monitor public safety. While proponents hail improvements in efficiency and safety, concerns arise regarding privacy intrusion. Citizens might feel constantly watched, with their every move tracked and analyzed. Debates rage about the appropriate balance between security and individual freedoms (Galič, 2022). Social welfare programs are increasingly incorporating AI for tasks like benefit allocation and fraud detection. While AI can streamline processes and identify fraudulent claims, it can also lead to impersonal and unfair treatment of citizens. Algorithms might mistakenly deny benefits to those who genuinely qualify, creating hardship and frustration. Additionally, some fear such systems could create a social credit system, where government services are tied to a citizen's ‘worthiness’ determined by AI (Ramond, 2024).

By enabling new methods of audience engagement, outcome measurement and value creation, Big data and AI are also revolutionizing the area of public relations (PR). It is being used for PR campaigns by developed economies such as the United States, Japan and the United Kingdom, which raises ethical and legal questions. The collection and analysis of large volumes of personal data for targeted campaigns raises ethical questions about consent and privacy (James, 2024). The application of AI in healthcare and medical education is equally expanding, making ethical application concerns more crucial than ever. AI enhances medical procedures including imaging, prognosis, diagnosis support and therapy recommendations for human health. The ethical and societal concerns surrounding the use of AI in healthcare are similar to those surrounding the use of personal data, function automation, reliance on assistive medical devices and ‘telehealth’ (Leimanis & Palkova, 2021). AI algorithms are being used by American healthcare providers to help them make judgements about patient care, including which patients need special attention or access to medical privileges. Researchers at UC Berkeley find indications of racial prejudice in algorithms (Obermeyer et al., 2019). Big data analytics and AI have been employed by fintech companies to evaluate vast amounts of data from several sources and generate independent recommendations or assessments (Li et al., 2022). By combining AI and Big data, fintech companies may offer more personalized financial services, improve operational efficiency and reduce costs (Ashta & Herrmann, 2021). But there are also privacy and ethical challenges with the combination of AI with Big data, including those related to ownership, control, justice, transparency, bias and discrimination (Hermansyah et al., 2023). Lack of transparency in AI-driven credit score or loan approval procedures can unfairly disadvantage some people, affecting their opportunities and financial stability (Laksamana et al., 2023).

The recruitment process has been completely transformed by the use of AI and Big data by enterprises. AI systems can provide a proficient automated hiring system that businesses may utilize to choose the most qualified applicants based on their qualifications. In video interviews, many companies are moving towards algorithms that score candidates based on several attributes (Li et al., 2021). However, several recent applications have ethical problems that violate equality laws; it is apparent that the hiring process is unjust and discriminatory. When it comes to hiring, unconscious racism, ageism and sexism are major factors. Acknowledging and minimizing these prejudices is essential (Gonzalez et al., 2022). These are just a few examples of how AI is shaping policy. It has a greater impact on the citizens' lives. Algorithmic bias can lead to discrimination in areas like loan approvals, job applications, and even criminal justice. Citizens from marginalized communities often bear the brunt of these biases. Many AI-driven policies lack transparency. Citizens often don't understand how decisions are made, making it difficult to hold authorities accountable for unfair outcomes. AI-powered surveillance raises concerns about individual privacy and the potential for misuse by authorities.

7. Findings: Harnessing Ethical Ecosystem for AI-Based Policies

AI's influence on policymaking is undeniable. While it offers potential benefits like improved efficiency, its impact on citizens' lives can be profound. Addressing concerns about bias, transparency and privacy is crucial. Open dialogue, public scrutiny and robust regulations are essential to ensure AI remains a tool for good, not a force for social division and control. By harnessing the power of AI responsibly, we can build a future where technology empowers citizens and fosters a more just and equitable society. For these reasons, certain measures must be enforced at various levels to ensure that AI is used responsibly and well within constitutional limits. It is necessary to investigate the structures that limit the usage of AI-based algorithms for policymaking purposes, their current domains and whether these bodies are successful in their goals of achieving safe and accountable AI usage. AI frameworks regulating policymaking are still in the initial stages of development in most governments and organizations who intend to deploy AI algorithms.

7.1 Organizational Level

The large corporations such as IBM have appointed an ‘AI Ethics Council’ consisting of various IBM stakeholders to review and make decisions regarding various policies and products (IBM, n.d.). Although information regarding the AI Ethics Board has been made publicly available, there is scarce information regarding the process and capacity in which these board members are appointed the decision-making authority. On the other end of the spectrum, it is highly interesting to note that Microsoft has recently dissolved their ‘ethics and society team’ that ensured responsible decisions revolving around their AI services and tools (Newton, 2023), opting to instead retain an ‘Office of Responsible AI’. The report claims that the changes suggested by the ethics team to protect the interest of consumers were discarded by Microsoft. These statements lead us to believe that ethical boards in such corporations do not have a final authority on decisions regarding AI products. Consequently, these boards do not hold much importance when it comes to ensuring responsible and ethical implementation of AI; hence, it can be speculated that organizations have passive ethical boards as a scapegoat to disperse questions of accountability and responsibility in the development of AI products. It also provides a certain sense of relief and satisfaction to the consumer in simply knowing about the existence of such ethical boards, as the average customer would not perform a deep delve into the actual power that is possessed by these boards.

7.2 National Level

Moving towards a bigger picture, a publicly available document, ‘Executive Order 13960’, designed for the regulation of AI products and services in the United States states that AI is encouraged within the limits of law, order and safety of the citizens. Its policies also address questions of responsibility that are to be upheld when government policies using AI algorithms are deployed (Federal Office of the President, 2020). However, these rules do not make explicit in what manner exactly are agencies required to present monitoring records, ensuring transparency in their process. The consequences for not complying with these policies are also not made explicit. Towards the end of the document, it is also stated clearly that this order does not apply to AI being used in the defence department, providing no further explanation for the same. Even if large governments like the United States are ensuring AI is getting deployed within certain limits, these regulations fail to ensure transparency to the citizens (instead of just the government) who are the subjects of the algorithms. AI systems that process the personal data of EU residents are impacted by the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which establishes a standard for data privacy in the EU. In particular, these regulations' emphasis on permission, data minimization and the right to explanation are pertinent to the transparency and accountability of AI (Patel, 2024).

7.3 Global Level

At an international level, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which is a transnational body with more than 30 member countries, has set up OECD.AI. OECD.AI has been described as a forum to shape trustworthy AI (Aaronson, 2022). It has also set up AI principles that require the member countries to adhere to. Unfortunately, there is no way to ensure that it can encompass a large number of member nations ready to comply with these principles as the decision to join OECD is also influenced by multiple sociopolitical factors and international relations amongst the member nations. It can be said that there is no exhaustive set of rules applying to all countries on the map that protects citizens from the misuse of AI and holds bodies accountable while ensuring a transparent process. The United Nations is a large international organization which has yet to come up with a strategy for this problem, apart from principles ensuring limits on AI within its systems.

8. Conclusion

Now attention can be focused on the direct implications and consequences of the unbridled usage of AI-based algorithms and what the future of AI in governance can entail through detailed examples and attractive features that AI has introduced in lives. The digital age is an algorithmic archipelago, a vast landscape where algorithms shape the experiences whether liked or not. Personalization is a way to provide individual users with the information and services that is relevant to them. While it is desirable, AI algorithms raise concerns about giving them so much control over our lives. Algorithms curate information based on past behaviour, creating what we call ‘filter bubbles’ that reinforce existing beliefs (Samuels, 2012). Many social media platforms, like Facebook and Instagram, promote engagement by recommending content which more often than not confirms the users' bias (Leonidas et al., 2017). This can lead to dichotomy in the society which is undesired. For instance, in the 2016 US elections, filter bubbles may have contributed to the spread of misinformation as a result influencing the voters (West & Bergstrom, 2021). They can also inherit and amplify social biases because of the data that they were trained on. For example, facial recognition software has been known to be more accurate for fair skin tones, potentially leading to discriminatory policing practices. Now whether that was because the data which the model was trained on had more fair skin toned people is a completely different thing, but in the end, this is one of the many challenges we would be facing. Nowadays even resume screening is done using these algorithms, and if we aren't careful enough the algorithm might just be filtering out candidates on the basis of something totally unrelated to the job. This raises concerns about equal opportunity and even potential lawsuits based on algorithmic bias. As the algorithms become more sophisticated, they may even start dictating the choices we make even if they weren't designed to do so. The so-called ‘Recommendation Systems’ can nudge us towards specific products and services, restricting our options without us knowing. As societies become increasingly dependent on algorithms, there's a growing need for transparency. Understanding how these algorithms work and the data they are trained on is crucial for their accountability. There are many ongoing efforts to develop regulations that promote transparency and fairness in algorithmic decision-making. For example, the European Union's GDPR grants individuals the right to access and control their personal data used in algorithms. Another one of the challenges which we dwell upon are, the spread of misinformation and ‘fake news’ in this digital age. Algorithms which are programmed/not programmed to promote engagement over truth can amplify the impact of such things thereby creating a false narrative. Additionally, the opacity of some algorithms such as ML models or DL models creates difficulties in tracking which content they recommend (Esteves, 2022, pp. 39–59). In conclusion, in this digital age the algorithmic archipelago presents both challenges and opportunities. While things like personalization and convenience are undeniable upsides, the potential downsides can't just be ignored. To have a sustainable growth of this landscape as well, we will have to be mindful of the power we give to these algorithms. Promoting transparency, developing legal frameworks to address the bias generated if any and promoting critical and ethical thinking are crucial to make sure that algorithms serve, rather than subjugate, our humanity. From a research perspective, it can be concluded that apprehensions surrounding Big data can take the form of accessibility, vulnerability, representativeness, etc. while deploying the decision-making capabilities of AI systems that are wholly based on decades of data collection using Big data.

9. Future Dilemmas

It is to ponder who exactly can be held responsible for limitations and consequences of Big data usage – the large corporations that collect data or researchers that use it? And even if somehow accountability is specified, how exactly will the people or systems responsible undo the existing apparatus that employs Big data heavily? How can the general public with little knowledge of these systems be reassured of correct usage of their data? These are the questions that individuals and organizations with the necessary resources and knowledge (and hence power) are still unwilling to answer.

An LLM program responds to user interaction. It fails to independently explore its latent space. The value of the results will rely on the researcher's expert evaluation, which is also responsible for designing the search. LLMs are not actually “ideas” but rather the results of an investigator's subjective research question. They are considered academic contributions only when they are published in a journal or pass peer review. Consequently, it can be inferred that an academic writer who use Google Bard or ChatGPT fulfills the criteria for academic authorship (Sison et al., 2023).

However, it is often the case that individuals who contributed to the creation of the final document are not given due credit. One common practice is to engage the services of “professional proof-readers” based on recommendations from reviewers; however, their acknowledgment in published works is rarely observed (Gill & Kaur, 2023). Typically, formal acknowledgment of peer reviewers is not very common. However, they contribute greater value to a book compared to workshop participants or the mentors of the writers. Although the double-blind peer review approach avoids the purposeful long-term contracting between authors and reviewers, it would be necessary for a mutually advantageous credit exchange (Bakla, 2023).

The accuracy of crediting items produced by LLM in the past appears questionable (Arighi et al., 2024; Kenthapadi et al., 2023; King et al., 2023). An intriguing use of generative AI is its utility as a tool for editing and augmenting written content. In this context, the researcher supplies ChatGPT with paragraphs, and perhaps entire texts, to be reviewed for the purpose of enhancing their readability. It is not practicable to individually cite each AI-assisted alteration in a footnote or author-date format, and it is not clear to readers what utility there would be in doing so (Duncan & McCulloh, 2023).

9.2 The Norm Against Plagiarism

Mentioning plagiarism as a potential objection to the utilization of LLMs in research will suffice to dissuade its adoption among more traditional scholars (Sysoyev, 2024). Minimizing charges can have a similarly detrimental impact on a researcher as committing plagiarism. But knowing the economic roots of the criterion against plagiarism helps we traverse the intricacies of LLM contributions to scholarship (Vinchon et al., 2023). It can be relatively difficult to define plagiarism formally. Plagiarism is a Latin term derived from the word “kidnapping.” Some literalists argue that this phrase refers to the act of kidnapping ideas. According to Dhammi and Ul Haq (2016), plagiarism can be considered a form of kleptomania. This convention is followed in the casual, daily descriptions. O'Connor (2023) highlights that plagiarism is essentially an act of deceit, where one pretends to be the author of someone else's work (O'Connor, 2014). This emphasizes that individuals are the primary victims of plagiarism rather than being mere results of a search query in the vast collection of human knowledge. Heckler et al. (2013) argue that it is imperative to provide a more accurate assessment of the detrimental effects of plagiarism. Plagiarism primarily has contextual impacts and is influenced by traditions. The definition of plagiarism varies among different professions and civilizations. Tran et al. (2018) argues that it is implausible to apply the regulations about plagiarism in academia to different businesses or cultures.

The standard against plagiarism is an extrajudicial technique to preserve intellectual property rights (Singh & Ganapathy, 2018). Plagiarism is neither against the law nor grounds for legal action. Copyright protects only text; ideas are not covered by it. Though they are confined to the sanctions imposed by professional groups, the courts have criticized lawyers for plagiarism, calling it “reprehensible” and “utterly unacceptable” (Masic, 2014; Roig, 2010). In academia and other sectors, this convention has evolved to defend the authorship economy from opportunistic action that would deny holders of their rights to the complete worth of their work. An LLM's ideas are truly their own and whether the LLM has ever encountered a violation of their property rights. Not only is language created by AI unable of matching (nonexistent) formal or informal criteria of plagiarism, but the use of LLMs in academics does not breach the framework of property rights that the rule against plagiarism attempts to safeguard (Berg, 2023; Bevolo, 2022). One major argument against plagiarism is that the plagiarist breaks an intellectual hierarchy. Readers who accept ideas from others are not able to monitor the evolution of such ideas from the outset. Modern neural network AI architecture is well-known for behaving as an opaque “black box” although it can understand the questions and the training data feed (Kandeel & Eldakak, 2024).

Scholars are accountable for developing their theories and arguments in a way that both explains and makes clear where they fit within an intellectual tradition and corpus of literature. The scholar's role and task are evident when considering the importance of opacity in neural network architectures (Gunjan & Zurada, 2021; Kashyap et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2021). They can either utilize an LLM as a tool to investigate a specific field of knowledge or employ an LLM as a guide to assist researchers in acquiring proficiency in that particular field of knowledge. The research paper, as the final outcome, must invariably focus on the specific area and the corresponding academic community. This discusses the ethical responsibility that research academics have toward their academic communities.

10. Future Scope of Generative AI in Academic Writing and Ethics

The potential for creative AI applications in the fields of academic research framework, from idea generation to apply ethical practices in publication is significant. As generative AI advances and gains popularity in several fields, its ability to improve ethical considerations and transform academic writing is predicted to grow. Modern AI algorithms that generate tables and data from structured data, suggest grammar and style, and organize unstructured ideas into coherent arguments can improve research and cognitive writing workflows. This strategy enables the collection of important results and facilitates the rapid identification of vast databases, therefore reducing the need for time-consuming literature reviews. Researchers can enhance their time management skills using these tools. These materials facilitate the enhancement of writing, reading, and speaking skills for non-native English speakers. By tailoring learning experiences, creative AI has the capacity to enhance students' academic performance and facilitate language acquisition. Several strategies to accomplish this objective including translating literary texts, enhancing language proficiency, and implementing grammatical alterations. Employing AI algorithms can assist students in improving their essay writing talents, fostering critical thinking abilities, and obtaining essay samples. Although ethical concerns exist, creative AI has the capacity to automate substantial portions of the writing process, particularly in the realm of instructional writing. This may impede the ability to engage in creative and critical thinking. AI may exhibit bias or errors when it excessively depends on incorrect data or methodologies. The ideas of ethics are vital for use in the classroom. Education is one of the most powerful tools for equalizing society; it is also a sovereign space where critical thinking flourishes. Hence, legislation should impute AI with ethical virtues and make those virtues visible to students as well as teachers. For instance, we could have AI methods that are applied side by side with human decisions to rectify any wrongful opportunities or treatment meted out to groups. It is crucial for human academics and AI systems to interact continuously if Socratic AI-supported research is to maintain the value of human intellectual production. This type of research allows individuals to deepen their understanding of complex subjects, to appraise divergent viewpoints, and even to revise their own ideas. The progress of AI will undoubtedly also lead to new research focused on educational institutions, with data analysis and predictive modeling leading the way. For any person to comprehend accurately the intricate acts of pattern recognition, significant data analysis, or complicated cognitive processes, it would be necessary for them to have an intelligence that surpassed our own in these domains and this is clearly not the case with current AI systems. Despite these limitations, however, many people are concerned about what might happen if such systems are used in important decisions, as they could potentially lead to serious mistakes. In response to these concerns, developers are working on ways to ensure that AI can be used responsibly and effectively alongside human decision-makers. The future of ethical AI in academic writing is here. As AI rises, so too does the risk that it will transform the educational environment in which we inhabit. It could make us more efficient if it can do what we current humans cannot conduct investigations with greater efficacy. And because resolution of issues is at the heart of both creative and analytical AI, these new tools, used responsibly, could enable scholars to see (and be wowed by) the transformative power of such a change.

11. Conclusion

The sudden growth of academia offers both dangers and opportunities for generative AI. Yet, overall, this new academic world presents a wonderful chance to establish the correct (sufficiently rigorous and intellectually honest) use of current technological possibilities especially since many of these technologies are now so user-friendly that even ordinary citizens can work with them. The research that happens in the academy must always be pristine from an ethical standpoint; accordingly, using technology in an unethical way is certainly not an opportunity but a danger. And so, by the same token, using technology correctly and I mean “correctly” in an as-ethical-as-possible way offers another good reason for relying on the academy for the generation of knowledge. Maintaining academic integrity in the collaborative and peer evaluation process is crucial. Rigorous peer evaluation is necessary for proper acknowledgment of scholarly endeavors, and this is even more important when using generative AI to perform tasks that are usually done by humans. A willingness to receive feedback and criticism is a must for academics who want to effectively use AI in their work. Research in AI raises ethical concerns because of its impact on society. If you imagine what kind of “society” a computer can construct based on the kind of data it's given, and on the nature of its “learning” you get some idea of what I'm talking about when I refer to the broader consequences of academic research. Finally, adopting a thorough strategy to reduce risks ensures that AI remains a valuable tool for academics and also ensures that we can ethically harness its capabilities for the betterment of society.

1.1.2 Machine Learning Techniques

ML is a sub-field of AI that learns from the dataset to improve the learning capabilities of expert systems (Górriz et al., 2020). ML-based AI techniques are more adaptive and dynamic need not to be developed and programmed explicitly for every new task (Chen et al., 2008). ML based systems can think and learn the given task using the data related specific domain (Mellit & Kalogirou, 2008). ML techniques are classified into supervised, unsupervised learning and reinforcement learning which are discussed as follows.

  • Supervised learning techniques
  • The supervised learning uses the labeled input data to perform classification and prediction tasks. The labeled dataset consists of labeled input and output parameters and the learning algorithm map the input datapoints with output data to get accurate results. There are two types of supervised learning techniques, namely classification and regression. The classification algorithms are known as classifier which is used to classify the category of labeled input using the training dataset and produces yes/no, spam/not spam and 0/1 as output classes. For example, spam detection, disease prediction, image classification, fraud detection, etc. are classification problems. There are two types of classifiers; binary and multi-class classifier. If the problem has two outcomes like yes/no, true/false, and 0/1, then it is known as binary classifier. Multi-class classifier consists of more than two classes for example types of crops, music classification. SVM is a well-known supervised classifier based on statistical learning theory. It has been applied to solve classification as well as regression problems. The SVM algorithm makes a best line or decision boundary to separate n-dimensional space into different classes. SVM has a significant number of applications in the field of energy prediction and forecasting. The classifiers provide high accuracy and work well with high dimensional space using significantly less memory. A supervised learning algorithm, random forest, is commonly used for classification and regression analysis (Kaur et al., 2022). It is an ensemble learning algorithm that combines multiple classifiers to solve a complex problem and improve the model's performance. The primary focus of random forest is to deal with a large number of values.
  • Decision trees are used for classification and prediction problems. It is flowchart like tree structure that learn from dataset. Decision tree is a classifier in the form of tree which consists of a leaf node and decision node. The leaf node is the target attribute or the outcome of the test and decision node perform some test on the attribute value. K-Nearest Neighbors (K-NN) is a classification algorithm initially used for pattern recognition and statistical analysis as a non-parametric technique. The input consists of K-closest data values which forms a cluster or group. When an unclassified value occurs, it is assigned to a group with close nearest neighbor.
  • Unsupervised learning techniques
  • Unsupervised learning techniques are useful to extract hidden patterns and deduce significant information from the unlabeled data. The commonly utilized unsupervised learning techniques are clustering and dimensionality reduction. The most widely used clustering techniques are K-means, hierarchical clustering, and DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise), which are used for forecasting and classification tasks. K-means is a distance based or centroid based statistical clustering algorithm in which k number of centroids are identified, and each data point is allocated to the nearest cluster. Hierarchical clustering approach creates the clusters by calculating the dissimilarities among data points. It constructs a tree in a top-down (divisive) and bottom-up (agglomerative) manner. DBSCAN is a density-based clustering algorithm used to identify noise in the data by highlighting the denser regions. Dimensionality reduction is an unsupervised learning approach has been used to transform the high dimensional space data to a lower dimension and eliminate redundant features. It has been applied to process large datasets efficiently. Principal component analysis (PCA) and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) are the most popular dimensionality reduction algorithms. PCA applies linear transformation and creates a new dimension to reduce the dimensionality of data. It is used as a preprocessing method while training ML models. LDA preserve the crucial information while reducing the dimensionality of data. LDA can be used to solve multi-class classification problems and design face detection algorithms and disease classification problems.
1.1.3 Deep Learning Techniques

Deep learning is a branch of AI based on the concept on several layers of neural networks. A well-trained neural network model performs very fast as compared to other AI models. Deep learning can perform complex tasks on large datasets and real-world AI applications in various sectors, including energy optimization, automobile, healthcare, manufacturing, etc (Fan et al., 2023). Various deep learning approaches such as artificial neural networks (ANN), convolutional neural networks (CNN), long short-term memory (LSTM), and autoencoders have been used by researchers to solve complex real-world problems. ANNs are computing networks inspired by biological neural networks. An ANN algorithm has the ability to learn from non-linear datasets and fluctuating data values (Mellit & Kalogirou, 2008). ANN consists of three layers: input, hidden, and output. In recent research, the ANN algorithm has been used for predictive analytics, character recognition, anomaly detection, etc. CNN has been widely adopted for image classification, processing satellite images and solving character recognition problems. It consists of several convolutional and sub-sampling layers to process the data that pass through several layers to classify input features. A supervised learning approach multilayer perceptron (MLP) belongs to class of ANN, which converts a set of input values to output value. MLP comprises three layers: input layer, hidden layer, and output layer. There can be one or more non-linear hidden layers (Mellit & Kalogirou, 2008). The perceptron has multiple layers connected using directed graphs. LSTMs is a special type recurrent neural networks (RNN) that can learn from long-term dependencies (Kaur et al., 2024). Its sequential network makes it suitable for time-sequence data prediction, processing text, and speech data. The LSTM comprises various cells, each consisting of input, output and forget gate, which control the flow of information going into and coming out of the cell. It also has a memory cell that keeps the information of previous time steps, contributing to the current cell's output. Autoencoder is a self-supervised learning model widely used in data processing tasks, denoising, feature extraction, and dimensionality reduction. Autoencoders are used to map data to lower dimensional space and transform it back to the original data.
