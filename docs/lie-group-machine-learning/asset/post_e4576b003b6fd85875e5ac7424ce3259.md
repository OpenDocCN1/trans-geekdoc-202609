The local finite partial set in LML is defined as follows:

**Definition 2.13.** Let the sample data set $P$ in the LML be a partially ordered set. If for any $x, y \in P$ and $x < y$, the interval $[x, y]$ is a finite set, then $P$ is called the local finite partially ordered set. If $P$ is a finite set, $P$ is called a finite partially ordered set.

**Definition 2.14.** Let the sample data set $P$ in LML be a local finite partially ordered set, $R$ be a commutative ring with the unit element, and $\mu(x, y)$ be a binary function defined on $P$ and valued in $R$, assuming that $\mu(x, y)$ satisfies the following three conditions:

- (1) For any $x \in P$, there is always $\mu(x, x) = 1$;
- (2) If $x \nleq y$, then $\mu(x, y) = 0$;
- (3) If $x < y$, then $\sum_{x \le z \le y} \mu(x, z) = 0$.

Let $\mu(x, y)$ be a Möbius function defined on $P$ and valued in $R$.

The Möbius function can be used to determine the parameters of the relationship between the states in the learning process. If the two states are equivalent, the function value is 1; otherwise, it is 0.

**Lemma 2.1.** Let the sample data set $P$ in LML be a local finite partially ordered set. $x, y \in P$, if $x < y$, then $d(x, y) < \infty$.

**Proposition 2.1.** The local finite partially ordered set of the sample data set $P$ in LML must have a Möbius function and is unique.

*Proof.* Let the sample data set $P$ in LML be a locally finite partially ordered set. First, we prove that there must be a Möbius function on $P$. We define

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mu(x, x)=1 \text{ for any } x \in P \\
&\mu(x, y)=0 \text{ if } x, y \in P \text{ and } x \nleq y
\end{aligned}
$$

Let $x, y \in P$ and $x \le y$ for the induction of $d(x, y)$ to define $\mu(x, y)$. When $d(x, y) = 0$, $x = y$, and $\mu(x, y) = 1$ as defined above. Set $d(x, y) > 0$. For $z \in P$ and $x \le z \le y$, there is obviously $d(x, z) < d(x, y)$; hence, $d(x, z)$ is defined. Thus,

$$
\mu(x, y) = -\sum_{x \le z \le y} \mu(x, z)
$$

According to the definition of $\mu(x, y)$, it is suitable for (1), (2), and (3). Thus $\mu(x, y)$ is the Möbius function on $P$.

Let $\mu'(x, y)$ also be a Möbius function on $P$. From (1), for any $x \in P$, we have $\mu(x, x) = 1$. Therefore,

$$
\mu'(x, x) = \mu(x, x) = 1 \text{ for any } x \in P
$$

Let $x, y \in P$. If $x > y$, according to (2), we have $\mu'(x, y) = 0$. Hence,

$$
\mu'(x, y) = \mu(x, y) = 0 \text{, if } x > y
$$

We set $x \leq y$. Mathematical induction is applied to $d(x, y)$ to prove that $\mu'(x, y) = \mu(x, y)$. When $d(x, y) = 0$, $x = y$, it is certified that $\mu'(x, x) = 1 = \mu(x, x)$. We set $d(x, y) > 0$. For $z \in P$ and $x \leq z \leq y$, obviously $d(x, z) < d(x, y)$. According to the induction hypothesis, $\mu'(x, z) = \mu(x, z)$. Then,

$$
\begin{align*}
\mu'(x, y) &= - \sum_{x \leq z \leq y} \mu'(x, z) \quad \text{(as } \mu'(x, y) \text{ is suitable for condition (3))} \\
&= - \sum_{x \leq z \leq y} \mu(x, y) \quad \text{(based on the induction hypothesis)} \\
&= \mu(x, y) . \quad \text{(as } \mu(x, y) \text{ is suitable for condition (3))}
\end{align*}
$$

**Proposition 2.2.** Let the sample data set $P$ in LML be a locally finite partially ordered set with the smallest element 0 and $R$ be the commutative ring with the unit element. Let $\mu(x, y)$ be a Möbius function defined on $P$ and valued in $R$. Further, $f(x)$ is a function defined on $P$ and valued in $R$. For any $x \in P$, make

$$
g(x) = \sum_{y \leq x} f(y) \ . \tag{2.3}
$$

Then,

$$
f(x) = \sum_{y \leq x} g(y)\mu(y, x) \ . \tag{2.4}
$$

Instead, let $g(x)$ be a function defined on $P$ and valued in $R$. For any $x \in P$, by (2.4), to define $f(x)$, (2.3) is set up.

*Proof.* Since $P$ is a locally finite partially ordered set with the smallest element 0, the interval $[0, x]$ is a finite set and the sum in (2.3) is a finite sum. Therefore, $g(x)$ defined by (2.3) is reasonable. Similarly, the sum in (2.4) is also finite and

$$
\begin{align*}
\sum_{y \leq x} g(y)\mu(y, x) &= \sum_{y \leq x} \left( \sum_{z \leq y} f(z) \right) \mu(y, x) \quad \text{(substituting (2.3))} \\
&= \sum_{z \leq x} f(z) \sum_{z \leq y \leq x} \mu(y, x) \quad \text{(exchange summation order)} \\
&= \sum_{z \leq x} f(z)\delta_{z, x} \quad \text{(according to condition (3))} \\
&= f(x)
\end{align*}
$$

where $\delta_{z, x}$ is the delta function.

By contrast,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{y \leqslant x} f(y) & = \sum_{y \leqslant x} \left( \sum_{z \leqslant y} g(z) \mu(z, y) \right) & \text{(substituting (2.4))} \\
& = \sum_{z \leqslant x} g(z) \sum_{z \leqslant y \leqslant x} \mu(z, y) & \text{(exchange summation order)} \\
& = \sum_{z \leqslant x} g(z) \delta_{z, x} & \text{(according to condition (3))} \\
& = g(x) \ .
\end{aligned}
$$

Where (2.4) and (2.3) are called the Möbius inversion formulas of (2.3) and (2.4), respectively.

Further, $\mu(x, y)$ is used as the control weight generated by the orbit to guide the generation of the new sample, and $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ are the transform functions of samples $x$ and $y$, respectively.

### 2.1.4 Gaussian coefficients and Gaussian polynomials in LML

The complexity of the objective function of learning depends on its representation. In the ideal case, a representation that matches a particular learning problem should be chosen. Thus, a common preprocessing strategy in machine learning involves changing the expression of sample data, such as

$$
x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n) \mapsto \phi(x) = (\phi_1(x), \ldots, \phi_N(x)) \ .
$$

This step is equivalent to mapping the sample data set to a new space, $F=\{\phi(x) \mid x \in X\}$. Example: the objective function

$$
f\left(m_1, m_2, r\right)=C \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \ .
$$

Given the law of gravitation, consider two objects with mass $m_1$, $m_2$ and distance $r$ to represent gravity. This law is expressed by observable quantities, i.e., mass and distance, but cannot be expressed by linear functions. However, if the coordinate is changed simply as

$$
\left(m_1, m_2, r\right) \mapsto (x, y, z) = \left(\ln m_1, \ln m_2, \ln r\right) \ ,
$$

the expression is given by

$$
g(x, y, z)=\ln f\left(m_1, m_2, r\right)=\ln C+\ln m_1+\ln m_2-2 \ln r
$$

and it becomes a linear relation.

This process proves that simple mapping of the sample data in the input space into another space can simplify the task. The sample data in the input space cannot be separated by a linear function, but it is possible in the new mapping space. This approach usually involves finding the minimal feature set that contains the necessary information about the original property, which is called dimensionality reduction:

$$
x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n) \to \phi(x) = (\phi_1(x), \ldots, \phi_d(x)) \quad d < n \ .
$$

It is useful for both computation and generalisation [10].

We map the sample data in LML to $F_q$. Let $F_q$ be a finite field of $q$ elements, $q$ be a prime power, and $n$ be a nonnegative integer. Make

$$
F^n_q = \{(x_1, x_2, \cdot \cdot \cdot, x_n) \mid x_i \in F_q, i = 1, 2, \cdot \cdot \cdot, n\} \ .
$$

The element $(x_1, x_2, \cdot \cdot \cdot, x_n)$ in $F_q$ is called the $n$-dimensional row vector on $F_q$. Addition and scalar multiplication of the $n$-dimensional row vectors are defined as follows:

$$
(x_1, x_2, \cdot \cdot \cdot, x_N) + (y_1, y_2, \cdot \cdot \cdot, y_N) = (x_1 + y_1, x_2 + y_2, \cdot \cdot \cdot, x_N + y_N)
$$

$$
\lambda(x_1, x_2, \cdot \cdot \cdot, x_N) = (\lambda x_1, \lambda x_2, \cdot \cdot \cdot, \lambda x_N), \quad \lambda \in F_q
$$

$F^n_q$ is the $n$-dimensional vector space on $F_q$, called the $n$-dimensional row vector space.

**Proposition 2.3.** Let $F_q$ be a finite field, where $q$ is a prime power, both $n$ and $m$ are nonnegative integers, and $F^n_q$ is the $n$-dimensional row vector space on $F_q$. Then, the number of $m$-dimensional subspaces in $F^n_q$ is exactly the same

$$
\frac{(q^n-1)(q^n-q)\cdots(q^n-q^{m-1})}{(q^m-1)(q^m-q)\cdots(q^m-q^{m-1})} \ . \tag{2.5}
$$

**Proof.** When $m = 0$, $F^n_q$ has a unique zero-dimensional subspace, which consists of the zero vector $\mathbf{0} = (0, 0, \cdot \cdot \cdot, 0)$. At this time, (2.5) is equal to 1. Hence, the proposition is established at $m = 0$.

Now, hypothesise that $m > 0$. When $n < m$, $F^n_q$ has no $m$-dimensional subspace, i.e., the number of $m$-dimensional subspaces of $F^n_q$ is equal to zero. However,

$$
\frac{(1-q^n)(1-q^{n-1})\cdots(1-q^{n-(m-1)})}{(1-q^m)(1-q^{m-1})\cdots(1-q^{m-(m-1)})} = \frac{(1-q^n)(1-q^{n-1})\cdots(1-q^m)(1-q^{m-1})\cdots(1-q)}{(1-q^m)(1-q^{m-1})\cdots(1-q)} = 0 \ .
$$

Thus, Proposition 2.3 is also established at $n < m$.

Finally, we investigate the situation of $n \ge m > 0$. Let $V$ be any $m$-dimensional subspace of $F^n_q$ and $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \cdot \cdot \cdot, \mathbf{v}_m$ be the basis of $V$. Here, $\mathbf{v}_1$ can be any nonzero vector in $F^n_q$ and $F^n_q$ has a total of $q^n$ vectors; thus, $\mathbf{v}_1$ has $q^n - 1$ possible choices. When $\mathbf{v}_1$ is selected, $\mathbf{v}_2$ can be any vector that is independent of $\mathbf{v}_1$ in $F^n_q$, and there is a total of $q$ vectors that are linearly related to $\mathbf{v}_1$; hence, there are $q^n-q$ possible choices for $\mathbf{v}_2$.
