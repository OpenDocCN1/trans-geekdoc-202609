
**Theorem 2.9.** Let $1 \leq m \leq n - 1$. For any $X \in \mathrm{L}_0(m, n)$, define

$$ r(X)= \begin{cases} \dim X, & \text{if } X \neq \mathbb{F}_q^n \\ m+1, & \text{if } X=\mathbb{F}_q^n \end{cases} \tag{2.21} $$

Then, $r: \mathrm{L}_0(m, n) \to \mathbb{N}$ is the *rank function* of *lattice* $\mathrm{L}_0(m, n)$.

**Theorem 2.10.** Let $1 \leq m \leq n - 1$. For any $X \in \mathrm{L}_0(m, n)$, define

$$ r'(X)= \begin{cases} m+1 - \dim X, & \text{if } X \neq \mathbb{F}_q^n \\ 0, & \text{if } X=\mathbb{F}_q^n \end{cases} \tag{2.22} $$

Then, $r': \mathrm{L}_R(m, n) \to \mathbb{N}$ is the rank function of lattice $\mathrm{L}_R(m, n)$.

Now, the *characteristic polynomial* of $\mathrm{L}_R(m, n)$ is given.

**Theorem 2.11.** Let $n > m \geq 0$. Then,

$$ \chi\left(\mathrm{L}_R(m, n), t\right)= \sum_{k=m+1}^{n} N(k, n) g_k(t) . $$

where

$$ N(k, n)=|\mathbb{M}\left(k, n\right)|=\left[\begin{array}{l} n \\ k \end{array}\right]_q= \frac{\prod_{i=n-k+1}^{n}\left(q^i - 1\right)}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(q^i - 1\right)} \tag{2.23} $$

is the number of $k$-dimensional subspaces in $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ and $g_n(x)$ is the Gaussian polynomial.

*Proof.* Mark $V=\mathbb{F}_q^n$, $L=\mathrm{L}_R(m, n)$, and $L_0=\mathrm{L}_R\left(\mathbb{F}_q^n\right)$. For $P \in L$, specify

$$ \mathbf{L}^P=\{Q \in \mathbf{L} \mid Q < P\}=\{Q \in \mathbf{L} \mid Q \geq P\} $$

and

$$ \mathbf{L}_0^P=\{Q \in \mathrm{L}_0 \mid Q < P\}=\{Q \in \mathrm{L}_0 \mid Q \geq P\} \ . $$

Obviously, $\mathbf{L}^V=\mathbf{L}$. For $P \in \mathbf{L}$, $P \neq V$, according to Theorem 2.8, there is $\mathbf{L}^P=\mathbf{L}_0^P$. Take any $P \in \mathbf{L}_0$, where $\mathbf{L}\left(\mathbf{X}_0^P, t\right)=g_{\dim P(q)}(t)$. Further, $r': \mathrm{L}_R(m, n) \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$ defined by (2.22) is the rank function of $\mathbf{L}=\mathrm{L}_R(m, n)$, i.e., there is a rank function $r'$ on $\mathbf{L}^V$, and $\mathbf{L}^V$ is the lattice with the maximum $\{0\}$ and the minimum $\mathbb{F}_q^n$. Hence,

$$ \chi\left(\mathbf{L}^V, t\right)=\sum_{P \in \mathbf{L}^V} \mu(V, P) t^{r'(0) - r'(P)} \ . $$

Think of $t$ as a given number. Here, $f(x)$ on the left-hand side of the formula $f(x) = \sum_{x \leq y} g(y)\mu(x, y)$ is taken as $f(V)=\chi\left(\mathbf{L}^V, t\right)$, and the right-hand side $g(y)$ is taken as $g(P)=t^{r'(0) - r'(P)} = t^{m+1 - r'(P)}$. Then,

$$ t^{m+1}=g(V)=\sum_{P \in \mathbf{L}^V} \chi\left(\mathbf{L}^P, t\right)=\sum_{P \in \mathbf{L}} \chi\left(\mathbf{L}^P, t\right) \ . $$

Again, we have
$$ t^{m+1} = \sum_{P \in \mathbf{L}_0} \chi(\mathbf{L}_0^P, t) . $$

According to Theorem 2.8, we can see that $\{P \in \mathbf{L}_0 \mid \dim P \leq m\} = \{P \in \mathbf{L} \setminus \{V^\perp\}\}$. Hence,

\begin{align}
\chi(\mathbf{L}, t) &= \chi(\mathbf{L}^V, t) = t^{m+1} - \sum_{P \in \mathbf{L} \setminus \{V^\perp\}} \chi(\mathbf{L}^P, t) \\
&= \sum_{P \in \mathbf{L}_0} \chi(\mathbf{L}_0^P, t) + \sum_{P \in \mathbf{L} \setminus \{V^\perp\}} \chi(\mathbf{L}^P, t) \\
&= \sum_{\substack{P \in \mathbf{L}_0 \\ \dim P > m}} \chi(\mathbf{L}_0^P, t) - \sum_{\substack{P \in \mathbf{L}_0 \\ \dim P \leq m}} \chi(\mathbf{L}_0^P, t) - \sum_{P \in \mathbf{L} \setminus \{V^\perp\}} \chi(\mathbf{L}^P, t) \\
&= \sum_{\substack{P \in \mathbf{L}_0 \\ \dim P > m}} \chi(\mathbf{L}_0^P, t) + \sum_{P \in \mathbf{L} \setminus \{V^\perp\}} \chi(\mathbf{L}^P, t) - \sum_{P \in \mathbf{L} \setminus \{V^\perp\}} \chi(\mathbf{L}^P, t) \\
&= \sum_{\substack{P \in \mathbf{L}_0 \\ \dim P > m}} \chi(\mathbf{L}_0^P, t) = \sum_{k=m+1}^{n} N(k, n)g_k(t)
\end{align}

where the representation of $A$ is given by (2.24).

Now, we discuss the geometry of $\mathbf{L}_0(m, n)$ and $\mathbf{L}_R(m, n)$.

**Theorem 2.12.** Given $1 \leq m \leq n - 1$, $\mathbf{L}_0(m, n)$ is a finite number of lattices.

**Theorem 2.13.** [8] Set $1 \leq m \leq n - 1$, then

-   (a) $\mathbf{L}_R(1, n)$ and $\mathbf{L}_R(n - 1, n)$ are finite geometric lattices;
-   (b) For $2 \leq m \leq n - 2$, $\mathbf{L}_R(m, n)$ is a finite atomic lattice, but not a geometric lattice.

*Proof.* By Theorem 2.8, $\mathbb{F}_q^0$ is the only minimal element, and the atoms in $m$-dimensional subspaces $\mathbf{L}_R(m, n)$ ($1 \leq m \leq n - 1$). Hence, $\mathbf{L}_R(m, n)$ is a finite atomic lattice.

For $X \in \mathbf{L}_R(m, n)$, define $r'$ by (2.22). By Theorem 2.9, $r'$ is the rank function of lattice $\mathbf{L}_R(m, n)$.

When $m = n - 1$, $\mathbf{L}_R(n - 1, n)$ is a finite number of lattices.

(b) Let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ be a base of $\mathbb{F}_q^n$. Because $2 \leq m \leq n - 2$, we have the desirable $U = \langle v_1, v_2, \dots, v_m \rangle$, $W = \langle v_{m+1}, \dots, v_{m+2} \rangle \in \mathbb{M}(m, n) \subset \mathbf{L}_R(m, n)$. It is easy to see that $U \wedge W = \mathbb{F}_q^0$ and $r'(U \wedge W) = 0$. If $m = 2$, then $U \vee W = \{0\}$. If $m \geq 2$, then $U \vee W = \langle v_{m+1}, \dots, v_{m+2} \rangle$. In both cases, there are $\dim(U \vee W) = m - 2$ and $r'(U \vee W) = m + 1 - (m - 2) = 3$. However, $r'(U) = r'(W) = m + 1 - m = 1$. Hence,

$$ r'(U \wedge W) + r'(U \vee W) \leq r'(U) + r'(W) \quad (2.24) $$

is invalid. Thus, for $U$ and $W$, $G_2$ does not hold. Hence, for $2 \leq m \leq n - 2$, $\mathbf{L}_R(m, n)$ is not a geometric lattice.

## 2.3.3 Learning algorithms and examples

The group elements formed by $n$-order regular matrices can be characterised by $r$-parameter $\alpha_i$ under certain conditions. Then, $A = A(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r)$, a continuous change of $r$ parameters, may produce the entire manifold. For $GL_n(\mathbb{F}_q)$, the $n^2$ elements of matrix $A$ calibrate the points in the $n^2$-dimensional Euclidean space. This change in the $n^2$ parameters describes the change relation from one point in Euclidean space to another. Thus, $GL_n(\mathbb{F}_q)$’s subgroups of various $r$ parameters can be represented by some $r$-dimensional subspaces in the $n^2$-dimensional Euclidean space. The change of data state in the learning system is proved by the transformation of the parameter.

More specifically, starting from any element $a$, a continuous change through the $r$ parameter produces an arc that links any pair of spaces in a group, forming the structure of the graph.

Here is an algebraic view of the Lie group’s topology, where the set $U^q(n, r)$ consists of integer $n + mq' (m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots, q)$ in any neighbourhood of any integer $n \in M$ (where $q$ is a fixed prime and $r$ is an integer). Selecting a $q$ defines a specific topology for M. For a given neighbourhood of $n$, the integer is fixed, $m$ is dynamic, and different $r$ values give the different neighbourhoods of $n$ [17].

Through connectivity and topology, consider specific learning issues as follows. Each sample $x_i$ is represented $x_i = (k_1, k_2, \dots, k_m)$ by specific attribute values, and assume that the problem has several categories, denoted by $C = (c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n)$. According to the attribute value, each sample in the sample set is represented in the learning subspace, and the samples of the same class have similar or equal attribute values. They should be connected to each other, and some attribute values of different classes may be similar or equal, but different attribute values are bound to exist. Of course, we can first determine the key attribute value $k_i$ (centre point) of each category, and by comparing the distance from $k_i$, we can effectively determine the category to which the sample belongs. A sample in the same class must exist in the neighbourhood of another sample, and different class sets are disconnected from each other.
