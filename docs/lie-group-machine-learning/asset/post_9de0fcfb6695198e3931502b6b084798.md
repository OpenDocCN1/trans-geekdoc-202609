
## Definition 8.5

If $\mathbf{V}$ is the affine contact of differential manifold $M, \theta: t \rightarrow \theta(t)$ is a curve that can be different, and $\theta(t) = d\theta(d/dt)$ is a vector in $T_{\theta(t)}(M)$, which is called the tangent vector of the curve in $\theta(t)$. If

$$
(\nabla_{\theta(t)}\theta(t))_{\theta(t)} = 0 \quad \text{(8.1)}
$$

then $\theta(t)$ is a geodesic.

The geodesic is the natural extension of the straight line segment in the Euclidean space. On any point on the surface, there is only one geodesic in the given direction. The geodesic is the partial shortest path in the differential manifold. For the sample data distributed on the manifold, the geodesic can show the local neighbourhood properties.

For example, in Figure 8.4(a), according to the definition of Euclidean distance, two points can be considered in the same neighbourhood, but if the data distribution of the information is considered, from the geodesic distance, the two points cannot be adjacent points, as shown in Figure 8.4(b). Thus, the nature of the data manifold structure can be clearly considered from the geodesic departure.

In the differential manifold, it is very difficult to directly calculate the distance of the geodesic, since the manifold is locally Euclidean. Hence, the smaller part of the manifold is often replaced by the tangent plane, so that the calculation of the geodesic can be transformed into the calculation of the Euclidean distance in the two-dimensional plane.

## Definition 8.6

Let $(M, x)$ be a differential manifold, where $X$ is a local coordinate system on $U$, and $r_j: \mathbb{R}^D \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is the $j$-th coordinate function on $\mathbb{R}^D$, i.e., $r_j(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n) = a_j$, so that the $j$th coordinate function of $x$ is $x_j: U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, i.e., $x_j = r_jX$.

## Definition 8.7

Let $(M, x)$ be a differential manifold, $a \in M$, and let $C^{\infty}(M, a, R)$ be the set of real-valued functions in the area of $a$. Then, the tangent vector at the point $a$ is a mapping, i.e., $v: C^{\infty}(M, a, R) \to R$. If $x$ is a local coordinate system in the area $a$, then there exists a real-valued $n$ tuple $(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n)$, for each $f \in C^{\infty}(M, a, R)$. The $n$ tuple satisfies

$$
v(f) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial l_i}(x^{-1}) \right|_{x(a)}. \quad \text{(8.2)}
$$

All the tangent vectors of $M$ at $a$ are represented by $T_a(M)$, which is called the tangent space of $M$ at $a$. If $x: U \to R^D$ is a coordinate system $(\partial/\partial x_i)$ at $a$ and is the direction vector about $x_i$, $((\partial/\partial x_1)$, $(\partial/\partial x_2)$, $\dots$, $(\partial/\partial x_n))$ is the base coordinate of the tangent space $T_a(M)$. If the inner production $g_{ij}(a) = <(\partial/\partial x_i)$, $(\partial/\partial x_j)>$ is a differential function on $U$, then the distance between $a$ and $b$ in the tangent space is

$$
d(a, b) = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n} g_{ij}(a)(a_i - b_i)(a_j - b_j)}. \quad \text{(8.3)}
$$

Alternatively, by the left and right linear transformation (conformal transformation), the high-dimensional Lie group of manifolds is mapped to the corresponding tangent space, and in the linear structure of the tangent space, the unmarked points are easily described.

## Definition 8.8

Let $A \in \mathrm{GL}(n)$. Then,

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} g_{1}^{1} & \dots & g_{1}^{n} \\ \dots & \dots & \dots \\ g_{n}^{1} & \dots & g_{n}^{n} \end{pmatrix} \text{ and } |A| \neq 0
$$

Hence, $\mathrm{GL}(n)$, which represents the multiplication of matrices, constitutes a group, and the unit matrix is the unit element of the group. Otherwise, $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ is an open subset of $R^{n^{2}}$; hence, $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ has smooth structure as an open sub-manifold of $R^{n^{2}}$. Assume that $A = (a_{i}^{j})$, $B = (b_{i}^{j}) \in \mathrm{GL}(n)$, where $a_{i}^{j}$ is the $j$ th row and $i$ th column element of $A$, and the $j$ th row and $i$ th column element of $B$ is

$$
(A \cdot B)_{i}^{j} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{k}^{i} b_{j}^{k} \, .
$$

In addition,

$$
(A^{-1})_{i}^{j} = \frac{\overline{A_{i}^{j}}}{\det A} \, ,
$$

where $\overline{A_{i}^{j}}$ represents the algebraic cofactor of the element $a_{i}^{j}$ of the determinant det $A$. Thus, the inverse matrix $A^{-1}$ is the rational formula of the element of the matrix $A$. Thus, the multiplication and the inverse operation of the group $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ are both smooth. Hence, $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ is an $n^{2}$-dimensional Lie group [42].

$I$ is used to represent the $n \times n$ unit matrix, which is the unit element of $\mathrm{GL}(n)$. The set of $n \times n$ real matrices is recorded as $M(n, n)$; obviously, $M(n, n) \equiv R^{n^{2}}$. The basis $E_{i}^{j}(i, j \leqslant n)$ in $M(n, n)$ is obtained, where $E_{i}^{j}(i, j \leqslant n)$ represents the $n \times n$ matrix whose $i$ th row and $j$ th column element is 1, and other elements are 0. Hence, any $n \times n$ matrix can be represented as

$$
X=\sum_{i, j=1}^{n} X_{j}^{i} E_{i}^{j} \quad \text{(8.6)}
$$

where $X_{j}^{i}$ represents the element in the $i$ th row and $j$ th column of matrix $X$. Further, according to (4.6), the coordinate of the point $X \in M(n, n) \cong R^{n^{2}}$ under the standard frame $(O ; E_{i}^{j})$ is $X_{j}^{i}$, i.e., the coordinate system in $M(n, n)$ is given. The natural standard frame produced under the coordinate system is $\left\{X, \partial / \partial X_{j}^{i}\right\}_{X}$, and

$$
\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{j}^{i}}\right|_{X}=E_{i}^{j}. \quad \text{(8.7)}
$$

Because $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ is the open subset of $M(n, n)$, the above coordinate system and natural standard frame of $M(n, n) \cong R^{n^{2}}$ can also fit $\mathrm{GL}(n)$. Let the multiplication in $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ be denoted by $\varphi$. Then, the expression of $\varphi$ in the above coordinate system is

$$
\left(\varphi(A, B)\right)_{j}^{i}=(A \cdot B)_{j}^{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n} A_{k}^{i} B_{j}^{k}. \quad \text{(8.8)}
$$

We now require the left invariant vector field produced by the leftward movement of the tangent vector $\vec{E}^{i}=\left.\partial / \partial X_{j}^{i}\right|_{I}$ at the unit element $I$ on the Lie group $\mathrm{GL}(n)$, denoted by $\vec{E}_{i}^{\perp}$. Then,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\vec{E}_{i}^{j}\right|_{A} &=(L_{A})_{* I}\left(\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{j}^{i}}\right|_{I}\right) \\
&=\sum_{k, l=1}^{n}\left.\frac{\partial(\varphi(A, X))_{j}^{k}}{\partial X_{l}^{i}}\right|_{X=I} \cdot\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{k}^{l}}\right|_{A} \\
&=\sum_{k, l, h=1}^{n} A_{0}^{k} g^{h}\left.\delta_{l}^{j} \frac{\partial}{\partial X_{k}^{l}}\right|_{A} \\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n} A_{i}^{k}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{j}^{k}}\right|_{A}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} X_{i}^{k}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{j}^{k}}\right|_{X}\right)_{X=A}. \quad \text{(8.9)}
\end{aligned}
$$

We directly calculate

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left[\vec{E}_{i}^{j}, \vec{E}_{k}^{l}\right] & =\left[\sum_{p=1}^{n} X_{j}^{p}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{i}^{p}}\right|_{A}, \sum_{q=1}^{n} X_{k}^{q}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{l}^{q}}\right|_{A}\right] \\
& =\sum_{p=1}^{n}\left(\delta_{k}^{l} X_{j}^{p}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{i}^{p}}\right|_{A}-\delta_{i}^{l} X_{k}^{p}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{j}^{p}}\right|_{A}\right) \\
& =\delta_{k}^{l} \vec{E}_{i}^{\perp}-\delta_{i}^{l} \vec{E}_{k}^{\perp}. \quad \text{(8.10)}
\end{aligned}
$$
