point of view, $\lVert \boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\rVert$ is the Euclidean distance between two points $\boldsymbol{x}$ and $\boldsymbol{y}$ in the linear space, and $\lVert \log \left(\boldsymbol{x}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\right) \rVert$ is the geodesic distance between two points in the Lie group manifold space. The geodesic distance $\lVert \log \left(\boldsymbol{x}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\right) \rVert$ from $\boldsymbol{x}$ to $\boldsymbol{y}$ is equal to the geodesic distance $\lVert \log \left(\boldsymbol{y}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}\right) \rVert$ from $\boldsymbol{y}$ to $\boldsymbol{x}$. In addition, the geodesic distance between any two Lie group elements is nonnegative, which satisfies the requirement that kernel matrix $K$ is a symmetric positive definite matrix.

According to the above analysis, we can get the Lie group matrix RBF kernel function

$k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y})=\exp \left(\frac{-\left\lVert\log\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\right)\right\rVert^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right)$. (9.65)

In the above equation, the nonlinear Lie group samples are mapped into infinite-dimensional space and the dot product is calculated. If the distribution of the samples in the original Lie group manifold space is in accordance with the radial characteristics, then using the kernel function will result in an ideal sample linearization result. For the fractional molecular part of (9.65), approximation of the RBM kernel function can be obtained by using the first-order BCH formula:

$k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y})=\exp \left(\frac{-\left\lVert\log\boldsymbol{y}-\log(\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right)$. (9.66)

# 9.5.4.2 Lie group polynomial kernel

The form of the polynomial kernel in the vector feature space is $k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y})=(\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{y}+1)^d$. The vector dot product is exchangeable, but the matrix multiplication is not exchangeable. Therefore, the vector-based polynomial kernel function cannot be used directly for matrix Lie group samples.

From matrix theory, we can define an inner product in the space of an $n \times n$ matrix.

$\langle\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y}\rangle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{H} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}\right)$ (9.67)

where $tr$ is a matrix trace operator and $\boldsymbol{y}^{H}=\overline{\boldsymbol{y}}^{T}$, which means that $\boldsymbol{y}^{H}$ is the conjugate transpose matrix of $\boldsymbol{y}$:

$\boldsymbol{y}^{H}=\left(\begin{array}{ccc} \overline{a_{11}} & \cdots & \overline{a_{1 n}} \\- & \ddots & \\ \overline{a_{n 1}} & \cdots & \overline{a_{n m}}\end{array}\right)=\boldsymbol{y}^{T}$. (9.68)

The inner product of the matrix defined by (9.67) can be used to derive geometric quantities of many matrix spaces. For example, the angle of two matrix vectors in the matrix space can be defined as $\theta=\arccos \left(\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{H} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}\right) /\left(\|\boldsymbol{x}\| \cdot \|\boldsymbol{y}\|\right)\right)$. At the same time, the inner product of the matrix conforms to all the properties of the inner vector product. Therefore, it also satisfies $\langle\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y}\rangle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{H} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T} \cdot \boldsymbol{y}\right)=\langle\boldsymbol{y}, \boldsymbol{x}\rangle$. With this property, it is easy to derive the polynomial kernel function based on the matrix Lie group:

$k(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y})=\left(\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{H} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}\right)+1\right)^{d}$. (9.69)

In the above equation, $d$ is any positive real number, usually a positive integer.
