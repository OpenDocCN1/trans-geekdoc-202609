
## 6.1.2.3 Covering group of the Lie group

**Definition 6.4.** Let $M, N$ be Hausdorff spaces. A continuous mapping $f: M \rightarrow N$ is called a covering mapping from $M$ to $N$. If $\forall p \in N$, we take $p' \in f^{-1}(p)$; then, there exists a neighbourhood $V_{p'}$ of $p'$ in $M$ such that $f(V_{p'})$ is the neighbourhood of $p$ in $N$, and $V_{p'}$ and $f(V_{p'})$ are homologous at $f$. Further, $M$ is called the covering space of $N$ and $(M, f)$ is the covering of $N$.

**Definition 6.5.** Consider the homomorphism of the Lie group $G_1$ to $G_2$, and if it is the covering of $G_2$, $G_1$ is called the covering group of $G_2$. For a unit element of $G_2$, $f^{-1}(e) = \Gamma$ is called the Poincaré group of the covering group $(G_1, f)$, with respect to $G_2$, and the number of elements of the Poincaré group is called the degree of the covering.

The Poincaré group has the following properties:

**Lemma 6.1.** Let $(G_1, f)$ be the covering of the connected Lie group $G_2$. The Poincaré group $\Gamma = f^{-1}(e)$ of $(G_1, f)$ with respect to $G_2$ is a discrete normal subgroup, so it is in the centre of $G_1$. If we take any $g_2 \in G_2$, $g_1 \in f^{-1}(g_2) \subset G_1$, then $f^{-1}(g_2) = g_1\Gamma = g_1f^{-1}(e) = \{g_1h \in G_1 | \forall h \in \Gamma\}$. The complete primitive of the midpoint of $G_2$ constitutes the left parcel of the topological group $G_1$ with respect to the normal subgroup $\Gamma$, and the topological group $G_1/\Gamma$ is isomorphic to the topological group $G_2$.

To maintain the covering nature, we introduce the following:

**Definition 6.6.** The two covering groups $(G_1, f_1)$ and $(G_2, f_2)$ of Lie group $G$ are called equivalent if there is an isomorphism $\varphi$ from $G_1$ to $G_2$ such that $f_1 = f_2 \circ \varphi$, i.e., an exchange diagram:

It is known from the definition that if the two groups $(G_1, f_1)$ and $(G_2, f_2)$ of Lie group $G$ are equivalent under $\varphi$, then the unit element $e$ of $G$ satisfies $\varphi(f_1^{-1}(e)) = f_2^{-1}(e)$, which leads to the isomorphism of the Poincaré group; hence, the covering state of the two groups is exactly the same.

**Definition 6.7.** The Hausdorff topological space $M$ is called simply connected if its closed paths (i.e., the closed curves between any two points) are homotopic to zero, then the first-order homotopy group of $M$ is zero, i.e., any closed path can be continuously shrunk to a point.

Note that simple connectivity and connectivity are irrelevant concepts, and a simply connected space is necessarily connected. Now, considering the connected manifold, because the manifold must be connected, the simply connected manifold is equivalent to the condition that for any two points $p$, $q$, any two continuous curves of $p$ and $q$ can be continuously shifted to each other.

**Theorem 6.2.** *If $N$ is a connected Lie group, then there exists a simply connected Lie group $M$ that is the covering group of $N$.*

*Proof.* From the theoretical knowledge of the topological covering space, where $N$ is a connected manifold, a simply connected manifold $M$ is the covering space of $N$; $P: M \to N$ is the covering mapping.

For a path $\mathbf{x}$: $[0, 1] \to N$ with $e_N$ as the starting point, $\mathbf{x}(0) = e_N$, the path class $[\mathbf{x}]$ to which $\mathbf{x}$ belongs is a point in $M$ and covers the mapping $P[\mathbf{x}] = \mathbf{x}(1)$.

For two paths $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}$ in $N$ with $e_N$ as the starting point, we define $\mathbf{z}$ as $\mathbf{z}$: $[0, 1] \to N$, $\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{x}(t)\mathbf{y}(t)$. We define multiplication of points in $M$ as $[\mathbf{x}][\mathbf{y}] = [\mathbf{z}]$ or $[\mathbf{x}][\mathbf{y}] = [\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}]$. This definition is significant because when $[\mathbf{x}^*] = [\mathbf{x}]$ and $[\mathbf{y}^*] = [\mathbf{y}]$, $H_1$ is the fixed-point homotopy of the path $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x}^*$, and $H_2$ is fixed-point homotopy of the path $\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{y}^*$. We define $H(s, t) = H_1(s, t)H_2(s, t)$, where $H$ is fixed-point homotopy of the path $\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{x}^*\mathbf{y}^*$, i.e., $[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}] = [\mathbf{x}^*\mathbf{y}^*]$. Further, $[e_N]$ (path class of constant path $e_N$ in $N$) $= e_M$ is the unit of $M$. In $M$, the inverse of point $[\mathbf{x}]$ is $[\mathbf{x}]^{-1} = [\mathbf{x}^{-1}]$, where $\mathbf{x}^{-1}(t) = (\mathbf{x}(t))^{-1}$.

After defining the multiplication, $M$ is obviously a group. The covering mapping $P([\mathbf{x}][\mathbf{y}]) = P[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{x}(1)\mathbf{y}(1) = P[\mathbf{x}]P[\mathbf{y}]$ is a group homomorphism.

Since $M$ is present in the open neighbourhoods $V^*$ of the neighbourhood $P(V^*)$ in $N$, $M$ can be given a differential structure, and $M$ becomes a Lie group. Further, $M$ and $N$ are locally differentially isomorphic. Thus, $\Lambda(M) = \Lambda(N)$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.3.** *Let $G$ be a connected Lie group. Then, there exists a unique (in the isomorphic sense) simply connected Lie group $\widehat{G}$, and the covering map $f$ on $\widehat{G}$ to $G$ such that $(\widehat{G}, f)$ is the covering group of $G$, which is called the universal covering group of $G$.*

*Proof.* Let $(\widehat{G}, f)$ be the universal covering manifold of manifold $G$, $f^{-1}(e) = \Gamma$. Let $e^* \in \widehat{G}$ and $a^*, b^* \in \widehat{G}$. Then, there are paths $r_1^*, r_2^*$ connecting $e^*$ and $a^*$, and $e^*$ and $b^*$, respectively, in $\widehat{G}$. Thus, $r_1 = f(r_1^*)$, $r_2 = f(r_2^*)$ are the paths from $e$ to $a = f(a^*)$ and from $e$ to $b = f(b^*)$, respectively. Therefore, $r = r_1 \cdot r_2$ is the path from $e$ to $ab$ in $G$.

Lift $r$ to the path whose starting point is $e^*$ in $\widehat{G}$, and set its end as $c^*$. Define multiplication in $\widehat{G}$ as follows: $a^*b^* = c^*$. It should be noted that $c^*$ does not depend on the choice of $r_1^*$ and $r_2^*$. Let $w_1, w_2$ be other paths connecting $e^*$ and $a^*$, and $e^*$ and $b^*$, respectively, in $\widehat{G}$. Then, $w_1, w_2$ are paths connecting $e$ and $a$, and $e$ and $b$, respectively, in $G$. Further, $w = w_1 \cdot w_2$ is the path from $e$ to $ab$ in $G$. Let $w^*$ be the lift of $w$, so that $r_i^*$ can be continuously changed to $w_i^*$ because $\widehat{G}$ is simply connected. Hence, $w_1$ can be continuously changed to $r_1$, and $w$ can be continuously changed to $r$. Since the starting and ending points of $w$ and $r$ are the same, the end of $w^*$ and $r^*$ is the same. Therefore, the $\widehat{G}$ multiplication of the above definition is well-defined.

The associativity of multiplication in $\widehat{G}$ can be obtained by the associativity of path multiplication.

From the definition, we also know that $e^*$ is a unit element. Further, $(a^*)^{-1} r_1^{-1}$ is the path connecting $e$ and $a^{-1}$ in $G$. It is lifted to the path connecting $e^*$ and $(a^*)^{-1}$ in $\widehat{G}$. Then, we know that $a^* (a^*)^{-1} = e^*$, i.e., $(a^*)^{-1}$ is the inverse element of $a^*$.

In summary, $\widehat{G}$ is a group. We know that $f$ is a group homomorphism from $\widehat{G}$ to $G$ because $f(a^* b^*) = ab = f(a^*) f(b^*)$.

Since $f$ is the covering mapping, the inverse operation and multiplication of $\widehat{G}$ are continuous, and the group structure of $\widehat{G}$ is a local group, we can prove that $\widehat{G}$ is a topological group. In addition, $\widehat{G}$ is not only a connected topological group but also a local Lie group; hence, there is a unique analytic structure such that $\widehat{G}$ is a Lie group.

Thus, the proof is complete. $\square$

If $(G_1, f_1)$ is also the universal covering group of $G$, then the homeomorphic mapping $f'$ of $\widehat{G}$ to $G_1$ makes $f = f_1 f'$. Thus, $f_1 f'(e^*) = f(e^*) = e$. Obviously, $L_{e_1^{-1}} f'$ still is a homeomorphism, and $L_{e_1^{-1}} f'(e^*) = e_1$. $L_{e_1^{-1}} f'$ is still referred to as $f'$. Then,

$$
f_1 f'(a^* b^*) = f(a^* b^*) = f(a^*) f(b^*) = f_1 f'(a^*) f_1 f'(b^*)
$$

Because $f_1$ is a local isomorphism, hence, in a unit neighbourhood

$$
f'(a^* b^*) = f'(a^*) f'(b^*)
$$

Further, $\widehat{G}$ and $G_1$ are connected, and $f'$ is a one-to-one correspondence; hence $f'$ is the isomorphism of the Lie groups.

> *Corollary 6.2. Let $(\widehat{G}, f)$ be the universal covering group of connected Lie group $G$. Then, $df$ is the isomorphic mapping of Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}_{\widehat{G}}$ to Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}_G$.*

This conclusion can be obtained from the fact that $f$ is the homomorphism of $\widehat{G}$ to $G$ and is locally an isomorphism.

> *Theorem 6.4. The connected group $G$ must be covered by a single covering group $(\widehat{G}, f)$, called the universal covering group of $G$. Further, $f$ is an isomorphism if and only if $G$ itself is a simply connected Lie group.*

Here, we can give two examples of covering groups:

- $f : \mathbb{R}^1 \rightarrow S^1, \quad f(t) = e^{2\pi i t}, \quad t \in \mathbb{R}^1 ; \quad$ thus, $\mathbb{R}^1$ is the covering group of $S^1$.
- $Ad : S^3 \rightarrow SO(3)$. For $SO(3)$, for any appropriate unit neighbourhood $V$, $Ad^{-1}(V)$ has two connected branches, i.e., it is a ‘two-sheeted’ covering, while the upper case is an ‘infinite multi-sheeted’ covering.
