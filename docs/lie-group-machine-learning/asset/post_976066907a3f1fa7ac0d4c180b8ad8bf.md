
is called the core of linear mapping $f$. $\operatorname{Im} f=\{f(X)|X \in V\}$ is called the image of linear mapping $f$.

It is easy to prove that $\operatorname{Ker} f$ and $\operatorname{Im} f$ are subspaces of $V$ and $W$, respectively. When $\operatorname{Ker} f=0$, $f$ is said to be single shot. When $\operatorname{Im} f=W$, $f$ is said to be surjective. When the linear mapping is surjective and injective, $f$ is called an isomorphic vector space. If there is an isomorphism $f$ between the vector spaces $V$ and $W$, it is also known as $W$ and $V$ isomorphism. Consider the mapping
$\begin{aligned} & a: V 1 \times V 2 \times \cdots \times V r \rightarrow R \\ & \forall X 1, \ldots, X r \in V, a(X 1, \ldots, X r) \in R \end{aligned}$ such that $\begin{aligned} & a\left(X 1, \ldots, X i+X i, \ldots, X r\right)=a\left(X 1, \ldots, X i, \ldots, X r\right)+a\left(X 1, \ldots, Y i, \ldots, X r\right) \\ , & a\left(X 1, \ldots, a X i, \ldots, X r\right)=a a\left(X 1, \ldots, X i, \ldots, X r\right) \\ , & a\left(X 1, \ldots, X i, \ldots, X j, \ldots, X r\right)=-a\left(X 1, \ldots, X j, \ldots, X i, \ldots, X r\right) \end{aligned}$ $(\forall i, j=1, \ldots, r, a \in R)$.

We call $a$ the $r$-valued inverse linear function on the vector space $V$ or the $r$-form on $V$.

**Definition 17.12.** Let $u$ and $v$ be vectors in $2 n$-dimensional space, where
$$
\begin{aligned} & u=\left[X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n}, \xi_{1}, \ldots, \xi_{n}\right]^{T} \\ & v=\left[Y_{1}, \ldots, Y_{n}, \eta_{1}, \ldots, \eta_{n}\right]^{T} . \end{aligned}
$$

Then, the inner product is defined as $\omega(u, v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_{i} \eta_{i}-y_{i} \xi_{i}\right)=u^{T} J v^{T}$ and $\omega(S u, S v)=\omega(u, v)$, where $J=\left[\begin{array}{cc} 0 & I_{n} \\ -I_{n} & 0 \end{array}\right]$, with the properties of $J^{-1}=J^{T}=-J$, $I_{n}$ is the $n$-order unit matrix. The intrinsic properties of the inner product are as follows:

- (1) Bilinear: $\omega\left(\lambda_{1} u_{1}+\lambda_{2} u_{2}, v\right)=\lambda_{1} \omega\left(u_{1}, v\right)+\lambda_{2} \omega\left(u_{2}, v\right)$;
- (2) Antisymmetry: $\omega(u, v) = -\omega(v, u)$;
- (3) Non-degenerate: For each $v \in R^{2 n}$, if there are $\omega(u, v)=0$ for all $u$, then $v=0$;

**Definition 17.13.** The real vector space $(R^{2 n}, \omega)$ of the symplectic product $\omega$ having the above condition is called a symplectic space.

**Definition 17.14.** If the linear transformation $S$ in the symplectic space $(R^{2 n}, \omega)$ satisfies $S: R^{2 n} \rightarrow R^{2 n}$, for all $u$, $v \in R^{2 n}$ we have $\omega(S u, S v)=\omega(u, v)$, then the linear transformation $S$ is a symplectic transformation or a regular transformation, and the symplectic transform preserves the symplectic product.

### Theorem 17.6
A linear transformation of symplectic space $S$ is a necessary and sufficient condition for symplectic transformation. It is
$$S^{T} J S=J .$$

Proof. In the symplectic space $(R^{2n}, \omega)$, there is a matrix of a symplectic transform $S$ on a symplectic basis $\{e_1, e_2, \ldots, e_n, e_{n+1}, \ldots, e_{2n}\}$ in $(R^{2n}, \omega)$, denoted by $S$. Then, for $X = \sum x^i e_i$ and $Y = \sum y^j e_j$.

From $\omega(S X, S Y)=\omega(X, Y)$,
$$\left(x^{1}, \ldots, x^{2 n}\right) S^{T} J S\left(\begin{array}{c} y^{1} \\ \vdots \\ y^{2 n} \end{array}\right)=\left(x^{1}, \ldots, x^{2 n}\right) J\left(\begin{array}{c} y^{1} \\ \vdots \\ y^{2 n} \end{array}\right)$$
where $J=\left(\begin{array}{cc} 0 & I \\ -I & 0 \end{array}\right)$ is the matrix representation of the symplectic form $\omega$ under $\{e_1, e_2, \ldots, e_n, e_{n+1}, \ldots, e_{2n}\}$, and $T$ is the transpose of the matrix. Hence,
$$S^{T} J S=J$$
is the full condition of the symplectic transformation.

\omega(\lambda_1 u_1 + \lambda_2 u_2, v) = \lambda_1 \omega(u_1, v) + \lambda_2 \omega(u_2, v);
\omega(u, v) = -\omega(v, u);
If \omega is a symplectic space,
If S^T JS = J

return 1.
else

End if.

### Definition 17.15
A $2n$-order matrix $B$ is a symplectic matrix if
$$B^{T}JB = J$$

### Definition 17.16
A $2n$-order matrix $B$ is called an infinitesimal complex if
$$JB + B^{T}J = 0$$

### Inference 17.1
Matrix
$$\left[\begin{array}{cc} I & B \\ 0 & I \end{array}\right], \quad \left[\begin{array}{cc} I & 0 \\ D & I \end{array}\right]$$
is an infinitesimal complex if and only if $B^{T} = B$, $D^{T} = D$.

### Definition 17.17
Let $(V, \omega)$ be a $2n$-dimensional symplectic space. If a set of vectors $e_{1}, e_{2}, \ldots e_{2n}$ of $(V, \omega)$ satisfies $\delta(e_i, e_j) = \delta_{ij}$ and $\omega(e_{i}, e_{j}) = \omega(e_{n+i}, e_{n+j}) = 0$ for $i \neq j$, and $\omega(e_i, e_{n+i}) = 1$, then we call it a symplectic basis (or octyl group) of $(V, \omega)$.

### Definition 17.18
Let $(V, \omega)$ be a symplectic space. An automorphism of $(V, \omega)$ is an isomorphism of $(V, \omega)$ to itself. Hence, $(V, \omega)$ is an element of the linear transformation group $\mathrm{Gl}(V)$ of $V$, and if it is denoted as $s$, it satisfies
$$\omega(\mathrm{s}x, \mathrm{s}y) = \omega(x, y), \forall x, y \in V$$

It is easy to know that the automorphisms of the self-isomorphism of $(V, \omega)$ constitute a subgroup of $\mathrm{Gl}(V, R)$, which we denote as $\mathrm{Sp}(V, \omega)$. In particular, the automorphisms of the standard symplectic space $(k^{2n}, \omega)$ are denoted as $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, k)$, and if $k = R$, $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, k)$ is abbreviated as $\mathrm{Sp}(2n)$ and called a $2n$-symplectic group.

Let $A_{0}$ be an antisymmetric 2 form on $R^{2n}$. The set of all subspaces that satisfy $\omega(x, x') = 0$ for any $x, x'$ in the subspace is called the kernel of $\omega$, denoted as $\mathrm{Ker}(\omega)$.
$$\mathrm{Ker}(\omega) = \{x | \omega(x, y) = 0, \forall y \in R^{2n}\}$$

When $\mathrm{Ker}(\omega)=\{0\}$, the form is called non-degenerate; otherwise, it is called degenerate. We can get
$$\operatorname{rank} \omega = \operatorname{dim} R^{2 n}-\operatorname{dim} \mathrm{Ker}\left(\omega\right)\tag{17.1}$$

### Theorem 17.7
A symplectic space $(R^{2 n}, \omega)$ must be even-dimensional.

*Proof.* In fact, since the symplectic form $\omega$ is non-degenerate, $\mathrm{Ker}(\omega)=\{0\}$. According to (17.1), $\operatorname{dim} R^{2 n}=\operatorname{rank} \omega$. Since the rank of an antisymmetric matrix is always even, the symplectic space must be even-dimensional. $\square$

### Definition 17.19
If a G-space $M$ has a symplectic structure $\omega$ and remains $\omega$ unchanged by the action of $G$, i.e., $\forall g \in G$, $\Phi_g: M \rightarrow M$ is a symplectic diffeomorphism, i.e., $\Phi_g^*\omega = \omega$, then $\omega$ is called a symplectic structure on $M$, and $(M, \omega)$ is called a symplectic manifold.

We let the basis of the $2l$-dimensional symplectic space be arranged in the following order
$$a=1, \overline{1}, 2, \overline{2}, \ldots, l, \bar{l}\tag{17.2}$$

Introduce $2l$-dimensional antisymmetric matrices $J$
$$J=I_{l} \otimes\left(i \sigma_{2}\right)=-J^{-1}=-J^{T}, \quad \operatorname{det} J=1 .\tag{17.3}$$

All sets of $2l$-dimensional pseudo-orthogonal matrices $R$ satisfying the following conditions constitute the real group $\operatorname{Sp}(2l, R)$ under matrix multiplication:
$$R^{\dagger}=R^{-1}, \quad R^{T} J R=J$$
The $\operatorname{Sp}(2l, R)$ group is a non-compact Lie group. All sets of $2l$-dimensional unitary matrices $u$ satisfying the following conditions constitute the unitary symplectic group $\mathrm{USp}(2l)$:
$$u^{\dagger}=u^{-1}, \quad u^{T} J u=J$$
The $\mathrm{USp}(2l)$ group is a compact Lie group. It is easy to prove that $R J R^{T}=J$ and $u J u^{T}=J$ from the definition.

The group spaces of the symplectic group and the unitary symplectic group are all connected; they are simple Lie groups; the dimension is $l \cdot(2 l+1)$. Through the corresponding transformation, we can relate the compact Lie group $\mathrm{USp}(2l)$ to the non-compact Lie group $\operatorname{Sp}(2l, R)$. If the parameters of the symplectic group are allowed to take complex values, the irreducible representations of the unitary symplectic group and the symplectic group are related, but the real parts of the parameters are different. This is the standard method for calculating the irreducible representation of non-compact Lie groups.

Because the irreducible representations of the symplectic groups and the unitary symplectic groups correspond to each other, the generators also correspond, except that the real terms of the parameters are different. We only discuss the tensor representations of these groups.
