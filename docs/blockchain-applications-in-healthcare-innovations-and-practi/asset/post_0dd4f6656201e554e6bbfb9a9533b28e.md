Contracts can be extremely simple transactions completed in seconds or minutes, or they can be very complicated and protracted transactions, including talks and tens of pages of written language with particular rights and duties that may take hours or months to accomplish. Smart contracts are currently classified as relatively basic transactions. All descriptions in (CoinDesk 2016b) were divided into two main types by the author: smart legal contracts and SC code. SC code is defined as "code that is validated, stored and implemented on a block-chain" (CoinDesk 2016b). The functioning of an SC is entirely reliant on the coding language that is used to show the agreement and the Blockchain’s capabilities. The term "smart legal contract" refers to code that may be used to supplement or replace legal contracts. The functionality of this SC is determined by legal, political and business institutions, rather than technology. A balance, secure storage and program code are all features of a smart contract. The contract condition is made up of the storage and balance. When the contract is fulfilled, the information is saved and changed on the Blockchain. Each contract is given a distinct 20-byte address. The contract code cannot be modified once it has been posted on the Blockchain. A contract may be executed by simply submitting a transaction to the contract’s address. This operation is performed by each agreement node (also known as miners) on the system in order to attain an agreement on its conclusion. The status of the contract will be revised as necessary. It can comprehend its personal storage, put funds into its account, deliver orders or payment via user profile deals and even build new contracts depending on the activity it gets.

## 2.2.1. Operating mechanism of smart contracts

SCs are made up of a collection of rules of procedure and logic called scenario response logic. In other terms, they are distributed, reliable shared programs implemented on Blockchain. The participants entering a contract must decide on contract agreements, leakage in contract terms, breach of contract obligations and outside validation information sources (oracles), and place it on the system as a smart contract to execute contract implementation on their own.

## 2.2.2. Applications of smart contracts

### 2.2.2.1. Finance

Smart contracts and Blockchain enhance transparency and trust among players, while reducing infrastructure, transaction and administrative expenses. The following are a few examples of smart contract applications in finance (Ojetunde et al. 2019).

- Security: the security sector entails complicated operations that are time consuming, wasteful, inconvenient and risky. Smart contracts can avoid the use of middlemen in the securities custody chain. The centralized clearance process necessitates time-consuming operations, as well as complicated external and internal reconciliation.

- Insurance: every year, the insurance industry invests millions of dollars in claim processing while losing millions to fraudulent claims. They could be used to automate insurance claims, validation and payment, boosting claim processing speed while reducing fraud and minimizing potential risks (Gatteschi et al. 2018).

- Trade finance: currently, trade finance is riddled with inefficiencies, and the business is particularly prone to theft. Furthermore, the trading procedures on the basis of paper finance must be modernized or supplanted with digitized activities. They enable organizations to autonomously initiate commercial activities based on pre-set criteria, increasing productivity by simplifying procedures and lowering fraud and compliance expenses.

### 2.2.2.2. Management (Lutz Maicher et al. 2016)

Smart contracts provided by Blockchain can give adequate accountability for management roles, duties and decision-making procedures. The following are some examples of use cases.

- Management of digital properties and rights: Maicher et al. (2016) argued for the use of smart contracts to authenticate intellectual property existence and authorship. Smart contracts may also be used to control digital rights. For example, once a musician’s work is used commercially, a dApp called Ujo Music enforces royalty payments (ConsenSys Mesh n.d.).

- Organizational management: most businesses are now governed and focused on a board of directors who make the bulk of decisions. The future of organizational administration is expected to be flattened and decentralized. Smart contracts can eliminate the need for intermediaries that impose false constraints and overly complicated laws.

- E-Government: smart contracts can help to increase the efficiency and authority of the E-Government by simplifying bureaucratic processes. Other smart contract applications include innovative payment methods for labor and pensions, E-Voting (Ayed 2017; McCorry et al. 2017) and so on.

### 2.2.2.3. Internet of Things

The IoT ecosystem comprises Internet-connected physical gadgets, cars, household appliances and other objects. The IoT can be incorporated in smart grids and other sectors. The existing centralized Internet infrastructure makes it impossible to meet IoT development objectives, such as the confidentiality of sensitive information and the trustworthiness of interactions between multiple devices. As a result, the integration of the IoT with Blockchain has become an unavoidable trend, and smart contracts will aid in automating complicated workflows, encouraging resource sharing, saving money and ensuring safety and efficiency (Ayed 2017).

### 2.2.2.4. Energy

Blockchain could power a decentralized energy system and implement smart contracts for power generation and trade in order to develop decentralized energy trading markets, and improve energy and consumption efficiency. Exergy (Exergy n.d.) is a Blockchain consortium platform that offers suitable energy markets for power trades via the existing power grid. Sun Exchange (Sun Exchange n.d.) is a marketplace based on Blockchain that allows customers to buy solar cells and then rent them out to organizations and communities in the sunniest regions in the world (mainly Africa). The income of its members is based on electricity generated through its solar cells. Sun Exchange will be responsible for the collection and distribution of monthly rent. Knirsch et al. (2018) provided a selection of trustworthy, autonomous and anonymity charging points affordability and range to electric vehicles.

## 2.2.3. Programming languages and platforms

Smart contracts may be created and deployed on a variety of Blockchain platforms. Platforms differ in their features. In this section, two common platforms, Ethereum and Hyperledger Fabric, are discussed.

- Ethereum: on the Ethereum platform, applications execute precisely as designed, with no downtime, censorship or other issues such as fraud or intervention from a third party. The Ethereum Virtual Computer (EVM) is a Turing-complete VM, which is used to implement bytecode (as previously stated, Solidity compiles bytecode). Ethereum apps are powered by Ether, the platform’s proprietary cryptographic asset token. Ether is used in the following ways: it is exchanged as an electronic asset similar to Bitcoin, and it is also used to reward nodes for completed calculations (ConsenSys 2016).

- Hyperledger Fabric: this is a distributed ledger technology. The Linux Foundation hosts Hyperledger Fabric. It offers high levels of discretion, robustness, customization and sustainability. It was created to allow for configurable solutions of specific parts, while also addressing the economic ecosystem’s intricacy and complexities. Container technology is used by Hyperledger Fabric to store smart contracts called "chaincode".

Ethereum smart contract scripts, as previously indicated in section 2.1, are created in a bytecode language, which is based on stack and implemented in EVM.
