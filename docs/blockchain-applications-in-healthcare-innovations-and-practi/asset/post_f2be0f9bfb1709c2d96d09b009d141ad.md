### 7.4.2. Blockchain as a savior

The fundamental pillars of Blockchain can support security/privacy controls when different regulatory requirements need to be implemented in the form of technical controls (Yue et al. 2016; Kuo et al. 2017; Shu and Jahankhani 2017; Jin et al. 2019):

- Let us start with decentralization. In traditional healthcare applications DBMS is used while Blockchain provides decentralization which makes different healthcare entities collaborate without any central management entity. This is not possible so easily with DBMS.
- With the help of Blockchain, the PHI is more secure from any tampering as the ledger cannot be altered as Blockchain only supports the creation and reading of the ledgers.
- In Blockchain due to cryptography and digital signature, all records are digitally signed by the source hence all records are source verifiable.
- Due to decentralization, as data is not with a single node, it can never suffer a single point of failure issues, this makes data always available.
- As every block is encrypted in Blockchain by default, it makes sure that the confidentiality of data is not hampered.
- Blockchain enables and strengthens patient rights to own, share and control their data.
- Permissioned Blockchain assures that malicious nodes cannot participate in the network.
- Most importantly, many regulatory requirements related to compliance and agreement between the healthcare entity and patient can be implemented using smart contracts and consensus algorithms.

### 7.5. Differential privacy

The idea of differential privacy (Ul Hassan et al. 2020) was first introduced by C. Dwork in 2006 for statistical datasets. To understand it mathematically, differential privacy is adding noise in the data to preserve its privacy. Consider a function that analyzes a dataset and computes statistical results such as mean, median and variance. Now, if the algorithm of this function is enhanced in such a way that by looking at the output no one can tell whether an individual’s data was used as input in the dataset or not, in this situation, the algorithm will be considered as differentially private (Ul Hassan et al. 2020).

Let us take a simple example: during market research, a team wants to know “how many people have watched the series Friends”, “how many have watched Suits” and “how many have watched Joker”. Now if the system answering these questions have algorithms which are differentially private, it will first add noise to the real values and then will provide the approximate data to the market research team. This approximate data will draw the same conclusions as what actual data would have done so in this way it will not affect the market research team too. This is the main benefit of differential privacy, i.e. the data can be used for analysis while guaranteeing the protection of individual’s privacy.

Let us take another example: a curator asks a medical data owner to check a dataset of patients who have died in the last five years in a particular city and answer for each patient whether they died of cancer. The medical data owner agrees to provide an approximate count based on an algorithm, rather giving actual data. The algorithm has been visualized in Figure 7.11. For each patient row, firstly the medical data owner will flip a coin, if it turns to be heads, then they will tell the truth to the curator. Yet if turns to be “tails”, then they will again flip a coin, now if it is “heads”, then they will answer “yes” and if it is “tails”, then they will answer “no” to the curator. In this way as per probability, the curator will know the truth almost half of the time, and for other half, there will be a random answer that will also have some “truth”, so the curator will have an approximate answer.

### 7.5.1. Local differential privacy versus global differential privacy (Ul Hassan et al. 2020)

In the local private system, each data generator will generate raw data, add noise to it and send data to the researcher. While in global privacy system, each data generator will firstly send data to a trusted curator, who will aggregate the data (or group the data), then add noise to it and provide it to the researcher. ### 7.5.2. Quantification of privacy and mathematical form of differential privacy (Sun et al. 2019; Ul Hassan et al. 2020)

Differential privacy quantifies privacy. It does not define privacy as a binary value, for example if data has been or has not been leaked, but quantifies privacy as “privacy loss” which is at risk of being exposed. The value for “privacy loss” will increase with the number of times data is processed. The terms, which are used in equation [7.1], are given as follows:

- the parameter ε represents the privacy loss;
- D1 and D2 represent the adjacent dataset;
- F is the randomized function for differential privacy and the range of F is D1 × D2;
- P is the probability which satisfies if and only if (O ∈ F), where O is the output of F. We obtain the following:

```
P[F (D1) ∈ O] ≤ exp(ε) × P[F (D2) ∈ O]      [7.1]
```

In this case, Range(F) is the maximum value for the function F and ε is the parameter for privacy loss which helps in controlling the privacy level.

Now as we have already explained the concept of differential privacy, it will be easier to understand how it is useful in Blockchain. The fundamental features of Blockchain, which make it the next generation of secured storage, and using differential privacy in Blockchain will enhance the privacy of Blockchain. - protection of sender and receiver addresses from punitive and feather forking attacks, and linking attacks;

### 7.6. Privacy by Design

Privacy by Design and Privacy by Default are frequently used terminologies, especially since new privacy regulations started coming into the picture. Privacy by Design aims to integrate privacy requirements and controls in all new systems where personal information is in scope. This means Privacy by Design recommends that from the very start of a product/system development, privacy requirements shall be considered and shall be integrated in the design and architecture of the system, so that when a system is developed, all privacy controls are embedded in the system. On the other hand, Privacy by Default talks about post development, i.e. privacy settings shall be enabled by default once a product/system is deployed in production.

The concept of Privacy by Design was first discussed by the former Canadian Information and Privacy Commissioner Ann Cavoukian, as she created several principles as foundation of Privacy by Design. The purpose of these principles is to make privacy integral to priorities of the organization, project objectives and design process. The seven principles of Privacy by Design are discussed (Kurtz et al. 2018; Romanou 2018; Wahlstrom et al. 2020) in Figure 7.13.

From the Blockchain point of view, many of these principles can be easily followed because of the fundamental features of the Blockchain. However, it is highly recommended while creating any system/application using Blockchain to first list all privacy requirements and then create a map that will be automatically satisfied by Blockchain features, the one that needs to be covered in consensus algorithms and smart contracts, and finally the one that needs add-on security/privacy features to be integrated into Blockchain to enhance its security and privacy capabilities such as differential privacy and proxy re-encryption.
