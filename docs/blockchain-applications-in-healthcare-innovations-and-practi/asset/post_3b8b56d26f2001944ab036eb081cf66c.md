### 8.1.1. Introduction to AI

Expert systems have also lately made headlines in the medical field, creating a heated discussion about whether AI doctors will ever be able to replace human doctors. Computers will not replace human doctors immediately, but AI will surely help physicians create good clinical decisions and may replace human judgment in some areas of healthcare (e.g. radiology). Recent successful AI applications in healthcare were made possible by the increased availability of healthcare data or the massive development in advanced analytics algorithms. Effective AI tools can extract clinically significant knowledge contained in vast amounts of data, assisting clinical decision-making (Al-Megren et al. 2018; Grishin et al. 2019; Han et al. 2019; Alsalamah and Nuzzolese 2020; Efremova and Teichmann 2020). The advantages of AI have been extensively studied in the scholarly literature. AI can use effective methods that can “learn” features from a big amount of healthcare data and then apply the results to improve modern medicine. It may also be able to learn from feedback and self-correct to enhance accuracy. Intelligent machines may support physicians by providing up-to-date medical information in the form of articles, textbooks and clinical procedures, allowing them to deliver proper patient care. Furthermore, any intelligence device could help with the decrease of diagnostic and treatment errors, is essential within human medicine. Furthermore, an AI device gathers useful data from a large number of patients to aid in creating real-time inferences for health risk alerts and predictions.

### 8.1.2. Introduction to Blockchain

"Blockchains are tamper evident and tamper-resistant digital ledgers applied in a distributed manner (i.e. without a central repository) and normally without the central authority,” according to the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) (e.g. a bank, company or government). This enables any number of participants to store transactions in something like a smart contract within a team at its most basic level. A few transactions may be changed until they have been published when the Blockchain network is in normal operation. Although there are important distinctions, the terms Blockchain and distributed ledger technology (DLT) are frequently interchanged, especially when it comes to determining the solutions’ business worth. A chain of data or transactions was defined as a group of situationally chained crypto signatures, which are related to from a hash, or preserved on shared ledgers, helped by a range of interconnected processes known as nodes. Nodes keep a copy of the entire chain on their computers, which is updated and maintained regularly.

Traditional programming, networks, databases and online applications are all built on well-known and proven underlying technologies including networking, hashing and encryption. It will approach architecture, trends, data exchange and implementation from a distinct angle. While Blockchain in healthcare is still in its infancy, early solutions have demonstrated the promise to lower healthcare costs, speed up business processes and increase knowledge access across disparate and diverse stakeholders working towards the same goal.

## 8.2. Use of AI in healthcare systems

EHRs have supported the healthcare industry’s digitalization, but the process has resulted in a plethora of challenges, including cognitive fatigue, repetitive paperwork and user burnout. Artificial intelligence has been used by EHR developers to create interfaces that are more intuitive and automate some of the repetitive tasks that previously took up a lot of a user’s time. According to Adam Landman, MD, Vice President and Chief Information Officer of Brigham Healthcare, customers spend a significant amount of time on three things: clinical analysis, bar coding and scanning through the in-basket (Azaria et al. 2016; Destefans et al. 2018; Kiktenko et al. 2018; Maxmen 2018; Tikhomirov et al. 2018). Natural language processing (NLP) approaches are insufficient; even though voice search and transcription could assist and enhance medical reporting. “We should be even more creative and consider adjustments like video recording a clinical session, similar to how police officers wear body cameras,” claimed the data scientist. “After that, you can use artificial intelligence to catalog these recordings for possible information retrieval.” Inside this future, software systems containing smart sensors will be brought to the bedside for doctors to use when placing orders, similar to using Siri, as well as Alexa at home.

Artificial intelligence could even manage routine tasks from its inbox, like narcotic prescriptions upon result updates. It can also help users complete their to-do lists by stressing items that require the clinician’s attention, according to Landman. Healthcare executives are dealing with a plethora of hitherto unseen sales, cost and quality challenges. Artificial intelligence, they believe, is the key to better decision-making since it can provide faster observations or lower mistake tolerances.

These efforts could include augmented intelligence, which goes beyond artificial intelligence by allowing humans to make the most informed decision possible, even if they occasionally fail.

As a result, a growing number of business and analytics professionals are striving to incorporate artificial intelligence (AI) into their forecasting processes to better address these pressing concerns. These efforts have far too often failed spectacularly. Leaders have struggled to leverage analytics into current systems, integrate or change workflows, and convey the benefits of AI.

However, when used without expert guidance (self-service), the aforementioned methodology was nearly always inefficient, and its range was too limited, limiting its usefulness in healthcare (Medicine Catalyst’s new healthcare service) (Yaji et al. 2018; Salah et al. 2019; Mattos et al. 2020).
