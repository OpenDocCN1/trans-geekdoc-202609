
## Role of Smart Contracts in Blockchain

Blockchain offers several advantages, including decentralization, persistence, anonymity and auditability. Cryptocurrency, banking sectors, risk assessment, the Internet of Things (IoT) and individual and public services are all examples of Blockchain applications. Because of the rapid expansion of cryptocurrencies, as well as its fundamental Blockchain technology, frameworks such as Hyperledger and Ethereum have emerged to enable a wide range of smart contracts. Smart contracts are computational systems designed to digitally ease, validate or control the implementation of new contracts. Smart contracts have a variety of applications in different domains, including investment banking economic models and the IoT, among others. This chapter covers the introduction to Blockchain technology along with its characteristics and types. It provides detailed information about smart contracts, their working, platforms, programming languages and application areas. It also covers the applications of Blockchain technology in different domains and concludes with the challenges in implementing smart contracts.

### 2.1. Introduction to Blockchain

Recently, cryptocurrency has piqued the interest of both practitioners and academicians. Bitcoin is generally referred to as the original cryptocurrency. With the capital market surpassing $10 billion in 2016, accomplishment has been achieved (CoinDesk 2016a). The Blockchain is Bitcoin's central mechanism. Blockchain technology was initially suggested in 2008 and was deployed in 2009 (Bitcoin n.d.).

Blockchain is similar to a distributed ledger, for all completed events recorded in a chain of blocks. As more blocks enter the system, it continues to grow. Decentralization, persistence, anonymity and auditability are important properties of Blockchain technology. Blockchain may operate in a decentralized environment by combining many basic technologies such as cryptographic hashing, digital signatures and a distributed consensus process. As a result, Blockchain has the potential to significantly reduce costs while also increasing efficiency. Furthermore, as the quantity of provider user information and vulnerable devices rises, the deployment of secure, reliable and verified services is critical. Unfortunately, trust is becoming increasingly rare as the amount of information thefts at monolithic technology companies rises, exposing huge amounts of private data. Centralization, a fundamental flaw in many software architectures, provides an uneven relationship among users and suppliers, making the personalized security of electronic information impossible while offering a single point of failure for hackers to attack.

Blockchain is a decentralized, irreversible transaction ledger in which every transaction is inextricably linked to the one before it. Working in untrusted distributed environments may well be safeguarded by both the ledger of information and distributed agreement on the authenticity of the transaction, as the Blockchain technology’s fundamental purpose. Furthermore, transactions incorporate operational code, allowing software services to be provided between untrusted users. Since it was initially used in cryptocurrencies, interest in Blockchain technology has grown in both industry and academia, as they assess the applicability and functioning of the underlying Blockchain technologies.

To enable Blockchain decentralization, nodes involved in transactions and block generation should be able to verify the validity of new updated blocks to the chain. In this scenario, the nodes should reach a consensus since there is no trustworthy centralized method to make such a judgment. Various consensus techniques have been used to achieve such an accord (Katal et al. 2021). These versions must all work towards the same goal: identifying whether transactions inside the Blockchain are legitimate by verifying the activity of each new block introduced to the chain. The differences are in who can add blocks and at what rate, as well as the issue used to reach agreement. Consensus is commonly used to describe “mining”, or the solution of a complicated problem that is easily verifiable but difficult to overturn.

The method by which a miner contributes additional blocks to the network is known as mining. Each block in a chain has its own distinct nonce and hash, and connects to the hash of a block before it is in the network, making the mining of a block challenging, particularly in big chains. Miners use sophisticated programs to tackle the extremely difficult mathematics problem of creating a valid hash. Making changes to any prior block in the chain involves re-mining not only the altered block, but also all following blocks. Hence, it is so difficult to manipulate Blockchains. Consider it “math safety”, because finding golden nonces requires a considerable amount of effort and computational power. Whenever a block is properly processed, the alteration is accepted by all nodes in the system and the miner is rewarded economically. Figure 2.1 shows the working of Blockchain technology.

### 2.1.1. Types of Blockchain

Public Blockchain: Anyone with Internet connectivity may use Blockchain technology in the public Blockchain (Idelberger et al. 2016). Everyone has access to the transaction details and the verification of the same. Members of the public Blockchain may engage throughout the consensus protocol. Bitcoin and Ethereum are two mechanisms that make use of public Blockchain technology.

- Hybrid Blockchain: a hybrid Blockchain’s information is either private or public. There are hybrid Blockchain systems that are partially decentralized. A Hyperledger is one of the major examples of approaches that employ hybrid Blockchain systems.

- Private Blockchain: the write privileges of the blocks in a private Blockchain are managed by a specific authority, whilst the read permission is either public or limited. The range of nodes that can join this Blockchain is restricted (Pilkington 2016). The Blockchain’s transaction information is not accessible to every node. This Blockchain is extremely safe.

### 2.1.2. Characteristics of Blockchain

- Decentralization: each operation in typical centralized transaction processing systems should be verified by a trusted centralized agency (e.g. the central bank). A Blockchain transaction can be performed between any two identities (P2P) without verification by a central authority. As a result, Blockchain has the potential to significantly reduce hosting expenditures (both planning and production costs), while also eliminating compatibility issues in the centralized computer.

- Persistence: since each transaction propagated over the network must be validated and stored in blocks dispersed across the entire system, every transmitted transaction will also be validated by many other peers and checked. As a result, any fabrication may be quickly recognized.

- Anonymity: a created address may be used by any entity to join the Blockchain system. Moreover, to avoid disclosing their identity, a person may create a huge number of addresses. There is no longer a centralized entity that safeguards the confidential information of the user. This method ensures a certain level of anonymity for the Blockchain’s transactions. It should be noted that Blockchain cannot ensure complete anonymity.

- Auditability: since each transaction on the Blockchain is verified and timestamped, users can easily track and audit prior records by connecting to the node in the decentralized system. Each operation in the Blockchain network may be monitored back to prior transactions repeatedly. It optimizes the transparency and traceability of Blockchain-stored data.
