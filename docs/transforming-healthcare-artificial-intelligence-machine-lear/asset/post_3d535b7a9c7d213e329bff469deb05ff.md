## 18.1 INTRODUCTION

Because of the rapidly declining ozone layer and fast-increasing global air pollution, more people are being diagnosed with skin cancer than all other cancers combined [1]. When compared to other kinds of skin cancer, melanoma has a very high death rate [2]. Melanocytes, or cells in the skin's outer layer, are said to be the ones that produce melanin, a pigment found in human skin, according to research on the science of skin cancer [3,4]. Different human bodies create diverse quantities and sorts of melanin imparts colour to the skin while concealing it from UV radiation from the sun. Numerous factors, as as extended sun exposure, an abundance of peculiar moles, certain skin types, and a family history of melanoma, might result in skin cancer [5]. Even melanoma has a high death rate, if it is discovered early, there should be a 99% probability of survival [6]. Because of this proximity, even experienced dermatologists may struggle to differentiate between benign and malignant tumours. To increase categorisation accuracy, dermatologists use a few techniques, such as the ABC rule (Aptical, Border, Colour, and Diameter), albeit human understanding is still required [7].

While researchers have extensively investigated the automatic interpretation of dermatoscopic images using neural networks, the field has recently gained traction and exhibited promising results compared to physicians [8]. Assessing skin illness using clinically obtained (macroscopic) images and a neural network is also feasible. However, this approach has been demonstrated to be revised to classify different diseases [9]. In clinical practice, dermatologists seldom employ a single modality to evaluate their patients. Instead, they have one or more in-person meetings when they converse with face to face. Doctors can examine each lesion thoroughly by combining dermatoscopic images with patient data (such as the time of change, onset and approximate age, gender, and location of sickness) and a clinical view [10].

The efficacy of skin cancer screening depends critically on the accuracy and competence of dermatologists doing skin examinations to diagnose issues [11]. However, in skin cancer screening settings, dermatologists achieve sensitivities higher than 80% [12]. The first study was conducted in 2017 on a CNN for image classification purposes. During training, this CNN also established its diagnostic criteria for the diagnosis of melanoma. Several articles by other authors have suggested that CNNs produced merely results in clinical practice to support medical diagnosis, as they may be more accurate than dermatologists in distinguishing melanoma [13,14]. In histology images, deep learning (DL) has recently been shown to be more successful than pathologists in recognising nevi and melanoma [15]. Nevertheless, a binary classification problem does not capture the reality of skin cancer screening, which necessitates taking entities from other disorders into account. Hence, a hybrid layered learning model is needed to encounter all these issues.

Even skilled dermatologists, distinguishing between normal and abnormal tumours is complex, highlighting the shortcomings of the methods used for diagnosis. This unites research into cutting-edge technology to improve classification precision. The study highlights the significance of integrating dermoscopic images with patient data and clinical assessment for thorough evaluation using neural network models, acknowledging the need for a holistic approach to skin cancer diagnosis. In addition, to address the challenges posed by the previous method, a new detection model is proposed, including the following contributions for efficient skin detection with results. The contributions are:

- The Gaussian filter model improves image quality by reducing noise, preserving edges, and enhancing image contrast, ensuring the efficacy of ensuing detection algorithms.
- The Attention U-Net approach pointed segments the images effectively to enhance the interpretability of the images for a more accurate and consistent diagnosis of cancer deformations.
- Grey Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) gives useful information by measuring the spatial correlations between pixel intensities in preprocessed images
- Compact feature representations that maintain discriminative information for cancer detection are identified with the aid of Adaptive White Shark Optimisation Algorithm (AWSO).
- A hybrid attention-based DCNN-BilSTM model is proposed to efficiently integrate spatial and temporal data. This model uses attention to focus features selectively and allows for end-to-end learning for optimal performance.
