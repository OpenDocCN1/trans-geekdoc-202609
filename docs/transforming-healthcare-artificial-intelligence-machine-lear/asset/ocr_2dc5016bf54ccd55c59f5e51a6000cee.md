#  18.1 INTRODUCTION

Because of the rapidly declining ozone layer and fast-increasing global air pollution, more people are being diagnosed with skin cancer than all other cancers combined [1]. When compared to other kinds of skin cancer, melanoma has a very high death rate [2]. Melanocytes, or cells in the skin's outer layer, are said to be the ones that produce melanin, a pigment found in human skin, according to research on the science of skin cancer [3,4]. Different human bodies create diverse quantities and sorts of melanin imparts colour to the skin while concealing it from UV radiation from the sun. Numerous factors, as as extended sun exposure, an abundance of peculiar moles, certain skin types, and a family history of melanoma, might result in skin cancer [5]. Even melanoma has a high death rate, if it is discovered early, there should be a 99% probability of survival [6]. Because of this proximity, even experienced dermatologists may struggle to differentiate between benign and malignant tumours. To increase categorisation accuracy, dermatologists use a few techniques, such as the ABC rule (Aptical, Border, Colour, and Diameter), albeit human understanding is still required [7].

While researchers have extensively investigated the automatic interpretation of dermatoscopic images using neural networks, the field has recently gained traction and exhibited promising results compared to physicians [8]. Assessing skin illness using clinically obtained (macroscopic) images and a neural network is also feasible. However, this approach has been demonstrated to be revised to classify different diseases [9]. In clinical practice, dermatologists seldom employ a single modality to evaluate their patients. Instead, they have one or more in-person meetings when they converse with face to face. Doctors can examine each lesion thoroughly by combining dermatoscopic images with patient data (such as the time of change, onset and approximate age, gender, and location of sickness) and a clinical view [10].
