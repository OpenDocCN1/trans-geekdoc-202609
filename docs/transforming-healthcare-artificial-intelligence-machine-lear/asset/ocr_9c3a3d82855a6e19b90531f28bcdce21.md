Low-income families frequently can't afford a balanced diet, so they eat a lot of cheap, high-calorie, low-nutrient items. Malnutrition can also be caused by societal and cultural norms that influence dietary preferences and methods of weaning (Motadi et al., 2023). For example, in certain communities, gender bias causes girls to receive less food and treatment, which raises their risk of malnutrition. Malnutrition rates are also influenced by environmental variables. Children's capacity to absorb and use nutrients may be hampered by inadequate sanitation, a lack of clean drinking water, and greater exposure to diseases, even in circumstances where food is readily available. Consequently, the frequency of chronic ailments like diarrhea and respiratory infections rises, exacerbating the state of malnutrition.

Effective prediction and prevention techniques are as important as a thorough understanding of the causes of childhood malnutrition to effectively address the issue. Recent advancements in data science and healthcare, particularly with regard to the application of deep learning (DL), neural networks (NNs), and machine learning (ML) algorithms, have created new opportunities for the diagnosis and treatment of malnutrition. These methods can detect children who are at danger of malnutrition at an early stage, allowing for prompt interventions to stop the disease from spreading (Guo and Zhang, 2020). To determine the probability of malnutrition in a certain population, predictive models can examine a number of indicators, including underweight, wasting, and stunting. Focusing on treatments where they most needed legislators and healthcare professionals to more efficiently distribute resources. The field of ML algorithms demonstrated significant potential in this aspect. Random Forest and Logistic Regression models, for example, have demonstrated efficacy in forecasting malnutrition by considering several characteristics such as socioeconomic status, availability of healthcare, and eating habits.

To promote fairness, openness, and the protection of vulnerable people, ethical standards must govern the creation and use of predictive models for childhood malnutrition. When gathering information about children and their families, informed permission is essential because it guarantees that participants are aware of the possible uses for their data as well as the risks and advantages associated with it. Allocating resources is rarely another important ethical consideration. To guarantee that interventions are perceived through various socioeconomic categories and geographical, predictive models must be created without bias. Furthermore, it's important to guard against stigmatizing and discriminating against kids who have been diagnosed as at risk of malnutrition and maintaining community trust and the long-term viability of predictive models depends on transparency in data gathering and model development (Fournier-Tombs, 2023). For predictive models to be applied in a way that is both population-driven and sensitive to cultural differences, policymakers and healthcare professionals need to collaborate closely with local stakeholders.
