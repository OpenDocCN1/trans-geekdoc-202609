ACL injuries may vary in severity from minor sprains or to major tears of the ligament. Both contact and non-contact injuries are common when the limb is not in contact and is accompanied by internal rotation and valgus stress. The incidence of ACL injuries seems to be more prevalent in women than in men, ranging from 2.4 to 9.7 times more frequent in female athletes participating in comparable sports, with ACL ruptures becoming typical trauma.

There are several ways in which the ACL can be strained or damaged. The most frequent technique is a quick rotation or cut while participating in sports. Crashes in cars or at work may potentially cause the ligament to rip. Occasionally, a “pop” or “snap” may have been observed or experienced at the location of the damage. Although it can vary, discomfort at the site of damage can be quite intense. Usually, an individual feels as though they have suffered severe harm and cannot continue to participate in sports or engage in other activities. As soon as the knee is injured, during the first few hours, inflammation occurs. However, if the knee gets chilled or splintered right away, the amount of inflammation can be reduced.

Typical, ACL injuries are categorized as grades 1, 2, or 3 [6]:

**Grade 1**: ACLs with minimal injury, such as those that are slightly strained still give the knee joint enough stability, are classified as grade 1 damages.
**Grade 2**: Describes a slightly torn and stretched ACL. Grade 2 injuries are infrequent.
**Grade 3**: ACL tears of this grade occur when the ACL is totally torn in two, leaving the knee joint without any support.

The following are the most typical signs of an ACL tear: A “Pop” Sound: Several claim to have felt or heard a pop in the knee at the moment of the injury. (2) Intense Pain: Sudden, excruciating knee pain that frequently makes it unable to perform an activity. (3) Immediate Inflammation: In the initial hours following a sporting event, inflammation typically takes place. (4) Uncertainty: A sensation of unsteadiness or the knee “giving away,” particularly when bending or pivoting is involved. (5) Restricted Range of Movement: Inability to fully bend or extend the knee. (6) Trouble Carrying Weight: Discomfort or agony happens when an individual walks on the afflicted limb [7].
