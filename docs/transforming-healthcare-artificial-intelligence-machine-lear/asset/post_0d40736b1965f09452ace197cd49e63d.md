## 4.1 INTRODUCTION

Childhood malnutrition is a major global health concern that has catastrophic effects on children's physical and mental development. The World Health Organization (WHO) states that malnutrition accounts for nearly 45% of all pediatric fatalities globally, making it a serious public health concern (Mahmood et al., 2022). Malnutrition is a worldwide problem, but it is more severe in low- and middle-income countries due to a number of social, cultural, environmental, and economic factors that contribute to its increased occurrence. The rate of malnutrition in India is startlingly high. Despite notable advancements in healthcare and economic prosperity, childhood malnutrition is still a problem. According to the Global Hunger Index (GHI) 2020, India is 94th out of 107 countries, indicating that the country still has major issues with malnutrition. The National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) shows that 35.5% of children under five (too short for their age) are wasted (too thin for their height), and 32.1% are underweight (too thin for their age) (Argwaw et al., 2023). These numbers highlight how malnutrition is complex and pervasive in India. The high rate of malnutrition in India is caused by several factors, such as low income, poor sanitation, little knowledge of appropriate child feeding practices, and restricted access to healthcare and nutrient-dense food. The COVID-19 epidemic has made matters worse by upsetting food supply networks, raising unemployment, and driving more families into poverty, all of which harm children's nutritional outcomes.

Children's malnutrition is a complex issue that is fueled by a wide range of variables, including cultural and environmental influences, poverty, poor access to nutrient-dense food, and inadequate healthcare. The interaction of these raises the danger of malnutrition and makes treatment more challenging. Due to restricted access to nourishing food and healthcare facilities, children from underprivileged populations in India—especially those living in rural areas—are disproportionately affected. Economic issues are a major contributor to the persistence of malnutrition.

Low-income families frequently can't afford a balanced diet, so they eat a lot of cheap, high-calorie, low-nutrient items. Malnutrition can also be caused by societal and cultural norms that influence dietary preferences and methods of weaning (Motadi et al., 2023). For example, in certain communities, gender bias causes girls to receive less food and treatment, which raises their risk of malnutrition. Malnutrition rates are also influenced by environmental variables. Children's capacity to absorb and use nutrients may be hampered by inadequate sanitation, a lack of clean drinking water, and greater exposure to diseases, even in circumstances where food is readily available. Consequently, the frequency of chronic ailments like diarrhea and respiratory infections rises, exacerbating the state of malnutrition.

Effective prediction and prevention techniques are as important as a thorough understanding of the causes of childhood malnutrition to effectively address the issue. Recent advancements in data science and healthcare, particularly with regard to the application of deep learning (DL), neural networks (NNs), and machine learning (ML) algorithms, have created new opportunities for the diagnosis and treatment of malnutrition. These methods can detect children who are at danger of malnutrition at an early stage, allowing for prompt interventions to stop the disease from spreading (Guo and Zhang, 2020). To determine the probability of malnutrition in a certain population, predictive models can examine a number of indicators, including underweight, wasting, and stunting. Focusing on treatments where they most needed legislators and healthcare professionals to more efficiently distribute resources. The field of ML algorithms demonstrated significant potential in this aspect. Random Forest and Logistic Regression models, for example, have demonstrated efficacy in forecasting malnutrition by considering several characteristics such as socioeconomic status, availability of healthcare, and eating habits.

To promote fairness, openness, and the protection of vulnerable people, ethical standards must govern the creation and use of predictive models for childhood malnutrition. When gathering information about children and their families, informed permission is essential because it guarantees that participants are aware of the possible uses for their data as well as the risks and advantages associated with it. Allocating resources is rarely another important ethical consideration. To guarantee that interventions are perceived through various socioeconomic categories and geographical, predictive models must be created without bias. Furthermore, it's important to guard against stigmatizing and discriminating against kids who have been diagnosed as at risk of malnutrition and maintaining community trust and the long-term viability of predictive models depends on transparency in data gathering and model development (Fournier-Tombs, 2023). For predictive models to be applied in a way that is both sensitive to cultural differences, policymakers and healthcare professionals need to collaborate closely with local stakeholders.

## 4.3 CHILD MALUTRITION

Millions of children worldwide suffer from malnutrition, which has a long-term negative impact on their development, health, and economic well-being. Effective interventions to combat child malnutrition are multifaceted and target both immediate and underlying causes. Improving the food habits of infants and early children is one of the main strategies. This encourage exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months and then introducing supplemental foods that are age- and nutrient-appropriate. Proper education on nutrition for caregivers is essential to ensure children receive adequate nourishment. Micronutrient supplementation is another vital approach, particularly in regions with widespread deficiencies. Iron, vitamin A, and zinc are commonly provided to address deficiencies that can lead to stunting, weakened immune systems, and increased susceptibility to infections.

Improving food security and access to clean water are fundamental in preventing malnutrition. Food distribution programs, cash transfers, and support for smallholder farmers can help ensure a stable supply of nutritious food. Additionally, access to safe drinking water and sanitation facilities helps to prevent diseases that exacerbate malnutrition. Finally, integrating healthcare services, such as regular growth monitoring, deworming, and treatment.
