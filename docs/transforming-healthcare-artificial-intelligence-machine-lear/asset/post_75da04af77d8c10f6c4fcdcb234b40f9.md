ACL damages are more common in New Zealand and Australia than in other nations, with 58.2 and 77.4 per 100,000 individuals, respectively. The incidence of ACL injuries among individuals between the ages of 6 and 18 has risen through the previous 20 years by at least 2.3% per year, with greater rates in females 16 years and boys 17 years old [51]. Greater involvement in exercise and sports may contribute to the rise in ACL tears amongst kids and teenagers by raising the likelihood of rupture. Knee injury registers are kept up to date by numerous nations, athletics teams, and organizations worldwide. ACL tears constitute 86.5% of knee harm, according to observational research conducted in northern India. According to the research, 44.3% of the instances of ACL tear occurred in individuals between the ages of 18 and 25.

The knee's ACL serves a number of purposes, including:

- Knee Stability: The ACL maintaining knee stability, particularly in restraining the shin bone from moving out front of the thigh bone.
- Deterring Twisting: The ACL assists in keeping the knee from twisting or deteriorating.

- Defying Rotation: The ACL aids in preventing the tibia's internal rotation in relation to the femur.
- Shock Absorption: If the foot strikes the ground, the ACL aids in shock absorption.

ACL injuries may vary in severity from minor sprains to major tears of the ligament. Both contact and non-contact injuries are common when the limb is not in contact and is accompanied by internal rotation and valgus stress. The incidence of ACL injuries seems to be more prevalent in women than in men, ranging from 2.4 to 9.7 times more frequent in female athletes participating in comparable sports, with ACL ruptures becoming typical trauma.

There are several ways in which the ACL can be strained or damaged. The most frequent technique is a quick rotation or cut while participating in sports. Crashes in cars or at work may potentially cause the ligament to rip. Occasionally, a “pop” or “snap” may have been observed or experienced at the location of the damage. Although it can vary, discomfort at the site of damage can be quite intense. Usually, an individual feels as though they have suffered severe harm and cannot continue to participate in sports or engage in other activities. As soon as the knee is injured, during the first few hours, inflammation occurs. However, if the knee gets iced or splintered right away, the amount of inflammation can be reduced.

Typical ACL injuries are categorized as grades 1, 2, or 3 [6]:

- **Grade 1**: ACLs with minimal injury, such as those that are slightly strained still give the knee joint enough stability, are classified as grade 1 damages.
- **Grade 2**: Describes a slightly torn and stretched ACL. Grade 2 injuries are infrequent.
- **Grade 3**: ACL tears of this grade occur when the ACL is totally torn in two, leaving the knee joint without any support.

The following are the most typical signs of an ACL tear:
- (1) A “Pop” Sound: Several claim to have felt or heard a pop in the knee at the moment of the injury.
- (2) Intense Pain: Sudden, excruciating knee pain that frequently makes it unable to perform an activity.
- (3) Immediate Inflammation: In the initial hours following a sporting event, inflammation typically takes place.
- (4) Uncertainty: A sensation of unsteadiness or the knee “giving away,” particularly when bending or pivoting is involved.
- (5) Restricted Range of Movement: Inability to fully bend or extend the knee.
- (6) Trouble Carrying Weight: Discomfort or agony happens when an individual walks on the afflicted limb [7].

## 104 Transforming Healthcare

ACL injuries can result from a variety of situations, including unexpected stops, rapid running direction changes, improper jumps or landings, contact injuries like football tackles, and excessive use of the leg from repetitious impact activities like sprinting, jumping, bending, or turning.

The patient's signs and symptoms, examination findings, the amount of growth still present in their growth plates, the kind of ligament damage, and the desired level of sports and activity all influence the options for therapy [8].

- **Nonsurgical**: For grade 1 injuries, treatment without surgery is typically effective. This requires restraining or immobilization, physical rehabilitation, and a cautious return to games and everyday tasks.
- **Surgical**: For patients with a grade 3 or full ACL injury, surgery is advised. The kind of ACL injury, the patient's open or closed growth plates, and other factors may affect the surgical alternatives. The ACL is one of the ligaments in the knee that can be repaired or replaced with an ACL surgery. The ACL, which joins the femur to the tibia, is essential for maintaining knee stability, particularly during activities that require abrupt ends, orientation shifts, and hops. ACL injuries may arise from slips or crashes, though they are predominant among athletes and people who participate in strenuous activities. ACL tears and other injuries can cause instability in the knee, make mobility hard, and increase the possibility of osteoarthritis of the knee.

Surgery is advised in cases where the ACL is serious, or the patient wishes to return to games or other pursuits with a stable knee, the knee is unstable following physical therapy or recovery, or if there is additional damage to the knee, such as meniscus, cartilage, or other ligaments.

A doctor can identify an ACL injury by taking the patient's medical records and doing a physical checkpoint. Examination of the other knee tissues is also carried out since ACL tears are frequently seen in conjunction with damage to other knee tissues, including the collateral ligaments and cartilage. MRI imaging can be used to validate the diagnosis of damage to the ACL; clinical determination is still possible. The most prevalent method for diagnosing ACL instability is MRI, which has an 86% sensitivity and a 95% specificity. Knee arthroscopy could be employed for diagnosis to differentiate among total, incomplete, and persistent tears [9]. Since arthroscopy is intrusive and necessitates anesthesia, it is not frequently used as the first step in diagnosis, despite being the gold standard due to its 92%–100% sensitivity and 95%–100% specificity. To check if there are fractures, X-rays are used. X-rays can show symptoms of osteoarthritis in the knee and any related bone fractures, but they cannot demonstrate soft tissues.

When there is a chance of complicated fractures, a CT scan may be advised to evaluate the bones. An MRI takes the internal arrangement of the body using powerful radio waves. ACL tears, for example, are better detected by MRI than other types of injuries. MRI scans the knee in three different planes: sagittal, coronal, and axial. MRI provides a highly precise way to recognize the different grades of injuries. The sagittal, or side view, of the magnetic resonance slice provides the simplest method for portraying the ligaments. MRI validates the diagnosis and determines whether any injuries are present. To define the severity of the ACL tear and to find further information, MRI is usually requested. MRI can produce precise pictures of soft tissues, such as cartilage and ligaments.

With deep learning (DL), convolutional neural network (CNN) models perform well in the classification of microscopic pictures. Computer vision research, especially in the field of medicine, has difficulties in accurately classifying the bone tissues and categorizing the outcome of segmentation using MRI; image segmentation is therapeutically significant. ACL injuries have been classified scans using DL models applied to MRI in a number of earlier investigations.

This paper presents an AlexNet that uses MR images to autonomously identify ACL damage. After a few seconds of hyperparameter adjustment, the suggested model accurately predicted the torn ACL injuries to identify osteoarthritis. Additionally, our work contributes to the advancement of a modified CNN that is able to classify healthy and ruptured ACLs following modifying hyperparameters. Additionally, it plots and evaluates accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity in order to thoroughly examine the outcomes of the experiment. The suggested methodology may be applied to quickly identify ACL tears in athletes in addition to hospitalized individuals with osteoarthritis.
