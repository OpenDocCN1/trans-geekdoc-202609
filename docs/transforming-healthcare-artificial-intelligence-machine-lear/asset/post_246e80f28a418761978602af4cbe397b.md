
## 5.2 RELATED WORKS

Xue et al. developed a fully automated approach featuring a DL model for ACL tissue segmentation (ACL-DNet) and a classifier for ligament status sensitivity (ACL-SNet) [11]. The efficacy of the method was calculated by metrics like sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC), comparing it to a group of three orthopedists on a holdout test set. The ACL-DNet achieved a Dice coefficient of 98%±6% on MRI datasets, while the classification demonstrated 97% sensitivity and 97% specificity. In contrast, alternative models had sensitivity ranging from 84% to 90% and specificity between 86% and 92%.

Liu and Wu developed an advanced DL model that integrated T-distribution-based slice attention mechanism with a penalty weight loss function to enhance ACL detection [12]. The T-slice attention module created a robust slice filtering system, while incorporating class relationships and replacing traditional cross-entropy loss with a penalty loss function significantly boosted classification accuracy. This approach improved diagnostic performance across six different backbone networks, with the VGG-Slice-Wheat model achieving an AUC of 0.9590.

Souras et al. introduced a highly discriminative novelty score that utilizes aleatory semantic uncertainty from the classes of the YOLOv5-nano object detection model [13]. For tissue density, they continue using global scores (probability vectors) when applying to the entire sagittal segment. The second part of their pipeline involves the MINIRECT series classification model to assess ACL tears in knees. To evaluate generalization, they conducted cross-database testing with two public databases (KneeMRI and MRNet) and a validation-only database from the University General Hospital of Larissa, Greece. Their method consistently outperformed out-of-the-art approaches on the KneeMRI dataset (p-value<0.05) and achieved higher accuracy and sensitivity over the MRNet dataset.

Zhang et al. presented a new approach for diagnosing ACL tears using a CNN optimized by Modified Golden Search Algorithm (MGSA) [14]. This method effectively extracts key features from MRI images of the knee with an optimized CNN architecture, achieving an impressive accuracy of 99.6%, surpassing other methods.

Wang et al. proposed a lightweight attention graph neural network (GNN) with a conditional random field (CRF), called ACGN, for classifying ACL ruptures in knee MR images [15]. They utilized a metric-based meta-learning strategy for independent testing through multiple node classification tasks and designed a lightweight feature embedding network using knowledge distillation to extract image features. GNN layers were employed to identify sample dependencies and complete the classification, with refining attentions in each GNN layer. To address overshooting and overfitting, they applied self-boosting attention, node attention, and memory attention for graph initialization, node updating, and layer correlation.

Zuo et al. proposed a novel approach to enhance ACL tear detection accuracy using an optimized Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) model, which captures the temporal features of MRI images [16]. This optimization employs the Flexible Fitness Dependent Optimizer Optimizer (FFDO), a problem-specific algorithm that generates and evaluates candidate solutions based on a fitness function. Applied to the MRNet dataset, this method outperformed existing state-of-the-art approaches, achieving a high accuracy of 97.95% for ACL tear, surpassing existing DL and machine learning methods.

Chan et al. developed a method for automatically detecting ACL tears in MRI images using a Hybrid Tasmanian Optimization (HTDO) algorithm [17]. The GRU network, based on Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), analyzes sequential data to detect temporal relationships. HTDO combines the strengths of Tasmanian Devil and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO). Tested on an MRI dataset of knee joints without ACL injuries, the approach outperformed other techniques in identifying ACL tears.

Joshi et al. created an effective method for detecting ACL tears using a convolutional neural network (ATD-CNN), which simplifies network complexity [18]. They added a self-attention mechanism to improve feature representation from MRI images, enhancing classification accuracy by filtering out information. To combat class imbalance, a generative adversarial network (GAN) generated a synthetic dataset. The ATD-CNN with self-attention achieved 90.10% accuracy on the original MRNet dataset and 93.93% on the augmented dataset, while the version without attention recorded 89.60% and 92.30%, respectively.

Wang and Yan proposed a methodology using a custom variant of the Snow Leopard Optimization Algorithm, called the Fractional-Snow Leopard Optimization Algorithm (FO-LOA), to extract features from MRI images, significantly improving ACL tear detection accuracy [19]. By effectively capturing critical features, this approach enhances diagnostic precision, reducing false negatives and positives. The FO-LOA model was rigorously tested on the MRNet dataset, achieving impressive performance metrics: 98.00% accuracy, 98.00% sensitivity, 97.00% precision, 98.00% specificity, 98.00% F1-score, and 88.00% Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC).

Wang et al. proposed a method with three components: Dual-Scale Data Augmentation (DDA) to enhance training data at both spatial and layer scales, a selective group (SG) attention module to capture relationships across various scales, and a fusion module to explore interrelationships for final classification [20]. Using a public MRNet dataset of knee MRI scans from 1,250 examples in axial, coronal, and sagittal views, their model, SGNET, demonstrated superior performance in ACL tear detection, achieving an accuracy of 0.9250, sensitivity of 0.9259, specificity of 0.9242, and an AUC of 0.9747.

Voeia et al. investigated the use of pre-trained convolutional networks with residual connections (ResNet) and image-processing techniques to identify ACL injuries and classify tear levels [21]. Their adapted ResNet 3D volumes created from 2D slices achieved peak accuracies of 97.15% with a custom split and 96.32% through Monte Carlo cross-validation, improving performance.

Cedeno-Moro et al. introduced a framework using stacked autoencoders (SAE) for analyzing ground reaction force (GRF) signals from the GaitRec database [22]. The analysis identifies features for knee ruptures and classifies their severity as high, mid, or mild. The process includes two stages: detecting the injury and evaluating features for severity. The framework achieved 87% accuracy and 90% F1-Score for detecting ruptures, as well as 86.5% accuracy with 87% F1-Score for severity classification.

In conclusion, a number of techniques for the automatic categorization of ACL rupture in MRI have been proposed from various sources used in this research. Furthermore, many methods were employed to calculate the efficacy of the models, which have shortcomings and require a lot of time even when they are automated. The fastest detection of knee problems is a tough endeavor due to the heterogeneity in MR images.

## 5.3 DATASET DESCRIPTION

From 2006, the Clinical Hospital Centre Rijeka in Croatia collected the KneeMRI dataset prospectively from examination reports performed on a Siemens Avanto 1.5T MR scanner using the proton density-weighted suppression technique [23]. Around 917 12-bit grayscale volumes of the left or right knee make up the dataset. Out of 172 partial ruptures (18.8%), 55 entirely ruptured (6.0%), and 690 healthy patients (75.2%) made up the collection. A double-blind method was used to diagnose the ACL in every volume recording. Specifically, each volume record labeled as either (1) healthy, (2) partially injured, or (3) entirely ruptured.
