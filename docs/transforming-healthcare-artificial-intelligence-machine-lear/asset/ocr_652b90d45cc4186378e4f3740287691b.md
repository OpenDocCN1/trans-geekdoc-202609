The patient's signs and symptoms, examination findings, the amount of growth still present in their growth plates, the kind of ligament damage, and the desired level of sports and activity all influence the options for therapy [8].

Nonsurgical: For grade 1 injuries, treatment without surgery is typically effective. This requires restraining or immobilization, physical rehabilitation, and a cautious return to games and everyday tasks.

Surgical: For patients with a grade 3 or full ACL injury, surgery is advised. The kind of ACL injury, the patient's open or closed growth plates, and other factors may affect the surgical alternatives. The ACL is one of the ligaments in the knee that can be repaired or replaced with an ACL surgery. The ACL, which joins the femur to the tibia, is essential for maintaining knee stability, particularly during activities that require abrupt ends, orientation shifts, and hops. ACL injuries may arise from slips or crashes, though they are predominant among athletes and people who participate in strenuous activities. ACL tears and other injuries can cause instability in the knee, make mobility hard, and increase the possibility of osteoarthritis the knee.

Surgery is advised in cases where the ACL is serious, or the patient wishes to return to games or other pursuits a stable knee, the knee is unstable following physical therapy or recovery, or if there is additional damage to the knee, such as meniscus, cartilage, or other ligaments.

A doctor can identify an ACL injury by taking the patient's medical records and doing a physical checkpoint. Examination of the other knee tissues is also carried out since ACL tears are frequently seen in conjunction with damage to other knee tissues, including the collateral ligaments and cartilage. MRI imaging can be used to validate the diagnosis of damage to the ACL, clinical determination is still possible. The most prevalent method for diagnosing ACL instability is MRI, which has an 86% sensitivity and a 95% specificity. Knee arthroscopy could be employed for diagnosis to differentiate among total, incomplete, and persistent tears [9]. Since arthroscopy is intrusive and necessitates anesthesia, it is not frequently used as the first step in diagnosis, despite being the gold standard due to its 92%–100% sensitivity and 95%–100% specificity. To check if there are fractures, X-rays are used. X-rays can show symptoms of osteoarthritis in the knee and any related bone fractures, but they cannot demonstrate soft tissues like the ACL. It is common to order a knee MRI for patients. If the medical records and assessment yield no clear answers, the scan can help resolve the likelihood of an ACL tear. If the scans yield information that is essential to define the best course of action for a particular patient, it also also be utilized to assess the meniscus or cartilage in the knee.
