
Finally, “cloud regression” encompasses the `general linear model` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_linear_model)], the `generalized linear model` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_linear_model)] (and thus logistic regression), as well as `weighted least squares` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_least_squares#Weighted_least_squares)] (and thus Bayesian regression). Via the mapping `z ↦ w`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si3.png) discussed in Section [1.1.1](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#s0015), it can accommodate splines as in `adaptive regression splines` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_adaptive_regression_spline)]. Both cloud regression and the `total least squares` method [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_least_squares)] minimize the sum of the squared distances between the data points and the shape, though my method does not give the response (called the dependent variable by statisticians) a particular status: in other words, it also works in the standard situation where there is no response, but just a cloud of points instead. In addition, my technique handles truly nonlinear situations, unlike the generalized linear model. For that reason, I call it the mother of all regressions.

This is not the first time a regression technique does not discriminate between dependent and independent variables: `partial least squares` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_least_squares_regression)] also allows for that. See also [[5]](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#br0025).

## 1.1.1 Previous Versions of My Method

The current version is much more general, simpler, and radically different from the first implementation. However, it may help to provide some historical context. Initially, the goal was to compute the sum of the squared distances between a set of points (the observations, or the “cloud”), and a prespecified shape `Γ[θ]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si4.png) (a line, plane, or circle) belonging to a parametric family driven by a multidimensional parameter `θ`.

The idea was as follows. Let `P[∞]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si5.png) be a fixed point located extremely far away from the shape, and `P` be a point of the training set. Draw the line `L[P]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si6.png) that goes through `P` and `P[∞]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si5.png), and find the intersection `Γ[θ] ∩ L[P]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si7.png) closest to `P`, between the shape and the line. Let `Q[θ]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si8.png) be this point. The point in question may not be unique or may not exist (depending on `θ`), but the distance `Δ[θ](P) = ||P - Q[θ]||`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si9.png) is, assuming there is an intersection. Then find `θ^(*)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si10.png) that minimizes the sum of `Δ[θ](P)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si11.png) computed over all training set points `P`. This `θ^(*)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si10.png), if unique, determines the shape that best fits the data. Traditional projection-based techniques compute the exact distance between a point and a shape, and therefore require the shape to be differentiable. The method based on `P[∞]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si5.png) works with shapes that are not differentiable. Some particular cases in the new implementation produce similar or identical results to those obtained with the `P[∞]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si5.png) method.

If the shape in question is a hyperplane and the context is traditional multivariate linear regression, then the shape is defined by `g(w, θ) = 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si12.png) where `w = (y, x[1], …, x[m])`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si13.png) and `g(w, θ) = θ[0]y + (θ[1]x[1] + ⋯ + θ[m]x[m])`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si14.png). Here `y` corresponds to the dependent variable, `x[1], …, x[m]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si15.png) to the features, and `θ[0], θ[1], …, θ[m]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si16.png) are the regression coefficients, with the constraint `θ[0] = -1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si17.png). In the new methodology, the constraint `θ[0] = -1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si17.png) is handled using a Lagrange multiplier, but other than that it leads to the same traditional solution. If there is an intercept, then `x[1] = 1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si18.png). In the end, the goal is to propose a technique that is both general and intuitive, following the modern trend of explainable AI [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explainable_artificial_intelligence)].

In a second version of my methodology (not the current version), I introduced a mapping system, which essentially is a change of coordinates associated to a link function. The goal was to transform the data so that after the mapping, it is more amenable to a simple solution. Also, it is an attempt at obtaining a scale-independent solution: whether your unit is a mile or a kilometer should have no impact on the solution. In its most general form, the observations and parameters are denoted as `z` and `φ`. The shape satisfies the equation `h(z, φ) = 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si19.png). The mapping is defined as `g(w, θ) = ξ(h(z, φ))`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si20.png) where `ξ: ℝ → ℝ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si21.png) is a strictly monotonic function, with `w = ν(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si22.png) and `θ = ϕ(φ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si23.png), for some multivariate one-to-one mappings `ν` and `ϕ`. All the computations are done in the `(w, θ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si24.png)\-space, thought it is possible to revert back to the original `(z, φ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si25.png) when computations are done, if ever needed.

I eventually dropped both `ξ` and simply ignored `φ` and `ϕ`, leading to a less abstract model, yet covering all practical cases. Thus in the current version, `h(z, φ) = g(w, θ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si26.png), and we do not care about `φ`. We may as well use `φ = θ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si27.png). The mapping `ν` gives rise to spline regression in the new method. However, when splines are used, they are prespecified rather than estimated, to avoid overfitting. Typically, they are chosen to simplify the computations.
