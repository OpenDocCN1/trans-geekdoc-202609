
```
$A^{n} = \left\{ \begin{array}{rlr} & {r_{1}^{n}Q_{1} + r_{2}^{n}Q_{2}} & {\quad\quad\text{~if~}r_{1} \neq r_{2},} \\ & {r_{1}^{n}Q_{1} + nr_{1}^{n - 1}Q_{2}} & {\quad\quad\text{~if~}r_{1} = r_{2},} \\ \end{array} \right.
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si11.png)(3.2)

with

```
$\left\{ \begin{array}{rlr} & {Q_{1} = (A - r_{2}I)/(r_{1} - r_{2})} & {\quad\quad\text{~if~}r_{1} \neq r_{2},} \\ & {Q_{2} = (A - r_{1}I)/(r_{2} - r_{1})} & {\quad\quad\text{~if~}r_{1} \neq r_{2},} \\ & {Q_{1} = I} & {\quad\quad\text{~if~}r_{1} = r_{2},} \\ & {Q_{2} = A - r_{1}I} & {\quad\quad\text{~if~}r_{1} = r_{2}.} \\ \end{array} \right.
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si12.png)(3.3)

Here the symbol `I` represents the 2 × 2![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si1.png) identity matrix. The last four relationships were obtained by applying formula [(3.2)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq6) to `A^n`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si13.png), with `n` = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si6.png) and `n` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si7.png). It is easy to prove (by recursion on `n`) that [(3.2)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq6), together with [(3.3)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq7), is the correct solution. If none of the roots is zero, then the formula is still valid for `n` =  − 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si2.png), and thus it can be used to compute the inverse of `A`.

## 3.2 Examples, generalization, and matrix inversion

For a `p` × `p`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si14.png) matrix, the methodology generalizes as follows. The quadratic polynomial becomes a polynomial of degree `p`, known as the **characteristic polynomial**, and linked to the **Cayley–Hamilton theorem** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley%E2%80%93Hamilton_theorem)]. If its roots are distinct, we have

```
$A^{n} = \sum\limits_{k = 1}^{p}r_{k}^{n}Q_{k},\quad\text{with~}\left( \begin{array}{r} Q_{1} \\ Q_{2} \\ \vdots \\ Q_{p} \\ \end{array} \right) = V^{- 1}\begin{pmatrix} I \\ A \\ \vdots \\ A^{p - 1} \\ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} {1\quad} & {1\quad} & {\ldots\quad} & 1 \\ {r_{1}\quad} & {r_{2}\quad} & {\ldots\quad} & r_{p} \\ {\vdots \quad} & {\vdots \quad} & {\ddots \quad} & \vdots \\ {r_{1}^{p - 1}\quad} & {r_{2}^{p - 1}\quad} & {\ldots\quad} & r_{p}^{p - 1} \\ \end{pmatrix}^{- 1}\begin{pmatrix} I \\ A \\ \vdots \\ A^{p - 1} \\ \end{pmatrix}$
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si15.png)(3.4)

The matrix `V` is a **Vandermonde matrix** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandermonde_matrix)], so there is an explicit formula to compute its inverse, see [[Wiki](https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Inverse_of_Vandermonde_Matrix)]. For a fast algorithm for the computation of its inverse, see [[9]](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#br0045). However, inverting `V` should be avoided as it is a **numerically unstable** procedure. Instead, approximations methods based on selected roots of the characteristic polynomial – those with highest or lowest moduli – are preferred. They are discussed in Section [3.4.2](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#s0075).

The determinants of `A` and `V` are respectively equal to

```
$|A| = {( - 1)}^{p}r_{1}r_{2}\cdots r_{p},|V| = \prod\limits_{1 \leqslant k < l \leqslant p}(r_{k} - r_{l}).$
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si16.png)

Note that the roots can be real or complex numbers, simple or multiple, or equal to zero. Usually, the roots are ordered by decreasing modulus, that is,

```
|r_1| ≥ |r_2| ≥ |r_3| ≥ ⋯ ≥ |r_p|.
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si17.png)

That way, a good approximation for `A^n`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si13.png) is obtained by using the first three or four roots if `n` > 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si18.png), and the last three or four roots if `n` < 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si19.png). In the context of linear regression, one of the main problems consists of inverting a matrix, that is, using `n` =  − 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si2.png) in formula [(3.4)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq8). Working with the first three roots only is equivalent to performing a **principal component analysis** (PCA) [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_component_analysis)] as well as PCA-induced dimension reduction. This technique can be used for data compression.

If some roots have a multiplicity higher than one, the formulas must be adjusted. The solution can be found by looking at how to solve an homogeneous linear recurrence equation. See Theorem 4 in Section 8.2 of “Math 55 Lecture Notes” [[3]](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#br0015), taught at Berkeley University.

### 3.2.1 Example with a noninvertible matrix

Even if `A` is noninvertible, some useful quantities can still be computed when `n` =  − 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si2.png), not unlike using a **pseudoinverse matrix** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore%E2%80%93Penrose_inverse)] in the **generalized linear model** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_linear_model)] in regression analysis. Let us look at the following example, using the methodology previously discussed:

```
$A = \begin{pmatrix} {1\quad} & 2 \\ {2\quad} & 4 \\ \end{pmatrix}\Longrightarrow A^{n} = 5^{n} \cdot \frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} {1\quad} & 2 \\ {2\quad} & 4 \\ \end{pmatrix} + 0^{n} \cdot \frac{1}{5}\left( \begin{array}{rr} {4\quad} & {- 2} \\ {- 2\quad} & 1 \\ \end{array} \right).$
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si20.png)

Note that `0^n` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si21.png) if `n` = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si6.png). The rightmost matrix attached to the second root 0 is of particular interest, and plays the role of a pseudoinverse matrix for `A`. If that second root was very close to zero rather than exactly zero, then the term involving the rightmost matrix would largely dominate in the expression of `A^n`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si13.png), when `n` =  − 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si2.png). At the limit, some ratios involving the (nonexistent!) inverse of `A` still make sense. For instance,

*   •  The sum of the elements of the inverse of `A`, divided by its trace, is (4 − 2 − 2 + 1)/(4 + 1) = 1/5![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si22.png).
*   •  The arithmetic mean divided by the geometric mean of its elements, is 1/2.

### 3.2.2 Fast computations

If `n` is large, one way to efficiently compute `A^n`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si13.png) is as follows. Let us say that `n` = 100![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si23.png). Do the following computations:

```
A² = A ⋅ A,  A⁴ = A² ⋅ A²,  A⁸ = A⁴ ⋅ A⁴,  A^(16) = A⁸ ⋅ A⁸,  A^(32) = A^(16) ⋅ A^(16),  A^(64) = A^(32) ⋅ A^(32),  A^(100) = A^(64) ⋅ A^(32) ⋅ A⁴.
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si24.png)

This can be useful to quickly get an approximation of the largest root of the characteristic polynomial, by eliminating all but the first (largest) root `r_1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si25.png) in formula [(3.4)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq8), and using `n` = 100![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si23.png). Once the first root has been found, it is easy to also get an approximation for the second, and then for the third. If, instead, you are interested in approximating the smallest roots, you can proceed the other way around, by using the formula for `A^n`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si13.png), with `n` =  − 100![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si26.png) this time. Note that `A^(-100)` = (`A^(-1)`)^(100)![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si27.png).
