
## 17.3

This section covers a large class of functions, starting with truncated modified Dirichlet series to assess the status of the hole in the orbit. I then move back to infinite Euler products in Section [17.3.2](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0070), but this time not over the full set of primes as in Section [17.2.2](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0045), but instead on infinite subsets arising from additive number theory. Some of these functions have no root if `σ` > `σ[0]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si99.png), with (say) `σ[0]` = 5/6![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si132.png). They thus satisfy a weaker version of GRH, called quasi-GRH. Section [17.3.3](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0095) covers non-Dirichlet functions that do not have an Euler product, but behave like the Dirichlet eta function `η(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si29.png) with regard to the orbit and its hole. Here `P` is the set of all primes, but the sine and cosine attached to `p^(-z)` = `σ^(-z)`cos(`t`log`p`) − `i``σ^(-z)`sin(`t`log`p`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si133.png) are now replaced by wavelets. Section [17.3.4](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0100) deals with noninteger primes (even matrices) that mimic the behavior of prime integers, and called Beurling primes. They come with Euler products too, and the corresponding Dirichlet series is now called a Dedekind zeta (or eta) function. Section [17.3.5](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0105) deals with random Dirichlet functions: their interest lies in the fact that the corresponding (random) Euler products converge, albeit conditionally, when $\frac{1}{2} < \sigma \leqslant 1$![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si134.png). Thus they satisfy a probabilistic version of GRH, in particular the absence of root if $\sigma > \frac{1}{2}$![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si17.png).

### 17.3.1 Finite Dirichlet series

Formula [(17.1)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq2) features a finite (Euler) product. However, the corresponding series, on the left-hand side, is infinite. Here I discuss a different approach, using a finite version containing the first `n` terms of the full Dirichlet series when `P` is the set of all prime numbers. The new function is defined as

$\eta(z,\beta,n) = \sum\limits_{k = 1}^{n}\delta_{k}\lambda_{k}p_{k}^{- z},$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si135.png)(17.8)

where `λ[k]` = 1/`k`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si136.png) for all `k` except `k` = 2![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si137.png). The coefficient `λ[2]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si138.png) is denoted as `β`. Here, `δ[k]` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si42.png) if `k` is odd, otherwise `δ[k]` = −1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si44.png). If `n` is infinite and `β` = 1/2![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si139.png), then `η(z, β, n)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si140.png) coincides with the standard Dirichlet eta function.

Despite the finite number of terms in the series, this approach is considerably more difficult. Unlike in Section [17.2.1](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0035), there is no simple product (finite or infinite), to represent the truncated `η` function. While the approach in Section [17.2.1](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0035) has a strong number theory flavor, here we are dealing with approximations and numerical analysis. Yet, the case `n` = 3![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si141.png) is trivial: the orbit fills a ring. The hole is a circle centered at (1, 0)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si142.png), not at the origin. Both the interior and exterior boundaries of the orbit are circles, with known radius. See [Exercise 17.4](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#enun0030) for a complete solution.

But the general case is much more complicated. Of course, the series corresponding to the finite Euler product results in an orbit with a solid hole of radius >0, always encompassing the origin, and not shrinking to a singleton when you display the full, infinite orbit. But that series always has an infinite number of terms. As you increase the number of terms in the truncated series, keeping the first `n` terms only and `β` = 1/2![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si139.png), the hole disappears when `n` ≥ 5![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si143.png) is small enough, only to reappear when `n` > 50![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si144.png). The hole then stays there all the way to `n` = ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si145.png). As `n` increases, it eventually shifts to the left on the X-axis, to encompass the origin. For instance, at ![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/fx002.jpg), the origin is inside the hole. This is based on visually inspecting the orbit at `σ` = 0.90![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si146.png), with ![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/fx003.jpg); see [Fig. 17.6](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#f0035).

For small values of `n` with no hole, increasing `β` is one way to reintroduce the hole in the orbit, as pictured in [Fig. 17.5](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#f0030). This leads to a possible new path to explore RH: using a large `n`, with `β` > 1/2![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si147.png) (the larger `β`, the larger the hole), and let `β` → 1/2![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si148.png) as `n` → ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si125.png). In [Exercise 17.4](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#enun0030), I discuss some conditions that guarantee the presence of a hole encompassing the origin, for small values of `n`.

### 17.3.2 Nontrivial cases with infinitely many primes and a hole

Now, let us get back to Euler products. Thus the associated Dirichlet series (the expansion of the product) always contains infinitely many terms, even if the product is finite. For a given `σ`, in order to get a hole encompassing the origin, with the distance between the orbit and the origin always ⩾ `ρ` at all times `t`, the following must be satisfied:

$\rho = \prod\limits_{p \in P}\frac{1}{1 + p^{- \sigma}} > 0.$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si150.png)(17.9)

This is briefly discussed at the beginning of Section [17.2.2.1](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0050). Note that this requirement is independent of the function `χ`. It is satisfied if the product is finite (that is, if the set `P` is finite), or if `σ` > 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si19.png), or if `σ` < 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si9.png) and `P` is infinite but sparse enough so that the product converges. This section discusses the latter.
