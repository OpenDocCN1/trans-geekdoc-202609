
It is more difficult, though possible, to obtain the higher moments `E[N^(r)(B)]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si25.png) or `P[N(B)=r]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si26.png) in closed form if `r>2`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si27.png). This is due to the combinatorial nature of the Poisson–binomial distribution. But you can easily obtain approximated values using simulations. Note that as `s→∞`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si8.png), the process tends to a Poisson process. Thus the point count distribution tends to a Poisson distribution. The convergence of the Poisson–binomial distribution to the Poisson distribution is known as `Le Cam's theorem` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Cam%27s_theorem)].

### 16.1.2 Periodicity and amplitude of point count expectations

For the sake of simplicity, I consider the one-dimensional case here. In short, the lattice is `ℤ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si28.png), and `(X[h], Y[k])`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si4.png) is replaced by `X[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si29.png) with `k∈ℤ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si30.png). The results extend to the 2D case, with double periodicity: one for each component.

Let `(X[k])`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si31.png), with `k∈ℤ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si30.png), represents the points of a one-dimensional Poisson–binomial process of intensity `λ` and scaling factor `s`. We are interested in point counts `N[τ](t)=N[B[τ](t)]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si32.png) in the interval `B[τ](t)=[t, t+τ]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si33.png). Let

`ϕ[τ](t)=E[N[τ](t)]`.

![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si34.png)

By virtue of Theorem 4.1 in [[20]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0100) (page 50), we have `ϕ[τ](t)=1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si35.png) if `τ=1/λ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si36.png). More generally, regardless of `τ`, the function `ϕ[τ](t)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si37.png) is periodic of period `1/λ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si38.png). That is, `ϕ[τ](t)=ϕ[τ](t+1/λ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si39.png). This latter statement is also true for `Var[N[τ](t)]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si40.png), `P[N[τ](t)=0]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si41.png), and `P[N[τ](t)=1]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si42.png). This fact is trivial if you look at formulas [(16.3)](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#deq4), [(16.4)](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#deq5), [(16.5)](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#deq6), and [(16.6)](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#deq7), used to compute the four quantities in question.

The amplitude of the oscillations is extremely small even with a scaling factor as low as `s=0.3`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si43.png) (assuming `F` is logistic). It quickly tends to zero as `s→∞`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si8.png). So the process is almost stationary, unless `s` is very close to zero. Thus, in most inference problems, the choice of the (nonoverlapping) intervals has very little impact. In particular, `ϕ[τ](t)≈λτ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si44.png). The small amplitude of `ϕ[τ](t)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si37.png) is pictured in [Fig. 16.1](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0010).

![Figure 16.1 Period and amplitude of ϕτ(t); here τ = 1, λ = 1.4, s = 0.3.](images/B9780443218576000205/gr001.jpg)

[Figure 16.1](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#cf0070) Period and amplitude of `ϕ[τ](t)`; here `τ=1`, `λ=1.4`, `s=0.3`.

The point counts divided by the length `τ` of the interval, especially averaged over a number of nonoverlapping intervals represent an excellent estimator of the intensity `λ`, regardless of the scaling factor `s`. In this simulation, `τ=1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si45.png), the true theoretical value is `λ=1.4`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si46.png), and the estimated value oscillates between 1.39975 and 1.4025. The `boundary effects` are ignored here, by focusing on intervals that are not too close to the observation window. Otherwise the estimator would be biased. In two dimensions, the interval is replaced by a square, and the length is replaced by the square root of the area, to estimate `λ`.

### 16.1.3 Testing the independence of point counts

As in Section [16.1.2](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#s0020), I illustrate the method in the one-dimensional case. Generalization to 2D is straightforward. I want to assess whether the point count distribution `N(B)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si19.png) in various nonoverlapping domains `B` are independent or not. Generally, one works with domains of same area. The most popular test of independence is the `χ²`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si47.png) (`Chi-squared`) test [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-squared_test)]. One drawback of `χ²`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si47.png) is that it requires binning the data. The bin size cannot be too small, and the bins may be arbitrary. My approach is different, and avoids this problem. It is also well suited to detect small deviations from independence.

It works as follows. I compare the empirical distribution of count frequencies with what it should be if the counts were independent. I offer two solutions: one based on the `R-squared` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficient_of_determination)], and one based on the `Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic` [[Wiki](https://bit.ly/3uJMMNK)]. The latter is similar to the approach discussed by Zhang in his article “A Kolmogorov–Smirnov type test for independence between marks and points of marked point processes” [[45]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0225), available online [here](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/electronic-journal-of-statistics/volume-8/issue-2/A-Kolmogorov-Smirnov-type-test-for-independence-between-marks-and/10.1214/14-EJS961.full).

Let `(X[k])`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si31.png) be the points of a Poisson–binomial process `M[A]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si48.png) of intensity `λ=1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si49.png) and scale factor `s=0.7`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si50.png), with a logistic `F`. Exercise 10 in [[20]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0100) (page 60) shows – using theoretical arguments – that the point counts are not independent. This proves that Poisson–binomial processes are different from Poisson processes. Here I establish the same conclusion via statistical testing. The purpose is to illustrate how the test works, so that you can use it in other contexts. I chose three intervals `B[1]=[-1.5, -0.5]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si51.png), `B[2]=[-0.5, 0.5]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si52.png), and `B[3]=[0.5, 1.5]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si53.png). The data consists of ![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/fx001.jpg)realizations of the process in question, each one consisting of 41 points `X[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si29.png), `k=-20,…,20`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si54.png). The number 41 is large enough in this case, to eliminate `boundary effects`. The data, computations and results are in the spreadsheet [PB_independence.xlsx](https://github.com/VincentGranville/Point-Processes/tree/main/Spreadsheets), described later in this section.
