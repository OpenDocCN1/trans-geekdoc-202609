
Let `p[1]` = 2![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si32.png) and `P` = {`p[1]`, `p[2]`, …, `p[d]`}![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si33.png) be a set of primes, listed in increasing order. Let `Q` = {`q[1]`, `q[2]`, `q[3]`, …}![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si34.png) be the set of all `p[1]^(a[1]) p[2]^(a[2]) p[3]^(a[3])`⋯![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si35.png) where the `a[i]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si36.png)'s are positive integers (including zero). The elements `q[1]`, `q[2]`, …![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si37.png) are also listed in increasing order. Thus `q[1]` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si38.png) and `q[2]` = 2![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si39.png).

The Dirichlet eta function `η_P(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si40.png) induced by `P` is then defined as

$$
\eta_P(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \delta_k q_k^{-z} = (1 - 2^{1-z}) \prod_{p \in P} \frac{1}{1 - p^{-z}},
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si41.png)(17.1)

where `δ_k` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si42.png) if `q_k`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si43.png) is odd, and `δ_k` = -1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si44.png) otherwise.

The **Euler product** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_product)] in formula [(17.1)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq2) is finite and has `d` factors, while the infinite series always converges. Indeed, it can be proved (see [Exercise 17.1](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#enun0015)) that

$$
q_k \sim \exp\left[ k^{1/d} \left( d! \prod_{p \in P} \log p \right)^{1/d} \right] \quad \text{as } k \rightarrow \infty.
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si45.png)(17.2)

If `P` is the set of all prime numbers, and thus `d` = ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si46.png), then the product in formula [(17.1)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq2) converges if `σ` > 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si19.png) while the **alternating series** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_series)] converges if `σ` > 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si30.png). This can be proved using the **Dirichlet test** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet%27s_test)], see [here](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/1042512/proof-of-convergence-of-dirichlets-eta-function). For this reason, the series is called the **analytic continuation** of the product [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_continuation)]. This is the reason why we are interested in the alternating series, where `δ_k` ∈ {−1, +1}![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si47.png), rather than in the series with `δ_k` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si42.png): the latter, equal to the infinite product, diverges if `σ` ≤ 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si48.png). This issue occurs only if `d` = ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si46.png). See [Exercise 17.3](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#enun0025) for details. When the product diverges, the alternating series converges, but it is not **absolutely convergent** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_convergence)].

Now let `τ_z` = |1 - 2^(1 - z)|![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si49.png). Assuming `z` = `σ` + `i t`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si2.png), the distance between `η_P(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si40.png) and the origin is equal to

$$
|\eta_P(z)| = \tau_z \prod_{p \in P} \frac{1}{|1 - p^{-z}|} = \tau_z \prod_{p \in P} \frac{1}{|1 - 2p^{-\sigma}\cos(t\log p) + p^{-2\sigma}|} > \tau_z \prod_{p \in P} \frac{1}{1 + p^{-\sigma}}.
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si50.png)(17.3)

Here |·|![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si51.png) stands for the distance to the origin, and referred to as the **modulus** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_value#Complex_numbers)] in complex analysis. An immediate consequence of formula [(17.3)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq4) is this: if `σ` ≠ 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si52.png) is fixed and strictly positive, and if `P` only has a finite number of primes, then there is always a zone around the origin, with a strictly positive area, that the orbit of `η_P(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si40.png) will never hit or cross. This is illustrated in [Fig. 17.1](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#f0010). The zone in question corresponds to a hole in the orbit, also called **repulsion basin** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractor)] in dynamical systems, for the red (`σ` = 0.5![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si53.png)) and blue orbit (`σ` = 0.75![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si252.png)). For the yellow orbit (`σ` = 1.25![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si55.png)), the origin is outside the boundary of the orbit. The origin in [Fig. 17.1](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#f0010) is the black dot in the white area. Note that the “center” of the hole, in all three cases, is not the origin, but further to the right on the X-axis. See [here](https://youtu.be/uMRp9FruOqE) the video corresponding to `P` = {2, 3, 5, 7}![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si56.png), and [here](https://youtu.be/MDBaJ8RsLA8) for `P` = {2, 3, 5}![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si57.png).

As you add more and more primes in `P`, the hole shrinks. In the end, when `d` = ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si46.png), the hole shrinks to a single point (the origin) if we plot the whole orbit, rather the restricted orbit to `t` ∈ [0, `T`]![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si58.png) with `T` = 1000![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si59.png), as in [Fig. 17.1](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#f0010). Also, the center of the hole moves to the left on the X-axis (real axis) as `σ` is decreased from (say) 1.25 to 0.55.

For a fixed `T`, the center of the hole is denoted as `z_0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si60.png). It lies on the X-axis, and depends both on `T` and `σ`. It is tempting to scale the `η` function and replace it by $\sqrt{t} \cdot (\eta_P(z) - z_0)$![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si61.png), in the hope that when `P` is the full set of prime numbers and `T` → ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si22.png), the hole does not shrink to a singleton or an empty set. However, no matter how fast growing the scaling factor is (as a function of `t`), the **universality property** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeta_function_universality)] of the Dirichlet eta function implies that this goal cannot be achieved. Generally speaking, working on finite Euler products and taking the limit `d` → ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si62.png), while very tempting, does not seem to lead to a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, despite numerous attempts by many mathematicians including myself.

### 17.2.1.1 Generalization using Dirichlet characters

Formula [(17.1)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq2) can be generalized as follows:

$$
\eta_P(z,\chi) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{\Omega(q_k)} q_k^{-z} = (1 - 2^{1-z}) \prod_{p \in P} \frac{1}{1 - \chi(p) p^{-z}},
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si63.png)(17.4)

where `χ(p)` ∈ {−1, +1}![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si15.png). The product and the series may have different domains of convergence. However, on the domain where both converge, they have the same roots (except when 2^(1 - z) = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si64.png), that is, when `σ` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si65.png) and `t` = 2`m`π/log 2![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si66.png) with `m` an integer).
