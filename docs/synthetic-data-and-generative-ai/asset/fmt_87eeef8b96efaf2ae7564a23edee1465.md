
Finally, I was interested in some original dimension-reduction technique. Not a true data-reduction technique, but it allows you to reduce the number of parameters by a factor of 2: consider `w` and `θ` to be complex, rather than real numbers, for instance, via a mapping `z` ↦ `w`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si3.png) from ℝ²![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si28.png) to ℂ![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si29.png), with `w` = `ν(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si22.png). A benefit is the possibility to use a `conformal map` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_map)] for `ν`, thus preserving angles. Such an example is the `log-polar map` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log-polar_coordinates)] `z` = exp(`w`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si30.png) with `g(w, θ)` = `z^θ` = exp(`θ w`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si31.png), which corresponds to using the polar coordinate system with `θ`, `z`, `w` ∈ ℂ![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si32.png): it makes things easier when dealing with circular data. The next step was to look at quaternions to reduce the number of parameters by a factor of 4, but there are a number of challenges. Anyway, I promised to keep things simple in this introductory presentation, so I will not discuss complex or quaternion mappings here. This is the topic of future research.

It is interesting to note that the problem of circle fitting has been quite extensively studied, see [[6]](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#br0030). Essentially, these methods solve the problem using `φ` and they are not trivial. The solution based on my method involves working with `θ` and leads to a very classic algorithm with a simple solution. The price to pay is that the `θ` parameters are less obvious to interpret: they are the coefficients of a quadratic form. To the contrary, the direct solution involves `φ` parameters that have obvious meaning: the radius of the circle, its center, and (in case of an ellipse) the rotation angle. However, my approach makes it a lot easier to generalize to ellipses and even far more complicated shapes, or hyperplanes for that matter, while at the same time having a solution that is even simpler than those discussed in [[6]](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#br0030) and applicable to the circle only. Of course, in this case there is a one-to-one mapping between `φ` and `θ`, see [here](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/1810677/center-and-axis-of-quadratic-surface). So you can always retrieve `φ` from `θ`.

## 1.2 Methodology, implementation details, and caveats

I encourage you to first read Section [1.1.1](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#s0015), as it provides a good amount of context. This section describes the details of the methodology. For simplicity, I do not describe the most general case, but a case that is general enough to cover all practical applications. I start by introducing the concept of data (called point cloud), parameter, and shape.

The `data` set is denoted as `W`, and consists of `m` + 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si33.png) variables and `n` observations. Thus `W` is an `n` × (`m` + 1)![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si34.png) matrix as usual. The `k`th row corresponds to the `k`th observation `W[k]` = (`W[k, 0]`, `W[k, 1]`, …, `W[k, m + 1]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si35.png). For backward compatibility with traditional models, I use the notation `W[k, 0]` = `Y[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si36.png) for the dependent variable or response (if there is one), and (`X[k, 1]`, …, `X[k, m]`) = (`W[k, 1]`, …, `W[k, m + 1]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si37.png) for the independent variables of features. The column vector corresponding to the response is denoted as `Y`, and the `n` × `m`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si38.png) matrix representing the independent variables is denoted as `X`. The whole data set `W` is referred to as the point cloud.

The `parameter` is a multivariate column vector denoted as `θ` = (`θ[0]`, `θ[1]`, …, `θ[d]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si39.png), with `d` + 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si40.png) components. Typically, `d` = `m`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si41.png) and `θ` satisfies some constraint, specified by `η(θ)` = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si42.png) for some function `η`. The most common functions are `η(θ)` = `θ^T θ` − 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si43.png), `η(θ)` = `θ[0]` + 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si44.png), and `η(θ)` = (`θ[0]` + ⋯ + `θ[d]`) − 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si45.png). Here `^(T)` denotes the matrix/vector transposition operator.

The purpose is to fit a `shape` to the point cloud. The most typical shapes, after proper mapping, are hyperplanes or quadratic forms (ellipsoids). The former is a particular case of the latter. The shape belongs to a parametric family of equations driven by the multivariate parameter `θ`. The equation of the shape is `g(w, θ)` = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si12.png), for some function `g`. Typical examples include `g(w, θ)` = `wθ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si46.png) and `g(w, θ)` = `wθ` − 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si47.png), with `d` = `m`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si41.png). The former usually involves an intercept: `X[k, 1]` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si48.png) for all `k` = 1, …, `n`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si49.png). Keep in mind that `w` and `θ` are vectors, but `g(w, θ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si50.png) is a real number, not a vector. Thus `wθ` represents a `dot product` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_product)].

### 1.2.1 Solution, R-squared, and backward compatibility

The shape that best fits the data corresponds to `θ` = `θ^*`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si51.png), obtained by minimizing the squares:

```
\theta^{\ast} = \underset{\theta}{\arg\min}\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{n}g^{2}(W_{k},\theta).
```
![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si52.png)(1.1)

The solution may not be unique. Uniqueness and numerical stability will be addressed in a future article, but the basics are covered in this book. The constraint `η(θ)` = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si42.png) guarantees that the solution requires solving a (sometimes nonlinear) system of `d` + 2![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si53.png) equations with `d` + 2![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si53.png) unknowns. In some cases, `d` ≤ `m`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si54.png) to avoid `model identifiability` issues [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identifiability)]. Also, a large `d` may result in `overfitting` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfitting)]. Then, you want `n` > `d`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si55.png) otherwise the solution may not be unique unless you add more constraints on `θ`. The solution `θ^*`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si10.png) is obtained by solving the system

```
\left\{ \begin{aligned} {\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta}[ g^{2}(W_{k},\theta)]} & {= \lambda\nabla_{\theta}[\eta(\theta)],} \\ {\eta(\theta)} & {= 0,} \\ \end{aligned} \right.
```
![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si56.png)(1.2)
