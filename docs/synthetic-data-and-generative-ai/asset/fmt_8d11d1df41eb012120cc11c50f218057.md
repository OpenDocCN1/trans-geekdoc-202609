
Other unusual Brownian motions, this time in two dimensions, are discussed in Section [12.2.1](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml). They include a family of Brownian motions, exhibiting an extraordinary strong clustering structure, depending on model parameters. Some Gaussian processes can have a behavior similar to “Type 1” depending on parameters, see [here](https://www.r-bloggers.com/2019/07/sampling-paths-from-a-gaussian-process/). For Gaussian processes, see also [[8]](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#br0040), especially Chapter 4. Finally, the top parameter values (root modulus, multiplicity, and so on) can be grouped into clusters. Each cluster leads to a specific type of time series. It makes our time series simulator – called a `generative model` – useful to produce synthetic data with parameter values matching those estimated on real-life time series. See also Section [1.4](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml) on how to accomplish this in a different context.

### 3.4.2 Solving Vandermonde systems: a numerically stable method

`Vandermonde systems` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandermonde_matrix)] are notoriously `ill-conditioned` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condition_number)]. This explains why nobody really solves them, and instead, uses other techniques. Yet they could potentially be used to solve many problems, including linear regression and `eigenvalues` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors)] via the characteristic polynomial, `Lagrange interpolation` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runge%27s_phenomenon)], Markov chains (computation of the `stationary distribution`), linear recurrence equations, principal component analysis (`PCA`), autoregressive time series, square root of a matrix, and more.

Using the methodology presented in this chapter, I propose a numerically stable algorithm to solve such systems, in a very indirect way. I illustrate my method on a particular example. Let us consider the autoregressive process

$$
X_n = \frac{3}{4}X_{n-1} + \frac{7}{8}X_{n-2} - \frac{3}{4}X_{n-3} + \frac{1}{8}X_{n-4},
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si105.png)(3.8)

with no white noise. In other words, let `τ = 0` in formula [(3.7)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq27). The characteristic polynomial has roots `r_1 = 1, r_2 = -1, r_3 = 1/2, r_4 = 1/4`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si107.png). Let the initial conditions be `X[0] = 1, X[-1] = 0, X[-2] = 0, X[-3] = 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si108.png) as in formula [(3.6)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq26). Thus, I will be solving the `Vandermonde system`

$$
\left( \begin{array}{rrrr}
1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
1 & -1 & 2 & 4 \\
1 & 1 & 4 & 16 \\
1 & -1 & 8 & 64
\end{array} \right)
\begin{pmatrix} q_1' \\ q_2' \\ q_3' \\ q_4' \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si109.png)(3.9)

We know that

$$
X[n] = r_1^{(n)}q_1' + r_2^{(n)}q_2' + r_3^{(n)}q_3' + r_4^{(n)}q_4'.
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si110.png)(3.10)

Computing `X[n]` iteratively using formula [(3.8)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq29), we find that `X[30] ≈ 1.6000`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si111.png) and `X[31] ≈ 1.0667`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si112.png). The influence of `r[3], r[4]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si113.png) is negligible in formula [(3.10)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq31), and we can reasonably ignore these two roots if `n` is large enough. Thus, if `n = 30`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si114.png) we have `1.6000 ≈ q_1' + q_2'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si115.png), according to [(3.10)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq31). And `n = 31`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si116.png) yields `1.0667 ≈ q_1' - q_2'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si117.png). These two equations allow us to get a very good approximation both for `q_1'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si118.png) and `q_2'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si119.png). We do not care about `q_3'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si120.png) and `q_4'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si121.png) as the associated roots `r[3], r[4]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si113.png) have almost no impact on `X[n]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si48.png) when `n` is large.

However, we can still compute them if we want to. Consider the subrecursion `{Y[n]}`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si122.png) with characteristic polynomial `(x - r[3])(x - r[4])`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si123.png), that is, `Y_n = 3/4 Y_{n-1} - 1/8 Y_{n-2}`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si124.png). Let the initial conditions be

$$
Y[n] = X[n] - (r_1^{(n)}q_1' + r_2^{(n)}q_2'), \quad \text{for } n = 0, -1.
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si125.png)(3.11)

Use the approximated values `q_1' = 1.333`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si126.png) and `q_2' = 0.2667`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si127.png) computed in the previous step. Clearly, by design,

$$
Y[n] = r_3^{(n)}q_3' + r_4^{(n)}q_4',
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si128.png)(3.12)

with `r_3 = 1/2, r_4 = 1/4`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si129.png). Also, since `r[3]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si130.png) dominates over `r[4]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si131.png), for `n` large enough, we have `Y[n] ≈ r_3^{(n)} q_3'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si132.png), that is, `q_3' ≈ r_3^{(-n)} Y[n] ≈ 2^{(n)} Y[n]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si133.png). Using formulas [(3.11)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq32) and [(3.12)](#Chp003_49-60_B9780443218576000072.xhtml#deq33) combined with the approximated values of `q_1', q_2'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si134.png) and `n = 15`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si135.png), one eventually obtains `q_3' ≈ 2^{15} Y[15] ≈ -0.6667`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si136.png). Now we are left with finding the last coefficient `q_4'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si121.png). It can be done using the linear recurrence with characteristic polynomial `x - r[4]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si138.png). The details are left as an exercise. The exact values are

$$
q_1' = \frac{4}{3} = 1.3333\ldots,\quad
q_2' = \frac{4}{15} = 0.2666\ldots,\quad
q_3' = -\frac{2}{3} = -0.6666\ldots,\quad
q_4' = \frac{1}{15} = 0.0666\ldots
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000072/si139.png)
