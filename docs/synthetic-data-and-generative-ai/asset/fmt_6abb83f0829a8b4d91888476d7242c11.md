### 13.3.2 Connection to the Poisson–binomial distribution

Depending on the sequence of random deviates `U[b]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si91.png), the new function `f[0]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si20.png) may have zero, one, or more than one integer root in any specific interval `[s, t]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si92.png). The expected number of integer roots is a random variable `N(s, t)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si93.png) with a **Poisson–binomial distribution** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_binomial_distribution)]. Assuming `s` and `t` are integers, the expectation, variance, and probability of no root are respectively

```latex
$E\lbrack N(s,t)\rbrack = \sum\limits_{k = s}^{t}\frac{1}{k},\quad{Var}\lbrack N(s,t)\rbrack = \sum\limits_{k = s}^{t}\frac{1}{k}\left( 1 - \frac{1}{k} \right),\quad P\lbrack N(s,t) = 0\rbrack = \prod\limits_{k = s}^{t}\left( 1 - \frac{1}{k} \right).$
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si94.png)

When `s, t`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si95.png) are very large and `t/s → λ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si96.png), the Poisson–binomial distribution is well approximated by a Poisson distribution of expectation `log λ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si97.png). A proof of this advanced result (not even taught in graduate classes) can be found in my book on stochastic processes [[1]](#Chp013_243-258_B9780443218576000175.xhtml#br0005), in Section 2.3.1. This result generalizes the well-known convergence of Binomial to Poisson distribution under some assumptions. It is a particular case of **Le Cam's inequality** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Cam's_theorem)]; see also [[2]](#Chp013_243-258_B9780443218576000175.xhtml#br0010).

Applied to the problem in Section [13.2.2](#Chp013_243-258_B9780443218576000175.xhtml#s0030) with `f[0](b) = a mod b`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si68.png), with `b ∈ [2900, 3200]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si98.png), the chance to find at least one root in that interval is approximately `1 - exp(-log λ) = 1 - 1/λ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si99.png). Since `λ = t/s`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si100.png) with `s = 2900`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si101.png), `t = 3200`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si102.png), the chance in question is about 9.4%. Note that `$t < \sqrt{a}$`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si103.png). In general, this condition is required for the result to be valid. The absence of root in the interval `$[2, \sqrt{a}]$`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si104.png) would mean that `a` is a prime number.

#### 13.3.2.1 Location of the next root: guesstimate

Let `T[s]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si105.png) be the location of the first root larger than `s`. I am interested in the ratio `R[s] = T[s]/s > 1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si106.png). From the previous discussion, the distribution of successive roots approximately follows a **nonhomogeneous Poisson process** when `s` is large. It is easy to prove that `R[s]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si107.png) has an infinite expectation. However, `log R[s]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si108.png) has a finite expectation. Let us compute it, using the Poisson approximation. We have

```latex
P(log R[s] > τ) = P[T[s] > s exp(τ)] = exp(-τ).
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si109.png)

Thus,

```latex
E[log R[s]] = ∫[0]^∞ exp(-τ) dτ = 1.
```
