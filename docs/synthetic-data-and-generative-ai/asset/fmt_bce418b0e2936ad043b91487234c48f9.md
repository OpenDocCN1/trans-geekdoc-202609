
There are different ways to simulate `radial processes`; the most popular method uses a bivariate Gaussian distribution for the child process. Poisson point processes with `nonhomogeneous` radial intensities are discussed in my article “Estimation of the Intensity of a Poisson Point Process by Means of Nearest Neighbor Distances” [\[19\]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0095). The focus is on radial, and thus nonhomogeneous intensity functions: in this case `λ` depends on the location, as opposed to a stationary Poisson process where `λ` is constant. Estimating the `intensity function` of such a process is equivalent to a `density estimation` problem, using kernel density estimators [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_density_estimation)].

### 16.2.4 Superimposed perturbed lattices: an alternative to mixture models

When the points of `m` independent Poisson–binomial processes with same distribution `F` are bundled together, we say that the processes are `superimposed`. The result may no longer be Poisson–binomial, unlike the superimposition of standard Poisson processes (itself a special case of Poisson–binomial, with `s = ∞`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si106.png)). Indeed, if the scaling factor `s` is small and `m > 1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si107.png) is not too small, the resulting process exhibits clustering around each lattice location. Also, the intensities or scaling factors of each individual point process may be different, and the resulting process may not be homogeneous. Superimposed point processes also called `interlaced` processes.

I first describe mixtures before moving to interlaced processes. The two models share many similarities, but also some differences. A `mixture` of `m` point processes, denoted as `M`, is defined as follows:

- We have `m` independent point processes `M[1]`, …, `M[m]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si108.png) with same distribution `F`.
- The intensity and scaling factor attached to `M[i]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si109.png) are denoted respectively as `λ[i]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si110.png) and `s[i]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si111.png) (`i = 1, …, m`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si112.png)).
- The points of `M[i]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si109.png) (`i = 1, …, m`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si113.png) are denoted as (`X[i_h]`, `Y[i_k]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si114.png).
- The point (`X[h]`, `Y[k]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si4.png) of the mixture process `M` is equal to (`X[i_h]`, `Y[i_k]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si114.png) with probability `π[i] > 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si115.png), `i = 1, …, m`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si112.png).

The `π[i]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si116.png)'s are the mixture proportions, and their sum is equal to one. While mixing or superimposing Poisson–binomial processes seem like the same operation, which is true for stationary Poisson processes, in the case of Poisson–binomial processes, these are distinct operations resulting in significant differences when the scaling factors are very small. The difference is most striking when `s = 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si10.png). In particular, superimposed processes are less random than mixtures. This is due to the discrete nature of the underlying lattice. However, with larger scaling factors, the behavior of mixed and superimposed processes tend to be similar.

[Fig. 16.6](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0040) represents a realization of `m` superimposed shifted stretched Poisson–binomial processes, called `m-interlacing`. For each individual process `M[i]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si109.png), `i = 1, …, m`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si112.png), the distribution attached to the point (`X[i_h]`, `X[i_k]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si117.png) (with `h`, `k ∈ ℤ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si5.png)) is

```math
P(X_{ih} < x, Y_{ik} < y) = F\left( \frac{x - \mu_{i} - h/\lambda}{s} \right) F\left( \frac{y - \mu_{i}^{\prime} - k/\lambda^{\prime}}{s} \right), \quad i = 1,\ldots,m.
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si118.png)

This generalizes formula [(16.1)](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#deq1). The parameters used for the model pictured in [Fig. 16.6](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0040) are:

- Number of superimposed processes: `m = 4`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si119.png); each one displayed with a different color,
- Color: red for `M[1]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si120.png), blue for `M[2]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si121.png), orange for `M[3]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si122.png), black for `M[4]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si123.png),
- Scaling factor: `s = 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si10.png) (left plot) and `s = 5`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si124.png) (right plot),
- Intensity: `λ = 1/3`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si125.png) (X-axis) and `λ' = √3/3`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si126.png) (Y-axis),
- Shift vector, X-coordinate: `μ[1] = 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si127.png), `μ[2] = 1/2`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si128.png), `μ[3] = 2`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si129.png), `μ[4] = 3/2`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si130.png),
- Shift vector, Y-coordinate: `μ[1]' = 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si131.png), `μ[2]' = √3/2`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si132.png), `μ[3]' = 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si133.png), `μ[4]' = √3/2`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si134.png),
- `F` distribution: standard centered `logistic distribution` with zero mean and variance `π²/3`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si135.png).

For simulation purposes, the points (`X[i_h]`, `Y[i_k]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si114.png) of the `i`th process `M[i]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si109.png) (`i = 1, …, m`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si112.png)), are generated as follows:

```math
X_{ih} = \mu_{i} + \frac{h}{\lambda} + s \cdot \log\left( \frac{U_{ih}}{1 - U_{ih}} \right),
```
![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si136.png)(16.11)

```math
Y_{ik} = \mu_{i}^{\prime} + \frac{k}{\lambda^{\prime}} + s \cdot \log\left( \frac{U_{ik}}{1 - U_{ik}} \right),
```
![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si137.png)(16.12)

where `U[i_j]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si138.png) are uniformly and independently distributed on `[0, 1]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si104.png) and `−n ≤ h, k ≤ n`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si139.png). I chose `n = 25`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si140.png) in the simulation – a window much larger than that of [Fig. 16.6](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0040) – to avoid `boundary effects` in the picture. The boundary effect is sometimes called `edge effect`. The unobserved data points outside the window of observations, are referred to as `censored data` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censoring_(statistics))]. Of course, in my simulations their locations and features (such as which process they belong to) are known by design. But in a real data set, they are truly missing or unobservable, and statistical inference must be adjusted accordingly [\[15\]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0075).
