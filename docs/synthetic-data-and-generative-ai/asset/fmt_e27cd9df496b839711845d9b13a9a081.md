
The standard symmetric 1D **random walk** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_walk)] fundamental to this chapter is a sequence `{S[n]}` with `n ≥ 0`, starting at `S[0] = 0`, and recursively defined by `S[n] = X[n] + S[n−1]`, for `n > 0`. Here `X[1]`, `X[2]`, and so on, are independent random variables with `P[X_n = 1] = P[X_n = -1] = 1/2`. Thus `{S[n]}` is a time-discrete stochastic process, and indeed the most basic one. In Sections [12.1.2](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#s0020) and [12.1.3](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#s0040), I drop the assumption of independence, leading to modified random walks such as those described in [[6](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#br0030),[9]](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#br0045). More general references include [[1](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#br0005),[8]](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#br0040).

With proper rescaling, a random walk becomes a time-continuous stochastic process `S[t]` called **Brownian motion** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownian_motion)], with `t ∈ ℝ⁺`. See the time series in gray in [Fig. 12.1](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#f0010), displaying a particular instance: it shows the first values of `S[n]` in a short window, giving the appearance of a Brownian motion. By contrast, each of the orange, red, and gray time series represents one instance of a specific type of non-Brownian motion. Sections [12.1.2](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#s0020) and [12.1.3](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#s0040) focus on these three types of processes, which are quasi-, but not fully random.

### 12.1.1 Three fundamental properties of pure random walks

The standard random walk `{S[n]}` (illustrated in gray in [Fig. 12.1](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#f0010)) is the base or reference process, used to build more sophisticated models. It has too many properties to list in this short chapter. However, the following are the most relevant to our discussion:

- **Law of the iterated logarithm** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_iterated_logarithm)]. In our context, it is stated as follows:

$$
\limsup\frac{|S_{n}|}{\sqrt{2nv\log\log n}} = 1\quad\text{as~}n\rightarrow\infty.
$$

(12.1)

Here, as per **Hartman–Wintner theorem** [[Wiki](https://encyclopediaofmath.org/wiki/Law_of_the_iterated_logarithm)], `v = Var[X[1]] = 1`. See [[8]](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#br0040), pages 118–123, for a version adapted to Brownian motions.

- Expected number of **zero crossings** in `S[1], …, S[n]`, denoted as `N[n]`. Here a zero-crossing is an index `0 < k ≤ n` such that `S[k] = 0`. For `n > 0`, we have (see [here](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/1684576/expected-of-returns-in-a-symmetric-simple-random-walk))

$$
E\lbrack N_{2n}\rbrack = - 1 + \frac{2n + 1}{4^{n}}\begin{pmatrix} {2n} \\ n \\ \end{pmatrix} \sim \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \cdot \sqrt{n}\quad\text{as~}n\rightarrow\infty.
$$

- Distribution of **first hitting time** to zero [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-hitting-time_model)], or first zero crossing after `S[0] = 0`, also called time of first return. The random variable in question is denoted as `T`. It is defined as follows: `T = n` (with `n > 0`) if and only if `S[n] = 0` and `S[k] ≠ 0` if `0 < k < n`. We have `P[T = n] = 0` if `n` is odd, and `E[T] = Var[T] = ∞`. Yet, our random walks cross the X-axis infinitely many times. We also have the following **probability generating function** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability-generating_function)] (see [here](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/64919/biased-random-walk-and-pdf-of-time-of-first-return)):

$$
\sum\limits_{n = 1}^{\infty}{(2x)}^{2n}P\lbrack T = 2n\rbrack = 1 - \sqrt{1 - 4x^{2}}\quad\text{if~}x \leqslant \frac{1}{4}.
$$

From this one can obtain

$$
P\lbrack T = 2n\rbrack = \frac{1}{(2n - 1)4^{n}}\begin{pmatrix} {2n} \\ n \\ \end{pmatrix} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}} \cdot n^{- 3/2}\quad\text{as~}n\rightarrow\infty,\quad E\lbrack T^{- 1}\rbrack = \int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - 4x^{2}}}{x}dx = 1 - \log 2.
$$

Note that `E[T^(−1)]` is finite, while `E[T]` is infinite. The fact that `E[T] = ∞` explains why the sequence `S[n]` can stay above or below the X-axis for incredibly long time periods, as shown in [Fig. 12.1](#Chp012_229-241_B9780443218576000163.xhtml#f0010) for the gray curve.

The above three statistics `|S_{n}|/\sqrt{2n\log\log n}`, `N[2n]`, and `T^(−1)` can be used to design tests of randomness for pseudorandom number generators. Indeed, the **prime test** in Chapter [11](#Chp011_203-228_B9780443218576000151.xhtml) relies on a number-theoretic version of the law of the iterated logarithm (LIL). The purpose is to detect very weak departures from randomness, even in sequences that are random enough to pass the classic LIL test, yet not fully random. In this chapter, the goal is to simulate quasirandom sequences, rather than creating new tests of randomness.

I now describe special types of modified random walks that lack true independence in the sequence `{X[n]}`. In particular, I discuss why they are special and of great interest, with a focus on applications.
