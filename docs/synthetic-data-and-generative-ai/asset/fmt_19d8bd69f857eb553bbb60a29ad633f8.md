
### 17.3.4 Beurling primes

Beurling primes can be defined for objects other than numbers, like polynomials or matrices. For instance, let `A` be a square matrix, and define `p[k]` = `exp`(`μ[k]` `A`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si267.png), where the `μ[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si268.png)'s are distinct, strictly positive real numbers ordered by increasing values, and linearly independent over the set of positive integers, so that the factorization in `Q[P]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si199.png) is unique. For instance, `μ[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si268.png) is the logarithm of the `k`th prime integer. Any element (matrix) `q` ∈ `Q[P]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si259.png) can be written as

$$
q = p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots = \exp(|q|A) = \sum\limits_{k = 0}^{\infty}|q|^{k}\frac{A^{k}}{k!},\quad\text{~with~}|q| = \sum\limits_{k = 1}^{\infty}a_{k}\mu_{k}.
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si269.png)

Here `a[1]`, `a[2]`, …![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si258.png) are integers ⩾0 and `|q|`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si270.png) is called the norm. We can define an order on `Q` as follows: if `q`, `q'` ∈ `Q`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si262.png), then `q` < `q'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si271.png) if and only if `|q|` < `|q'|`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si272.png). Note that `q`, `q'`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si273.png), and the `p[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si112.png)'s are matrices. We can build Dirichlet series and Euler products for these primes, and study properties when $\frac{1}{2} \leqslant \sigma < 1$![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si10.png), as we do for standard prime integers. For more on Beurling primes, see [\[5\]](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#br0025) and [\[13\]](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#br0065).

### 17.3.5 Stochastic Euler products

There are different ways to randomize functions related to Euler products. You may want to randomize `L[P](z, χ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si107.png) or `η[P](z, χ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si88.png) using `complex random variables` [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_random_variable). Or you may want to randomize the real or imaginary parts of these functions, or their norm. These random products have gained a lot of interest recently, at they provide insights about RH and its generalized version, GRH. For a recent reference on `random Euler products`, see [\[15\]](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#br0075). I briefly discussed randomized multiplicative functions such as random `Rademacher functions` [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rademacher_distribution) in Section [17.2.2.2](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0055). More on this topic can be found in Chapter [11](#Chp011_203-228_B9780443218576000151.xhtml) and in [\[9](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#br0045)–[11](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#br0055),[15\]](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#br0075). My recent book on stochastic processes [\[8\]](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#br0040) discusses tiny random perturbations applied to Dirichlet series: it shows that the hole at the origin, observed on any finite portion of the orbit if 0.5 < `σ` < 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si90.png), quickly vanishes if you slightly modify the series.

Here I focus on randomizing `L[P](z, χ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si107.png). Let `z` = `σ` + `i t`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si2.png) be fixed, 0.5 < `σ` < 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si90.png), and for each prime `p` ∈ `P`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si165.png), `χ(p)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si3.png) be a random variable equal to +1 or −1 with probability 0.5. The `χ(p)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si3.png)'s are assumed to be independent. It follows immediately that

$$
\text{E}\lbrack L_{P}(z,\chi)\rbrack = \prod\limits_{p \in P}\text{E}\left\lbrack \frac{1}{1 - \chi(p)p^{- z}} \right\rbrack = \prod\limits_{p \in P}\frac{1}{1 - p^{- 2z}} = L_{P}(2z,\chi_{0}),
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si274.png)

where `χ[0](·)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si275.png) is the constant function equal to one. Note that the product converges if `σ` > 0.5![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si115.png). So this type of randomization extents the abscissa of convergence from `σ` > 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si19.png) to `σ` > 0.5![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si115.png). Now let the random variable `ρ²`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si276.png) be the square of the distance to the origin:

$$
\rho^{2} = |L_{P}(z,\chi)|^{2} = \prod\limits_{p \in P}|1 - \chi(p)p^{- z}|^{- 2} = \prod\limits_{p \in P}\frac{1}{1 - 2p^{- \sigma}\chi(p)\cos(t\log p) + p^{- 2\sigma}}.
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si277.png)(17.14)

We have

$$
\text{E}\lbrack\rho^{2}\rbrack = \prod\limits_{p \in P}\text{E}\left\lbrack |1 - \chi(p)p^{- z}|^{- 2} \right\rbrack = \prod\limits_{p \in P}\frac{1 + p^{- 2\sigma}}{{(1 + p^{- 2\sigma})}^{2} - 4p^{- 2\sigma}\cos^{2}(t\log p)}.
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si278.png)

In particular,

$$
\prod\limits_{p \in P}\frac{1}{1 + p^{- 2\sigma}} = \frac{L_{P}(4\sigma,\chi_{0})}{L_{P}(2\sigma,\chi_{0})} \leqslant \text{E}\lbrack\rho^{2}\rbrack \leqslant \prod\limits_{p \in P}\frac{1 + p^{- 2\sigma}}{{(1 - p^{- 2\sigma})}^{2}} = \frac{L_{P}^{3}(2\sigma,\chi_{0})}{L_{P}(4\sigma,\chi_{0})}.
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si279.png)(17.15)

Again, `χ[0](·)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si275.png) is the constant function equal to 1. The maximum is attained when `t` = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si25.png). If `P` is the full set of primes, formula [(17.15)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq26) becomes `ζ`(4`σ`)/`ζ`(2`σ`) ≤ E[`ρ²`] ≤ `ζ`³(2`σ`)/`ζ`(4`σ`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si280.png), where `ζ` is the `Riemann zeta function` [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_zeta_function). Similar bounds are available for E[`ρ`]![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si281.png). It is interesting to note that by averaging over all potential `χ(·)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si4.png)'s, the orbit has a hole encompassing the origin, with a strictly positive radius. This is in contrast to the nonrandom case, where the hole is reduced to a point regardless of `χ` (assuming 0.5 < `σ` < 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si90.png)).

Let `P` = {`p[1]`, `p[2]`, …}![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si160.png) with the primes listed in increasing order. Now let us define the random variable $L_{\chi}(n) = \sum_{k = 1}^{n}\chi(p_{k})$![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si282.png). It satisfies the `law of the iterated logarithm` [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_iterated_logarithm), stated in formula [(17.7)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq10), and translating here to

$$
\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\lim\sup}\frac{|L_{\chi}(n)|}{\sqrt{n\log\log n}} = C,
$$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si283.png)
