
## 16.2 Cluster Processes

[Fig. 16.3](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0020) and [Fig. 16.4](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0025) show two extreme cases. The parent process modeling the cluster centers is Poisson–binomial. It is simulated with intensity function `λ = 1`, using a uniform distribution for `F`. The `scaling factor` is `s = 0.2` in [Fig. 16.3](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0020), and `s = 2` in [Fig. 16.4](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0025). The left plot is a zoom-in. Around each center (marked with a blue cross in the picture), up to 15 points are radially distributed, creating the overall cluster structure. These points are the actual, observed points of the process, referred to as the child process. The distance between a point `(X', Y')` and its cluster center `(X, Y)` has a `half-logistic distribution` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-logistic_distribution)]. The simulations shown here are performed using formulas [(16.9)](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#deq12) and [(16.10)](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#deq13):

$$
X' = X + \log\left( \frac{U}{1 - U} \right)\cos(2\pi V),
$$
(16.9)

$$
Y' = Y + \log\left( \frac{U}{1 - U} \right)\sin(2\pi V).
$$
(16.10)

Here `U` and `V` are independent uniform deviates on `[0, 1]`. The `quantile function` `Q(U) = \log\frac{U}{1 - U}` corresponds to a standard logistic distribution.

The contrast between [Fig. 16.3](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0020) and [Fig. 16.4](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0025) is due to the choice of the `scaling factor` `s`. The value `s = 0.2`, close to zero, strongly reveals the underlying lattice structure. Here this effect is strong because of the choice of `F` (it has a very thin tail), and the relatively small variance of the distance between a point and its associated cluster center. It produces repulsion among neighbor points: we are dealing with a `repulsive process`. When `s = 0`, all the randomness is gone. Modeling applications include optimum distribution of sensors (for instance, cell towers), crystal structures, and bonding patterns of molecules in chemistry.

By contrast, `s = 2` makes the cluster structure much more apparent. This time, there is `attraction` among neighbor points: we are dealing with an `attractive process`. It can model many types of structures, associated to human activities or natural phenomena, such as the distribution of galaxies in the universe. [Fig. 16.5](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0030) provides an example, related to the manufacture of kitchen countertops.

![Figure 16.5 Manufactured marble lacking true lattice randomness (left).](images/B9780443218576000205/gr005.jpg)

**Figure 16.5** Manufactured marble lacking true lattice randomness (left).

[Fig. 16.5](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0030) shows luxury kitchen countertops called “Inverness bronze Cambria quartz”, on the left. While the quality (and price) is far superior to all other products from the same company, the rendering of marble veins is not done properly. It looks man-made: not the kind of patterns you would find in real stones. The pattern is too regular, as if produced using a very small value of the `scaling factor` `s`. An easy fix is to use patterns generated by the cluster processes described here. To increase randomness, increase `s`. The picture on the right shows a more realistic rendering of randomness.

### 16.2.3 References on Cluster Processes

Typical examples of cluster point processes include `Neyman–Scott` (see [here](https://hpaulkeeler.com/tag/neyman-scott-point-process/)) and `Matérn` (see [here](https://hpaulkeeler.com/simulating-a-thomas-cluster-point-process/)). Useful references include Baddeley's textbook “Spatial Point Processes and their Applications” [[4]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0020), Sigman's course material (Columbia University) on one-dimensional `renewal processes` for beginners, entitled “Notes on the Poisson Process” [[39]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0195), Last and Kenrose's book “Lectures on the Poisson Process” [[27]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0135), and Cressie's comprehensive 900-page book “Statistics for Spatial Data” [[11]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0055). Cluster point processes are part of a larger field known as `spatial statistics`, encompassing other techniques such as geostatistics, kriging, and tessellations. For `perturbed-lattice point processes` (lattice-based processes), more closely related to the theme of this chapter (lattice processes), and also more recent with applications to cellular networks, see the following references:

- “On Comparison of Clustering Properties of Point Processes” [[9]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0045)
- “Clustering and percolation of point processes” [[8]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0040)
- “Clustering comparison of point processes, applications to random geometric models” [[10]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0050)
- “Stochastic Geometry-Based Tools for Spatial Modeling and Planning of Future Cellular Networks” [[26]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0130)
- “Hyperuniform and rigid stable matchings” [[28]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0140)
- “Rigidity and tolerance for perturbed lattices” [[38]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0190)
- “Cluster analysis of spatial point patterns: posterior distribution of parents inferred from offspring” [[37]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0185)
- “Recovering the lattice from its random perturbations” [[42]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0210)
- “Geometry and Topology of the Boolean Model on a Stationary Point Processes” [[44]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0220)
- “On distances between point patterns and their applications” [[29]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0145)

More general references include two comprehensive volumes on point process theory by Daley and Vere-Jones [[12]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0060), [[13]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0065), a chapter by Johnson [[23]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0115), books by Møller and Waagepetersen focusing on statistical inference for spatial processes [[32]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0160), [[33]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0165), and “Point Pattern Analysis: Nearest Neighbor Statistics” by Anselin [[3]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0015) focusing on point inhibition/aggregation metrics. See also [[31]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0155) by Møller, and “Limit Theorems for Network Dependent Random Variables” [[24]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0120), available online [here](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.01059).
