
So, given a root `b^(\*)` > 2000![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si111.png), one would expect the next one to be of the order `e` ⋅ `b^(\*)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si112.png), with `e = 2.718…`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si113.png) This is consistent with the successive roots displayed at the beginning in Section [13.3.1](#Chp013_243-258_B9780443218576000175.xhtml#s0045).

### 13.3.2.2 Integer sequences with high density of primes

The Poisson–binomial distribution, including its Poisson approximation, can also be used in this context. The purpose is to find fast-growing integer sequences with a very high density of primes, see [here](https://mathoverflow.net/questions/374305/sequences-with-high-densities-of-primes-how-to-boost-them-to-get-even-more-and).

The probability for a large integer `a` to be prime is about `1/log a`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si114.png), a consequence of the prime number theorem [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem)]. Let `a[1]`, `a[2]`, …![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si115.png) be a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers, and `N` denote the number of primes among `a[n]`, `a[n+1]`, …, `a[n+m]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si116.png) for some large `n`. Assuming the sequence is independently and congruentially equidistributed, then `N` has a Poisson–binomial distribution of parameters `p[n]`, …, `p[n+m]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si117.png), with `p[k] = 1/log a[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si118.png). It is unimportant to know the exact definition of congruential equidistribution. Roughly speaking, it means that the joint empirical distribution of residues across the `a[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si119.png)'s, is asymptotically indistinguishable from that of a sequence of random integers. Thus a sequence where 60% of the terms are odd integers does not qualify (that proportion should be 50%).

This result is used to assess whether a given sequence of integers is unusually rich, or poor, in primes. If it contains far more large primes than the expected value `p[n] + … + p[n+m]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si120.png), then we are dealing with a very interesting, hard-to-find sequence, useful both in cryptographic applications and for its own sake. One can build confidence intervals for the number of such primes, based on the Poisson–binomial distribution under the assumption of independence and congruential equidistribution. A famous example of such a sequence (rich in prime numbers) is `a[k] = k² - k + 41`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si121.png) [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulam_spiral)]. If `n, m → ∞`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si122.png) and `p[n]² + … + p[n+m]² → 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si123.png), then the distribution of `N` is well approximated by a Poisson distribution, thanks to Le Cam's theorem.

## 13.5 Connection to synthetic data: random functions

First, there is a strong similarity between the various transforms used to magnify the roots and beautify the curves, and the concept of `transformers` in `Seq2seq` neural networks [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seq2seq)]. But the true connection to synthetic data is about how the simulations can be used to generate random functions mimicking the deterministic ones that we deal with in real life. This also applies to the random Rademacher functions discussed in Section [17.3.5](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml), mimicking deterministic multiplicative functions, also related to prime numbers as in this section.

The functions discussed here have a variable number of roots, a certain behavior (piecewise constant, roots are spaced out) and a certain potential range of values depending on the argument. These values, for a specific argument, are typically equally likely to show up. There is also some relative independence between (say) `f(b)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si1.png) and `f(b+1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si124.png). These are the parameters of the real-life functions `f(b) = a mod b`![Image](images/B9780443218576000175/si4.png) where `a` is a large fixed integer number with few divisors. Remember that the initial goal was to factor a product a two very large primes – a very hard problem of considerable interest in cryptography. Here the product in question is the number `a`.
