
## Exercises

- Prove that when `s` → ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si8.png), the limiting distribution of `T` is `Rayleigh` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayleigh_distribution)] of mean $\frac{1}{2\lambda}$![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si243.png).

- Show by simulations or logical arguments, that unlike in the one-dimensional case, `E[T]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si244.png) depends on `s`.

- Also, show that depending on `F`, the maximum `nearest neighbor distance`, computed over the infinitely many points of the process, can have a finite expectation. Is this true too when `s` → ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si8.png), that is, for stationary Poisson point processes?

- Finally, what is `T`'s distribution if `T` is replaced by the distance between an arbitrary location in ℝ²![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si166.png), and its closest neighbor among the points of the process?

## Solution

In two dimensions, the fact that `E[T(λ, s)]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si245.png) depends on `s`, is obvious: if `s` = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si10.png), it is equal to $\frac{1}{\lambda}$![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si246.png), and if `s` = ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si106.png), it is equal to $\frac{1}{2\lambda}$![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si243.png). Between these two extremes, there is a continuum of values, of course depending on `s`. The maximum `nearest neighbor distance` (over all the infinitely many points) always has a finite expectation if `F` is uniform, regardless of `s` < ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si247.png). To the contrary, for a Poisson point process, the maximum is infinite, see [here](https://mathoverflow.net/questions/412891/maximum-nearest-neighbor-distance-for-a-poisson-point-process/412895#412895).

Now let us prove that `T` has a `Rayleigh` distribution when `s` = ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si106.png), corresponding to a Poisson process of intensity `λ`²![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si1.png). We have `P(T > y) = P[N(B) = 0]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si249.png), where `B` is a disc of radius `y` centered at an arbitrary point of the process, and `N` is the `point count`, with an exponential distribution of mean `λ`²`μ(B)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si250.png) with `μ(B)` = `π``y`²![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si251.png) being the area of `B`. Thus `P(T > y) = exp(−λ²πy²)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si252.png), that is, `P(T < y) = 1 − exp(−λ²πy²)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si253.png). This is the CDF of a `Rayleigh` distribution of mean $\frac{1}{2\lambda}$![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si243.png).

## Exercise 16.4

Cell networks: coverage problem. Points are randomly distributed on the plane, with an average of `λ` points per unit area. A circle of radius `R` is drawn around each point. What is the proportion of the plane covered by these (possibly overlapping) circles? What if `R` is a random variable, so that we are dealing with random circles? Such `stochastic covering problems` are part of `stochastic geometry` [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_geometry)] [[14]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0070),[[40]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0200). See also Hall's book on coverings [[21]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0105). Applications include wireless networks [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_geometry_models_of_wireless_networks)].

### Solution

The points are distributed according to a Poisson point process of intensity `λ`. The probability that an arbitrary location `x` in the plane is not covered by any circle is the probability that there is zero point from the process, in a circle of radius `R` centered at `x`. This is equal to `exp(−λπR²)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si254.png). Thus the proportion of the plane covered by the circles is `1 − exp(−λπR²)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si255.png). Now, let us say that we have two types of circles: one with radius `R`[1]![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si256.png), and one with radius `R`[2]![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si257.png), each type equally likely to be picked up. This is like having two independent, superimposed Poisson processes, each with intensity `λ`/2![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si258.png), one for each type of circle. Now the probability `p` that `x` is not covered by any circle is thus a product of two probabilities:

$p = \exp\left( - \frac{\lambda}{2}\pi R_{1}^{2} \right) \times \exp\left( - \frac{\lambda}{2}\pi R_{1}^{2} \right) = \exp\left( - \lambda\pi\frac{R_{1}^{2} + R_{2}^{2}}{2} \right).$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si259.png)

$1 - p = 1 - \exp\left\lbrack - \lambda\pi\sum\limits_{k = 1}^{m}p_{k}R_{k}^{2} \right\rbrack,$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si263.png)(16.14)

You can generalize to `m` types of circles, each type with a radius `R`[`k`]![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si260.png) and probability `p`[`k`]![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si261.png) to be picked up, with 1 ≤ `k` ≤ `m`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si262.png). It leads to the proportion of the plane covered by at least one circle. If `R`, the radius of the circle, is a continuous random variable, the sum in formula [(16.14)](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#deq20) must be replaced by `E[R²]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si264.png). A related topic is the smallest circle problem [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallest-circle_problem)]. See also [[36]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0180).

## 16.4 Special topics

This section covers special topics of interest, that do not fit well in the previous sections. Minimum contrast estimation is a powerful, yet simple, inference technique applicable to many problems, especially when the true parameters are impossible to compute, buried into some hidden layer, or face identifiability problems. This topic is discussed in Chapter [18](#Chp018_361-386_B9780443218576000229.xhtml) (Section [18.3.5](#Chp018_361-386_B9780443218576000229.xhtml)). Here I focus on the specifics related to perturbed-lattice point processes. Identifiability issues and the hidden model (the lattice) are also discussed in this section.

### 16.4.1 Minimum contrast estimation and explainable AI

The idea behind `minimum contrast estimation` is to use proxy statistics as substitutes for the parameter estimators. It makes sense here as it is not clear what combination of variables represents `s`. The goal is to estimate (`λ`, `s`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si219.png) using proxy parameters (`p`, `q`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si265.png) to be defined shortly, then map (`p`, `q`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si265.png) back to (`λ`, `s`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si219.png) to get the estimates that we are interested in. In particular, (`λ`, `s`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si219.png) – the intensity and scaling factor – is easy to interpret while (`p`, `q`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si265.png) is rather obscure. Thus estimates of (`λ`, `s`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si219.png) lead to `explainable AI`, while (`p`, `q`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si265.png) do not.
