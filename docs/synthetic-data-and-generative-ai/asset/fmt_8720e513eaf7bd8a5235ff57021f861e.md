
In this case `q[k] = k`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si317.png). Note that `k^(-z) = k^(-σ) ⋅ [cos(t log k) + i sin(t log k)]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si318.png). It suffices to prove that the series with `k`th term equal to `k^(-σ) cos(t log k)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si319.png) cannot converge, even though `cos(t log k)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si320.png) oscillates infinitely often between positive and negative values, as `k` increases. The reason is because `log k`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si321.png) grows too slowly. When `k` is very close to a multiple of (say) `2π`, too many consecutive terms are all positive, and `k^(σ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si322.png) does not grow fast enough (if `σ < 1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si9.png)) to keep the partial sums bounded and converging. To the contrary, if `log k`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si321.png) is replaced by `$\sqrt{k}$`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si323.png) in the cosine function, and `σ = 0.75`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si252.png), then the series converge: the corresponding integral between 0 and ∞ is equal to `$\sqrt{2π/|t|}`$![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si324.png).

## Exercise 17.4

### Truncated Dirichlet function

Determine the area covered by the orbit, for a fixed value of `σ`, when `n` = 3![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si141.png) and `t` runs though all the positive real numbers in formula [(17.8)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq11). Generalize to `n` = 4![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si325.png).

### Solution

Let us assume that the center of the hole is (1, 0)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si142.png) in this case. Proving it is left to the reader. Then the square of the distance between a point on the orbit at time `t` and the origin is equal to

```
d^{2}(t) = \left\lbrack \beta^{\sigma}\cos(t\log 2) - \lambda_{3}^{\sigma}\cos(t\log 2) \right\rbrack^{2} + \left\lbrack \beta^{\sigma}\sin(t\log 2) - \lambda_{3}^{\sigma}\sin(t\log 2) \right\rbrack^{2} = \beta^{2\sigma} + \lambda_{3}^{2\sigma} - 2\beta^{\sigma}\lambda_{3}^{\sigma}\left\lbrack \cos(t\log 2)\cos(t\log 3) + \sin(t\log 2)\sin(t\log 3) \right\rbrack = \beta^{2\sigma} + \lambda_{3}^{2\sigma} - 2\beta^{\sigma}\lambda_{3}^{\sigma}\cos\left( t\log\frac{2}{3} \right).
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si326.png)

Since the cosine takes all values between −1 and +1, infinitely often with period `2π/(log 3 - log 2)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si327.png), the outer boundary of the orbit is a circle of radius `ρ`[1]![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si328.png) centered at (1, 0)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si142.png), and the inner boundary (that is, the shape of the hole) is a circle of radius `ρ`[2]![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si329.png) also centered at (1, 0)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si142.png). Here

```
\rho_{1} = \sqrt{\beta^{2\sigma} + \lambda_{3}^{2\sigma} + 2\beta^{\sigma}\lambda_{3}^{\sigma}} = |\beta^{\sigma} + \lambda_{3}^{\sigma}|,\quad\rho_{2} = \sqrt{\beta^{2\sigma} + \lambda_{3}^{2\sigma} - 2\beta^{\sigma}\lambda_{3}^{\sigma}} = |\beta^{\sigma} - \lambda_{3}^{\sigma}|.
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si330.png)

Thus the larger the `β`, the bigger the hole. I assumed that `β`, `λ`[3] ≥ 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si331.png).

The case `n` = 4![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si325.png) is considerably more complicated. The center is no longer (1, 0)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si142.png), but typically (`c`, 0)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si110.png) with `c` < 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si332.png). Also, the shapes may no longer be circles. There may or may not be a hole. However, if `β` is large enough, there will be a hole, big enough to encompass (1, 0)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si142.png). The square of the distance to (1, 0)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si142.png) is now

```
d^{2}(t) = \beta^{2\sigma} + \lambda_{3}^{2\sigma} + \lambda_{4}^{2\sigma} - 2\beta^{\sigma}\lambda_{3}^{\sigma}\cos\left( t\log\frac{2}{3} \right) - 2\beta^{\sigma}\lambda_{4}^{\sigma}\cos\left( t\log\frac{2}{4} \right) - 2\lambda_{3}^{\sigma}\lambda_{3}^{\sigma}\cos\left( t\log\frac{3}{4} \right).
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si333.png)

The minimum possible value for `d`²(`t`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si334.png) is `d`[min] = `β`^(2`σ`) + `λ`[3]^(2`σ`) + `λ`[4]^(2`σ`) − 2`β`^(`σ`)`λ`[3]^(`σ`) − 2`β`^(`σ`)`λ`[4]^(`σ`) − 2`λ`[3]^(`σ`)`λ`[3]^(`σ`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si335.png). If `d`[min] > 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si336.png), there is a hole big enough to encompass (1, 0)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si142.png).

## Exercise 17.5

### The orbit covers a dense area in the complex plane

The purpose of this exercise is to prove a particular case: if `σ` < 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si9.png), then lim inf |`L`[`P`](`z`, `χ`)| = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si337.png) regardless of `χ`. The infimum [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_inferior_and_limit_superior)] is over all complex numbers `z`. Here we assume that `P` is an infinite subset of primes (or the full set), and that the sum of `p`^(−`σ`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si338.png) over all `p` ∈ `P`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si165.png) diverges. This proves that the orbit is dense around the origin under certain conditions. It implies, under these conditions, that the hole shrinks to a singleton (the origin) if |`L`[`P`](`z`, `χ`)| > 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si339.png) for all `z`, and to an empty set if `L`[`P`](`z`, `χ`) = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si340.png) for some `z`.

### Solution

Assume the Euler product is finite, and contains only the first `d` primes `p`[1], …, `p`[`d`] ∈ `P`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si341.png). Let `z` = `σ` + `i``t`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si2.png) as usual, with `t` large enough so that `t`log `p`[`k`]![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si342.png) gets extremely close to a multiple of `π`, say `m`[`k`]`π`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si343.png), for all `k` = 1, …, `d`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si344.png). This is possible thanks to **Kronecker's theorem** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronecker%27s_theorem)]. Then `sin`(`t`log `p`[`k`])![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si345.png) gets very close to 0, and `cos`(`t`log `p`[`k`])![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si346.png) gets very close to either −1 or +1 depending on whether `m`[`k`]![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si347.png) is odd or even. Thus, the imaginary part of `L`[`P`](`z`, `χ`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si107.png), involving the sine terms only, gets very close to 0. The real part, involving the cosine terms only, gets very close to

```
S(\chi^{\star}) = \sum\limits_{k = 1}^{\infty}\chi^{\star}(k)\chi(k)k^{- \sigma},
```

![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si348.png)

where

- The function `χ`^⋆![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si349.png) is a **completely multiplicative** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_multiplicative_function)] and thus entirely defined by its values on prime numbers,
- `χ`^⋆(`p`[`k`]) ∈ {1, +1}![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si350.png) if `p`[`k`] ∈ `P`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si351.png) and 1 ≤ `k` ≤ `d`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si352.png), otherwise `χ`^⋆(`p`[`k`]) = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si353.png),
