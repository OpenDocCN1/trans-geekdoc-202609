
## 17.1 Riemann Hypothesis and Generalized Riemann Hypothesis

The complex plane is the standard two-dimensional space: the real axis is the horizontal or X-axis; the imaginary axis is the vertical or Y-axis. The Riemann Hypothesis (RH) states that the Riemann zeta function `ζ(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si8.png) defined earlier, has no root (that is, `ζ(z) ≠ 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si12.png)) if $\frac{1}{2} < \sigma < z$![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si13.png). I use the notation `σ = ℜ(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si14.png) to indicate that `σ` is the real part of the complex number `z = σ + it`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si2.png). Throughout this text, a positive number is a number ⩾0. A number >0 is called strictly positive.

The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) makes the same statement as RH, for a larger class of well behaved functions, not just `ζ(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si8.png). In short, it applies to Dirichlet functions where `χ(·)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si4.png) is completely multiplicative and periodic; these are called Dirichlet-*L* functions. Here, we limit ourselves to `χ(p) ∈ {−1, +1}`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si15.png). However, we also consider `χ(·)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si4.png)'s that are not periodic. The classic nontrivial periodic `χ(·)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si4.png) is `χ = χ[4]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si16.png), the nontrivial Dirichlet character modulo 4. It leads to an Euler product suspected to converge if $\sigma > \frac{1}{2}$![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si17.png). Proving the convergence, even only at `σ = 0.99`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si18.png), would be a major milestone towards proving GRH. Of course, the product converges if `σ > 1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si19.png). If `χ(·)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si4.png) is allowed not to be periodic, there are known cases, discussed in this chapter, that meet the requirements of GRH. Typically, these functions are much less interesting and do not satisfy a Dirichlet-like functional equation.

### 17.1.2 Orbits and holes

An original concept, the hole of the orbit of a Dirichlet function, is introduced in this chapter for the first time. It is epitomized in [Fig. 17.1](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#f0010) dealing with finite Euler products, and in [Fig. 17.5](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#f0030) featuring the orbit of truncated Dirichlet series (the truncated expansion of an infinite product or, in other words, the partial sums). In the RH and GRH contexts, the hole is present if $\frac{1}{2} < \sigma < 1$![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si20.png) is fixed and `0 < t < T`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si21.png) is bounded. But as `T → ∞`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si22.png), the hole shrinks to a singleton at the origin in the absence of roots ($\frac{1}{2} < \sigma < 1$![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si20.png)), or to an empty set if roots are present (`σ = 1/2`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si24.png)). Studying modified Dirichlet functions that always have a hole may be key to making progress towards RH and GRH. In particular, it is interesting to study the behavior at their limit as they approach standard Dirichlet functions, and the hole slowly evaporates. This is the topic of Sections [17.3.1](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0065) and [17.3.3](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0095).

![Figure 17.1 Three orbits (σ = 0.5,0.75,1.25) with finite Euler product: P = {2,3} (left) vs {2,3,5} (right).](images/B9780443218576000217/gr001.jpg)

Figure 17.1 Three orbits (`σ = 0.5,0.75,1.25`) with finite Euler product: `P = {2,3}` (left) vs `{2,3,5}` (right).

The hole is a circle of maximum radius, with center on the X-axis (due to symmetry), that the orbit never crosses. The center of the hole may not be at the origin (see Section [17.2.2.1](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#s0050) for examples), even when there is no root. The absence of root can be caused by a hole, or because the orbit never gets too close to the Y-axis. The orbit on a fixed domain `0 < t < T`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si21.png), for a fixed `σ`, is defined as the set of all possible values of the corresponding Dirichlet function in the complex plane, as `t` (called the “time”), varies continuously between `t = 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si25.png) and `t = T`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si26.png). Here, `t` is the imaginary part of the argument `z = σ + it`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si2.png). The full orbit corresponds to `T = ∞`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si27.png). The size of the hole, as well as its presence/absence and location, depends on `σ`, and of course on `T`, `P`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si28.png), and `χ(·)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si4.png).

Finally, the hole is called a repulsion basin in the context of dynamical systems. The orbit may be bounded if `σ > 1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si19.png) or unbounded otherwise (in that case, typically extending to the entire complex plane).

## 17.2 Euler products

I start with finite Euler products, where everything works fine: here the set `P` is a finite subset of the prime integers; there is no convergence issue, and orbits always have a hole. I first introduce the Dirichlet version `η(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si29.png) of the Riemann function `ζ(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si8.png), as we need it to extend the convergence domain from `σ > 1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si19.png) to `σ > 0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si30.png) in order to study the behavior (presence of a hole and/or roots) when `0 < σ < 1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si177.png). For `ζ(z)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si8.png), the function `χ(·)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si4.png) is constant and equal to 1. I then move to arbitrary `χ(·)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si4.png)'s including Dirichlet characters modulo 4, and to infinite products. The function `χ` is extended to all integers via the formula `χ(ab) = χ(a)χ(b)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000217/si5.png): this extension leads to the fundamental formulas [(17.4)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq5), [(17.5)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq6), and [(17.6)](#Chp017_329-360_B9780443218576000217.xhtml#deq7), linking the Euler product to its Dirichlet series expansion whenever both converge. The Euler product establishes the connection between the analytic properties of Dirichlet functions, and the distribution of prime numbers.
