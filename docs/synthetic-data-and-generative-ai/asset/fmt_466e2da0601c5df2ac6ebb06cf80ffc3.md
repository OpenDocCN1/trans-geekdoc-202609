
• For the second data set, `MSE(3,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si226.png) evaluated at `θ^*(3,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si235.png) is better than when evaluated at `θ^*(1,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si236.png). This does not mean anything: of course, `MSE(3,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si226.png) is always best at `θ^*(3,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si235.png), by design. However, the ratio of these two MSEs, `ρ = 0.01092/0.00686 ≈ 1.59`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si237.png), is quite high here. To the contrary, for the first data set `ρ ≈ 1.39`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si238.png) is much smaller. Thus model `(3,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si226.png) is more justified for the second data set than for the first one.

Note that the computation of `MSE(1,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si231.png) or `MSE(3,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si226.png) is performed without knowing which cluster a point is assigned to. Indeed, point allocation is discussed nowhere in my method: you find the centers without allocating points to specific clusters. Once the two centers `θ[A]^*`, `θ[B]^*`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si193.png) have been computed, each point `W[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si74.png) is assigned to the closest cluster. Proximity is measured as the distance between the point and the cluster center. Then choose the model – `(1,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si231.png) or `(3,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si226.png) – minimizing the sum of these distances.

It is my guess that replacing `||W[k] - θ[A]||²`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si239.png) and `||W[k] - θ[B]||²`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si240.png) by `||W[k] - θ[A]||^(p[A])`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si241.png) and `||W[k] - θ[B]||^(p[B])`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si242.png) in the `Kmeans` procedure will yield better results similar to my method. Again, `θ[A]`, `θ[B]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si188.png) are the two cluster centers, and `W[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si74.png) is the `k`th observation. Even `p[A] = p[B] = 1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si243.png) could lead to significant improvements in `Kmeans` in the presence of outliers (for instance, outliers from a large cluster spilling over to a nearby smaller cluster). This approach is somewhat similar to **K-medians clustering** [[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-medians_clustering)]. Using linear rather than power weights may have the same effect.

### Conclusions

My method frequently works better than `Kmeans` to detect the centers when clusters strongly overlap and are unbalanced. However, this assumes that you have a mechanism to choose between models `(1,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si231.png), `(2,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si232.png), and `(3,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si226.png). If you know beforehand that your data is highly skewed as in [Fig. 1.7](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#f0050), then model `(3,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si226.png) is a good candidate to begin with. When the clusters are well separated and one or two of the distributions are asymmetric, model `(1,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si231.png) tends to correctly identify the cluster medians, rather than the standard centers (the mean).

The method is simple and fast: it does not perform point allocation. You can use it to find initial center configurations as first approximations in more complex algorithms, or in the context of “unsupervised” logistic regression to detect the two clusters when there is no independent variable. Exact solutions such as [(1.12)](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#deq17) are available in any dimension for two and more clusters, for instance, for the `(2,2)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si244.png), `(2,2,2)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si245.png), and `(4,2)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si246.png) models. Other original clustering algorithms are described in Chapter [5](#Chp005_83-102_B9780443218576000096.xhtml).

Next steps: test on asymmetric synthetic data modeled as a mixture of normal and gamma distributions with unequal cluster sizes and variances, reduce volatility, investigate the model `(1,1/3)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si247.png) – the sister of `(3,1)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si226.png) – and generalize the method to two or three dimensions and more than two clusters. One of the goals is to identify when my method performs better than `Kmeans`, to learn more about `Kmeans` and further improve it.

#### 1.3.4.4 Python code

The Python code `mixture1D.py` for the one-dimensional case in Section [1.3.4.1](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#s0085) is also on GitHub [here](https://github.com/VincentGranville/Machine-Learning/blob/main/Source%20Code/mixture1D.py). Note that `W[A]`, `W[B]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si248.png), and `W` are vectors, respectively with `n[A]`, `n[B]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si249.png), and `n` elements. The vector operations (multiplications and so on) are implicitly performed component-wise, without using a loop on the elements. When `p = 2`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si214.png), `Error` corresponds to the mean squared error. I use color transparency – the parameter `alpha` in the histogram function `plt.hist` – to visualize the overlap between the two components of the Gaussian mixture: see result in left plot, [Fig. 1.5](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#f0040).

The optimum `θ[A]^*`, `θ[B]^*`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si193.png) (the cluster centers) are obtained via Monte Carlo simulations, using ![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/fx013.jpg)sampled `θ[A]`, `θ[B]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si188.png) per test. On the screenshot showing convergence to the optimum, you can see that `θ[A]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si195.png) and `θ[B]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si196.png) are randomly flipped back and forth within each test. The algorithm senses that there are two distinct centers; however, it cannot tell which one is labeled `A` or `B`. After all, this is **unsupervised learning**. To address this issue, when computing the confidence intervals, I use the notation `θ[A]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si195.png) for the center on the left, and `θ[B]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si196.png) for the other one. That is, `θ[A] < θ[B]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000059/si250.png). Finally, I run 40 tests to determine a 95% confidence interval for the optimum values. Results are displayed in the screenshot in [Fig. 1.5](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#f0040). Zoom in for a better view.

## 1.4 Connection to synthetic data: meteorites, ocean tides

The examples in this chapter are based on simulated data. Simulations are used in synthetic data as follows. Say you have a collection of meteorite images and the goal is to classify them based on their shape, summarized by the ellipse parameters discussed in Section [1.3.2.2](#Chp001_1-34_B9780443218576000059.xhtml#s0055). Each type of meteorite corresponds to a specific set of parameter values. For instance, elongated meteorites have a high eccentricity. The eccentricity is determined by the ratio of the parameters `ap` and `bp` in the Python code (respectively the length of the semimajor and semiminor axes). To generate elongated meteorites, set a specific range in your simulations for the ratio in question. This generative model technique is referred to as **parametric bootstrap**.
