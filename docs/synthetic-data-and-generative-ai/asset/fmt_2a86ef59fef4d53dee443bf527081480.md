
Here `F` is the logistic distribution, and I chose `r` = 2![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si171.png). Any `r` > 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si169.png) except `r` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si179.png) would work. If `λ[0]` = 1.45![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si180.png) and `τ[0]` = 0.77![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si181.png), then `τ′` = (`λ[0]`)²`τ[0]` = 1.61![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si182.png). Looking at the `E[T²(1, s′)]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si183.png) table, to satisfy `E[T²(1, s′)]` ≈ 1.61![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si184.png), you need `s′` = 0.65![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si185.png). Thus `s[0]` = `s′`/`λ[0]` = 0.45![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si186.png). These numbers match those obtained by simulation. To view or download the table, look at the `E[T²]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si187.png) tab in [`PB_inference.xlsx`](https://github.com/VincentGranville/Point-Processes/tree/main/Spreadsheets).

The equation `E[T²(1, s′)]` = `τ′`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si188.png), where `s′`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si176.png) is the unknown, can be solved using numerical methods. The easiest way is to build a granular table of `E[T²(1, s)]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si189.png) for various values of `s`, by simulating Poisson–binomial processes of intensity `λ=1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si49.png) and scaling factor `s`. Then finding `s′`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si176.png) consists in browsing and interpolating the table in question the old fashioned way, to identify the value of `s` closest to satisfying `E[T²(1, s)]` = `τ′`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si190.png). This can, of course, be automated. There are two ways to perform the simulations in question:

*   •  Generating one realization of each process with a large number of points (that is, one realization for each 0 < `s` < 20![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si191.png) with `λ=1`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si49.png) and `s` increments equal to 0.01),

*   •  Or generating many realizations of each process, each one with a rather small number of points.

Either way, the results should be almost identical due to **ergodicity** if the same `F` is used in both cases. The simulations also allow you to compute the theoretical variance of the estimators in question (at least a very good approximation). This is useful when multiple estimators (based on different statistics) are available, to choose the best one: that with minimum variance. Simulations also allow you to compute **confidence intervals** for your estimators.

#### 16.3.1.3 Alternative estimation method

It is also possible to use the point count `N(B)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si19.png) to estimate `s`. The idea is to partition the state space (the real line in one dimension, where the points reside) into short intervals `B[k]` = \[`k`/`λ`, (`k` + 1)/`λ`\[![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si192.png), `k` = 0, ±1, ±2![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si193.png), and so on, covering the observed points; beware of the **boundary effect**. This assumes that `λ` is known or estimated. Let `N[k]` = `N(B[k])`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si194.png) be the random variable counting the number of observed points in `B[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si195.png). We have `E[N[k]]` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si196.png). Also `Var[N[k]]` ≤ 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si197.png) does not depend on `k` thanks to the choice of `B[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si195.png) (see Section [16.1.2](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#s0020)). The variance is maximum and equal to 1 when `s` = ∞![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si106.png).

It is possible, for any value of `s` and `λ`, to compute the theoretical variance `v(λ, s)` = `Var[N[k]]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si198.png) using either simulations or formula [(16.4)](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#deq5) with `a=0`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si199.png) and `b=1/λ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si200.png). It slightly depends on `F`, but barely. Now compute the empirical variance of `N[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si201.png) as the average (`N[k]` − 1)²![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si202.png) across all the `B[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si195.png)'s, based on your observations, assuming `λ` is known or estimated. This empirical variance is denoted as `v[0](λ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si203.png). The estimated value of `s` is the one that makes the empirical and theoretical variances identical, that is, the *unique* value of `s` that solves the equation `v(λ, s)` = `v[0](λ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si204.png). This method easily generalizes to higher dimensions, see Section [16.3.2.1](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#s0110). The fact that `E[N[k]]` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si196.png) is a direct consequence of Theorem 4.1 in the same book.

See the `N[k]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si201.png) tab in [`PB_inference.xlsx`](https://github.com/VincentGranville/Point-Processes/tree/main/Spreadsheets) for a Poisson–binomial process simulation with a **generalized logistic** `F` and computation of `E[N[k]]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si205.png) and `Var[N[k]]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si206.png) in Excel.

### 16.3.2 Spatial statistics, nearest neighbors, clustering

Here the focus is on 2D point processes. Section [16.3.2.3](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#s0120) features an original test to determine whether the point distribution – more precisely the nearest distances – in a particular data set is consistent with that of a Poisson process. In short, it tests whether you are dealing with a realization of a **Poisson process**, or a more complex process such as an `m-mixture`: a superimposition of Poisson–binomial processes.

#### 16.3.2.1 Inference for two-dimensional processes

Let us assume for now that we are dealing with a single two-dimensional Poisson–binomial point process. Some of the methodology discussed in Section [16.3.1](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#s0085) for the one-dimensional case can be generalized to higher dimensions. The point count distribution in a square of side 1/`λ`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si38.png) has expectation equal to 1. So, one way to estimate `λ` is to partition the window of observations `W` into small squares $B_{h,k}(\lambda) = \left\lbrack \frac{h}{\lambda},\frac{h + 1}{\lambda}\left\lbrack \times \left\lbrack \frac{k}{\lambda},\frac{k + 1}{\lambda}\lbrack \right. \right. \right.$![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si207.png) for various values of the (unknown) `λ`, compute the number of points `N[h, k](λ)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si208.png) (called **point count**) in each of these squares, and find `λ` that minimizes the empirical variance

$v(\lambda) = \sum\limits_{h,k}\left( N_{h,k}(\lambda) - 1 \right)^{2}$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si209.png)

computed on the observations. The sum is over `h`, `k` ∈ ℤ ∩ `W′`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si210.png), where `W` is the window of observation, and `W′`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si211.png) is slightly smaller than `W` to mitigate **boundary effects**. In short, your estimate of the **intensity** `λ` is defined as $\lambda_{0} = \underset{\lambda}{\arg\min} v(\lambda)$![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si212.png).
