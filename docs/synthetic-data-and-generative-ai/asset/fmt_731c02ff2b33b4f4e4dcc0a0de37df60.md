
$E\\lbrack\\exp( - itT^{\\ast})\\rbrack = \\frac{1}{1 + t^{2}} = E\\lbrack\\exp( - itZ\_{1})\\rbrack \\times E\\lbrack\\exp( - itZ\_{2})\\rbrack = \\left( E\\lbrack\\exp( - itZ\_{1})\\rbrack \\right)^{2}.$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si333.png)

Thus `E`[`exp`( − `i` `t` `Z[1]`)] = (1 + `t`²)^( − 1/2)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si334.png). Taking the inverse **Fourier transform** to retrieve the density of `Z[1]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si335.png), which is the density attached to `F`, one finds

$f(x) = \\frac{1}{2\\pi}\\int\_{- \\infty}^{\\infty}\\frac{\\cos(tx)}{\\sqrt{1 + t^{2}}}dt = \\frac{1}{\\pi}K\_{0}(x),$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si336.png)

where `K[0]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si337.png) is the modified **Bessel function** of the second kind \[[Wiki](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/ModifiedBesselFunctionoftheSecondKind.xhtml)\]. More about the Laplace distribution and its generalization can be found in [\[25\]](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#br0125). The cases when `T^*`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si330.png) is Gaussian or Cauchy are easy because these distributions belong to **stable families of distributions** \[[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_distribution)\]: in that case, `F` is respectively Gaussian or Cauchy.

### 16.4.5 Record distances between an observed point and its vertex

[Fig. 16.10](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0065) shows the points (`X[h]`, `Y[k]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si4.png) of a Poisson–binomial process with a logistic `F`, in blue. Their lattice vertices (`h/λ`, `k/λ`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si96.png) are shown with little red crosses. The arrows connected both. Here, `λ` = 1![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si49.png). This picture shows how far away a point can be from the **vertex** it is attached to. If `s` = 0![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si10.png), both locations coincide, but when `s` is large, the distances can be arbitrarily large.

![Figure 16.10 Each arrow links a point (blue) to its vertex (red): s = 0.2 (left), s = 1 (right).](images/B9780443218576000205/gr016.jpg)

Figure 16.10 Each arrow links a point (blue) to its vertex (red): `s` = 0.2 (left), `s` = 1 (right).

Note that both plots (left and right in [Fig. 16.10](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0065)) have the same number of points. But points are clustered in some areas, and sparse in other areas on the right plot, giving the impression that there are fewer of them. Clearly, the empirical distribution of the distance between nearest neighbors (especially extreme distances), or the average area of the largest empty zone, can be used to estimate the **scaling factor** `s` once `λ` is known or estimated.

This brings me to my next discussion, namely **extreme values**, or **records**. This is part of a field know as **order statistics** \[[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_statistic)\] or extreme value theory \[[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_value_theory)\]. Extreme values are different from **outliers** \[[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outlier)\]: they can be predictable, with known distribution. To the contrary, outliers are usually considered as errors, glitches, or data points obeying a different model. In any case, both have an impact on the window of observations, delimited by the “boundary”, and have the potential to introduce biases.

One question is how far a point can be from its vertex, and how frequently such “extremes” occur. Even more interesting is the reverse question, associated to the inverse or **hidden model**: can a point (`X[h]`, `Y[k]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si4.png) close to the origin, well within the small window of observations, have its vertex very far away? Such a point will not be generated by the point process simulator. It will be unaccounted for, introducing a bias. This happens with increased frequency as `s` increases, requiring a larger and larger observation window.

Unless `F` has a finite support domain (for instance, if `F` is uniform), unobserved points in the small window of observations – even though their expected number is finite and rather small – can be attached to any arbitrary vertex, no matter how far away. In two dimensions, the probability `P[R > r]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si338.png) that the distance `R` between a point and its lattice location is greater than `r` is

$P(R > r) = \\int\_{- \\infty}^{\\infty}\\int\_{- \\infty}^{\\infty}\\chi(x^{2} + y^{2} > r)F\\left( \\frac{x}{s} \\right)F\\left( \\frac{y}{s} \\right)dxdy,$

![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si339.png)

where `χ(A)`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si340.png) is the indicator function, equal to one if `A` is true, and to zero otherwise.

The distance `R` corresponds to the length of the arrow, in [Fig. 16.10](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0065). If `F` is Gaussian, then `R` has a **Rayleigh distribution** \[[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayleigh_distribution)\]. In two dimensions, the distance between two nearest neighbor points, for a stationary Poisson point process, also has a Rayleigh distribution, see Section [16.3.2.3](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#s0120) and [Exercise 16.3](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#enun0025).

#### 16.4.5.1 Distribution of records

Now let `M[n]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si341.png) be the maximum distance between a point and its vertex, measured over `n` points of the process, randomly selected. In other words `M[n]` = `max` (`R[1]`, …, `R[n]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si342.png) where `R[i]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si343.png) (`i` = 1, …, `n`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si344.png) is the distance between the `i`th point, and its vertex. Depending on `F`, the standardized distribution of `M[n]`![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si341.png) is asymptotically **Weibull**, Gumbel, or Fréchet: these are the tree potential **attractor distributions** in the context of extreme value theory \[[Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_value_theory)\]. The Rayleigh distribution is a particular case of the Weibull distribution. Surprisingly, in `d` dimensions, the distribution of the **nearest neighbor distances**, for a stationary Poisson point process, is also Weibull, see Section [16.3.2](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#s0105).

[Fig. 16.11](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0070) shows (on the Y-axis) the distance `R` between a point (`X[h]`, `Y[k]`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si4.png) and its vertex (`h/λ`, `k/λ`)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si96.png). These are the same points as on the right plot in [Fig. 16.10](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0065); `R` represents the length of the arrows. The points are ordered by how close they are to the origin (0, 0)![Image](images/B9780443218576000205/si345.png), and the X-axis represents their distance to the origin, that is, their norm. By looking at [Fig. 16.11](#Chp016_293-327_B9780443218576000205.xhtml#f0070), it is easy to visualize the extreme values of `R`, and when they occur on the X-axis.
