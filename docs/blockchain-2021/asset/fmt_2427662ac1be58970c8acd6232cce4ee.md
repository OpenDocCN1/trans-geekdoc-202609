## How Is Ether Different from Bitcoin?

Through market capitalization, Ether is the world’s second-largest virtual currency. According to consumer valuation, it is just second to Bitcoin (`BTC`). On January 3, 2009, Bitcoin was first published, and Ethereum’s Blockchain was introduced on July 30, 2015. Unlike Bitcoin, the overall sum of ether tokens has no fixed limit—it fluctuates and expands in response to demand.

As a consequence, Ethereum’s network is considerably bigger than Bitcoin’s, and it is predicted to begin to grow further throughout the future. An additional distinction between the two is that although Bitcoin keeps a purely abstract record of balances, developers to the Ethereum’s Blockchain will use the programming language they’re coding to construct further with to create “smart contracts.”

As a result, Ethereum network transactions can involve executable code, while data associated with Bitcoin network transactions are often only used for record-keeping. The time it takes to create a new block differs significantly between the two virtual currencies. The confirmation of a new block in the Ethereum network takes seconds, while the Bitcoin counterpart takes minutes. Most notably, the networks’ general goals are distinct. Bitcoin was developed as a safe peer-to-peer online financial mechanism as an alternative to conventional currencies.

The Ethereum Blockchain was designed to make contracts and apps easier to use, and Ether is the currency that allows these transfers to take place. Ether was never meant to be a replacement for other currencies or mediums of trade. Rather, it serves to simplify and monetize the Ethereum platform’s activities. The Ethereum network currently follows Bitcoin, so these two technologies do not clash in theory.

### Mining

Miners will use the Proof-of-Work approach to verify transactions in Bitcoin. The same is true of Ethereum. With Proof-of-Work, miners all around the world compete to be the first to connect a block to the Blockchain by solving a difficult mathematical puzzle. Ethereum, on the other hand, can move to a system known as a Proof-of-Stake.

An individual may mine or verify transactions in a block depending on how many coins he owns with Proof-of-Stake; the greater a person’s mining ability, the more coins he owns. When a miner contributes a block to the network of Bitcoin, he is awarded 12.5 Bitcoins. Every 210,000 blocks, the incentive is supposed to be halved.

The payout will be halved again in 2020 when it will be cut from 12.5 Bitcoins per block to 6.25 Bitcoin per block. When a block is applied to the Ethereum network, a miner, or verifier, gets three Ether as compensation, which can never be halved.
