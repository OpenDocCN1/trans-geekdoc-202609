However, the forward and reverse recovery restrict the high-frequency switching activity, and the high turn-on voltage (approximately 3 V for SiC devices) exacerbates the ON-state loss. Since Schottky diodes are essentially majority-carrier devices, they can function at high repetition rates with negligible loss; however, the trade-off between blocking voltage capacity and ON-state voltage drop limits the number of high-voltage Schottky diodes that can be produced. A JBS diode combines the high-blocking voltage capacity of a PIN diode with the low ON-state voltage drop and high-frequency switching capability of a Schottky diode. The schematic cross-sectional architecture of the JBS diode is based on the PIN and Schottky diode designs.

The forward conduction mode, which is largely dictated by the metal-semiconductor Schottky barrier level, exhibits a unipolar current flow over the channels between the implanted P sites. This phenomenon is explained by the presence of interdigitated Schottky and P regions. Low ON-state losses and high-frequency switching come from insufficient ON-state voltage drop caused by insufficient injected minority carriers, which prevents bipolar switch-on. The P-N junctions become reverse-biased during switching blocking because the depletion region spreads over the P+ fingers and shields the Schottky junction from the strong electric field, reducing the leakage current, as described in [29]. ### 8.4.1.2 Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor (MOSFET)

Metal-oxide-semiconductor majority-carrier transistors, often referred to as power MOSFETs, are frequently appropriate for low-voltage, high-power circuits. A MOSFET is activated by applying a suitable bias voltage to its gate terminal, which is isolated from the semiconductor by an oxide layer. The current that passes via a surface channel is controlled by the threshold voltage of an ultrathin inversion layer. MOSFETs are ideal for use as switches in high-power circuits because of their very high input impedance and oxide interface at the gate input terminal. For high-frequency switch-mode applications, MOSFETs are the favored option because of their high-frequency switching, which allows the size of energy-capable components to be reduced, as described in [30].

Despite their previously shown advantages, silicon power MOSFETs are limited in their ability to block voltage by the trade-off between blocking voltage and ON-state resistance (RDS(on)). Reasonably priced silicon MOSFETs have breakdown voltages that are almost equal to 1,000 V. Nevertheless, there are several major disadvantages to the greater breakdown voltage, including very high ON-state resistance that restricts channel current or high input capacitance, which raises switching losses and gate drive requirements. A silicon carbide power MOSFET combines the high-blocking voltage capacity of the SiC material with the standard advantages of a MOSFET. In high-voltage applications, where silicon IGBTs now predominate, a SiC power MOSFET sets itself apart from other semiconductors due to its capacity to block high voltage while retaining a low ON-state resistance.

Business-grade SiC power MOSFETs are made by STMicroelectronics, ROHM Semiconductors, and CREE; commercial power MOSFETs typically have a blocking voltage rating of 1,200 V. Among the SiC power MOSFETs that are marketed, CREE produces the greatest blocking voltage of roughly 1,700 V that a single device can handle, as described in [31]. A co-packaged antiparallel SiC Schottky barrier diode (SBD) and a ROHM MOSFET (SCH2080KE) are connected to prevent power losses during reverse conduction. ### 8.4.1.3 Junction Field-Effect Transistor (JFET)

As previously mentioned, the current passing via a thin channel close to the oxide-semiconductor contact, as described in [32], represents the conduction system in a MOSFET. This suggests that the architecture of the device has to have an enhanced oxide-semiconductor interface. In the case of SiC, carbon-related surface states lead to restricted electron mobility at the channel surface and a high density of interface states in a thermally grown oxide layer. The main reason for the in-depth investigation of SiC JFET is the reliability of SiC MOSFET performance and stability on the oxide-semiconductor interface. JFETs are majority-carrier devices with properties comparable to power MOSFETs in terms of switching and conduction.

The continuous conduction path and control mechanism are where a MOSFET and JFET diverge most. In a MOSFET, the current flow is controlled at the surface of the device, but in a JFET, it is controlled through the device bulk. Applying a bias voltage at the oxide interface limits the channel current in MOSFETs, whereas a reverse-biased P-N junction limits the channel current in JFETs. SiC JFETs are better known for their resistance to temperature changes and other operating conditions, as they do not depend on the oxide-semiconductor interface. The fact that JFET devices operate in a normally-on mode with zero gate threshold voltage is one of their main drawbacks, meaning that they are frequently ON. The two configurations of the power junction field-effect transistor (JFET) structure are vertical (VJFET) and lateral (LJFET). The current path from channel to source determines the main difference between the two systems. The JFET structure is not the subject of a detailed analysis in this work. Infineon Technologies AG and United Silicon Carbide Inc. (USCi) create business-grade packaged SiC power JFETs, rated for 1,200 V breakdown voltage [33]. The devices are packaged in a TO-247-3 container and have a maximum operating temperature of 175°C for Infineon 1,200 V SiC JFETs, composed of power units made of silicon carbide. Large-scale solar energy collection systems demand more power than discrete SiC power devices can provide. Power modules offer several power semiconductor devices stacked on a single substrate, providing physical confinement, electrical and thermal interaction, and safety. A module's constituent parts can be assembled in a number of ways, from simple high-power diode modules to complex half and full bridge MOSFET/IGBT modules. It is capable of integrating many control circuits, including temperature sensing and shutdown, undervoltage protection, overcurrent, and gate driver, thanks to the power module's flexibility. One example of such a device is the intelligent power module (IPM). All SiC modules and hybrid (a combination of silicon and silicon carbide devices) are offered for commercial SiC power modules. Large suppliers supply some of the SiC power modules that are sold commercially, as described in [34].

## 8.5 CONCLUSION

Because solar cells are often less productive than other environmentally friendly power sources such as wind, it has been difficult to effectively harness solar energy. When these factors are taken into account, the cost of using CPVs or multi-junction solar cells with relatively good efficiency limits the options. When operating at maximum efficiency, the PV inverter architecture should be able to reduce capital investment. The unique material properties of SiC, compared with silicon, have enabled the development of devices such as MOSFETs, JFETs, and diodes. Presenting, analyzing, and comparing the SiC-based PV inverter with its silicon-based counterpart makes it clear that the former is more efficient and increases the percentage of the system that is made of silicon, which lowers the inverter cost. In a SiC-based system, the reduced material cost, especially the significant reduction in the size of passive components, offsets the high device cost. Similarly, the rapid development and commercialization of SiC power devices have resulted in a continuous cost decline. Although SiC is still a relatively new technology, the stability of the power devices is confirmed by a substantial amount of test data.
