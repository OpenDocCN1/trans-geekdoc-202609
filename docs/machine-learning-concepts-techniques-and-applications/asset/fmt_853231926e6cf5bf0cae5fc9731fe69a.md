
$$\mathbf{P}\left( \mathbf{D} \right) = 98\%\textbf{and} \textbf{therefore} \mathbf{P}\left( \mathbf{D}\hat{} \right) = 100 - 8 = 2\%$$

Now the conditional probability that a fraudulent activity happening is detected given that it has happened is:

$$\mathbf{P}\left( \mathbf{D} \mid \mathbf{F} \right) = 98\%\textbf{and} \textbf{therefore} \mathbf{P}\left( \mathbf{D} \mid \mathbf{F}\hat{} \right) = 6\%$$

1. With these probabilities we can use the Bayes theorem to calculate the probability that no fraudulent activity happened even if the system detected that it had happened (Equation 3.22):

$$
\begin{array}{lcl}
{P\left( F\hat{} \mid D \right)} & = & \frac{P\left( D \mid F\hat{} \right)P\left( F\hat{} \right)}{P\left( D \mid F\hat{} \right)P\left( F\hat{} \right) + P\left( D \mid F \right)P\left( F \right)} \\
& = & \frac{2\% \times 92\%}{2\% \times 92\% + 98\% \times 8\%} \\
& = & 0.1901 \\
\end{array}
$$

2. Now we use the Bayes theorem to calculate the probability that a fraudulent activity actually happened when the system detects it has happened (Equation 3.23):

$$
\begin{array}{lcl}
{P\left( F \mid D \right)} & = & \frac{P\left( D \mid F \right)P\left( F \right)}{P\left( D \mid F \right)P\left( F \right) + P\left( D \mid F\hat{} \right)P\left( F\hat{} \right)} \\
& = & \frac{98\% \times 8\%}{98\% \times 8\% + 2\% \times 92\%} \\
& = & 0.8099 \\
\end{array}
$$

3. We will first calculate the following probabilities:

$$P\left( D\hat{} \mid F \right) = 100\% - 98\% = 2\%$$

$$P\left( D\hat{} \mid F\hat{} \right) = 100\% - 6\% = 94\%$$

Now we use the Bayes theorem again to calculate the probability that a fraudulent activity happened but it was not detected by the system (Equation 3.24):

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
{P\left( F \mid D\hat{} \right)} & = & \frac{P\left( D\hat{} \mid F \right)P\left( F \right)}{P\left( D\hat{} \mid F \right)P\left( F \right) + P\left( D\hat{} \mid F\hat{} \right)P\left( F\hat{} \right)} \\
& = & \frac{2\% \times 8\%}{2\% \times 8\% + 94\% \times 92\%} \\
& = & 0.0018 \\
\end{array}
$$

4. Now we again use the Bayes theorem to calculate the probability that a fraudulent activity did not happen and it was correctly not detected by the system (Equation 3.25).

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
{P\left( F\hat{} \mid D\hat{} \right)} & = & \frac{P\left( D\hat{} \mid F\hat{} \right)P\left( F\hat{} \right)}{P\left( D\hat{} \mid F\hat{} \right)P\left( F\hat{} \right) + P\left( D\hat{} \mid F \right)P\left( F \right)} \\
& = & \frac{94\% \times 92\%}{94\% \times 92\% + 2\% \times 8\%} \\
& = & 0.9982 \\
\end{array}
$$

### 3.3.8 Bayes Theorem and Machine Learning

The Bayes theorem gives a mathematical formula for determining conditional probability. The Bayes theorem is very widely used in machine learning. Some of the important criteria when we fit a model are the choice of weights and thresholds. ### 3.3.9 Probability of Continuous Variables for Modelling the World

Now let consider the case of probability of continuous variables. The probability function that accompanies a continuous random variable is a continuous mathematical function that integrates to 1. Let `x` be a real number. Then we need to describe beliefs over `x` where `x` may be joint angles, price of stock, and so on.

Let `x` ∈ R^N. How do we describe beliefs over x? e.g., x is a face, joint angles

In the case of continuous variables, we describe the data in terms of a probability distribution function (PDF). One common distribution that is used to describe the data is the Gaussian or normal distribution. This distribution can be defined by the parameters mean `μ` and standard deviation `σ` ([Figure 3.19](#ch3.xhtml#fig3_19)). This is a bell-shaped curve with different centers and spreads depending on `μ` and `σ`.

![Figure 3.19 Normal or Gaussian distribution.](images/fig3_19_B.jpg)

[Figure 3.19 Normal or Gaussian distribution.](#ch3.xhtml#R_fig3_19)

Now the question is, why do we use a Gaussian distribution? This is due to the fact that it has convenient analytic properties; it is governed by the central limit theorem and works reasonably well for most real data. Though it does not suit all types of data, it acts as a good building block. The values of the data point `x` is given by the function (Equation 3.23)

(3.23)

$$f\left( x \right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mathit{\pi\sigma}}}e^{- {(x - \mu)}^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}$$

where `m` = mean, `s` = standard deviation, `π` = 3.14159, and `e` = 2.71828.

#### 3.3.9.1 Characteristics of a Normal Probability Distribution

**A normal probability distribution is bell-shaped** with a single peak at the center of the distribution, and

1. It is **symmetrical** about the mean.

2. It is **asymptotic**: The curve gets closer and closer to the `x-`axis but never actually touches it. To put it another way, the tails of the curve extend indefinitely in both directions.

3. The location of a normal distribution is determined by the mean, `m`, and the dispersion or spread of the distribution is determined by the standard deviation, `σ`.

4. The arithmetic **mean, median**, and **mode are equal**.

5. The total **area under the curve is 1.00**; half the area under the normal curve is to the right of this center point, and the other half to the left of it.

6. If two independent random variables follow a Gaussian distribution, then the sum of the two random variables also follows the Gaussian distribution.

#### 3.3.9.2 Standard Normal Probability Distribution

* A random variable that has a normal distribution with a **mean of zero and a standard deviation of one** is said to have a **standard normal probability distribution**.

* The letter `z` is commonly used to designate this normal random variable.

* The following expression (Equation 3.24) converts any normal distribution into the standard normal distribution:

(3.24)

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$
