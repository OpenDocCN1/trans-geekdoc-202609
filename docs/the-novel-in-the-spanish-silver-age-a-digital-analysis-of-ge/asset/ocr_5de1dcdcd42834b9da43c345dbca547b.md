Except for the works by Underwood (2016, 2019) mentioned previously, all the research on genres from Computational Linguistics and Digital Humanities presented in Chapter 2.2 is closer to the cross-sectional synchronic perspective. Some examples include Kessler, Nordic, and Schütz (1997), Stamatatos, Faktotakis, and Kokkinakis (2000), Santini (2011), Jickers (2013), Schöch (2013), Underwood (2014) or Hettinger et al. (2016). In all these cases, the year of publication and the historical development of the genres are of little importance and the analysis plots have no historical axis.

An interesting theoretical contribution comes from Schaeffer (1989, translated into Spanish in 2006), proposed a historically open understanding of genres, since “the classifier identity of a text is always open” (Schaeffer 2006, 101-2, my translation). For Schaeffer, labels are assigned in a chaotic process by several authors, creating a “patchwork lexicologique” (1989, 66) and thus the primary work of the researcher should not be in assigning labels, rather to “analyze the functionality of the genre, whatever they are, and to try to see what they are referring to” (Schaeffer 2006, 53, my translation).

Textomena have been studied in the field of Corpus Linguistics considering genres. An important authority on this topic is Biber, who analyzed how linguistic features are distributed in different genres (or register in terminology, Biber 1992, 332) and the correlation between the features using multiple factor analysis. These “bottom-up statistical analyses” (Biber 2014, 7) examine correlations between linguistic features, grouping linguistic units that tend to correlate and clustering them together in more general patterns, also called dimensions.$5$A score for each dimension can be computed for each text and a mean value calculated for each genre (Biber 2014, 13). “After the statistical analysis is completed, dimensions are interpreted functionally” (Biber 2014, 11).
