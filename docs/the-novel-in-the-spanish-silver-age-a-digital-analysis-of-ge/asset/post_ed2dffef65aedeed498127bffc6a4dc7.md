
He used Common Factor Analysis to distinguish mainly between two different concepts of categorization of texts: genres and text-types. For him, genres are categorizations based on external criteria, text-types are classes that are based exclusively on linguistic data. From the point of view of Machine Learning, this concept of genre can be understood as classes in a supervised task that are extracted from a third source like a catalog, while text-types result from clusters based on linguistic features. A discussion of the diachronic development of Biber’s terminology can be found in Santini (2004).

One of the first attempts at genre classification is undertaken by Kessler, Numberberg, and Schütze (1997). In their study, they analyze various categories, such as genre (editorial, legal, non-fiction, fiction), brow (popular, middle, uppermost, high), and narrative (narrative against non-narrative). For that, they use the Brown corpus, working with different supervised algorithms and extracted types of linguistic annotation as features (morphological, syntactic, punctuation, and lexical information). When the task is performed in a multi-class way, they obtain an accuracy of 0.79 (with a baseline of 0.33) using a two-layer neural network. The results achieved a mean accuracy over 0.9 when the classes are binary. Even when the structural cues (annotated by NLP tools) obtain better results than the superficial ones (lexical and punctuation), they assess the computational costs as unjustified (Kessler, Numberberg, and Schütze, 1997, 38). One of the largest caveats of this research is the unexplained mass sampling of the corpus, erasing 40% of the original texts without explaining the criteria. Besides, the three analyzed classes (brow, narrative, and genre) were not theoretically justified, and neither was their association tested. Furthermore, they modified the labels of the corpus (Kessler, Numberberg, and Schütze, 1997) and did not publish the data, so it is impossible to access how the whole process was.

From the same year is the work of Sigley (1997), who applies Principal Component Analysis (PCA) (in comparison to the Common Factor Analysis preferred by Biber) to a collection of texts from New Zealand. His aim is to create a principal component that correlates with the subjective understanding of what characterizes a text as more or less formal. For that, he models a series of tokens, annotating and grouping them with specific linguistic information and applying z-scores transformation. Finally, he employs PCA and obtains the different components by calculating the correlation of the principal component from numerical values from a group of annotators, with a correlation of 0.93. The results allow him to confirm the utility of the features and PCA in order to visualize the genres of the used corpora with two principal components and clusters them in a last step. Sigley's research and the one by Rauber and Müller-Kogler (2001) are two of the papers that applied clustering methods for genre analysis, at least before the rise of DH. Rauber and Müller-Kogler present a tool that allowed analyzing corpora through Self-Organizing Maps with the idea of using it for browsing libraries.

One of the first works about genre classification with texts in a language other than English is Statatos, Fakotakis, and Kokkinakis (2000), who examined several genres in a corpus of Modern Greek. Following Biber, they consider numerous kinds of linguistic and technical features, as example lexical features, grammatical information, vocabulary richness, and further information from NLP tools such as stemming. They compare the combination of features with 30 most frequent words and use Multiple Regression for analyzing the corpus. Their results show that their combination of features had fewer errors than both authorship and genre. From the current perspective, this work shows a clear advantage: the frontier of most frequent words has been largely overcome and raises the question whether the chosen features would still yield better results. The first article considering Spanish texts for genre classification, to my knowledge, is Serviño et al. (2004), who apply classification algorithms for journalistic speeches in a big corpus of Argentinian newspapers. They evaluate various algorithms and the annotation from several lexical tools and report an accuracy between 0.82 and 0.96 for different genres. Nevertheless, they select 10 out of 19 genres that achieve higher results in the first sections of the evaluation, a step that can be criticized as cherry picking.

The work by Berninger, Kim, and Ross (2008) presents a different approach to the genre problem: they focus on a way of collecting a corpus for genre analysis through student submissions. This is one of the works that analyzes the inter-annotator agreement about the labels of genres, fairly general in this research (poems, music sheets, curriculum vitae, scientific articles...). The agreement of the annotators is generally low, varying between 37% and 54% for all genres, although some show higher correspondence (handbooks, CVs) in contrast to others that could be understood at chance level (magazine article, business report, technical manual). In the genre palette they considers poems and books of fiction (probably the novel), whose agreement is around 75%, a surprisingly low result for such canonized genres. A possible explanation could be that they try to examine a larger number of genres (70), causing a large baseline (around 4%).

## 2.2 Genre in Digital Humanities: Methods, Features, and Data Representation

One of the most ambitious works about genre has been carried out by DH. Her study has several corpora, using features of many types: lexical, grammatical, pragmatic, and graphical (the layout of the websites, the only research that has used tags as parts of the features). She employs unsupervised, supervised, and rule-based techniques. Over various chapters, she discusses different models of understanding genre (hierarchical, prototype model, family resemblance), creating experiments to answer basic questions about genre that are seldom analyzed in other papers, such as the inter-annotator agreement or whether single-class classification is enough to analyze a genre. She concludes that genres are not mutually exclusive categories, proposing the zero-to-multi genre classification scheme in which each text might belong to n genres: individualization (a text does not belong to any genre) and hybridism (a text belongs to several genres) are common characteristics of genres that should be taken into consideration.

In her work of 2010, Santini papers the classification of genres of several corpora of websites, using the same features (lexical, grammatical, semantic, and format facets) as each corpus supplied. For the different corpora, results of accuracy lay between 0.42 and 0.94.

Finn and Kushmerick (2006) use the art from the theoretical and the computational approach to classify different aspects of the text such as its objectivity and the polarity of reviews. These are nowadays clearly related to sentiment analysis and not to genre classification. An interesting aspect of the paper is that instead of mixing different types of features (lexical and grammatical) into the same data frame, they calculate for each one a decision tree, and on top of it, an ensemble decision tree that takes a final decision. One of the later papers on classification that can still be placed in Computational Linguistics is Petrentz and Webber (2011), which suggest another form of evaluating the results genre classification. In their opinion, genre classification should not only be defined by accuracy but also through stability over different topics following the idea that genre should be independent of its theme.

## 2.2.5 Literary Genre in the Past Years

The two last papers of the previous section show a historical development in the area. In the later years of the 2000s and the beginning of 2010s, Computational Linguistics has shifted its interest from genre classification towards other types of textual categorization, especially semantic or topic related (Blei 2012), with a few exceptions such as the application of Deep Learning or the use of graphical features from the cover (Chiang, Ge, and Wu 2015). However, these years have seen a rising number of DH tackling through computational methods. Examples are Ramsay with networks and subgenres of Shakespeare (2003), Yu with the evaluation of different parameters in the classification of eroticism and sensitivity (2008), or Kestermont et al. also investigate the influence of genre in stylometry (2012).

A group at the Stanford Literary Lab (Allison, Heuser, Jockers, and Moretti) and Wimbledon published in 2011 the first Literary Lab Pamphlet, in which they analyze subgenres of the novel using PCA and clustering based on lexical and typographical information. Moretti had already done work about genres (2005), proposing the hypothesis that literary subgenres could have a certain periodicity in their development. One of the results of the group is that some highly frequent lexical units are useful markers for genres, but at the same time are highly interpretable and offer little new knowledge about their studied object: "If all in an audience work, and all women blue, the colors would differentiate them perfectly, and tell us nothing about them" (18). They clarify metaphors about genres, for example, buildings with "distinctive features at every possible scale of analysis: mortar, bricks, and architecture" (8), or compare genres with an iceberg "with a visible portion floating over the water, and a much larger part below, and extending to unknown depths" (25).
