These novels are not what a reader from the 21st century classify as erotic: They do describe physical scenes that are not treated in other genres, “in the eviction of sexual intercourse, most narr confess their failure in expressive capacity, quite often, one or several dotted lines are interspersed in a narrative that continues only with a temporary indication” (Rivalen Güeiro 2008, 197, my translation) or use highly metaphorical descriptions (Rivalen Güeiro 2008, 196). The erotic novel has been subjected to erotic criticism, even when authors produced complex texts tackling questions about family, abortion, divorce, or erotic aspects of the female and male roles of the bourgeois catholic society (Rivalen Güeiro 2008, 22). The narrations show a pedagogical aim, with long essay fragments, fragments and their plots end in many cases in dramatic and moralizing ways (García Lara 1980, 2186; Rivalen Güeiro 2008, 61). That leads García Lara to use the “social eroticism” as label (1986, 28). Although the erotic novel achieved a huge success, especially in the male middle classes (García Lara 1980, 215; 1986, 20 and 59), it has been “curiously and historically ignored by the collective memory” (Rivalen Güeiro 2008, 21, my translation). It belongs to low-brow literature (“infiltratura,” García Lara 1986, 19) and is often treated by researchers as a sociological phenomenon than a literary one (García Lara 1980, 216). A highly canonized author, Baroja, fashions a scene in one of his best-known novels, el árbol de la ciencia, in which erotic novels are mentioned:

One day he was shocked to see that the bookseller had to fifteen volumes with a cover on which a naked woman appeared. These were types of French-style novels: pornographic novels, clumsy, with a certain psychological varnish made for the use of military, students, and people of little mentality.– Asked Andres the bookseller.-Yes, it is the only thing that sells.(Baroja, originally published in 1911, edition by Caro Baroja 1985, 216, my translation).

The disdain for this subgenre and its readers (“of little mentality”) is obvious. This can have prevented the classification of erotic novel in cases of works written by highly canonized authors in which eroticism is central, like Las sonatas by Valle-Inclán, El gran hotel by Gómez de la Serna, or several of Pérez de Ayala’s works. Still, it is generally accepted that some important authors wrote erotic novels, such as Blasco Ibáñez, Dicenta, de Burgos, and Bazán. The two monographs mentioned before point out some of the typical features of these novels: Titles with female names or female references, starred by a middle-class woman, urban contemporary settings (normally Madrid or Paris), often with boat or train trips, descriptions of interior spaces, underwear, female bodies, and male faces are recurrent (Garcia Lara 1986; Rivalan Guéño 2008). A similar subgenre, intended for female readers, was the novela rosa, which, has reached even lower steps of the canonical stratification (Alvarez 2000).

## 2.1.9 Social Novels

Social novels are a major trend during the 19th century, becoming a bestseller and the fundamental genre for many decades of the serials (Pedrza Jiménez and Rodríguez Cáceres 1982, 236–37). Their readers are part of the working class and the authors try to illustrate a thesis against social injustice, normally normed by lower-class protagonists in urban settings. It has its origins in the French model of Eugène Sue and Les Mystères de Paris (1842–1843). They portray social in a simplistic scheme of good and bad social groups (Ferreras 1987, 70–71). Its development influenced other subgenres of the same period such as the costumbrist, historical, and realistic novel. The works of authors who started writing in the period of naturalism like Blasco Ibáñez (La barraca, 1988) or Sawa (Declaración de un vencido, 1987) are often classified as social novels around the turn of the century.

The label is used some decades later, especially in the 1930s, for some canonized works. In this decade, many authors were followers of pure and abstract art, guided by the idea art for art's sake, popularized by Ortega y Gasset (in Spanish: "el arte por el arte") in one of his influential works The Dehumanization of Art (1925). Nevertheless, this tendency found an answer, reconciled, with a turn to contemporary questions (Castañar 1992, 40; Aubert 2001, 15; Ródenas de Moya 2009). Two of the best representatives of this subgenre are Siente (Siete domingos rojos, 1932, Viaje a la aldea del crimen, 1934) and Díaz Fernández (El blaco, 1928: cfr. Fernández Cifuentes and Gonzalo Santonja 1984, 642), but also authors like Concha Espina, Arderius, Arconada, Benavides, and Salazar were part of it. The setting is typically an urban space in Spain, it takes place during a recent incident as wars or scenes of repression, and the protagonists tend to be a group of people (sometimes coming from the left) or archetypes (Castañar 1992, 30). The aim of many of these books was to criticize and mobilize lower classes of society and intellectuals (Castañar 1992). This becomes blatantly clear in Viaje a aldea del crimen, in which the author recounts that, ultimately, responsible for the brutal repression was Manuel Aza, Prime Minister of Spain at the time.

## 2.1.10 One-Author Labels: Nivola, Greguería, and Episodio

In Section 2.1.2, I have observed that researchers of this period have a strong tendency to investigate one author independently. This trend forced by the very authors, who, in many cases, coined their own label, similar to genres, without making explicit whether it should readily considered as a different category. The best-known case is Unamano's nivola, a text that was printed on the cover of his most important novel, Niebla (1914, 'Mist'). In a fictional preface, one of the characters, Victor, claims to be inventor of the concept of nivola and uses it to classify earlier novels by Unamuno (such as Amor y pedagogía, 1902). The edition of 1935 presented a third prologue (presenting Unamuno as author), in which this genre is explained as (my translation):

The novel, the epopee, and the drama impose themselves on the one who believes to be. On the agonists, their supposed creatures, are imposed upon him. Luz, Adam and Eve, later, And this one is nivola, epopee or tragedy! [...] invention of nivola. [...] This is the mist, this is the nivola, this is the legend, this is the eternal life... In a dialogue with the protagonist of Niebla (Augusto), the author of the first prologue (Victor) mentions the theoretical origin of this label:

I have heard Manuel Machado, the poet, Antonio’s brother, who once brought a sonnet to Don Eduardo, to read it to him, was written in Alexandrian verse or some heterodox form. He read it and Don Eduardo said: "But that’s not sonnet..." "No, sir," said Machado, "it’s not soneto, it’s... Well, with my novel, it’s not going to be a novel, but ... what did I call it?, nivulo... no, no, nivola, that’s it, nivola! Then one will have the right to say that it violates the laws of the novel... I invent the genre, and inventing a genre is nothing more than giving a new name, and I give it the laws I like (Unamuno, originally published in 1914, edition by Valdés 1982, 200, my translation).

The fictional text presents a mix of philosophical, playful, and provocative intentions of "inventing a genre" by two steps: creating a label and assigning some rules. Since the publication of this work by Unamuno, libraries, publishers, and researchers have used the label in their descriptions and metadata, some of them arguing in favor of taking it as a genre (Olávers 1993, 16; Landiera 1985, 37), so a century after his publication, the label can be found in the very basic information of Wikidata about Niebla.

Many other authors coined what I call author-labels: Labels as if they designated genres (or subgenres of the novel) that were only fashioned by one single author, such as Galdós (*episodio nacional*), Pérez de Ayala (*novela-normativa*), the example *nivola* of Unamuno (*novela novativa*, *novela simbólica*), Baroja (*novela de acción*), Azorín (*novela impresionista*), Valle-Inclán (*espervento*) Blasco-Ibáñez (*novelas valencianas*), *novela grande*, or Gómez de la Serna with several ones: *greguería*, *novela de la nebulosa*, *novela grande*, or *multitudinaria*. Mainer states about this last author that "he freed the genres" (Mainer, Alvar, and Navarro 1997, 596, my translation), while Umbral points out that he is a "writer without genre" "pretended genre" (1984, 226–29, my translation). These *greguerías* are, along with the *nivolas*, one of the best-examples. In this case, the texts are composed of sentences in prose, normally quite short, in some cases lacking a verb, typically amusing, surprising, poetic, and transcendental (Senabre 1984). Although it would not be typically considered as a subset of the novel, it is not formally related to any other genre prose (Rdenas de Moya 2000, 49). Here is a selection of *greguerías* (my translation, in the original they do not form any stanza or paragraph, nor do they need to follow a certain order):

- Slice of watermelon: mood of blood.
- The O is the yawn of the alphabet.
- Eternity envies the mortal.
- The kiss is hunger for immortality.
- The head is the fishbowl of ideas.
