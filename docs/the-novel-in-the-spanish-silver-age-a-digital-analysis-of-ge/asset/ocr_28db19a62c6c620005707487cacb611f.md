The distribution of subgenres over decades in Figure 14 shows a tendency to contain one or two subgenres that are far more common than the rest during any given decade. This could be expected from labels like naturalist novel, which could be understood as a term that describes a literary period: The naturalist novel would be the novel published at the end of the 19th century. Indeed, realist novels are more frequent in the 1880s than in the 1920s or the 1930s, although its frequency in the 1910s is also one of the highest. The most important aspect of this figure is to observe that the realist novel does not have a stronger association with a certain decade than the rest. Other subgenres that are not so strongly associated with a literary period do show clear peaks in some decades, such as the social novel in the 1910s, the erotic in the 1900s and 1910s, or the historical novel in the 1920s.

The subgenres show correlations with other variables that make it difficult, in many cases impossible, to balance them and have similar numbers of texts. I have shown why it is not possible to balance subgenres through chronology. I will discuss at the end of this chapter, this is also the case for the author's gender. This "undesired correlation" (Schö 2017a, 226) could be seen as an intrinsic phenomenon between categories of literary (or even humanistic) data. Another variable that also shows a strong association with the subgenres is the author, a category that is plotted (only the five most frequent labels) in the heat map of Figure 15.
