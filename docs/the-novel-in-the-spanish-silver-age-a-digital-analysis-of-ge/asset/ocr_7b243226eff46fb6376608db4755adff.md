All the articles I will mention in this chapter have understood genre as categorical, therefore applying classification to its study. In most of the works about the topic, each text can belong to several genres. That is why it has been treated as a multi-label problem, in which the labels are transformed into binary classes: whether the text belong to the adventure novel or not; whether the same text belongs to the naturalist novel or not, etc. (See Section 2.3.3.2). However, some articles have preferred to treat genres as a multiclass problem in which each text belongs only to one category (cf. Kessler, Numberg, and Schütze 1992; Hettinger et al. 2016). The model of binary classifiers already already from the classic scholastic model of taxonomies of genres in which each text hung from a single genre, as a leaf hangs from a single branch, which will be presented in Section 2.3.2.1. Binary genres in that every text belong to any number of classes. Using the binary labels of the genres, Santini (2011) developed a theoretical model for the analysis of genres of the Web, she proposed a scheme that makes explicit that each text can belong to any number of genres, from zero (individuality) to several (hybridism), which will be presented in more detail in the next section.



Genre has been an analytical category in several areas of Digital Humanities, especially those who use text as their primary data. In stylometry, the corpora are mostly composed of a single genre (like theater plays) or subgenre (like tragedy) because it is expected that different genres exhibit linguistic deferences.
