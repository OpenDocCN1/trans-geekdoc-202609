These novels are not what a reader from the 21st century classify as erotic: They do describe physical scenes that are not treated in other genres, “in the eviction of sexual intercourse, most narr confess their failure in expressive capacity, quite often, one or several dotted lines are interspersed in a narrative that continues only with a temporary indication” (Rivalen Güeiro 2008, 197, my translation) or use highly metaphorical descriptions (Rivalen Güeiro 2008, 196). The erotic novel has been subjected to erotic criticism, even when authors produced complex texts tackling questions about family, abortion, divorce, or erotic aspects of the female and male roles of the bourgeois catholic society (Rivalen Güeiro 2008, 22). The narrations show a pedagogical aim, with long essay fragments, fragments and their plots end in many cases in dramatic and moralizing ways (García Lara 1980, 2186; Rivalen Güeiro 2008, 61). That leads García Lara to use the “social eroticism” as label (1986, 28). Although the erotic novel achieved a huge success, especially in the male middle classes (García Lara 1980, 215; 1986, 20 and 59), it has been “curiously and historically ignored by the collective memory” (Rivalen Güeiro 2008, 21, my translation). It belongs to low-brow literature (“infiltratura,” García Lara 1986, 19) and is often treated by researchers as a sociological phenomenon than a literary one (García Lara 1980, 216). A highly canonized author, Baroja, fashions a scene in one of his best-known novels, el árbol de la ciencia, in which erotic novels are mentioned:

One day he was shocked to see that the bookseller had to fifteen volumes with a cover on which a naked woman appeared. These were types of French-style novels: pornographic novels, clumsy, with a certain psychological varnish made for the use of military, students, and people of little mentality.– Asked Andres the bookseller.-Yes, it is the only thing that sells.(Baroja, originally published in 1911, edition by Caro Baroja 1985, 216, my translation).

The disdain for this subgenre and its readers (“of little mentality”) is obvious. This can have prevented the classification of erotic novel in cases of works written by highly canonized authors in which eroticism is central, like Las sonatas by Valle-Inclán, El gran hotel by Gómez de la Serna, or several of Pérez de Ayala’s works.
