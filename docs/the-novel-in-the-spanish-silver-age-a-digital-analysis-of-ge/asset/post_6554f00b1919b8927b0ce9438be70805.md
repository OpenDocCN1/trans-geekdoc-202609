How can these two clusters (or groups) of texts be understood? I have evaluated both of them and they do not show a stronger dependency on authorship or decade than other subgenres – both clusters contain many texts written by several authors in several decades. The clusters fit neither a specific group of authors nor a generational change. Although both clusters show similarities to subgenre, they do not map an already known subgenre – cluster 217 shows a stronger association with modernist and philosophical novels; adding other texts with similar direct communication (letters), novels with autodiegetic narratives to this subgenre.

Are these similarities only observable in the linguistic features? No, both clusters have shown numerous features in the metadata and annotation that distinguish them significantly. Nevertheless, differences become obvious in groups. While cluster 50 shows only a few features differentiating texts, cluster 217 is definable in terms of protagonist, chronological and geographical setting, and relationship to the author, showing a great coherence in terms of metadata.

Are these clusters subgenres hidden until now? This depends on how the researcher defines subgenres and the perspective of their study (Section 2.3.4). The researcher can undertake a longitudinal perspective, analyzing them as the communicative process between author and reader, who will require observing the clusters under these terms. In my research, I have adopted a cross-sectional perspective, allowing different institutions to identify the lists of authors and assign them to texts, also retrospectively. As the results of Chapters 6.1 and 7.1, these clusters can be predicted by linguistic and literary features. From this perspective, subgenres are groups of texts which share linguistic and literary phenomena and can be identified and labeled respectively by other agents (publishers, scholars). These clusters fit this definition of subgenre, behaving similar to others. They are coherent groups of texts in linguistic and literary terms that do not map authorship, decade, or generation. I do not have a better way of understanding these clusters or ascribe them to the category of subgenres.

What should these clusters be called? What exact label should be used for them? They could be referred to as cluster 217 (a cold place holder), "somewhat autobiographical novels, normally with a male protagonist traveling through rural Spain, with much description and reflection, and little action and communication" (a long descriptive label), or a label that could be coined, such as monodialogue novels (cluster 50) or rural-intimist novels (cluster 217), making clear that they are considered subgenres.

However, I am proposing to use the concept of buolic for the last group, which is applied in many European traditions in several periods and in combination with other genres (such as buolic poetry or pastoral plays). The concept of buolic is frequently associated with one of the authors that represent this subgenre: Miró (Alisent 1988; Fernández Palmer 2019; Benyeto Pérez 1980; Lina Corona 2009; Baquero Goyanes 1973). If the label is accepted, we could consider that a buolic novel existed and practised mainly between 1908 and 1927, with many examples such as Miró, Valle-Inclán, Azorin, or Jarnés, i.e. the buolic novel. The concept reflect many characteristics of these novels, such as perception, description of nature, and a rural setting. However, the label buolic novel is really satisfying as a secondary matter. The important finding is the identification of these groups of texts.
