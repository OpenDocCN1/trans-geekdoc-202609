By the end of the decade, the naturalist novel branched off into continuations (Bretz 1992, 79). First, Galdós was again responsible for a historical milestone using his works Realidad and la incógnita (from from 1889) (Beyrie and Aubert 2001, 31; Lisorgues 2001, 53). These two novels broke with the reality tendency using meta-literary unresolved references that led the readers to decide whether they should use the novels as realistic or not. Canonical authors remain partially within these post-realistic subgenres, close to the modern novel, writing non-realistic texts such as Morsamor (1889) by Valera, El saludo de las brujas (1889) by Bazán, or el caballero encantado (1909) by Galdós (Longhurst 1999, 6–7); Beyrie and Aubert 2001, 32). Second, theism of the new erotic novel gained importance and became the fundamental aspect of the following decades. Third, there is a more morbid version of the naturalist novel, with Sawa (La mejor de todo el mundo, 1885) as its major representative by Pattison 1965, 137–38). These novels are integrated in the decadent style of modernity and are rather parallel to the canon.

In any case, both realist and naturalist novels endured during the rest of the period: the trend of naturalism returned in the 1910s (with authors like Fernández Flóriz; or de Burgos) evolving later into the social novel, while younger authors published realist novels (Concha Espina, Zamacois, or Cossio).

# 2.1.6 Historical and Adventure Novels

In the European tradition, the historical novel has its roots in Walter Scott (Lukács 1955) with novels like Ivanhoe (1819-1820) and Waverley (1814) as prototypes. They represent the starting point of a genre that spread across Europe and was practiced in different languages (Ferreras 1987, 30–31; Montesinos 1980, 79–80).
