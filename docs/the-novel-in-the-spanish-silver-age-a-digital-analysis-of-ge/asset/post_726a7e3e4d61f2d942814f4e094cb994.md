By the end of the decade, the naturalist novel branched off into continuations (Bretz 1992, 79). First, Galdós was again responsible for a historical milestone using his works *Realidad* and *la incógnita* (from 1889) (Beyrie and Aubert 2001, 31; Lisorgues 2001, 53). These two novels broke with the reality tendency using meta-literary unresolved references that led the readers to decide whether they should use the novels as realistic or not. Canonical authors remain partially within these post-realistic subgenres, close to the modern novel, writing non-realistic texts such as *Morsamor* (1889) by Valera, *El saludo de las brujas* (1889) by Bazán, or *el caballero encantado* (1909) by Galdós (Longhurst 1999, 6–7); Beyrie and Aubert 2001, 32). Second, theism of the new erotic novel gained importance and became the fundamental aspect of the following decades. Third, there is a more morbid version of the naturalist novel, with Sawa (*La mejor de todo el mundo*, 1885) as its major representative (Pattison 1965, 137–38). These novels are integrated in the decadent style of modernity and are rather parallel to the canon.

In any case, both realist and naturalist novels endured during the rest of the period: the trend of naturalism returned in the 1910s (with authors like Fernández Flóriz; or de Burgos) evolving later into the social novel, while younger authors published realist novels (Concha Espina, Zamacois, or Cossio).

## 2.1.6 Historical and Adventure Novels

In the European tradition, the historical novel has its roots in Walter Scott (Lukács 1955) with novels like *Ivanhoe* (1819-1820) and *Waverley* (1814) as prototypes. They represent the starting point of a genre that spread across Europe and was practiced in different languages (Ferreras 1987, 30–31; Montesinos 1980, 79–80). In the Spanish tradition, this subgenre has been profoundly and meticulously analyzed by Ferreras (1987, 1988). Scott’s works arrived in Spain mainly by way of translations, the first one around 1823 (Pedraza Jiménez and Rodríguez Cáceres 1982, 191). Some of the first examples of Spanish historical novels are *Ramiro, conde de Lucanorum* by Rafael Húmara, or *Los bandos de Castilla* by Ramón López Solver (printed in 1830, Ferreras 1987, 37). Already in the next decade the subgenre evolved in Spain with distinctive features, becoming more politicized and developing into one of the major trends in the publishing industry (González García 2005; Ferreras 1987, 32–33). The typical plot of these texts shows the vicissitudes of a hero within Manichean logic, in many medieval times, with unrealistic elements (such as wizards or witches), a melodramatic ending, and it often starts with a found manuscript (Ferreras 1987, 69–70); Román Gutiérrez 1988, 144). The goal of these authors is, on one side, to amuse the reader; on the other side, to address contemporary political topics through historical settings. Therefore, the aim of teaching history stays in the background. During the decade of the 1840s, the importance of adventures grew, branching off into historical adventure novels (Pedrazza Jiménez and Rodríguez Cáceres 1982, Ferreras 1987, 32–33) or social novels published in the form of serials (folletín).

As mentioned before, decades later in 1870, a young writer published *La fortuna de oro*, set in the Madrid of the 1820s. Who became Galdós's first novel (Lisorgues 2001; Byrne and Aubet 2001, 27), who became the most important author in the following decades. Only three years later, he started his *Episodios nacionales* ('National episodes'), a series of 46 novels published during the rest of his life, narrating different chapters of Spanish history of the 19th century, the majority written in first person, many of them sharing characters or even protagonists (Estévez 2013). The label coined by Galdós (*episodios nacionales*) was published on the cover of each edition and was traditionally used as either being related to the historical novel or as a subtitle of it (recently discussed in Isaís 2017, 32–34), but overlapping with other subgenres as intrigue, picaresque, or adventure novels (Dendle 1992, 29–30). The idea of historical novels was also projected by authors such as Costa or Valle-Inclán, who could not fulfill it all. Baroja accomplished a similar project some decades later, labeling it *Memorias de un hombre de acción* ('Memories of a man of action'), also with recurring characters, protagonists (mainly Eugenio de Aireneta), and with a first-person narrative.

Other authors (Bazán, Unamuno, Valle-Inclán, Azorín, Blasco Ibáñez, Concha Espina, Sender) also wrote historical novels without this series. One of the most common topics of historical novels were wars, especially the Peninsular Napoleonic Wars and the Carlist Wars, but also the First World War or, in later decades, the Spanish campaigns against the revolts in its colonies in present-day Morocco and Western Sahara. Many of these texts are also related to historical or war novels.

As I have already mentioned, adventure novels (*novelas de aventuras*) are understood in the history of literature as a continuation of the first historical novels, with Scott as its common starting point but also with other international examples such as Dumas, Poe, or Verne (Lara López 2000, 99 and 108). Many of these texts, historical elements disintegrated and unforeseen incidents grew in importance (Ferreras 1987, 32–33). Two of its most fundamental authors were: Manuel Fernández y González, a very prolific author with works like *Allah-Akbar: ¡Dios es grande!; or *El pastelero de Madrid* (1862) (Pedraza Jiménez y Rodríguez Cáceres 1982, 230–32; Lara López 2000, 105); and Enrique Pérez Escobar, with texts like *El cura de la aldea* (1863) (fletines), modifying the structure of the works to create distributed as serials (Román Gutiérrez 1988, 157). Many of these texts were distributed to create cliffhangers in each chapter (Ferreras 1987, 61) and are still kept in the bounds of low-brow literature, somehow included in literary research.

After 1880, some works by Pío Baroja, but also from his brother Ricardo, are often classified as adventure novels. In many cases, they take place in the past or contemporary times depict important historical events. As Lara López, the adventure novel has its golden period during the first third of the century (2000, 106) and started to be labeled *de aventuras*. Its most typical features are the action and physical risk for the protagonist, exotic journeys and a fast narrative rhythm (98).

## The Novel in the Spanish Silver Age

In the later decades of the silver age, comedy writers (novelas humorísticas) became a trend practised by a generation (who wrote mainly theater plays) that better known (1998, 27). Also, names of this comedy generation are de Gavilán, Neville, Javier Poncela, Mihura and López Rubio. Their works were primarily published during the 1920s. Fernández Fló was the first author of the silver age who dedicated a great deal of his work, with texts like *El mal Carabel* (1931) or *El hombre que compró un automóvil* (1932) (Echeverría Pazos 1987). De la Serna used humor in his novels, for example in *Rebéca!* (1936) (Flores Requejo 2000). Jardiel Poncela, who wrote mainly theater, used humor in his novels, like *La tournée de Dios* (1932), a narration about an official of God to Spain and everything that goes wrong (Pérez 1993; Pedro Dominguez 1994). Other authors such as Carrero, Ros, or Pemán produced similar novels, continuing the trend before the Civil War. Several researchers have observed aspects like fantasy, autobiography, eroticism, and irrationalism are used to represent intellectual comedy and satire (Echeverría Pazos 1987, 94–96; Pérez 1993, 48). In many cases the stories were buried with critical traces, as *Relato inmoral* by Fernández Fló, which addresses the social situation of single mothers.

The topics eroticism and sex have already appeared in the description of different subgenres of the period, mainly in the naturalist novel (Garcia Lara 1986, 29). After the 1890s, a section of the naturalist novel evolved into a separated subgenre, the erotic novel, following again the model of the French literature. This type of novels and novellas (novelas cortas) has been analyzed in two monographs, each with very different methodologies and aims: while Rivalan Guegu describes a specific and large corpus, Analyzing separately particular aspects (2008), the influential work of Garcia Lara takes a rather abstract perspective (1986).

The most frequently mentioned writers in relation to the erotic novel are Zamacois (*El seductor*, 1902) and Trigo (*Del frio al fuego*, 1906, Si, sé por qué).
