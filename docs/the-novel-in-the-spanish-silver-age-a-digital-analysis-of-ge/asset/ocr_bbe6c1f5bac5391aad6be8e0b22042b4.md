As I have already mentioned, adventure novels (novelas de aventuras) are understood in the history of literature as a continuation of the first historical novels, with Scott as its common starting point but also with other international examples such as Dumas, Poe, or Verne (Lara López 2000, 99 and 108). Many of these texts, historical elements disintegrated and unforeseen incidents grew in importance (Ferasbrar 1987, 32–33). Two of its most fundamental authors were: Manuel Fernández y González, a very prolific author with works like Allah-Akbar: ¡Dios es grande!; or El pastelero de Madrid (1862) (Prajra Jiménez y Rodríguez Cáceres 1982, 230–32; Lara López 2000, 105); and Enrique Pérez Escobar, with texts like El cura de la aldea (1863) (fletines), modifying the structure of the works to create distributed as serials (Román Gutiérrez 1988, 157). Many of these texts were distributed to create cliffhangers in each chapter (Ferasbrar 1987, 61) and are still kept in the bounds of low-brow literature, somehow included in literary research.

After 1880, some works by Pío Baroja, but also from his brother Ricardo, are often classified as adventure novels. In many cases, they take place in the past or contemporary times depict important historical events, using the label *novelas de acción* (‘action novels’) instead of *novelas de aventuras*. As Lara López, the adventure novel has its golden period during the first third of the century in pulp magazines finally coining the label *de aventuras*, and evolved after the Civil War towards Western novels (2000, 106 and 120) or started to be labeled as juvenile novels (Martínez de la Hidalgo 2000, 46). Its most typical features are the action and physical risk for the protagonist, exotic journeys and, a fast narrative rhythm (Lara López 2000, 98–100).
