The result of this last label is surprising, because is one of the few that can be related to a semantic feature in the plots and to the intention of the books. Looking at how it was used on the covers, it is observed that it appear only on the covers of Valdés. In general, in the contingency table one can see that the labels of the first editions are strongly associated with the author. The chi-square test with author name and first edition labels a p-value very close to zero (1.2e-42), so the association is highly significant. This means the labels found on the covers are highly depend on the authors who wrote them.

But are the rest of the sources free of this association? Are the current publishers or scholars not influenced by the association of some authors' name with specific labels? As I have demonstrated in the analysis of agreement between sources in Section 5.1.6, covers are a central source of labels when comparing to the rest. If covers are associated with the author, it is reason to expect that others will share it. For this, I run the chi-square test, each time comparing each source with the name of the author. From the nine different sources, of them show meaningful associations with the name of the author (p-value < 0.001): the literary manual MdLE, the first edition, the covers in the National Library, and any annotation. To better compare the results, the p-values can be plotted as bars in Figure 59, with a logarithmic axis, which shows the probabilities that each source is associated with the name of the author. The larger the p-value, the less probable it is that the label and the name of the author are associated with each other.

The labels provided by publishers on Amazon, ePubLibre, the manual HdLE, DB-peatia, and Wikidata not seem to have a direct association with the authors. The four other sources show p-values below 0.001, the distance between them is also notable: The lowest is for MdLE, followed by the first edition, and with many zeros, the information of the covers, and any annotation. ## 5.2.4 Conclusions

In this section, I have run several experiments about the status of labels on the cover of the first publication. I have presented and evaluated two competing hypotheses, i.e. that these labels should be either closer to internal features or to the author.

As a first step, I have observed that the number of works labeled in the first publication is relatively small, with a high tendency to use very specific labels for one or two authors.

As a second step, I have classified these subgenres based on linguistic features before comparing them to the classification of the rest of the sources. The labels of the first publication achieve lower results and show greater variance than the rest.

As a third step, I have calculated whether the first edition is associated with the name of the author, and I compared it to the rest of the sources. The first edition is one of the sources that shows the strongest dependency on the writer's name.

For this corpus, selecting only the labels from the first edition would cause a strong reduction of the analyzed labels, the researched texts, with lower results in classification using linguistic data, with a stronger dependency on authorship. That is why I have decided to use information from all covers and not only the first one as a source with the same status regardless of whether they come from publishers, manuals, or my own annotation.
