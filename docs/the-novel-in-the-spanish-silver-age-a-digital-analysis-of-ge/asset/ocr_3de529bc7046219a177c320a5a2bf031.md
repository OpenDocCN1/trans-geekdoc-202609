Historical and war novels share France as a typical setting, as well as vocabulary about soldiers, war, or fighting. The three subgenres share semantic features about militia, people, and rank (the three blue nodes in the middle). All these features are distinctive to a different degree for each category. The z-score for militia in war novels is higher than the other two, which can be seen in the thickness of the edges. This means that subgenres share features to different degrees.

The sixth observation states that perfect classification is very rare, which leads to the idea that the features do not hold a set of necessary and sufficient conditions to be considered part of a genre. If this was the case, computational methods working on complex features would identify these conditions and achieve perfect results. Texts have only to show a certain number of features to belong to a genre, just as people do not share all the traits to belong to a family.¹ To observe this, the three nodes are added in Figure 106 to a single tripartite graph of texts (green), features (blue), and genres (purple).
