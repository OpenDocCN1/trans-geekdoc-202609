Researchers use frequencies of linguistic features as an easy but efficient proxy for more complex phenomena: Exotic settings should be expressed through verbs related to boats and names about plants, animals, or food, a child protagonist should be verbalized to a certain degree with vocabulary siblings and school, etc. (Allison et al. 2011, 24; Jokers 2013, 92; Underwood 2016; Schöch 2017b).

In this section, I apply the metadata about the content of the novel, presented and described for the entire corpus in Chapter 3.2. This contains manually annotated information about the protagonist, the geographical and temporal setting of the plot, the narrator, the relation to the author, the ending, etc. All this data will be called *literary characteristics of the novel* and this section will be treated as *literary features*. In contrast, the several linguistic annotations (presented in Chapter 4.1) will from now be called *linguistic features*, such as the frequencies of tokens, grammatical information, TEI-tags, sentence length, etc.

Nominal definitions of subgenres are normally expressed in combination of literary characteristics (protagonist, setting, plot, end) and some characteristics about the structure, style, theme or aim (see Chapter 2.1). For example, historical novels could be nominallyinally defining as a novel set in the past and with a realistic representation of the world. If metadata about all historical novels is available, would it really be the case that all novels follow this pattern? The use of literary phenomena encoded as metadata and passed to the algorithm as features is a bridge between two tasks that have been considered until now to be very different: the classification and definition of subgenres.

This section has three goals:

+   1. Evaluate whether literary metadata used as features can yield higher results in a classification task than linguistic features.
2. Observe whether the subgenres are correctly classified with linguistic features similar results with literary metadata.
3. Establish whether there is less variance in the results of the classification than using linguistic features.
