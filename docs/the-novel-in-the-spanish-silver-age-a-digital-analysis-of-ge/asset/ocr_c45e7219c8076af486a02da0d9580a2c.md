It is traditionally accepted that although this model is able to formally define concepts (from areas including law or the natural sciences, as legal institutions, substances, units of measure or being), it fails to accurately distinguish cultural and social concepts with blurry borders, for example literary concepts (Vivas 1968; Atkins and Rundell 2008, 416–17; Hempfer 2014).

This rigid process of assigning a categorical label to each referent based on the necessary and sufficient conditions of the concept is, in many contexts, under classification. In many cases it is accepted that the referent should be labeled with a single term, as animal belongs to a single genus rather than to several. This concept of classification differs from that of Machine Learning, where distinct features are used to identify the sharing a label from those, rather than search for necessary and sufficient conditions. In addition, the computational classification is differentiated into multi-class (each instance belongs to a single class, where each novel belongs to a subgenre) and multi-label (each instance can belong to several classes, where a novel is classified as both adventure and historical). See Section 2.2. for details.

Which genre labels should be classified (understood either from the logical or the computational perspective) is an open question, but in many cases the basic set of possible genres (or genre palette) has its roots in again classic philosophy. Plato is normally the first authority on genres when viewed chronologically, and he applied a three-part distinction, using the relationship between poet and text as criteria: The poet himself talks (dithyramb), lets others speak (agedy and comedy), and a combination of both (epic, Garrido Gallardo 1980; Zymner 2003). Aristotle’s Poetics became one of the most influential works of all time in literature (Garrido Gallardo 1988, 9; Genette 1988; Garcia Barrio and Huerta Latino 1992; Schaeffer 2006, 7). It is a short text with the goal of describing several literary genres which became normative over time (Zymner 2003, 11–12). Aristotle distinguished two main genres for: the mode of imitation (lysis in Plato, Genette 1980), and the register of the imitation. Garrido Gallardo argues that Poetics has a mainly textual-linguistic perspective for the genres (1988, 17). In any case, this influential text comprised also a three-part schema, in many cases understood as the distinction between drama, epic and lyric. However following Genette, Aristotle actually differentiated between dithyramb, epopee and drama (1988, 186–87).
