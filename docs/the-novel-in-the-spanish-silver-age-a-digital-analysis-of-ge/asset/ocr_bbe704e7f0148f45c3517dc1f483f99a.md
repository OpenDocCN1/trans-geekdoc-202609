This improves to 0.86 when linguistic and literary features are concatenated, and up to 0.89 using chapters instead of entire volumes as instances. This means that the results are accept high, but still far from being perfect. However, this lack of perfection is not a problem in Digital Humanities research. In comparison, the classic scholastic-structuralist model does require the necessary and sufficient conditions to correctly classify all instances. In computational terms, this would mean that a series of features has to be present in all instances of a category, and be absent in all instances that do not participate in the category. These expectations are much higher than for Machine Learning, in which accept high scores (around 0.9 F1-score) are positively evaluated, and in some cases, perfect results are viewed with a certain skepticism. Besides, there is no expectation of finding the necessary and sufficient conditions, but rather that different features define whether instances are part of the category or not. In other words, Machine Learning (and specifically the use of multi-label tasks) more accurately fits the expectation of the family resemblance model (see Section 2.3.2.3).

However, the multi-label classification task and the family resemblance model require the instance to participate in categorical terms, not in degree. A person either belongs or not belong to a family, a text either belongs or not belongs to a subgenre. Nonetheless, scholars tend to describe these relations in degrees: The texts mainly to subgenre. Since the labels were originally gathered from several sources, this number can be used as the base for ordinal labels. These have been compared with the probabilities of the classification for each category. The correlations range from strong to very strong, meaning that both humans and algorithms have similar manners of assigning the information about subgenre, not in categorical terms in degrees. These results fit the prototype theory, in which some instances are better representatives of a category.

In any of the tasks, there is always a variance in results: Some subgenres are clearly classified than others. The traditional explanation is that the linguistic features are better proxies for some subgenres than others (Allison et al. 2011, 24; Jokers 2013, 92). A classification run using complex metadata shows similar variances. The concatenation of both types of features still shows a variance in the results. These results contradict the idea that superficial features are not able to correctly map complex features, but rather that some subgenres just have more accurate features than others.
