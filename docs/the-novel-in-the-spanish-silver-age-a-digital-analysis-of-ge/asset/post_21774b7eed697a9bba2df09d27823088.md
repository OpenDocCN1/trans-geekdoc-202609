## 2.1.4 The Novel and its Borders

As discussed, the literary genres, and the novel one of them, show a strong tendency of seeking freedom, heterogeneity, and new forms during this period. In some cases, this leads to a certain proximity to other genres, such as theater, poetry, biography, and beyond. This proximity to other genres can be understood as the origin of specific subgenres of the novel as the dialogue novel or the lyrical novel. They can be observed as heteronyms of the novel (types of novels) or as overlaps between the novel and other literary genres (in Section 2.3.3, I will present several ways of structuring several genres). Probably the most evident subgenre that exemplifies the closeness between novels and theater is the dialogue novel, which is defined as a text made of only (or mainly) dialogue, using the traditional typographical convention of plays:

- Name of the character.- Dialogue (information about movement or details in italics as stage directions).

Some researchers treat these novels as a phenomenon of the 20th century (Garrido Dominguez 2009, 776; Spang 2009, 1296–97; Jiménez 1998, 20), it should be taken into consideration that previous authors, like Galdós, wrote in this category during the 19th century (Escobar Bonilla 1997). Furthermore, it can be argued that they stem the classic dialogue genre, which was very common in previous centuries. Lissorgue interprets these novels as a new narrative technique that provides more freedom to the characters and keeps the narrator from assuming his natural role (2001, 71). Besides this subgenre, the influence of the theater has been tracked in other forms, for example as a general characteristic of the prose of the del 98 (Pedraza Jiménez and Rodríguez Cáceres 1986, 115; Rubio Jiménez 1998, 20).

## 2.1.5 Realist and Naturalist Novel

After giving an overview of the novel and its relationship to other major genres, in chronological order, starting with the realist and naturalist novel. These two subgenres are rooted in the decade of the 1870s and are often treated together during consecutive decades. Many Spanish authors worked in both categories are Galdós, who is considered as the initiator of the new tendencies (Ferreras 1988, 37). Both subgenres share a common European framework in which they evolved with a certain delay in comparison to other national traditions (Ferreras 1988, 12; Lissorgues 2001, 57).

The historical event that is conventionally referred to as the initial phase of the realist novel in Spain is the Revolution of 1868 when the liberal bourgeoisie led the first attempt to create a democratic system in Spain (as a parliamentary monarchy under Amadeo I, later as the First Republic). To reference the concept of *generación* del 68, which is the most canonical accounts of the novel of that time belong: Galdós, Bazán, Clarín, and Valera (Ferreras 1988, 12–13). The literary starting point is normally two years later, 1870, with the publication of *Fontana de oro* by Galdós (Pedraza Jiménez and Rodríguez Cáceres 1982, 33).

## The Novel in the Spanish Silver Age

The relation between novel and lyrical poetry is more complex, as the large number of different labels proves: novela lírica, novela poética, novela poemática, poema en prosa, prosa-poética, etc. The closeness between these two genres was hypothetically an important feature of the late years of romanticism (Montesinos 1980, 139–42). Even if this imbrication could a more obvious form in the last decades of the silver age (avant-garde) than in the late 19th century (Ródenas de Moya 2000, 89–90; Mainer 2009; Millares 2013, 235), the tendency is probably more visible after the turn of the century with modernism (Ródenas de Moya 2000, 80–81). For Mainer, the classic realist novel is replaced by a “densely poetic and profoundly introspective story” (Mainer 2009, 235, my translation). When talking about lyrical novels, the author Miró is often mentioned (Landeiro 1985, 117; Jiménez and Rodríguez Cáceres 1991; Lozano Marco 2002), but others such as Valle-Inclán, Azorin, or Pérez de Ayala (Ródenas de Moya 2000, 81; Villanueva Nieto 1983).

A new group of prose writers emerged in the avant-garde were identified as lyrical novels: Jarnés (Mainer 2009, 243), Gómez de la Serena (Pedraza Jiménez and Rodríguez Cáceres 1991, 125), or Antonio España (Militares 2013, 59). The latter states in his novel Pájaro Pinto to “between the novel and the poem there is already an area of interference, truly suggestive?” (Espina 2004, originally published in 1927, my translation). For Ródenas de Moya, the lyrical novel became one of the major subgenres of the novel in the later decades (2000, 72) that even affected prose in general (39).

Urera Terremochcha 1999, 269–71) or Juan de Mairena (1936), respectively. In addition, they also wrote other pieces of works mixing poetry and prose: Diario de un poeta recién nacido (1916) or De un canjero físico (1933).
