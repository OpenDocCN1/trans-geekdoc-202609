# 8.5.1 Evaluation of Centrality

An aspect that is more suitable for a formal evaluation is the classification results. In Section 8.3, I have suggested that the genres that have more distinctive features (specific features not shared with other genres) are being pulled out of the network, as in the case of the dialogue novels, which is a genre that yields high results in the classification. From this, the hypothesis follows that the genres with low results would be expected to be placed in the periphery.⁷ There are several ways in which the centrality of nodes in a graph can be measured. My interest is not in measuring how many edges each node has, but rather in capturing whether the neighbors of the nodes (novels and features) are isolated (the case for the peripheral nodes) or linked to other nodes (which is expected in the central nodes). This is what the eigenvector centrality measures (Bonacich 1987), and is defined as: the centrality of a node should be the centrality of its neighbors, normalized by some factor λ (Zweig 2016, 248). This centrality measure has already been applied to measure centrality in bipartite graphs (Faust 1997), and from this point, when I refer to centrality in the graph, subgenres like *greguerias, episodio nacional*, or erotic novels stand clearly in the periphery of the graph (observed with several layout algorithms), and they also have some of the lowest eigenvector centrality values. In other words, the perceived centrality in the figure correlates strongly with the eigenvector centrality.⁸ The exact values for all subgenres are shown in Figure 109.
