## 8.7 Using the Tripartite Graph Model of Genre for Descriptions

As any model, this aims to contain the most important characteristics of the object represented. A good model for genre should be able to provide the basic information for adequate descriptions. How descriptions of genres can be measured and evaluated is an open question that is not going to find an answer in this work. However, any literary scholar has certain expectations of what should be included in a definition of a genre. In this section, I present a series of real definitions (Pawlowk's terminology, 1980, see Section 2.3.1) of some subgenres based on empirical data. A complete list of the 26 categories that can be accessed in the Appendix of this work.

In general, these descriptions provide a short paragraph summarizing the most important information of each subgenre in terms of their characteristics in the graph, extension, intention, and similarity. First, some characteristics of the categories in the graph are described, such as their centrality, specificity of the features, or canonicity. The intention is expressed through their distinctive semantic, textual, and literary features, which are all highly interpretable. These features are distinctive not only in the larger version of the corpus, CoNSSA, but also in its canonical version, CoNSSA-canon, which represents a controlled statistical population (see Section 3.1.6). This means that these features are distinctive not only for the representations of these

subgenres in this corpus, but in a representative sample of canonical texts. Thereafter, the extension is expressed through the number of texts in the corpus and its best representatives (i.e. the texts to which the largest proportion of sources assigned a label). Finally, I state the similarity to other subgenres in terms of common neighbors (shared features and texts).

All data is derived from the tripartite graph in different ways: by the size of the nodes, the values of the edges, the number of nodes with specific characteristics, by calculating the eigenvector centralities, or the common neighbors. In other words, the following paragraphs are textual phrases of what has already been plotted as the entire network of subgenres, texts, and features. In this section, I describe four subgenres: the realist novel, literary fiction, the historical, and the erotic novel. These are the same descriptions contained in the Appendix, which also offers definitions of all the other categories.
