More specifically, it is expected to deliver improved availability due to no-single point of failure (Kshetri, 2017), data integrity due to consensus validation and data traceability (Kshetri, 2017; Liu et al., 2018), and access control through blockchain-driven key management among other benefits (Lei et al., 2017).

Albeit its recency and its initial objective of supporting cryptocurrency, blockchain got implemented across different sectors and business verticals. Some of those sectors include healthcare Kuo et al. (2017), Shi et al. (2019), energy (Goranović et al., 2017), supply chain (Dujak and Sajter, 2019), finance (Tapscott and Tapscott, 2017) and many others (see Abou Jaoude, 2019; Ji et al., 2020). Blockchain has influenced the financial industries in various ways. According to Chang et al. (2020), blockchain technology has changed the business models of traditional banking by reducing cost and value transfer and controlling financial risks. Due to innovations in data storage and transmission, blockchain can be used to optimize global financial infrastructure hence minimizing costs and value transfer (Chang et al., 2020).

One area where blockchain has been observed as a promising platform is in securing the IoT ecosystems of those sectors. For example, blockchain was used as an architectural framework for IoT ecosystems in healthcare to secure automated remote patient monitoring (Griggs et al., 2018), in supply chain to trace food safety (Tian, 2017), and e-commerce to secure supply chain finance (Lakhani et al., 2020). Recent research proposes various blockchain-based solutions for IoT implementation and in different applications. A good majority of this literature focuses on improved security as the main advantage of blockchain technology. Still, there are a myriad of blockchain-related benefits to IoT that has been described that are not related to security. Non-security related IoT weaknesses that can benefit from blockchain include scalability (Sultan et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2019), interoperability (Ali et al., 2018; Dai et al., 2019; Pohrmnen et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2019), performance (Yang et al., 2019), standardization (Ali et al., 2018) and complexity (Dai et al., 2019). Notwithstanding their importance, those weaknesses fall outside of the scope of our review. From this literature, ten literature reviews pertaining to Blockchain for IoTs (see Table 1) were identified, out of which six focused on the security related aspects. That said, none of these reviews provided a comprehensive framework to organize the pertinent literature. Only three articles organized the extant IoT security threats and, to a lesser extent, countermeasures according to either a 3-layer architecture comprised of perception/device, communication/network, application/software (see Pohrmnen et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2019) or 4-layer architecture (see Sengupta et al., 2020) which adds data as a distinct layer. Moreover, the reviews that focus on security do not consider all goals of security: confidentiality, integrity, availability, authentication and non-repudiation. For example, Sengupta et al. (2020) focused on issues such as the central point of failure, privacy, traceability and accountability. Kamran et al. (2020), Sultan et al. (2018) and Kshetri (2017) focused on how the immutable nature of blockchain addresses the threat lack of data integrity seen in IoT ecosystems. Some reviewed focused on specific areas of IoT applications. For example, Yang et al. (2019) investigated neuro-informatics and purposed to describe how blockchain architectures can be applied to secure the associated IoT ecosystems. Wei et al. (2019) reviewed cryptography algorithms, authentication and consensus protocols that address persistent privacy and identity management vulnerabilities in IoT ecosystems. Similarly, Pohrmnen et al. (2019) reviewed privacy-related issues that were specific to software-defined networking (SDN) based on IoT networks and mapped their resolutions to SDN-based blockchain architecture.

Lastly, some authors acknowledge the existence of technical challenges arise from implementing blockchain as an architecture to improve the security of IoT ecosystem. The computational cost of transaction verification, the pseudo anonymity and the often permissionless traits of blockchain can constrain implementations of blockchain to handle IoT networks (Jesus et al., 2018; Kshetri, 2017).
