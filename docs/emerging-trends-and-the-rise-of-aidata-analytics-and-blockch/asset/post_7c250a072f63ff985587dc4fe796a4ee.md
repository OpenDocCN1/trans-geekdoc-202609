Several fields such as the food business (Hang et al., 2020), finance (White et al., 2020); Tapscott and Tapscott, 2017; Chang et al., 2020a, b), stock market (Ji et al., 2020), e-voting systems (Baudier et al., 2021) and health systems (Mukherjee and Singh, 2020) are showing great interest in blockchain technology. Within supply chain management, blockchains are primarily introduced to manage the flow of a company’s distribution channel (Helo and Hao, 2019; De Giovanni, 2020). While traditional supply chains often seek to establish a multi-stakeholder collaborative environment through mutual trust to remove communication barriers and improve efficiency (Korpela et al., 2017), they face several critical issues due to centralized planning and coordination, which limits control (Korpela et al., 2017), lowers transparency and reduces the trust in existing mechanisms. Operating blockchains efficiently, especially in e-commerce platforms, requires all involved parties to participate in creating a cooperative supply chain network, including the global manufacturers and e-tailers that resell luxury products (Niu et al., 2021). As the rise in counterfeit products and scandals about product quality can negatively impact the entire supply chain (Abeyrathne and Monfared, 2016), introducing blockchains can help re-establish trust and transparency and improve the quality and speed of supply chain management while reducing risk (Kshetri, 2018).

Owing to its decentralized and shareable data system that can be used by multiple suppliers, blockchain technology is especially suitable for luxury companies, as it enables them to trace their products. Therefore, this study aims to answer the following research questions:

- (1) How can improving the traceability of luxury goods help optimize supply chain management?
- (2) Within the luxury business, what conditions and contexts could influence the implementation of blockchain solutions?
- (3) Can blockchains solve the ongoing issues faced by luxury firms, such as counterfeiting and the gray market?

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the theoretical background on blockchain, supply chain management and luxury brands, focusing on the luxury goods ecosystem and need for the traceability of luxury products. Section 3 presents the qualitative methodology, in which 12 stakeholders (managers of international luxury brands and blockchain experts from the luxury sector) were interviewed to gather diverse points of view on supply chains, blockchains and traceability. Sections 4 and 5 present and discuss the results. Finally, Section 6 provides the theoretical and managerial contributions of the study, followed by the limitations and future research directions.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Current challenges: the protection of the luxury brand

Numerous studies have struggled to define luxury items or brands because of their subjective nature (Cristini et al., 2017). However, these brands have some key characteristics (Ko et al., 2019) such as (1) high quality (Potter et al., 2015), (2) authentic value, (3) prestigious image, (4) premium price, (5) deep customer connection and (6) uniqueness (Lamming et al., 2000). Rod et al. (2015) also highlight luxury brands’ ability to signify high-end wealth levels, social status and stylishness. Empirical studies differentiate between “fashion-sensitive” products such as apparel, shoes, bags and accessories and less fashion-sensitive products such as furniture, cars, watches and yachts (Caniato et al., 2009); symbolic luxury products (e.g. jewelry, shoes, bags, accessories); and technical luxury such as sports cars and watches (Brun and Castelli, 2008). However, to analyze this market, understanding the motivations and expectations of consumers is crucial. Many empirical studies of expensive wine (Beverland, 2009), luxury fashion (Hagtvedt et al., 2014) and jewelry have determined the various issues (Ko et al., 2019) influencing consumption, such as status, values (Kapferer and Laurent, 2016), pride (McFerran et al., 2014), uniqueness and self-confidence. Finally, some scholars have also found that sex (Wang and Griskevicius, 2014), country of origin (Godey et al., 2012; Kastanakis and Voyer, 2014) and cultural differences (individualism vs collectivism) could impact consumer behavior and the degree of attraction toward luxury brands (Bian and Forsythe, 2012; Zhan and He, 2012; Kim et al., 2012).

Luxury brands face stiff competition from low-quality fake products, which impacts their reputation and necessitates the use of protective measures against counterfeit products and the gray market. Failing to do so could dissuade customers from making future purchases, harming not only the specific product but also the overall brand (Toledo and Villas-Boas, 2019). According to Castelli and Sianesi (2015), the success of the luxury fashion industry primarily depends on protecting brand value, preserving quality/authenticity and maintaining efficient supply chains. Luxury brand consumers are concerned about their quality, authenticity and origin. For example, a diamond’s origin and specification are certified through a paper certificate (Caniato et al., 2009; Choi, 2019). However, these documents can be falsified; therefore, ensuring the certificate’s quality requires the seller’s expertise.

### 2.2 Blockchain and counterfeiting

Luxury brands use several technologies to identify and prevent counterfeiting (Guercini and Runfola, 2009). Anti-counterfeiting technologies are mainly based on four attributes: difficulty of duplication, easy identification, visibility without special equipment and complexity of reuse (Li, 2013). These technologies can be overt or covert (Bansal et al., 2013). Overt technology such as holograms, color-shifting ink, security threads, watermarks and sequential product numbering are visible, simplifying the authentication of products. These techniques are often used in anti-counterfeiting packaging and can be integrated into the product as well.

By contrast, covert technology such as security packaging papers, security ink or printing, UV ink, thermochromic ink, digital watermarks and biological and chemical taggants are hidden and visible only to authorized administrators. Global trade has further enabled the use of digital technologies for tracing and tracking products, such as radio frequency identification (RFID), barcodes and Web portal tools (Ting and Ip, 2015). An RFID tag attached to a product’s packaging transmits a signal to the company’s information system center, which helps locate the product.

However, once copied or damaged, these widely used product security codes cannot prevent counterfeiting (Wilson and Grammich, 2020), limiting their application (Lancaster, 2017). To fight counterfeiting as well as gray and copycat markets, the International Chamber of Commerce has created an institute called the Business Action to Stop Counterfeiting and Piracy, which represents more than 45 million companies in over 100 countries.

According to Ringsberg (2014), the traceability of a product comprises logistics traceability and qualitative traceability. The former refers to the tracking from raw materials to finished products and vice versa, whereas the latter focusses on storage and distribution conditions. Additionally, blockchain technology can be applied to commodity anti-counterfeiting because it prevents data tampering and enables transaction traceability (Toyoda et al., 2017). It particularly helps address the trust deficit in traditional products due to the circulation of fake products as well as protect consumer rights (Aitzhan and Svetinovic, 2016).
