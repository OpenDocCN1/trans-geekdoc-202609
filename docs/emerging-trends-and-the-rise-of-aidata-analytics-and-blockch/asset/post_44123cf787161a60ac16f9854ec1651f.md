
The blockchain traceability anti-counterfeiting platform project is a prominent example of cooperation between the manufacturing and transportation enterprises to implement anti-counterfeiting blockchain solutions (Meraviglia, 2018; Liu et al., 2020; Lee and Pilkington, 2017). This platform is considered to be safe, reliable, tamper-proof and traceable because it uses state sensors and RFID devices to collect product information, which is stored in a blockchain system. Additionally, it allows consumers to verify the authenticity of purchased products after logging into their accounts on the platform (Yazdani et al., 2019). However, the luxury goods market has recently seen the emergence of several anti-counterfeiting blockchain platforms and solutions. First, Everledger, established in April 2015, prevents the counterfeiting of diamonds by recording their source and transfer in a blockchain database (Underwood, 2016), including such details as origin, certification, weight and color (Montecchi et al., 2019). Second, the BlockVerify program tracks the origins of luxury goods (Lee and Pilkington, 2017). Third, VeChain, developed in 2018 in China, provides luxury brands with anti-counterfeiting chips within intelligent design contracts, protecting their commodity rights (Meraviglia, 2018). Fourth, Luxochain [3] combines several technologies besides blockchain to control the authenticity of luxury products before their purchase. Fifth, LVMH developed a blockchain-based platform called AURA in 2019 in partnership with Microsoft and ConsenSys. Finally, other well-known platforms such as eBay, Lazada, and Tmall Global have mobilized blockchains to verify quality with positive results on increasing customer trust (Niu et al., 2021). Anti-counterfeiting platforms are divided into three categories: enterprises’ self-built platforms (with difficult-to-maintain systems), third-party platforms (which struggle to gain the trust of enterprises and consumers) and government-built platforms (mainly used for commodity traceability) (Yang et al., 2017).

### 2.3 Blockchain and traceability

Product traceability can track all stages of production and commercialization, from raw material extraction to the delivery of finished goods. To increase the brand’s traceability and accountability, several researchers have highlighted the importance of integrating blockchain into supply chain management (Cole et al., 2019; Tönnissen and Teuteberg, 2020). In fact, blockchain’s algorithms ensure the integrity of supply chain management (Duan et al., 2020; Hughes et al., 2019) by focusing on product traceability, disruptively transforming organizational and user behavior (Bumblauskas et al., 2020). Blockchain-based traceability is safer, more transparent and efficient (Banerjee et al., 2018; Kshetri, 2018). Additionally, all transactions are recorded in separate blocks and stored within the blockchain, reducing the chances of data tampering or deletion (Xu et al., 2019) and improving the verification of information exchange in permanent and unalterable ways (Feng et al., 2020). The key differences between a standard database and a blockchain ledger are that a standard database is stored in one location and can be read/written, controlled and modified by an administrator, while a blockchain ledger has multiple identical copies stored across a network of computers, which reduces the chances of the modification or deletion of data from its ledgers by distributing administrative powers across the network’s membership (Garrand and Fielke, 2020). Blockchains are especially useful to supply chains (Liu et al., 2020), as they improve the accessibility of data for all stakeholders (retailers, suppliers, manufacturers and customers) while ensuring data privacy (Maity et al., 2021). Therefore, several sectors such as food and wine (Maity et al., 2021; Ranaweera et al., 2021) and fashion (Agrawal et al., 2021) have started implementing blockchain solutions. The World Health Organization claims that 10% of pharmaceutical products are counterfeit, which directly impacts human lives. Therefore, the Medledger system has been developed to increase the efficiency of drug companies’ supply chain transactions by providing transparency and traceability (Uddin, 2021).

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Grounded theory (GT)

This study uses an exploratory qualitative approach to investigate the interactions between luxury brands and the providers of blockchain solutions in supply chains or traceability projects. Additionally, unlike previous studies, it adopts the grounded theory (GT), which is often used to analyze complex (Flint et al., 2012), new issues (Strauss and Corbin, 1998). Moreover, this method is useful for developing a global explanation, model or theory shaped by participants’ viewpoints who are familiar with the particular issue (Corbin and Strauss, 2008; Strauss and Corbin, 1998), especially for understanding how people conceptualize and experience a phenomenon. This study considers the respondents’ conception of the traceability of luxury goods, which involves an understanding of the content, determining factors and outcomes of such a phenomenon. After collecting the respondents’ perspectives, it then formulates a theoretical proposition on the traceability of luxury goods. Therefore, using this method, we propose new insights into the implementation of a new technological solution (blockchain) in the luxury goods domain.

Furthermore, to analyze all the transcribed interviews, this study uses the manual coding method based on the axial coding process (Strauss and Corbin, 1990, 1998). Data are reassembled in new ways following an open coding stage, allowing associations between a category (“second-order category”) and its sub-categories (“first-order category”) and reaching toward “aggregate dimensions,” as presented in Table 1.
