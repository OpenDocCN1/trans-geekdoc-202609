# 7.1 Time consumption

A blockchain network transaction could be cumbersome, often taking hours to process logging transaction due to the decentralized complexities (Moin et al., 2019). It consumes a lot of time to identify and compute header to header information of large datasets of blockchain networks (Moin et al., 2019). For some blockchain technologies, the validation of transaction can take up to 10 minutes thus creating a problem for real time applications (Kouicem et al., 2018) such as patient health tracking or hazard detection and communication. Moreover, there is variance in processing time. This variation is brought about by the computing capabilities of the devices in the network (Kumar and Mallick, 2018). Devices with different computing capabilities take varying time to encrypt data hence the variance in processing time (Kumar and Mallick, 2018). It is unrealistic to assume that IoT devices running on a shared blockchain network would have analogous processing powers.

# 7.2 Bandwidth consumption

Blockchain consumes a lot of bandwidth to validate many transactions generated by the IoT devices (Kouicem et al., 2018). New blockchain transactions requires broadcasting to all peers which results in large communication overhead. Additionally, all the peers need to synchronize and retain the copy to blockchain further adding to the overhead (Sengupta et al., 2020). Indeed, the poor throughput of blockchain impacts its ability to scale up to wide usage in large IoT ecosystems (Dai et al., 2019; Kumar and Mallick, 2018).

# 7.3 Energy consumption

The majority of popular blockchain technologies need to perform multiple computations to achieve consensus hence leading to energy wastage (Sengupta et al., 2020). While providing greater security, the verification algorithm run on multiple times to the peers drastically increasing energy consumption and reducing operational efficiency (Sengupta et al., 2020).

# 7.4 Storage consumption

The requirement of storing all transactions in one node introduces a problem of excessive system storage due to properties of distributed storage (Yang et al., 2019; Kumar and Mallick, 2018). Size could be manageable at first but over the years, the blockchain might grow to undesirable magnitudes for small low-cost IoT devices to handle. As an example, the bitcoin blockchain grew from 6 gigabytes in 2013 to a colossal 270 gigabytes in 2020 [1]. That said, enhanced security is inscribed in those blockchain features such is in the case of immutability.

# 8. Future research

Notwithstanding the aforementioned capabilities of blockchain in solving IoT security issues, it remains inadequate in physically securing the IoT devices from destruction or tampering. Devoid from a healthy physical security strategy, attackers can physically access IoT networks via any IoT device (Yang et al., 2017). According to Asif et al. (2021), there are two main categories of physical security attacks: invasive and non-invasive attacks. The former attack involves direct manipulation of the processor whereas the latter requires attackers to be in proximity in order to learn the device’s specifications and modify its code to hack essential information.

Our review of the literature hints to the shortfall of blockchain technology as a conduit for the physical security of IoT devices given the lack of focus in the relevant research on this aspect. Future research on blockchain for IoT should also focus on securing the physical environment for the connected devices. It is crucial to continue securing the environment as the increase of connected infrastructure raises the vulnerability for physical cybersecurity
