Rehman and Apergis (2019) investigated the relationship between Bitcoin and Ethereum and various commodity futures, like gold, silver, copper and oil, by using the casualty on quantile method proposed by Balcilar et al. (2016). The results indicate that in most quantiles, there is a causality from cryptocurrencies to commodities futures regarding mean and volatility. Urquhart and Zhang (2019) studied the relationship between Bitcoin and several currencies by employing an asymmetric dynamic conditional correlation model. Their results indicate that Bitcoin is correlated positively and negatively with currencies implying that Bitcoin can be used as a hedger and diversifier for these currencies depending on the time and the currency in question. Bouri et al. (2018a) applied the vector auto-regression (VAR) GARCH model to daily data spanning from July 19, 2010, to October 31, 2017, to check the interconnection among Bitcoin and other conventional assets. The study's finding suggests that Bitcoin returns are related quite closely to other conventional assets, and the Bitcoin market is not isolated completely. Ciaian et al. (2018) analyzed the relationship between Bitcoin and gold prices, several exchange rates and the Treasury maturity rate and showed that Bitcoin prices are affected in both the short and long runs by the prices of those assets. Bouri et al. (2017) investigated the connection between Bitcoin and commodity indices by focusing on energy commodities by employing an asymmetric dynamic conditional correlation model. It was found that there is a connection between Bitcoin and commodity, and Bitcoin can play as a hedge and safe haven for those indices. Ji et al. (2019b) investigated the information interdependence between leading cryptocurrencies and commodities via a time-varying entropy approach. It was revealed that cryptocurrencies are increasingly integrated within the commodity market. Kurka (2019) studied the interaction between some traditional assets, like currencies, stocks and commodities, and Bitcoin using the volatility spillover method proposed by Diebold and Yilmaz (2009, 2012). They concluded that there was no considerable connection between cryptocurrencies and traditional assets, but there was considerable shock transmission between those assets and Bitcoin in some periods. Also, Baur et al. (2018) investigated the relationship between Bitcoin and traditional assets, including stocks and bonds, by analyzing the statistical properties of Bitcoin. It was shown that Bitcoin is not correlated with traditional asset classes. In addition, Corbet et al. (2018b) studied the linkage among several cryptocurrencies and a group of conventional financial assets. The study's findings reveal that cryptocurrencies are highly interconnected; however, they are not related to conventional assets. Charfeddine et al. (2020) investigated the relationship between several cryptocurrencies and commodities by using the DCC GARCH model and copula approach, and the results show that the cross-correlations between cryptocurrencies and those assets are weak. So cryptocurrencies are poor hedgers for those assets. In addition, the relationship between cryptocurrencies and those assets can be affected by economic and financial shocks. Also, Zeng et al. (2020) analyzed the interconnection between Bitcoin, stock, oil and gold by employing a method developed by Diebold and Yilmaz (2009, 2012, 2014). The results indicated a weak connection between Bitcoin and conventional assets. Tiwari et al. (2019) examined the correlation between several cryptocurrencies and S&P 500 by applying the copula–ADCC–EGARCH method. The result indicates that the correlation between the stock index and cryptocurrencies is weak so that cryptocurrencies can be used as hedgers for portfolios.

On the other hand, the literature on the stock of technology companies is mainly focused on the correlation and spillover effect between the stock prices of these companies and oil prices (Bondia et al., 2016; Kocaarslan and Soytas, 2019; Nasreen et al., 2020; Sadorsky, 2012). In addition, Sadorsky (2003) investigated the macroeconomic determinants of conditional volatility of US technology stock price. The study's findings reveal that the stock prices of technology companies are significantly affected by the conditional volatilities of oil prices, the term premium and consumer price index (CPI).
