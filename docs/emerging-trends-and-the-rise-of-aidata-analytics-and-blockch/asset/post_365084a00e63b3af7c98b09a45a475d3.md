Albeit its recency and its initial objective of supporting cryptocurrency, blockchain got implemented across different sectors and business verticals. Some of those sectors include healthcare Kuo et al. (2017), Shi et al. (2019), energy (Goranović et al., 2017), supply chain (Dujak and Sajter, 2019), finance (Tapscott and Tapscott, 2017) and many others (see Abou Jaoude, 2019; Ji et al., 2020). Blockchain has influenced the financial industries in various ways. According to Chang et al. (2020), blockchain technology has changed the business models of traditional banking by reducing cost and value transfer and controlling financial risks. Due to innovations in data storage and transmission, blockchain can be used to optimize global financial infrastructure hence minimizing costs and value transfer (Chang et al., 2020).

One area where blockchain has been observed as a promising platform is in securing the IoT ecosystems of those sectors. For example, blockchain was used as an architectural framework for IoT ecosystems in healthcare to secure automated remote patient monitoring (Griggs et al., 2018), in supply chain to trace food safety (Tian, 2017), and e-commerce to secure supply chain finance (Lakhani et al., 2020). Recent research proposes various blockchain-based solutions for IoT implementation and in different applications. A good majority of this literature focuses on improved security as the main advantage of blockchain technology. Still, there are a myriad of blockchain-related benefits to IoT that has been described that are not related to security. Non-security related IoT weaknesses that can benefit from blockchain include scalability (Sultan et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2019), interoperability (Ali et al., 2018; Dai et al., 2019; Pohmen et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2019), performance (Yang et al., 2019), standardization (Ali et al., 2018) and complexity (Dai et al., 2019). Notwithstanding their importance, those weaknesses fall outside of the scope of our review. From this literature, ten literature reviews pertaining to Blockchain for IoT (see Table 1) were identified, out of which six focused on the security related aspects. That said, none of these reviews provided a comprehensive framework to organize the pertinent literature. Only three articles organized the extant IoT security threats and, to a lesser extent, countermeasures according to either a 3-layer architecture comprised of perception/device, communication/network, application/software (see Pohmen et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2019) or 4-layer architecture (see Sengupta et al., 2020) which adds data as a distinct layer. Moreover, the reviews that focus on security do not consider all goals of security: confidentiality, integrity, availability, authentication and non-repudiation. For example, Sengupta et al. (2020) focused on issues such as the central point of failure, privacy, traceability and accountability. Kamran et al. (2020), Sultan et al. (2018) and Kshetri (2017) focused on how the immutable nature of blockchain addresses the threat lack of data integrity seen in IoT ecosystems. Some reviews focused on specific areas of IoT applications. For example, Yang et al. (2019) investigated neuro-informatics and purposed to describe how blockchain architectures can be applied to secure the associated IoT ecosystems. Wei et al. (2019) reviewed cryptography algorithms, authentication and consensus protocols that address persistent privacy and identity management vulnerabilities in IoT ecosystems. Similarly, Pohmen et al. (2019) reviewed privacy-related issues that were specific to software-defined networking (SDN) based on IoT networks and mapped their resolutions to SDN-based blockchain architecture.

Lastly, some authors acknowledge the existence of technical challenges arise from implementing blockchain as an architecture to improve the security of IoT ecosystem. The computational cost of transaction verification, the pseudo anonymity and the often permissionless traits of blockchain can constrain implementations of blockchain to handle IoT networks (Jesus et al., 2018; Kshetri, 2017).

Notwithstanding the value of the extant reviews on blockchain-based IoT security, we contend that no single survey covers the full IoT security spectrum and addressed the five goals of security in a comprehensive manner (see Table 1). Moreover, we observed that little attention was paid to the architectural layer in which the threat can persist. Lastly, it is not clear from those reviews whether blockchain can replace conventional security mechanisms or complement them. A thorough review of all IoT security threats affecting any of the five security goals on all IoT architectural layers is necessary. It is also necessary to identify the current mechanisms that are put in place to counter those threats. Once we arrive at such a comprehensive view of the current state of IoT security, an adequate evaluation of blockchain applicability can be assessed.

## 3. Research goals

The overarching goal of our systematic literature review is to elucidate the role of blockchain in securing IoT ecosystems. IoT security research is extensive investigating abundant security threats and mechanisms to address them. To this end, our first goal is to organize the relevant literature on IoT security threats and how these threats are addressed using conventional mechanisms. More importantly, we investigate whether blockchain helps improve the current state of IoT security or adds more vulnerability to an already fragile ecosystem. In order to achieve those objectives, we organized the existing literature according to (1) the five security goals put forward by Alaba et al. (2017) and Jesus et al. (2018) and (2) the four-layer IoT architecture specified by Sengupta et al. (2020). The conclusion of this work aims to answer one overarching question pertaining to the suitability of blockchain as a security solution for IoT ecosystems.
