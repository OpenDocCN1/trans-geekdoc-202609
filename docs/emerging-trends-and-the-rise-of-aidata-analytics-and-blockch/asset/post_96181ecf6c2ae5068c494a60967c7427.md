## 2. Related work

The study derives its applications from the affect theory, which organizes human emotions into nine categories, namely, joy and excitement for positive emotions; a surprise for neutral emotions; and anger, disgust, dissmell, distress, fear and shame for negative emotions (Tomkins, 1962). Affect theory is adapted from the psychology domain and focuses on organizing affects, sometimes called emotions. Affect theory is further explored in philosophy, gender studies and art theory (Catherall, 2006). Lately, affect theory has been gaining attraction in the field of Information Sciences (Kratzwald et al, 2018). We have used affect theory for analysis, where we have taken both text and images data.

The study is further based on the behavioral decision theory (Simon, 1959), which states that humans are prone to have cognitive biases while making decisions, and the decisions are entirely dependent on the situation. Thus, an individual will decide based on his/her state of mind and the environmental conditions. This presents a unique opportunity to marketers where they can analyze the customers' state of mind and present the alternatives to them. For instance, a customer who is identified in a state of sadness can be offered a discount on a particular product. Research also suggests that behavioral strategy redefines strategic directions in an organization (Lovallo and Sibony, 2018).

### 2.1 Text-based affective computing

Researchers have focused on various approaches such as lexicon-based approach, machine learning models and deep learning models for understanding affective computing and sentiment analysis. The difference between affective computing and sentiment analysis is that sentiment analysis detects only the polarity of the text, and affective computing detects the different emotions in the text. We have described the literature for both affective computing and sentiment analysis.

### 2.1.1 Lexicon based approaches

Various researchers studied lexicon-based methods for sentiment analysis (Ding et al, 2008; Melville et al, 2009; Taboada et al, 2011). Lexicon-based approaches assume that the nature of contextual sentiment is an aggregate sum of the sentiment orientation of each word or phrase. The lexicons were compiled manually in the beginning, but later, scholars started using machine learning for lexicon-based approaches. The shift from manual to machine learning was primarily because manual labelling was prone to errors, inflexible and expensive. One of the significant difficulties for both manual and machine learning approaches in lexicon-based is understanding the linguistic structures among social media users, including the usage of slang, acronyms, emoticons and sarcasm.

Turney (2002) analyzed sentiments of reviews based on their semantic orientation. The lexicon-based approaches were used in the financial domain by Tetlock et al. (2008), who used the daily content of the very popular Wall Street Journal column to develop the measure of sentiment daily. Researchers like Wang and Xu (2018) have leveraged deep learning for doing text analytics to detect fraud in automobile insurance. They have used Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)-based text analytics for fraud detection.

### 2.1.2 Machine learning-based approaches

Machine learning-based approaches help in understanding the sentiments by training the samples (Chaffar and Inkpen, 2011). Earlier research has used different machine learning models for inferring sentiments from various sources. For instance, researchers have used the random forest technique (Gordeev, 2016) and the support vector machine (Chatzakou et al., 2017) for sentiment analysis. Another common approach in machine learning is based on linguistic features such as bag-of-words, with subsequent term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) weighting (Strapparava and Mihalcea, 2007).

### 2.1.3 Deep learning

Deep learning is an advanced form of machine learning which uses a neural network to predict the next value based on the previous values. Various deep learning models have been used in affective computing. For instance, researchers have used a CNN to classify Twitter tweets (Kalchbrenner et al., 2014). Further, different models of recurrent neural networks (RNNs) have also been used in sentiment analysis. RNN models which are used in affective computing are models such as LSTM (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) and GRU (Cho et al., 2014). One of the latest studies by Felbo et al. (2017) has used the LSTM model to predict the sentiment of tweets. Some of the researchers have demonstrated an automatic audio-visual recognition using the LSTM model (Wöllmer et al., 2013). Deep learning was also used for sales forecasting in the fashion retail industry (Loureiro et al., 2018). Another study by Mahmoudi et al. (2018) has used models like LSTM, GRU and CNN to understand the investor sentiments in the stock market. Researchers have used the combination of CNN and LSTM models for vision-based modal frequency detection (Yang et al., 2020). The combination of CNN and LSTM models were also used for emotion charting using physiological signals such as those of skin response or electrocardiogram (Dar et al., 2020). One of the latest studies has also used deep reinforcement learning (DRL) approaches to predict the stock prices and determine effective trading strategies (Li et al., 2019). The authors recommended that the hybrid approach should be adopted in trading, where traders can use Adaboost for the stocks they are familiar with. In contrast, DRL approaches can be adopted for the new stocks. Researchers also used DRL for resource allocation in mobile edge computing (Huang et al., 2020). Another latest study proposed the automatic detection of cyberbullying using deep learning based decision tree classifier (YuvaraJ et al., 2021). Researchers have also proposed DNA deep learning cryptography, which aims at hiding data in DNA sequence using deep learning techniques (Kalsi et al., 2018). Researchers have also used deep learning to predict corporate distress from text segments of annual reports (Matin et al., 2019). Recently, researchers have used deep learning to signal the closure of restaurants by investigating online consumer reviews (Tao and Zhou, 2020). Deep learning finds its applications in predicting the rear-end collision of vehicles (Chen et al., 2018) and determining the human activity recognition while using some of the IoT devices (Abdel-Basset et al., 2020). Researchers have further proposed a deep learning based framework for writing research papers (Mahalaksmi et al., 2018). Lately, it has been found that deep learning algorithms are used to detect novel coronavirus disease (Dhiman et al., 2021) and minimize the requirement of a large number of COVID-19 CT scans (Abdel-Basset et al., 2021). The authors proposed a novel FSS-2019-nCov that uses a semi-supervised learning-based few-shot segmentation to effectively target the COVID-19 virus.
