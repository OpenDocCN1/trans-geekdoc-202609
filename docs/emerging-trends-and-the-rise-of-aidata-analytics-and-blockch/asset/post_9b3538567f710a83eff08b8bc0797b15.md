## 2.2 Image-based affective computing

Image-based affective computing can be categorized into studies based on the feature extraction method and studies based on the image background. Over the past years, deep learning has increasingly become a hot topic of research and has obtained cutting-edge performance in several domains, including facial expression recognition. Deep learning demonstrates high-level abstractions of various nonlinear transformations and representations through hierarchical architectures.

### 2.2.1 Feature extraction method

Some of the researchers, like Ghani and Krema (2014), have analyzed the sentiment of the images by the feature extraction method. Researchers have proposed several approaches for extracting emotions from sequences and frames, including hand-crafted features and learned feature-based models. Hand-crafted features make use of a predesigned appearance or any geometrical features (Pantic and Patras, 2006) on the face to infer the facial information. Other hand-crafted features include Local Binary Pattern (LBP) (Valstar et al., 2012), Scale Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) (Hu et al., 2008) and Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) (Dahmane and Meunier, 2011). Researchers like Renda et al. (2019) have also used ensemble strategies for deep learning to identify facial expression recognition in literature. Deep learning techniques were also used for biological-image classification using CNN in combination with texture-based feature extraction methods (Affonso et al., 2017).

### 2.2.2 Background of the image method

Some researchers like Machajdik and Hanbury (2010) have used the background and color to classify the emotions depicted in the image. Recognition of facial expressions and emotions is a very challenging problem in the real world because there is a significant difference in intra-class distance measure and great similarity in inter-classes distance measure. CNNs can produce comparable representations for pictures containing distinct features for a single individual. On the other hand, the learned features of any images with the same or similar expression may differ in the different results because of the above challenges. Scholars like Meng et al. (2017) have proposed an Identity-Aware CNN called IACNN, which can reduce the differences by including personal features. The IACNN was focused on personal features, using both an identity-sensitive and an expression-sensitive contrastive loss. Still, they have not considered other variables such as illumination and head-pose.

Another study by Wen et al. (2016) focused on intra-class compactness for facial recognition. Deep learning finds its application in computer vision, including image and video data (Malik and Singh, 2019). One of the latest studies by Guan et al. (2019) has proposed a deep learning-based recommendation tool named Deep-MINE, which integrates multiple sources of content such as product images, product descriptions and review comments for recommendation purposes. Another study by Park et al. (2017) has proposed a face alignment method that uses a deep learning technique and employs both local feature learning and recurrent regression. The present study used facial emotion recognition to classify the sentiment depicted by the image.

## 3. Data collection and framework

This section describes the data collection method and the framework used in the study.

### 3.1 Data

We have conducted affective computing by taking both text and images data for analysis. For text data, we have used four standard datasets, i.e. PsychExp dataset (Wallbott and Scherer, 1986), ISEAR dataset (Wallbott and Scherer, 1986), Literary Tales dataset (Alm, 2008) and Headlines dataset (Strapparava and Mihalcea, 2007). PsychExp dataset consists of 7,840 records belonging to six classes, i.e. joy, fear, anger, sadness, disgust, shame and guilt. The ISEAR dataset is a similar dataset and consists of records of self-report of experiences. Headlines dataset is also known as SemEval-2007 dataset and consists of annotated headlines. Literary Tales is a larger dataset consisting of affect labelled literary tales.

The affective computing for images was carried out on images extracted from Instagram. Images from Instagram were extracted using the Application Program Interface (API) of Instagram. The images obtained from Instagram are then classified into two classes of sentiments, namely, happy class and sad class, based on their facial expressions. We have used hashtags like #laughingfaces, #happy for happy class, and #crying, #depressed, #sad for sad classes to get data from Instagram API. The details of the hashtags used on Instagram for getting images are shown in Table 1. A total of 28,148 images were collected using these tags. After that, we filtered images containing human face through OpenCV in Python. After filtering and manually removing all outliers from the dataset, a corpus of 7,638 images was obtained, which was our final dataset used in the analysis. Then we cropped all the human faces present in the images in Python. Along with the image data from Instagram, we have also collected user's text posts.
