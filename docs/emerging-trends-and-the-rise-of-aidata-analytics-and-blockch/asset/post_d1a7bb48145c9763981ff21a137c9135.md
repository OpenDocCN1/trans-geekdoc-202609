## 4. Findings

### 4.1 Digitalization

Luxury firms are considerably challenged by the organizational complexity of their supply chains. For instance, they must deal with numerous third parties with different profiles, locations (often overseas) and processing methods, making the digitalization of supply chains complicated. Additionally, they struggle to trace the components used in their production processes, as expressed by Interviewee 2: “I use so many types of software and processes, (. . .) so many suppliers, how can I suddenly digitalize all of my supply chain?” (I2). According to Interviewee 8, the COVID-19 pandemic saw the supply chain and logistics practices of luxury firms transform from direct deliveries to customers to sales from online-only stores and wholesalers. This shift complicated luxury firms’ supply chain management by forcing them to deal with worldwide online orders for a wider range of goods. “E-commerce and data management are essential for an efficient supply chain and to transform the luxury sectors’ supply chain ecosystem” (I8). Moreover, online sales have drastically changed the seasonality of sales, making sales planning more complex. “Before we had a clear seasonality of sales linked to each season, which was similar each year; e-commerce has destabilized the sales seasonality, now it’s tough to anticipate sales levels!” (I8).

### 4.2 Supply chain and traceability dimensions in luxury

The increase in the range of luxury products and their worldwide distribution amidst growing demand has directly impacted firms’ value chain, making it opaque. While luxury brands could previously control the manufacturing process from raw materials to finished products, nowadays, these brands must outsource their non-iconic products to meet growing demand for luxury goods. They also lack long-term relationships with their raw materials suppliers, reinforcing the opacity of their overall ecosystem. In the luxury field, control of brand image is critical. Therefore, all the respondents raised the importance of the brand’s reputation, embodied through high product quality. Interviewee 5 expressed their fear of overlooking a fault in the supply chain due to a lack of control over all the third parties involved in the production process. “Usually, luxury brands know their suppliers; however, without being fully in charge of their production processes, they are unaware of all the transactions and processes. For example, who supplies the leather? Who is the tanner? The constant changes in suppliers prevent knowledge about the supplier of the supplier of the supplier” (I5).

However, blockchain can certify the traceability of luxury goods by all the parties in the blockchain and guarantee the greater authenticity of products via a digital certificate: “All transactions are certified, which helps optimize our supply chains” (I6); “The stakeholders must agree to get their data registered and certified within the blockchain” (I11).

### 4.3 Blockchain in luxury goods

The implementation of blockchains relies on the relationship between luxury brands’ expectations and their external stakeholders such as consumers. This is especially true in the case of scandals linked to supply chain issues, which could be solved using blockchain solutions. “Each time, this sector of activity suffers from controversies or scandals” (I10). In fact, owing to the scandals reported by the media in recent years, customers, especially millennials, demand total transparency from luxury brands about environmental (pollution, organic) and ethical issues (working conditions of suppliers, animal testing). A luxury brand’s reputation is no longer sufficient to elicit brand loyalty, which could influence customer preferences. “There is distrust among customers (...). When journalists investigate, for example, a rabbit breed used by brand X, the other brands start to worry about the potential ‘bad buzz’ they could receive the next time. If you do not trace the raw materials, you cannot justify the [brand’s] origin” (I5).

While some luxury players may be able to control the downstream side of their supply chains, others may choose to distribute their products outside their usual network (e.g. perfume, wine). Furthermore, companies that outsource manufacturing cannot control the exact quantities of goods produced by their suppliers; therefore, some of their excess products could be sold to the market by other actors. This is the concept of “true/false products.” “For the companies selling champagne, perfume, etc., you run the risk of losing control of the supply chain. Therein lies the blockchain’s value” (I1).

Blockchain technology could strengthen firms’ corporate values by confirming the origin of green products and reinforcing their brand image, contributing toward brand rejuvenation. Their use of disruptive technology may confirm their position as a cutting-edge company as expected of luxury brands. “With blockchains, we enter a new world (...). We always need to differentiate ourselves from our competitors, using technology is a solution. We must deal with a paradox of balancing manually produced products using existing or traditional processes and those produced using recent technology such as blockchain, 3D printing, and virtual and augmented reality” (I7).

Therefore, blockchains can offer firms product relationship management for a better customer care policy for all customers, known and unknown. They enable luxury brands to enrich products’ value, including that of second-hand products, by tracking their lifecycles. “Brands do not know the real owners of their products (...); with blockchain, luxury brands can follow the product’s cycle through product relationship management, in which the product itself is a means of warrantying customer anonymity. Instead, communication can be targeted at the product owned, improving the customer experience” (I11).

Finally, implementing the blockchain solution directly influences the luxury ecosystem and customer expectations, as expressed by Interviewee 10. “Look at Walmart, for example, obligating its suppliers to implement product traceability in its food ecosystem. Nestlé and other key players have also done the same.” (I10).

### 4.4 Blockchain and decision-making processes within luxury brands

Luxury firms’ size and structure directly determine the implementation of blockchain solutions. The luxury sector is mainly composed of family firms. Therefore, depending on their size, the decision-making process could be fast or slow. Moreover, some companies may be reluctant to share information about a public blockchain solution, whereas those with an open-minded innovation culture tend to readily adopt this technology: “Well-known brands do not share their information and make it 100 percent visible and accessible; their participation depends on the type of blockchain solutions implemented” (I10); “XX is not famous for the acceptance of innovative technologies such as digital and e-commerce; it is considered as a laggard” (I3).

However, COVID-19 has demonstrated the importance of controlling supply chains and accelerated the digitalization of luxury brands’ processes, as expressed by interviewee 3: “Who has contributed to the digitalization of the luxury business in the recent months? a) the CEO, b) the CDO, or c) coronavirus? The correct answer is ‘c’, COVID-19! Implementing an e-commerce solution was the only way to sell during the lockdown; (. . .) even if the CEO does not understand [the technology behind] blockchain, they can give the green light if they consider the technology as a solution to their issues” (I3). Consequently COVID-19 appeared to accelerate luxury stakeholders’ need to implement blockchain solutions in their organization. “Since the COVID-19 crisis, I noticed a boom in demand from key luxury and fashion players for optimizing their supply chain management” (I2).

Moreover, owing to its organizational impact, a blockchain solution must be internally approved (e.g. by the CEO, CIO, chief financial officer, chief operating officer (COO), chief marketing officer (CMO)) and then approved by the company’s external suppliers. The organization’s approval process is often more time-consuming than the implementation itself: “We met the CEO tasked with sustainability; then dealt with the CMO regarding the history; and the financial officer regarding the budget; and finally, the CIO and COO who leverage the blockchain” (I2); “The main complexity lies in motivating suppliers’ participation, understanding the flows of processes and information storage, implementing data collection, and registering them on a blockchain. A blockchain is just a tool that stamps and dates a document” (I5).

A CDO interviewee highlighted the importance of return on investment (ROI) for the implementation of blockchains, which most decision-makers perceive as complicated. “Currently, you must take actions that have an immediate impact on business; however, personally, this is an old-school attitude. We must all work together on long-term projects” (I9). Understanding short-term ROIs can influence decision-makers in luxury companies to implement blockchain solutions more positively compared with long-term ROIs. One reason for their reluctance toward blockchains is that the savings linked to the destruction of a counterfeit market do not seem immediate to them. “Decision-makers believe that the benefits from implementing a blockchain to prevent counterfeiting will be visible only within the next ten to fifteen years” (I11).

However, owing to their unfamiliarity with blockchains, luxury brands’ decision-makers tend to rely on third parties to implement this technology. At times, third parties are recommended by decision-makers’ family members and selected based on their relationship rather than expertise or congruence with the blockchains. “When I decided to try blockchains, my personal network suggested some contacts; that is how I met X. I selected X because they seemed smart and agile and suggested a customized idea that immediately rang a bell” (I7).

According to Interviewee 2, demonstrating the “plug and play” capability of blockchain applications could help fast-track the implementation of blockchain solutions and address stakeholders’ concerns about the resources required to make this possible. “The other problem is the resources required to train people to implement the technology. If we can demonstrate that it is ‘plug and play’ and can be controlled by their phones, it reduces their worries about embracing blockchains” (I2).
