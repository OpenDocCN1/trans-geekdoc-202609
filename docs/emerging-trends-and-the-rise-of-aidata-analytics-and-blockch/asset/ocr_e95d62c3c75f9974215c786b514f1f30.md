However, to analyze this market, understanding the motivations and expectations of consumers is crucial. Many empirical studies of expensive wine (Beverland, 2009), luxury fashion (Hagtvedt _et al._, 2014) and jewelry have determined the various issues (Ko _et al._, 2019) influencing consumption, such as status, values (Kapferer and Laurent, 2016), pride (McFerran _et al._, 2014), uniqueness and self-confidence. Finally, some scholars have also found that sex (Wang and Griskevicius, 2014), country of origin (Godey _et al._, 2012; Kastanakis and Voyer, 2014) and cultural differences (individualism vs collectivism) could impact consumer behavior and the degree of attraction toward luxury brands (Bian and Forsythe, 2012; Zhan and He, 2012; Kim _et al._, 2012).

Luxury brands face stiff competition from low-quality fake products, which impacts their reputation and necessitates the use of protective measures against counterfeit products and the gray market. Failing to do so could dissuade customers from making future purchases, harming not only the specific product but also the overall brand (Toledo and Villas-Boas, 2019). According to Castelli and Sianesi (2015), the success of the luxury fashion industry primarily depends on protecting brand value, preserving quality/authenticity and maintaining efficient supply chains. Luxury brand consumers are concerned about their quality, authenticity and origin. For example, a diamond’s origin and specification are certified through a paper certificate (Caniato _et al._, 2009; Choi, 2019). However, these documents can be falsified; therefore, ensuring the certificate’s quality requires the seller’s expertise.

# 2.2 Blockchain and counterfeiting

Luxury brands use several technologies to identify and prevent counterfeiting (Guercini and Runfola, 2009). Anti-counterfeiting technologies are mainly based on four attributes: difficulty of duplication, easy identification, visibility without special equipment and complexity of reuse (Li, 2013). These technologies can be overt or covert (Bansal _et al._, 2013). Overt technology such as holograms, color-shifting ink, security threads, watermarks and sequential product numbering are visible, simplifying the authentication of products. These techniques are often used in anti-counterfeiting packaging and can be integrated into the product as well.

By contrast, covert technology such as security packaging papers, security ink or printing, UV ink, thermos chromic ink, digital watermarks and biological and chemical taggants are hidden and visible only to authorized administrators. Global trade has further enabled the use of digital technologies for tracing and tracking products, such as radio frequency identification (RFID), barcodes and Web portal tools (Ting and Ip, 2015). An RFID tag attached to a product’s packaging transmits a signal to the company’s information system center, which helps locate the product.

However, once copied or damaged, these widely used product security codes cannot prevent counterfeiting (Wilson and Grammich, 2020), limiting their application (Lancaster, 2017). To fight counterfeiting as well as gray and copycat markets, the International Chamber of Commerce has created an institute called the Business Action to Stop Counterfeiting and Piracy, which represents more than 45 million companies in over 100 countries.

According to Ringsberg (2014), the traceability of a product comprises logistics traceability and qualitative traceability. The former refers to the tracking from raw materials to finished products and vice versa, whereas the latter focusses on storage and distribution conditions. Additionally, blockchain technology can be applied to commodity anti-counterfeiting because it prevents data tampering and enables transaction traceability (Toyoda _et al._, 2017). It particularly helps address the trust deficit in traditional products due to the circulation of fake products as well as protect consumer rights (Aitzhan and Svetinovic, 2016).
