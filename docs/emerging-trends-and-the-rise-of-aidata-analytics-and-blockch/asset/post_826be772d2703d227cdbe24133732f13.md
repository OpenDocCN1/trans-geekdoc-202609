
## Deep learning based affective computing

Saurabh Kumar

Indian Institute of Management Indore, Indore, India

## Abstract

Purpose – Decision-making in human beings is affected by emotions and sentiments. The affective computing takes this into account, intending to tailor decision support to the emotional states of people. However, the representation and classification of emotions is a very challenging task. The study used customized methods of deep learning models to aid in the accurate classification of emotions and sentiments. Design/methodology/approach – The present study presents affective computing model using both text and image data. The text-based affective computing was conducted on four standard datasets using three deep learning customized models, namely LSTM, GRU and CNN. The study used four variants of deep learning including the LSTM model, LSTM model with GloVe embeddings, Bi-directional LSTM model and LSTM model with attention layer. Findings – The result suggests that the proposed method outperforms the earlier methods. For image-based affective computing, the data was extracted from Instagram, and Facial emotion recognition was carried out using three deep learning models, namely CNN, transfer learning with VGG-19 model and transfer learning with ResNet-18 model. The results suggests that the proposed methods for both text and image can be used for affective computing and aid in decision-making. Originality/value – The study used deep learning for affective computing. Earlier studies have used machine learning algorithms for affective computing. However, the present study uses deep learning for affective computing.

Keywords Affective computing, Deep learning, Visualization, LSTM, CNN, GRU

Paper type Research paper

## 1. Introduction

We live in the age of social media, where people publicly convey their emotions and feelings on social media platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, Twitter and Snapchat. The emotions expressed on social media platforms can be positive, negative and neutral (Tomkins, 1962). Some of the studies have found that people on social media mirror the positive or negative emotions that their friends express in their posts (Kramer, 2015). People who see more “positive” posts in their feeds responds more positively, while the people who see more “negative” posts in their feeds responds with negative feelings of their own (Kramer et al., 2014). Thus, the expression of emotions on social media plays a significant role for the individuals and their immediate connections on social media. Human emotions can be expressed in the form of anger, joy, sadness, surprise, trust, anticipation and so on. Affective computing, in general, refers to the techniques for recognizing, interpreting, detecting and predicting such human emotions (Picard, 2000).

Affective computing has lately become a matter of great interest to various researchers from various domains like those of artificial intelligence and social science research. The analysis of affective computing can be grouped into two streams, namely, unimodal analysis and multimodal analysis. The unimodal analysis refers to the analysis of only one mode of affective computing, i.e. either text OR images OR audios OR videos. On the other hand, the multimodal analysis refers to multiple modes of affective computing, i.e. either the combination of text and images OR a combination of images and videos OR a combination of text, audio, images and so on. The current work uses two separate unimodal analyses for understanding affective computing. The unimodal analysis was conducted on both text and image data.

Affective computing is widely examined with the use of traditional machine learning methods. However, very few studies like Kratzwald et al. (2018) examined the recent

advancement of deep learning approaches in understanding affective computing. The study has explored affective computing using the deep learning method only on text data (Kratzwald et al., 2018). The current work further explores the research by using both text and image data for understanding affective computing.

There is an old adage which states that images or picture is worth a thousand words. Thus, images speak louder than words and can play a much crucial role in expressing emotions. Therefore, the analysis of the sentiments of images becomes very much pertinent. Some of the researchers like Ghani and Krema (2014) have studied the sentiment of the images by feature extraction method, while other researchers like Machajdik and Hanbury (2010) have used background and color of the images for classifying the emotions of the image. The present study uses the facial emotion recognition method to classify the sentiment depicted by the image.

The affective computing for text data is carried out on four standard datasets, namely PsychExp dataset (Wallbott and Scherer, 1986), ISEAR dataset (Wallbott and Scherer, 1986), Literary Tales dataset (Alm, 2008) and Headlines dataset (Strapparava and Mihalcea, 2007). PsychExp dataset consists of 7,840 records belonging to six classes, i.e. joy, fear, anger, sadness, disgust, shame and guilt. The ISEAR dataset is similar and consists of records of self-report of experiences. Headlines dataset is also known as SemEval-2007 dataset and consists of annotated headlines. Finally, Literary Tales is a larger dataset consisting of affect labelled literary tales. On the other hand, affective computing for images was carried out on images extracted from Instagram. Images were extracted using the Application Program Interface (API) of Instagram. The images obtained from Instagram are then classified into two classes of sentiments, namely, happy class and sad class.

We have used various deep learning models for effectively capturing affective computing for both text and images. For text-based affective computing, we have used three types of deep learning models- Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). We have also deployed various variants of LSTM, GRU and CNN models to improve the accuracy of text classification. For LSTM Models, we have used four different variants of LSTM Models: simple LSTM model with randomly initialized embeddings, LSTM model with Global Vector (GloVe) embeddings, Bi-directional LSTM model and LSTM model with an attention layer. Similarly, four variants of GRU models were also used to improve the accuracy of text classifications. The variants of GRU includes those of the GRU model with randomly initialized embeddings, GRU model with GloVe embeddings, Bi-directional GRU model and GRU model with an attention layer. The third type of deep learning model used in the study was the CNN model with randomly initialized embeddings and the CNN model with GloVe embeddings. The Bi-directional CNN model and the CNN model with the attention layer are not possible in deep learning. Bi-directional CNN gives the same output as that of CNN because the entire thing is taken as an input in one instance. For image-based affective computing, we have used three deep learning models: CNN model with three convolutional layers and three fully connected layers, transfer learning model with pre-trained VGG-19 network and transfer learning model with pre-trained ResNet-18 network. Further, we have presented the visualization of images analysis using t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of Instagram images.

The present study draws its inspiration from some of the existing deep learning techniques used in earlier researches (Kratzwald et al., 2018; Kraus and Feuerriegel, 2017) and then extend it to the context of affective computing. Furthermore, we have used an additional deep learning model, namely CNN in addition to the standard LSTM and GRU used in earlier researches of Kratzwald et al. (2018). Besides, we have also used an attention layer in both LSTM and GRU. Finally, we have further compared our results with the earlier proposed techniques to validate the importance of the proposed methodology.
