Yermack (2013) claims that Bitcoin fails in fulfilling these functions and concludes that Bitcoin mostly resembles assets. Also, Peetz and Mall (2017) believe that Bitcoin is a speculative asset whose price has a bubble. For instance, Baur et al. Glaser et al. (2014) investigated the intention of the investors in the Bitcoin market by employing the econometrics method to analyze whether investors regard Bitcoin as a currency or speculative asset. They conclude that new investors in the Bitcoin market view it as a speculative investment rather than a currency.

Some studies claim that cryptocurrencies belong to a new class of assets. For example, White et al. (2020) try to determine the asset Bitcoin class by comparing Bitcoin behavior to other asset classes and using various statistical and econometrics methods. They conclude that the behavior of Bitcoin is similar to technology-based products or bubble events rather than currency. This result was also confirmed by Maurer et al. (2013) that regard Bitcoin as a technology rather than currency. Ram (2019) investigated the nature of Bitcoin by employing statistical methods; their result indicates that Bitcoin is a distinct class of asset and investment and has a low correlation with other classes of assets.

The interaction between different cryptocurrencies is analyzed extensively. For instance, Katsiampa et al. (2019) studied conditional volatility spillover effects and conditional correlations among several cryptocurrencies by utilizing a pair-wise bivariate Baba, Engle, Kraft and Kroner (BEKK) model. The results reveal that cryptocurrency price volatility is dependent on its past shocks and volatility, and there are bi-directional volatility spillover effects between all cryptocurrencies, showing the existence of positive time-varying conditional correlations between these cryptocurrencies. Sifat et al. (2019) investigated the lead-lag relationship between Bitcoin and Ethereum prices by employing different econometrics methods, like autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) and wavelet coherence. The result indicates that there is a bidirectional relationship between these two cryptocurrencies. González et al. (2020) examined the interaction between the price return of several cryptocurrencies using the ARDL method, and the results indicate that there is positive intercedence between the price return of cryptocurrencies. The results also indicate that Bitcoin price return has a high persistent impact on the price return of other cryptocurrencies. Katsiampa et al. (2019) examined the pair relationship between Bitcoin, Ether and Litecoin by employing a bivariate BEKK model and found bivariate shock transmission between Bitcoin and both Litecoin and Ether. The result also indicates that there are mostly positive dynamic conditional correlations between cryptocurrencies. Ji et al. (2019a) investigated the connectedness between several cryptocurrencies by employing the model proposed by Diebold and Yılmaz (2012, 2015). The results indicate that the shocks and volatility from Bitcoin and Litecoin have the strongest impact on the other cryptocurrencies compared to others.

Some literature focuses on the interconnection between cryptocurrencies and different classes of assets; for instance, Bouri et al. (2018b) studied the relationship between Bitcoin prices and aggregate commodity index and gold prices by employing the quantile ARDL model. The results indicate that the information from gold and commodity index prices can be used to predict Bitcoin prices. In addition, they found that the relationship between Bitcoin and both the aggregate commodity index and gold is asymmetric. Gajardo et al. (2018) studied the asymmetry of cross-correlation between Bitcoin and gold prices, oil price, several currencies and Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) by employing multifractal asymmetric detrended cross-correlation method. The result of the study indicates that Bitcoin had a different relationship with gold and oil prices and the DJIA index compared to other currencies. In addition, there are greater multifractal spectra in cross-correlation between Bitcoin and commodity prices and stock index (DJIA).

Rehman and Apergis (2019) investigated the relationship between Bitcoin and Ethereum and various commodity futures, like gold, silver, copper and oil, by using the causality on quantile method proposed by Balcilar et al. (2016). The results indicate that in most quantiles, there is a causality from cryptocurrencies to commodities futures regarding mean and volatility. Urquhart and Zhang (2019) studied the relationship between Bitcoin and several currencies by employing an asymmetric dynamic conditional correlation model. Their results indicate that Bitcoin is correlated positively and negatively with currencies implying that Bitcoin can be used as a hedger and diversifier for these currencies depending on the time and the currency in question. Bouri et al. (2018a) applied the vector auto-regression (VAR) GARCH model to daily data spanning from July 19, 2010, to October 31, 2017, to check the interconnection among Bitcoin and other conventional assets. The study's finding suggests that Bitcoin returns are related quite closely to other conventional assets, and the Bitcoin market is not isolated completely. Ciaian et al. (2018) analyzed the relationship between Bitcoin and gold prices, several exchange rates and the Treasury maturity rate and showed that Bitcoin prices are affected in both the short and long runs by the prices of those assets. Bouri et al. (2017) investigated the connection between Bitcoin and commodity indices by focusing on energy commodities by employing an asymmetric dynamic conditional correlation model. It was found that there is a connection between Bitcoin and commodity, and Bitcoin can play as a hedge and safe haven for those indices. Ji et al. (2019b) investigated the information interdependence between leading cryptocurrencies and commodities via a time-varying entropy approach. It was revealed that cryptocurrencies are increasingly integrated within the commodity market. Kurka (2019) studied the interaction between some traditional assets, like currencies, stocks and commodities, and Bitcoin using the volatility spillover method proposed by Diebold and Yilmaz (2009, 2012). They concluded that there was no considerable connection between cryptocurrencies and traditional assets, but there was considerable shock transmission between those assets and Bitcoin in some periods. Also, Baur et al. (2018) investigated the relationship between Bitcoin and traditional assets, including stocks and bonds, by analyzing the statistical properties of Bitcoin. It was shown that Bitcoin is not correlated with traditional asset classes. In addition, Corbet et al. (2018b) studied the linkage among several cryptocurrencies and a group of conventional financial assets. The study's findings reveal that cryptocurrencies are highly interconnected; however, they are not related to conventional assets. Charfeddine et al. (2020) investigated the relationship between several cryptocurrencies and commodities by using the DCC GARCH model and copula approach, and the results show that the cross-correlations between cryptocurrencies and those assets are weak. So cryptocurrencies are poor hedgers for those assets. In addition, the relationship between cryptocurrencies and those assets can be affected by economic and financial shocks. Also, Zeng et al. (2020) analyzed the interconnection between Bitcoin, stock, oil and gold by employing a method developed by Diebold and Yilmaz (2009, 2012, 2014). The results indicated a weak connection between Bitcoin and conventional assets. Tiwari et al. (2019) examined the correlation between several cryptocurrencies and S&P 500 by applying the copula–ADCC–EGARCH method. The result indicates that the correlation between the stock index and cryptocurrencies is weak so that cryptocurrencies can be used as hedgers for portfolios.

On the other hand, the literature on the stock of technology companies is mainly focused on the correlation and spillover effect between the stock prices of these companies and oil prices (Bondia et al., 2016; Kocaarslan and Soytas, 2019; Nasreen et al., 2020; Sadorsky, 2012). In addition, Sadorsky (2003) investigated the macroeconomic determinants of conditional volatility of US technology stock price. The study's findings reveal that the stock prices of technology companies are significantly affected by the conditional volatilities of oil prices, the term premium and consumer price index (CPI).
