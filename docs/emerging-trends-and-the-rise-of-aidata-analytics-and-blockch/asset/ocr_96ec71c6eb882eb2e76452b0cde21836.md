Relevant laws, norms, regulations and principles should also be supplemented or amended to support the introduction of blockchain. Introducing a new system such as a blockchain digital identification system requires an understanding of and compliance with applicable laws. Therefore, to increase the applicability and usefulness of a blockchain-based digital identity management system, it must be accompanied by extensive knowledge of national laws and regulations, and of data protection laws in particular, so that these can be improved to better support such a system. Research proposing a successful blockchain adoption model for governments can also contribute to improved policies and enforcement.

Third, there may be a need for research into the creation of a blockchain ecosystem. Many countries and companies are developing technologies in a mutually exclusive manner in an attempt to take the lead in the market, and there is little sharing of research results and technologies. However, to activate the full potential of identity management systems and strengthen sovereignty through the use of blockchain technology, it is necessary to establish an ecosystem consisting of financial institutions, mobile carriers, governments and platforms (Wadhwa, 2019). To this end, a consortium including regulators, blockchain technology companies and infrastructure operators should be established, and there is also a need for research into appropriate policymaking and the design of a suitable business model. In the same vein, research should be conducted from a long-term perspective to examine the benefits and added value that can be gained from a blockchain-based identity system. Core technologies such as AI, cloud, big data and IoT will be applied everywhere in the near future, and money, products, space and everything else will be shared, thus causing the entire ecosystem to change. It is, therefore, suggested that future research examine long-term measures that can be taken, as well as the preparations that can be made. It is additionally suggested that infrastructure research be conducted. Fourth, there are still challenges remaining with digital transformation in the public sector. Most of the literature argues that advancements can be made in technology implementation, but that the real difficulties lie in the changes in the structure and the culture that must follow, as well as in the integration and participation of citizens into and in digitalized public services (Sivarajah et al., 2015). As indicated by Mergel et al. (2019), a digital transformation needs to be seen as a holistic approach rather than as the simple digitization of a process. Research needs to analyze how the organizational structure and process in the public sector can change when adopting advanced technologies and how digitization could change the relationships with citizens and other stakeholders.

Blockchain-related academic research is still in its infancy. Although a number of systematic literature review papers have examined blockchain-related topics (Alketbi et al., 2020; Casino et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2018), there are still substantial research gaps in blockchain research, including usability, scalability and social issues such as ethical dilemmas involving security and privacy (Yli-Huumo et al., 2016); these issues should be addressed in future studies. While digitization in public sectors has many potential benefits, little attention has been paid to the ethical, social, privacy and security issues that could potentially arise. For example, governments and companies could use data analytic tools and big data algorithms to identify users and access private data without the consent of the individual(s) in question. Users (in this case, citizens) often do not fully understand how their information and opinion can be used and manipulated. Researchers should pay attention to ethical challenges in digital use in the public sector (Domanski et al., 2018). As many scholars have pointed out, ethics are not limited to the existing laws. Therefore, new ethical frameworks are needed to address the ethical challenges resulting from digitization in the public and private sectors (Chang et al., 2020).
