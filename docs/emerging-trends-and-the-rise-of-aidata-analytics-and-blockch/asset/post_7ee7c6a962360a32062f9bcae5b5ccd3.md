## 6.4 Novel IoT security threats as a result of blockchain

### 6.4.1 RQ4: Does blockchain pose novel security threats when implemented to safeguard IoT ecosystems?

The review of the literature did not reveal new security threats when implementing blockchain in IoT, except for only one threat that is uniquely identified as blockchain triggered (see Moin et al., 2019). The large amount of data generated by IoT devices presents the most significant vulnerability. Since blockchain requires time to process transaction, a large amount of data can slow down its performance. With many heterogeneous devices, security measures might not be adequate enough to handle a large-scale blockchain. This poses security threats such as malware injection and backdoor installations when security software for different data is inappropriately designed.

## 7. Discussion

Our systematic review of the literature provides evidence of blockchain's superiority to conventional and centralized IoT infrastructure and handling security. Blockchain, with its intrinsic design features, is found capable of addressing the IoT security threats that were identified in the literature (see Table 5). Perhaps more importantly, it provides a supercharged level of security that cannot be achieved with the established IoT security approaches. Indeed, as Table 5 shows, security is achieved at the four different layers of IoT devices and, according to the extant literature (see Section 6.3), at a much-improved security level. The distinctive architectural features of blockchain, combined with cryptography and other borrowed security principles provide a sturdier security platform for IoT ecosystems. Nevertheless, the elevated security standard achieved with blockchain technology comes at a cost. Our focus in this paper was to provide a comprehensive view of IoT threats so as to identify and compare the conventional threat countermeasures with blockchain-based security countermeasures. While attempting to answer the question of novel threats that stem from adopting blockchain technology to manage IoT security, a number of authors highlight major hurdles facing industry-wide acceptance. According to Jesus et al. (2018), IoT devices are low-power and low-computing technologies. Accordingly, very few resources are dedicated to ensuring their security (Jesus et al., 2018). Due to its ledger-based, decentralization and inherent security principles, blockchain has prospects to becoming the main infrastructure for IoT. But blockchain, in its current format, is a heavy technology. It is time-consuming, energy-inefficient, requires excessive storage and generates large bandwidth. Those traits are conflicting with low-resource technologies such as IoTs.

### 7.1 Time consumption

A blockchain network transaction could be cumbersome, often taking hours to process logging transaction due to the decentralized complexities (Moin et al., 2019). It consumes a lot of time to identify and compute header-to-header information of large datasets of blockchain networks (Moin et al., 2019). For some blockchain technologies, the validation of transaction can take up to 10 minutes thus creating a problem for real-time applications (Kouicem et al., 2018) such as patient health tracking or hazard detection and communication. Moreover, there is variance in processing time. This variation is brought about by the computing capabilities of the devices in the network (Kumar and Mallick, 2018). Devices with different computing capabilities take varying time to encrypt data hence the variance in processing time (Kumar and Mallick, 2018). It is unrealistic to assume that IoT devices running on a shared blockchain network would have analogous processing powers.

### 7.2 Bandwidth consumption

Blockchain consumes a lot of bandwidth to validate many transactions generated by the IoT devices (Kouicem et al., 2018). New blockchain transactions requires broadcasting to all peers which results in large communication overhead. Additionally, all the peers need to synchronize and retain the copy to blockchain further adding to the overhead (Sengupta et al., 2020). Indeed, the poor throughput of blockchain impacts its ability to scale up to wide usage in large IoT ecosystems (Dai et al., 2019; Kumar and Mallick, 2018).

### 7.3 Energy consumption

The majority of popular blockchain technologies need to perform multiple computations to achieve consensus hence leading to energy wastage (Sengupta et al., 2020). While providing greater security, the verification algorithm run on multiple times to the peers drastically increasing energy consumption and reducing operational efficiency (Sengupta et al., 2020).

### 7.4 Storage consumption

The requirement of storing all transactions in one node introduces a problem of excessive system storage due to properties of distributed storage (Yang et al., 2019; Kumar and Mallick, 2018). Size could be manageable at first but over the years, the blockchain might grow to undesirable magnitudes for small low-cost IoT devices to handle. As an example, the bitcoin blockchain grew from 6 gigabytes in 2013 to a colossal 270 gigabytes in 2020 [1]. That said, enhanced security is inscribed in those blockchain features such as in the case of immutability.

## 8. Future research

Notwithstanding the aforementioned capabilities of blockchain in solving IoT security issues, it remains inadequate in physically securing the IoT devices from destruction or tampering. Devoid of a healthy physical security strategy, attackers can physically access IoT networks via any IoT device (Yang et al., 2017). According to Asif et al. (2021), there are two main categories of physical security attacks: invasive and non-invasive attacks. The former attack involves direct manipulation of the processor whereas the latter requires attackers to be in proximity in order to learn the device’s specifications and modify its code to hack essential information.

Our review of the literature hints to the shortfall of blockchain technology as a conduit for the physical security of IoT devices given the lack of focus in the relevant research on this aspect. Future research on blockchain for IoT should also focus on securing the physical environment for the connected devices. It is crucial to continue securing the environment as the increase of connected infrastructure raises the vulnerability for physical cybersecurity exploitation. To this end, a hybrid technology could be implemented, i.e., blockchain security features can be integrated with traditional security mechanisms which makes it a promising future research topic. IoT devices can signal invasive interference to their IoT ecosystems that central authorities can physically verify. Device specifications that hackers require to conduct invasive attacks can be concealed from nearby attackers which can be supplemented by blockchain ecosystems modified to detect out-of-service or possibly tampered with IoT devices within their networks.

Our endeavor to systematically review the relevant literature on blockchain-enabled IoT security yielded an exhaustive framework of IoT security threats along with the traditional and blockchain mechanisms to address those threats. The extant research does not take a holistic approach to investigate how blockchain technology can address IoT security altogether but instead focus on specific threats where blockchain has a superior impact. Accordingly, our framework can be used to further investigate IoT security as a system. Specifically, future research can empirically examine whether IoT ecosystems that intelligently and economically incorporates traditional mechanisms with blockchain features are indeed more protected than using solely traditional IoT security methods or blockchain technology. That said, the implementation of blockchain technology in IoT ecosystems should focus more on gauging security with respect to the needs of the industry that it intends to support. In other words, there is no blockchain versatile enough for all IoTs. A configurational approach is required to align the IoT security requirements with the consumption of resources of different blockchain features is necessary in order to realize the proper balance between security, efficiency and feasibility.

## 9. Conclusion

Blockchain technology has the potential to overcome the aforementioned challenges and become the technology of choice when implementing wide-scale IoT ecosystems. Our literature review reveals that the technology can address with supremacy the massive inventory of security threats that faces IoT devices. Besides, blockchain as a novel technological architecture does not yet stem new security threats that are noteworthy of mentioning. That said, this technology, in its current form and principles, requires substantial energy, storage, bandwidth and time resources. IoT ecosystems, especially in areas such as supply chain, healthcare and smart living cannot yet afford those resources given the low-cost and low-resource premise of IoT infrastructure. To this end, considerable transfigurations are necessary to adapt blockchain to the various industries and contexts. For instance, in private (trusted) IoT ecosystems, decentralized consensus mechanisms might not be necessary; thereby reducing the time and energy wastage. Pertaining to the incessant size growth of blockchain, forking (and self-forking) can help reduce the blockchain storage requirements to manageable volumes (Magnani et al., 2018; Basu, 2019).
