### Abstract

**Purpose** – Most businesses strive to control the efficiency of their supply chains; however, luxury firms face additional challenges from counterfeit, gray market and copycat products. Blockchain technology can address these issues and enhance firms’ supply chain management, guaranteeing the traceability and origin of luxury products. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the utility and contexts influencing the implementation of blockchain technology to optimize supply chain management and prevent fraud in the luxury industry.

**Design/methodology/approach** – The research uses a qualitative approach based on the grounded theory method. Data are collected by semi-structured interviews with 12 stakeholders working on blockchain applications in the luxury business sector.

**Findings** – Highlighting the problems faced by luxury brands’ supply chains, this study presents blockchain technology as a solution for disintermediation, traceability and transparency in the luxury goods sector. The constraints faced by luxury brands incorporating this technology into their ecosystem include the knowledge gap, the multiplicity of third parties involved in the production process and bias toward short-term returns on investment.

**Originality/value** – Blockchains promote greater transparency and efficiency within supply chains, which builds consumer trust and improves brand revenue. Considering luxury brands’ reluctance to adopt blockchains, this study suggests that luxury firms adopt a staggered implementation of private blockchain networks starting with a small number of third-party suppliers.

**Keywords** Blockchain, Luxury brands, Supply chains, Traceability, Counterfeit, Gray market

### 1. Introduction

Globalization and the internet have helped cross-border e-commerce proliferate over time (Liu and Li, 2020). Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic has boosted the digitalization of cross-border e-commerce, particularly in the luxury goods sector, which has had to adapt its supply chain solutions to remain stable (Shen et al., 2020). However, despite being relatively resilient, luxury firms have faced additional challenges from the decrease in tourism (D’Arpizio et al., 2020) and the increase in digital consumption (Achille and Zipser, 2020) during recent lockdowns. Therefore, businesses must aim to establish trusted supply chain networks to protect their partnerships with stakeholders and secure international transactions (Koh et al., 2012; Chang et al., 2020b). Additionally, supply chain management in the luxury sector needs to address some unique challenges such as:

- product complexity from sourcing a large number of components from third-party suppliers for production (Lamming et al., 2000);
- heterogeneous stakeholders (manufacturers, suppliers, distributors and customers);
- an increase in counterfeiting due to the rise of the gray market (catalyzed by demand for second-hand luxury goods) and the copycat market (Wang et al., 2020).

Counterfeiting involves substituting branded luxury products with low-quality items through authentic, reused or imitative packaging that uses fake labels or logos. Thus, counterfeiting undermines brand value and reputation, diminishes goodwill and consumer confidence, and violates intellectual property rights and trademarks (Soon and Manning, 2019). According to Berman (2008), there are four distinct types of counterfeit products:

- an inexpensive, low-quality copy, without traditional packaging, purchased through an unusual distribution channel by consumers who are perfectly aware of their counterfeit nature;
- genuine products that are reverse-engineered through stolen or copied blueprints, where the consumer is unaware of their counterfeit nature;
- counterfeit products produced by a current or former outsourcing supplier without the manufacturer's authorization or the customer's knowledge (Parloff et al., 2006);
- products produced by outsourcing suppliers that did not meet the manufacturer's standards and were not destroyed.

By contrast, gray products are genuine products that are imported without the brand owner's authorization (Antia et al. (2006)) and violate intellectual property legislation by infringing on the exclusive rights of exclusive importers, thereby jeopardizing the luxury market. To understand its mechanisms, the gray market must be analyzed with respect to its pricing, distribution channel, impact on the brand manufacturer and consumer attitude. Gray markets mainly occur because of the price gaps between countries and retail networks and the recent growth of the copycat market, which indirectly threatens the luxury market through imitative luxury products (Hou et al., 2020) sold under a different brand or logo. Thus, luxury brands have started to seek different measures to protect themselves. For example, the French multinational LVMH has decided to invest €40m each year to improve its products' traceability [1]. This includes monitoring the second-hand market to establish affiliate links with the new owner of its products. One study stated that second-hand products contribute 7% of the luxury market, and this sector of the market is growing by 12% every year [2]. However, 80% of second-hand market consumers use digital platforms to buy or resell their luxury products, complicating their traceability. Therefore, this study investigates whether blockchain can solve the problems of luxury brands, notably counterfeiting and gray markets.

First proposed by Nakamoto (2008), the creator of bitcoins, a blockchain is a distributed network and chain of cryptographic blocks (Lu, 2019) that forms a decentralized and secure network (Puthal et al., 2018) by verifying only valid transactions (Taylor et al., 2020). A blockchain's decentralized data storage works on peer-to-peer transactions, making it difficult to alter an individual block and thus improving security. Blockchain technology was developed in four stages (Angelis and da Silva, 2019): blockchain 1.0 primarily focused on transactions, especially in cryptocurrencies and digital payment systems; blockchain 2.0 encompassed privacy, smart contracts and blockchain tokens such as Ethereum; blockchain 3.0 decentralized applications, storage and computing; and, finally, blockchain 4.0 incorporated artificial intelligence. This technology is often considered to be disruptive (Frizzo-Barker et al., 2020), i.e. it is capable of both destroying business processes (Wessel and Christensen, 2012) and radically transforming existing business models (Brousseau and Penard, 2007; Tönnissen and Teuteberg, 2020). However, it can also reduce fraud and identity theft (Kursh and Gold, 2016), increase efficiency, lower transaction costs (Zheng et al., 2017; Kshetri, 2018) and propose decentralized business models for multi-service networks to execute multiple services at different prices (Leal et al., 2021).

Recent studies distinguish between three types of blockchains. Public or open blockchains allow anyone to transact or enter the blockchain network as seen in bitcoins; private or closed blockchains focus on private transactions; and consortia are mainly used by a network of organizations such as banks (Morkunas et al., 2019; Song et al., 2019). Blockchain networks (Lu, 2019; Viriyasitavat and Hoonsopon, 2019) are based on the following principles:
