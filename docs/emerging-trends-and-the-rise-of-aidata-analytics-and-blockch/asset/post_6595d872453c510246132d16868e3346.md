### 2.2 Blockchain challenges

Although blockchain has many benefits, it also has many associated challenges that must be resolved, such as security, scalability, usability, reliability and flexibility (Batubara et al., 2018; Yi-Huumo et al., 2016). Blockchain enhances privacy and data protection, but its open and immutable nature can raise other ethical issues (Clavin et al., 2020); e.g. Bitcoin transactions have been reported to disclose sensitive information (Goldfeder et al., 2017). Further, as a public ledger that stores information, there is the potential for data leakage. In other words, there are crucial confidentiality and security issues involved with blockchain that must be resolved. In addition, traditional access control and redundant data storage can lead to inadequate security protection for sensitive data, such as medical data in medical storage systems. Given these security and privacy issues, researchers have proposed privacy-preserving IoT- and blockchain-based systems to ensure data authentication and confidentiality (Kumar and Bhalaji, 2021; Yang et al., 2019). For example, Elisa et al. (2018) have proposed a framework of secure and privacy-preserving blockchain-based e-government systems. Zheng et al. (2018) have suggested improving privacy and anonymity in blockchain systems, such as by using a mixing service that “provides anonymity by transferring funds from multiple input addresses to multiple outputs” and a proof of zero-knowledge, which is a type of coin validation for transactions.

An ethical framework for new technologies has also been proposed (Chang, 2021; Tang et al., 2020): Tang et al. (2020) proposed a theoretical framework of blockchain ethics to encourage the ethical implementation of blockchain technology. This is a framework intended to enhance the benefits of blockchain technology while strengthening its ethical implementation. It includes understanding the ethical and moral challenges of blockchain, building applications and business models, then applying appropriate regulations to the blockchain (Tang et al., 2020). Similarly, Chang (2021) proposed an ethical framework for big data and smart cities. This ethical framework has been proposed as an attempt to minimize social concerns, such as the fear of the disclosure of personal information, which may arise in an open digital environment. As technology advances rapidly along with the public understanding of the ethical issues associated with such new technology, relevant laws and regulations have to be considered as well. Despite these growing ethical concerns, there has been little research examining the ethics of emerging technologies (Fischer, 2018).

In terms of the technical considerations involved in the open, distributed and complex nature of blockchain solutions, the issues of latency and scalability have to be improved as blockchain continues to evolve (Vukolic, 2015). Due to the limited capacity of each block, most cryptocurrencies have a low transaction rate. For instance, the bitcoin transaction rate per second is significantly lower than that of other credit card transaction organizations such as VISA and PayPal (Chang et al., 2020; Swan, 2015). In an attempt to overcome these limitations, sharding proposals are being introduced in many communities; sharding is the act of storing state and transaction history in each shard, which can significantly improve the overall performance by breaking the blockchain into smaller chains (Abuidris et al., 2019; Casino et al., 2019). In addition to critical issues regarding access control, privacy and performance, blockchain systems must consider whether all entities are interoperable and extensible so that users can coordinate different applications in a heterogeneous environment (Bertino and Takahashi, 2011). Further, the biggest challenge to be addressed for blockchain is regulatory support for legislation involving the technical, environmental and ethical issues related to blockchain. An immutable blockchain property is that data cannot be removed, which can lead to privacy concerns. However, general data protection regulations require organizations to store personal data only for as long as required for their original purpose. These regulations should be reviewed with respect to the new data transaction methods offered by blockchain and other emerging technologies. It should be noted that changes in the legal framework often lag behind the pace of technology development due to the involvement of multiple actors (Batubara et al., 2018).

For blockchain application to the government sector, it is also important to establish a new governance model for usability and acceptance. Most of the problems associated with using blockchain for recording and verification have been attributed to the strict regulations on data protection and privacy, as well as a lack of infrastructure and public understanding (Stanford Business, 2019). In addition, most countries do not have clear legislation regulating digital identities based on blockchain.

### 2.3 E-government and blockchain

With the continued innovation in emerging technologies, e-government or digital government initiatives have increased substantially worldwide (Baheer et al., 2020; Gil-García and Flores-Zúñiga, 2020; Tambouris et al., 2014). For example, major countries such as Denmark, Australia, South Korea and the UK are now using advanced technologies in their government sector. The governments of most countries are even specifically participating in blockchain-based projects for public services to varying extents. South Korea has already launched significant data initiatives in its government sector (UN, 2020; Kim et al., 2014), while Estonia has introduced electronic residency (Savage, 2018). The United Nations (UN) investigated the level of e-government development in each country in terms of new technology adoption, legal and institutional frameworks, strategy and implementation, and user satisfaction (UN, 2020); Table 1 lists the leading countries in e-government development according to the results of this survey. Europe is a leader in e-government development as well as the provision of online services, with Denmark having the highest E-Government Development Index (EGDI) value among the top seven countries in Northern Europe. South Korea is a global leader in online service provision and has the best EGDI performance in Asia.

As presented in the table, many countries are implementing policies that enable the sharing of information, including government data and policy-related data, through digitization to induce active citizen participation in policymaking and to ensure process transparency. As a result, blockchain technology is attracting attention from many governments and public services. Blockchain-based digital identity systems enhance government innovation and lead to economic advantages by providing secure and reliable authentication online. Specifically, they reduce the costs associated with customer service and increase satisfaction with fast and reliable service transactions. Blockchain-based electronic voting systems, as have been proposed in many countries, have also been reported to prevent security attacks and manipulations while increasing transparency (Dararomla and Thebus, 2020).

While implementing new technologies in government services can have a wide variety of benefits, practical implementations of such technologies in the government sector are rare, and the existing ones are still in their infancy; there are also certain problems and challenges that remain to be solved. These concerns include several factors beyond technical issues, such as a lack of policy enforcement, a lack of user awareness, a lack of information control and a lack of knowledge on digital government architecture (Baheer et al., 2020).
