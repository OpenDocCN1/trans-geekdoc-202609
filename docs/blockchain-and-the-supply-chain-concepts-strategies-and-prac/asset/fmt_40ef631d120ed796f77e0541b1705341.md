
We believe that improving the way suppliers can gather and administer the required evidence (ie the documentation requirements for the application of the 0 per cent VAT rate) is a first step. With the underlying technology maturing and parties getting more familiar with using applications such as `VATBL0CK`, blockchain could take a more central role within the VAT domain itself. Blockchain could be used as a basis for the application of the 0 per cent VAT rate as blockchain itself (and/or in combination with other technologies, eg IoT, Optical Character Reading (`OCR`) and other machine learning applications) can ensure proof that goods have been sent or transported from the country of dispatch. In the future, applications’ use of blockchain could ensure compliance by design when it comes to the application of the 0 per VAT rate.

### TradeTrust: digital transfer of ownership of the electronic bill of lading^([23](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en23))

The TradeTrust project envisions the creation of a sandbox environment in which the first legally binding, interoperable, paperless transfer of ownership of an `EBL` can be conducted on a live shipment. The project kick-off was in April 2019 in Singapore, when delegates from the Maritime Port Authorities (`MPA`)^([24](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en24)) and the Infocomm Media Development Authority (`IMDA`),^([25](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en25)) together with the Port of Rotterdam and Blocklab, hosted a two-day workshop with members of Singapore’s maritime, trade and banking community. In October 2019 a technical deep dive was organized around token-enabled digital transfer of ownership. In January 2021, the first TradeTrust-enabled transfer of ownership of an `EBL` was performed (Port of Rotterdam, 2021).

Since December 2019, the project has been actively supported by the Dutch ministry of economic affairs and climate policy, `ICTU`, `Spark!`, `LivingLab` and the `2Tokens` project. TradeTrust is an implementation of the UNCITRAL model law on electronic transferable records (`MLETR`)^([26](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en26)) and is an active project under `UNCEFACT`.

TradeTrust is ‘a set of governance & legal frameworks, standards and future-ready digital infrastructure’, facilitating ‘the interoperability of electronic trade documents^([27](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en27)) exchanged between different digital ecosystems’.^([28](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en28))

It does so by separating the responsibility of tracking legal ownership from the data in the document. While the digital document is stored off-chain under the strict control of the original data owner, the ownership thereof can securely be managed on a distributed ledger (in our case `Ethereum`). Users are free to decide on how they want to digitally exchange the `EBL` data (eg email, `sftp`). Anyone in possession of a digital copy of the document can verify the integrity and ownership of the document on the public blockchain.

Each document can be ‘identified uniquely by a document hash’.^([29](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en29)) ^([30](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en30)) TradeTrust documents are based on the `OpenAttestation`^([31](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en31)) specification, with each document containing ‘information on the schema type, certificate data, hidden data (from privacy filter) and a signature’^([32](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en32)). TradeTrust documents can be serialized to a (`.tt`) file which is simply a `JavaScript Object Notation (JSON)` file that conforms to this specification. TradeTrust is payload agnostic; in other words, there are no restrictions on how the document’s content is formatted. In this way interoperability with any existing `EBL` data exchange formats is ensured. The project is open source and can be used by anybody under the `Apache 2.0` licence.

As mentioned earlier, TradeTrust separates the ownership of an item that a document of title represents from the content of the document. Ownership can be tracked on the blockchain in a secure fashion without storing the document’s data publicly on the blockchain.

TradeTrust tracks the ownership of assets using a smart contract. This smart contract adheres to the `ERC-721` token standard,^([33](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en33)) which defines a common software interface for handling ownership of non-fungible tokens. A token is a very large, globally unique number that represents a digital or real world entity, in this case a document of title like the bill of lading. At the core, an `ERC-721`-compatible contract simply maps token identifiers, or the asset they represent, to the `Ethereum` address which owns the token and therefore the asset.

Each document in TradeTrust is linked to a non-fungible token on an `ERC-721` contract. The link between token and document is created through the hash^([34](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en34)) of the document’s content (the `.tt` file). The hash is a very large, globally unique number and can be used as the token identifier. A hash acts like a fingerprint of a piece of data: every hash is unique; it is impossible to get back the original data from only the hash and hashing the same data always creates the same hash.

According to TradeTrust’s singularity principle, so long as the token framework is properly implemented, it is ‘practically impossible’^([35](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en35)),^([36](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en36)) for a hash – and therefore the reference to a document – to be duplicated. This is partially due to `Ethereum`’s necessity for smart contracts to have unique addresses, and partially due to the smart contract itself guaranteeing that an ID can only be registered once in the registry.

`ERC-721` smart contracts can be used to track ownership of assets by representing them as a token and assigning an `Ethereum` address as its owner. However, in many real world applications, ownership is not always that binary. Documents of title, specifically the bill of lading, confer legal asset ownership to a beneficiary. However, the beneficiary is not always the holder of the physical document. To claim the goods, the beneficiary also needs to be in possession of the physical document. So, while the holder does not actually own the assets represented by the document, it can be used as leverage or collateral. Banks often hold the physical documents as collateral, and many trade finance processes rely on this distinction.

The `ERC-721` standard does not natively support such advanced ownership constructs. But it does allow other smart contracts to own tokens, as contracts are also represented by `Ethereum` addresses. TradeTrust has therefore developed the title escrow contract that adds the distinction between holder and beneficiary on top of the core functionality of the `ERC-721` contract.

This works as follows: when an `EBL` is issued – in blockchain terms this boils down to a token being minted – the token is not owned directly by the beneficiary’s `Ethereum` address. Instead, the issuer also creates a title escrow contract, in which the initial beneficiary and holder (their `Ethereum` addresses) are named. The token is then owned by the address of the newly created instance of the title escrow contract.

The title escrow contract allows the holder to transfer his rights as a holder to a new address. The holder further has the right to transfer the title to a new `Ethereum` address, which can again be a new instance of the title escrow contract. This title transfer has to be endorsed by the beneficiary, though. The endorsement through the beneficiary is the equivalent of the beneficiary signing the back of the paper bill of lading.
