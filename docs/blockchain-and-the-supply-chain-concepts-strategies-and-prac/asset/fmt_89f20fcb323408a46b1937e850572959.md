
Therefore, it’s no surprise that two of the largest and most successful industries over the last century – electronics and automotive – also happen to be the two most globalized sectors. Automotive supply chains deal with a dizzying array of parts made from a host of inputs, including steel, glass, rubber and plastic, sourced from a global network of suppliers.

The same is true of electronics. The supply chain for semiconductors, the key elements of most consumer and industrial electronic systems, spans continents.

Even where there is a predictable, well-functioning international trading system, companies face risks and challenges to their supply chains that they must effectively manage in order to preserve and protect their brand and reputation in the market. Among other things, companies need to:

*   evaluate the costs of inputs, such as labour and sourcing of raw materials, tariffs, taxes and customs rules, to determine how best to set up their supply chains as well as when to alter them;
*   ensure that their products and processes conform with relevant regulatory and market requirements, including with respect to health, safety, the environment, labour and employment, consumer protection, sustainability, anti-corruption, human rights, competition, taxation and good manufacturing practices;
*   navigate economic and cultural differences between countries with different regulations, customs, transportation and infrastructure capabilities, and quality systems;
*   ensure that products are manufactured and delivered to consumers and other end users on time and on budget, balancing lean manufacturing with inventory elasticity;
*   protect themselves and their data, guarding against the theft of intellectual property and cyber intrusions by updating their practices and use of technology;
*   manage unexpected disruptions, for example those caused by natural disasters, common carrier logistics interruptions, shipping industry bankruptcies or labour issues at seaports.

A well-managed and efficiently operated supply chain provides competitive advantages that reduce costs and maximize profits. Conversely, poorly managed supply chains may result in inefficiency, compliance failures and customer dissatisfaction. Companies with well-defined legal and regulatory strategies surrounding their supply chain management programmes benefit from more frequent commercial opportunities, access to higher-calibre resources and talent, and see consistent gains in quality and efficiency.

Effective supply chain management takes affirmative planning and, while this is easier to accomplish during a time of geopolitical and policy stability, it is even more necessary to address in times of disruption. There have been occasional events such as natural disasters, like the 2011 Japanese tsunami, that have dealt setbacks to globalization and disrupted global supply chains, but the shocks have been temporary.

### Modern waves of migration

The supply chain is greatly impacted during times of mass migration. The major migration waves which shaped the world included the Great Atlantic Migration from Europe to North America in the 1840s. From 1801 to 1914, about 7.5 million migrants moved from European to Asiatic Russia (ie Siberia), and between the First and Second World Wars approximately 6 million more followed.

Forced migrations for the slave trade and mass expulsions have been controversial and sensitive topics of human history. Although they have had a significant impact on history and international relations, these accounts have been much less mentioned and recorded. The most massive slave migrations were conducted by European slave traders operating in Africa from the 16th to the 19th century. There are debatable figures of nearly 20 million slaves being consigned to the Americas during this time. As they were transported by sea, countless died during the voyages across the Atlantic.

The most significant mass expulsions were carried out by Nazi Germany, which deported 7–8 million people during the Second World War (1939–45). Meanwhile, the Soviet Union expelled 9–10 million ethnic Germans from Eastern Europe into Germany in the closing year of the war.

Since the Second World War, the most significant migration has involved groups from developing countries moving to the industrialized nations. Some 13 million migrants became permanent residents of Western Europe between the 1960s and the 1980s, and more than 10 million permanent immigrants were admitted legally to the United States in that same period.

Coming a little eastwards from Europe, there was another massive expulsion count in South Asia. It is believed that nearly 14 million people migrated during the partition of British India into India and Pakistan in the late 1940s. Most of the migrations in the second half of the 20th century were refugees fleeing wars. In the 1980s, 3–4 million people fled Afghanistan.

Other than that, we see that the dominant trend in internal migration during the 20th century was the movement from rural to urban areas. As a consequence, urban growth since the Second World War has naturally been rapid in much of the world, particularly in developing countries. But even if we look at the World Wars, we see that numerous inventions and research developments took place at that time.

## Japan’s reconstruction and the gift of TQM

Japan’s rapid growth from the 1950s to the 1980s is credited to the adoption by the Japanese industry of Deming’s quality management principles, which produce unprecedented levels of productivity and quality.

Japanese industrialists took to Deming’s teachings with religious fervour and helped transform Japan into an industrial superpower by the 1980s, beginning to outperform American and European industries. Even so, Deming and his work remained unrecognized in the country of his birth.

In 1980, an NBC TV documentary titled ‘If Japan can, why can’t we?’ showed how and why Japan’s industry was beating that of the US and featured Deming’s central contribution to Japan’s revival. The documentary not only popularized Deming’s work in the US, it also helped kick-start a quality control movement in that country. Then in 1982, Deming published his famed quality management philosophy based on 14 principles of management in the book *Quality, Productivity, and Competitive Position*.

In 1984, the United States Navy adopted Deming’s teachings to improve its operational effectiveness. The Navy termed the initiative ‘Total Quality Management’ (TQM) a year later. Soon, the US federal government adopted the TQM principles, with the private sector following to regain its position as the world’s most competitive industry.

TQM also led to the augmented development in the USA of robotics and automation, which was invented in the US but taken to widespread use in Japan during the reconstruction period.

## Cognitive computation: surpassing human capability

Humans have been using devices to compute since the Stone Age and will continue to do so far into the future. Prayer beads and tally sticks made of wood and animal bones were the earliest counting devices found to be in use around 20,000 BC in the Upper Paleolithic era. The abacus, a more compact form of a calculating tool, was being used to teach arithmetic in Babylonia around 2700 BC.
