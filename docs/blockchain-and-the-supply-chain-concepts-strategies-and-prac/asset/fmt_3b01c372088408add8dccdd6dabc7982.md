
# Escaping the Gravitational Pull of Motherships

Escaping the gravitational pull of three Deliver motherships (PoR, ABN-AMRO and Samsung SDS) turned out to be challenging and consumed a disproportional amount of resources that could have been put to better use. As explained below, the Deliver project was managed by a steering committee; however, each of the leads had its own mothership ‘shadow’ steering committee to manage. With ABN-AMRO and Samsung SDS gone, that gravitational pull was reduced dramatically. Not only was there now only one mothership left to contend with, but most positions were filled by Blocklab; although a 100 per cent subsidiary of the Port of Rotterdam, it operates at arm’s length of the port and can make decisions autonomously within the budget, provided agreed targets are met.

Based on our experiences within Blocklab we’re now restructuring our approach towards venture building, by making the transition from laboratory project to venture earlier. To start with, this requires identification of the proof of value at the earliest possible stage of the project. Especially for ventures that fall outside the usual line of business, such as is the case for digitization projects for a port authority, establishing a ‘dollars-and-cents’ business case can be challenging, especially in the beginning, as there’s often no base case to fall back on. A simple rule of thumb we have used successfully at Blocklab is to find co-creators willing to invest in kind or in cash as a first validation of the business case. We might think that a certain project is the best invention since sliced bread, but if we cannot convince one or more other companies to put in their resources, it is probably not such a good idea. Also, with time comes knowledge and experience, which allows Blocklab to faster identify pockets of value and their corresponding business case.

Now, asking a corporate to ‘just give us the money and get out of the way’ is not much of a strategy. Also, without the PoR, Blocklab would be nowhere near as successful and Deliver’s founding members’ long-term vision, agenda, brand and network proved extremely valuable early on in the project. However, as the project gains momentum, it is no longer the big corporate logo on the business card that should get you invited to pitch the product, but the product itself! Establishing an own identity for the project can be done in many ways but setting it up as a separate entity with its own management team and budget, and running it like any other start-up, agile, without corporate overhead and skin-in-the-game, gives the best chance to escape the gravitational pull of the motherships. Ideally you want to have already a management team that has done the trick from start-up to scale-up before and covers all the roles, but in most cases a full-time CTO, CCO, CEO and COO for a start-up is overkill. A venture studio, staffed with people who have done the trick before and who can mentor the ideator and the first employees of the start-up, help with finding external funding, grow the business and further build the team, will increase the chances of success.

## If You Give a Boy a Hammer . . .

Blockchain isn’t the solution to all the world’s problems. In effect, when we take a closer look at the architecture of Naviporta (see ‘The key Naviporta components’) we see that off-chain components and the `Google Cloud Platform (GCP)` play an equally important role in delivering the required functionality. In general, a blockchain transaction requires more processing power than, say, writing a transaction to a database and is therefore more expensive. When designing Naviporta we looked consciously at what transactions required blockchain notarization and which didn’t. Notarization of the ownership of a negotiable electronic bill of lading makes sense as such an asset can represent millions of dollars in value, and the additional cost of notarization pales in comparison. However, notarization of each individual read-out from an `Internet-of-Things (IoT)` device on board a shipping container is, in most cases, not needed.

# Naviporta’s Whitepaper

At the time of writing the second edition of the book (December 2021), the Naviporta whitepaper was still a work in progress. While this may seem odd as most blockchain whitepapers are written prior to the launch of the protocol, we reasoned that writing a whitepaper earlier would have resulted in a theoretical piece that would have glossed over some of the challenges associated with setting up a consortium blockchain, such as unique selling points (USPs), governance and initial use cases. Furthermore, it would also have been very much PoR’s view on the Naviporta infrastructure. In 2021 we engaged with several port authorities in Asia-Pacific, Australia, Africa and Europe to validate our findings and tighten up the content. This is expected to continue throughout the first quarter of 2022. Therefore, the following paragraphs represent Naviporta’s existing views on several key aspects normally found in a whitepaper, such as vision, core values and governance.

## Vision

In recent years, various digital initiatives emerged on the market that try to solve inefficiencies in today’s global supply chains. Key ports such as Singapore (Wragg, 2021) and Qingdao (GSBN, 2021) are investing in a blockchain-based digital infrastructure, and companies such as freight forwarders, large carriers (eg Maersk and COSCO) and large e-commerce companies (eg Amazon and Alibaba) are building physical and digital infrastructures to support end-to-end supply chains.

A digital infrastructure that takes a cross-industry approach to optimization of global supply chains and has interoperability, neutrality and data sovereignty as its core values will be a powerful instrument to alleviate the many issues supply chains now face as highlighted in [Chapter 2](#05_chapter02.xhtml#ch02_sec_001). This digital infrastructure, utilizing blockchain technology, acts as a common frame of reference over the many siloed systems of record that exist nowadays in global supply chains. As such, it would constitute a foundational infrastructure on which third-party developers and other ecosystems deploy their digital services. This foundational infrastructure acts as a digital highway and is open and accessible for all participants, guaranteeing a level playing field for any actor in global supply chains, taking into account regulatory compliance, eg `Know Your Customer (KYC)` and `Anti Money Laundering (AML)` regulation. A wide range of digital services will be offered by various third parties, providing value to end-users (consumers), leveraging the infrastructure to connect data from various services and flows and hence removing inefficiencies and manual handovers. Besides access to all necessary services to facilitate end-to-end supply chain processes, underlying assets and data must be re-usable, eliminating the necessity to recreate and validate the same data at every step in the process, further reducing inefficiencies and data inaccuracies and automating supply chain processes for all parties in the supply chain. The assets and data should all be digital, minimizing the physical paper flow.
