
# Is Ethereum perfect?

The short answer is ‘no, it isn’t!’. But we found ways around its imperfections without having to make changes at a client level. Two of the imperfections deserve some more attention.

First, the Ethereum mainnet is often criticized for its low scalability, with transactions per second between 15 and 20 TPS, while large centralized applications such as Alipay or Mastercard can process hundreds of thousands of transactions per second. One important factor contributing to that is the proof of work consensus algorithm, used by the mainnet. As we choose to have our own chain that, while compatible with the EVM, runs independently from the mainnet, we were able to adopt another consensus algorithm. We choose the Clique proof of authority consensus mechanism (Szilágyi, 2017). Tests have shown that we can scale to 500~1000 TPS while still making use of the existing Google cloud infrastructure. Also, EVM compatible protocols such as Avalanche^([40](#09_chapter06.xhtml#ch06_en40)) and Solana offer scalability in the range of 3000 TPS to 4500 TPS, providing potential alternatives in the case of further scaling being needed.

Second, the increasing value and price volatility of Ethers, and other publicly traded cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, is making transactions very expensive. And although some publicly traded companies do now have Bitcoins on their balance sheet (Bitcoin Magazine, 2021), most corporates today steer clear of utilizing them. By deploying our own chain, we were able to circumvent the use of cryptocurrency as we do not need to reward the public miners for generating a block or performing a transaction. To remain compatible with the EVM we did generate Naviporta coins, but these coins currently have no value and are not publicly traded. Their usage is to keep the existing logic of how transactions are paid for on the Ethereum mainnet intact, to prevent having to make changes to the EVM. Also, the computing power required for running the Clique consensus algorithm is much less than for proof of work or proof of stake protocols. This reduces energy consumption, and further lowers the operating costs for the validators.

We will start with a description of the so-called core components, ie those components that are foundational to the network itself and cannot be replaced by a similar component offered by another third party. Note that this doesn’t mean that these Naviporta components are not interoperable with other similar components, deployed on other infrastructures and networks, it is just that by replacing them with another third-party component the essence of Naviporta as an infrastructure would be subverted. After that we will describe the infrastructure services. These services, while currently being offered by Naviporta, can be replaced by offerings from a third party.

## CORE COMPONENTS

Currently the blockchain network utilizes the Google Cloud Platform (GCP) and the signer nodes are deployed over different geographical regions. Each node contains a full copy of the blockchain ledger. In the previous section we discussed at some length the underlying protocol and the use of the EVM.

We also briefly touched upon the Clique proof of authority consensus algorithm. Such a proof of authority scheme is based on the idea that blocks may only be minted by trusted signers. The responsibility of creating the next block is shared among a set of authorities and is done in a round-robin fashion (Polarsparc, 2020). As more consumers and producers onboard on the infrastructure, more sophisticated and less predictable block validation schedules could be implemented. We are currently examining various technical and non-technical mitigation strategies.

Each block header contains the digital signature of the miner of that block, which allows anyone obtaining a block to verify it against a list of authorized signers (Szilágyi, 2017). New or obsolete signers can be added or removed to maintain a dynamic, up-to-date list of signers. While we are aware of some of the limitations of the proof of authority consensus, we believe that by the way we have organized the governance of the Naviporta chain, the decision-making power is distributed over a geographically dispersed and neutral group of network participants, ie ports. The criteria for becoming a consortium member are also fully transparent and we actively seek to further expand the consortium over the next two years to increase its geographical distribution. This increases the difficulty of a coordinated distributed denial of service (DDoS) attack on signers; an often-voiced criticism of proof of authority consortium blockchains.

The core also includes an asset registry that can be used by notaries on the service layer to register assets. The main responsibility of the asset registry is to keep a mapping between assets and their origin and current managing notary. The managing notary is the notary that currently manages the asset on behalf of the owner. This registry serves the following:

1.  Someone that receives an asset can use the registry to find the current managing notary and request meta-data from it through the notary.
2.  It allows the notary that initially registered the asset to be retrieved (proof of origin/existence).
3.  It allows for asset tracking even when it has been transferred to multiple notaries.
4.  It gives Naviporta the possibility to trace back the history of any asset registered on the blockchain ledger in case of problems, bugs, clean-up or a notary producer default.
5.  It can act as a single registration source that producers can use to ensure an asset is only ‘registered/used’ once. For example, a negotiable bill of lading that acts as collateral against debt, such as a letter of credit.

On the service level there can be many notaries that share the same asset registry. These notaries can add specific business logic, such as the transfer of asset ownership when some condition is met, or shared ownership by a consortium of participants, and have a commercial purpose.

The other Naviporta core service is the identity provider. Not only will Naviporta have many parties and users collaborating and consequently providing services on the infrastructure that require authentication and identification, but also machines, for example oracles or IoT. This means that authentication and identifications are very important aspects and offered by the core to avoid any hassle in authenticating each party. Identification and authentication can get very complex very quickly on an infrastructure such as Naviporta. For example, a user representing a certain company creates a shipment in a third-party application, such as QuayConnect (see ‘QuayConnect’) and wants to make use of an eCMR service that is offered by another third party. The latter requires that company and/or user-specific data must be shared between two services that each have their own identity provider. Of course, the user does not want to log in separately on each identity provider. Taking this one step further, the eCMR is stored in a digital vault offered by yet another service provider. To ensure seamless authentication and identification, a single sign-on is required that offers on-behalf-authentication to enable the QuayConnect application that the user has logged into and uses the data received on authentication to request data at the vault on behalf of the user in a secure and traceable manner.
