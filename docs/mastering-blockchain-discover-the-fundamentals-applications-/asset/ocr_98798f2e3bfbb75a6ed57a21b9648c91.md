b) **Private Blockchains:** Private blockchains, also referred to as permissioned blockchains, are restricted networks where participation is limited to specific entities. These entities, often pre-approved participants, possess the authority to validate transactions and access the blockchain. Unlike public blockchains, private blockchains prioritize privacy and confidentiality, making them suitable for industries where data access control is critical, such as healthcare, supply chain management, and financial institutions. Private blockchains may not rely on native cryptocurrencies and can employ consensus mechanisms like Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) or variants of Proof of Stake (PoS) to achieve consensus among participants. c) **Consortium Blockchains:** Consortium blockchains are a hybrid model that combines aspects of both public and private blockchains. In a consortium blockchain, a predefined group of organizations or entities forms a consortium to jointly operate and govern the network. Consortium blockchains offer shared infrastructure where multiple participants collaborate to maintain the blockchain. This type of blockchain enables industries and sectors with multiple stakeholders to work together while maintaining a certain level of control and privacy. Applications of consortium blockchains include trade finance, interbank transactions, and supply chain networks.
