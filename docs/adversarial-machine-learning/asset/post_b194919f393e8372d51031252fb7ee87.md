## LEMMA C.2 The modified \( \tilde{I}(\mathbf{x}) \) score is non-decreasing in \( q_i \) for all tokens (indexed by \( i \)).

**Proof** We show that the derivative of \( \tilde{I}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \) with respect to \( q_k \) is non-negative for all \( k \). By rewriting, Equation (5.3) in terms of \( \tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \) as \( \tilde{S}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = 1 - \chi_{2n}^2 (-2 \log (\tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}))) \), the chain rule can be applied as follows:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial q_k} \tilde{S}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \frac{d}{d\tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})} \left[ 1 - \chi_{2n}^2 (-2 \log (\tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}))) \right] \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial q_k} \tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})
$$

$$
\frac{d}{d\tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})} \left[ 1 - \chi_{2n}^2 (-2 \log (\tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}))) \right] = \frac{1}{(n-1)!} \left( - \log (\tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})) \right)^{n-1}.
$$

The second derivative is non-negative since \( 0 \le \tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \le 1 \). Further, the partial derivative of \( \tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \) with respect to \( q_k \) is simply \( \frac{\partial}{\partial q_k} \tilde{s}_{\mathbf{q}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \prod_{i \neq k: \tilde{x}_i = 1} q_i \ge 0 \). Thus, for all \( k \),

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial q_k} \tilde{S}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \ge 0.
$$

By an analogous derivation, replacing \( q_i \) by \( 1 - q_i \),

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial q_k} \tilde{H}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \le 0.
$$

The final result is then given by

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial q_k} \tilde{I}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial}{\partial q_k} \tilde{S}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) - \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial}{\partial q_k} \tilde{H}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) \ge 0.
$$

However, unlike the simple products, the statistics \( s_{\mathbf{q}}(\cdot) \) and \( h_{\mathbf{q}}(\cdot) \) have unusual behavior because the function \( \delta(\cdot) \) sanitizes the token scores. Namely, \( \delta(\cdot) \) is the indicator function of the set \( \mathbb{T}_{\mathbf{x}} \). Membership in this set is determined by absolute distance of a token's score from the agnostic score of \( \frac{1}{2} \), i.e., by the value \( g_i \triangleq |q_i - \frac{1}{2}| \). The \( i^\text{th} \) token belongs to \( \mathbb{T}_{\mathbf{x}} \) if \( i) \) \( \tilde{x}_i = 1 \) \( ii) \) \( g_i \ge Q \) (by default \( Q = 0.1 \) so all tokens in \( (0.4, 0.6) \) are excluded) and \( iii) \) of the remaining tokens, the token has among the largest \( T \) values of \( g_i \) (by default \( T = 150 \)).

For our purposes, for every message \( \hat{\mathbf{x}} \), there is some value \( \tau_{\mathbf{x}} < \frac{1}{2} \) that defines an interval \( (\frac{1}{2} - \tau_{\mathbf{x}}, \frac{1}{2} + \tau_{\mathbf{x}}) \) to exclude tokens. That is

$$
\delta(\hat{\mathbf{x}})_i = \tilde{x}_i \cdot \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } q_i \in (\frac{1}{2} - \tau_{\mathbf{x}}, \frac{1}{2} + \tau_{\mathbf{x}}) \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}.
$$

Clearly, for tokens in \hat{x}, \delta (\hat{x})_i steps from 1 to 0 and back to 1 as q_i increases. This causes s_q (\hat{x}) to have two discontinuities with respect to q_i: it increases linearly on the intervals [0, \frac{1}{2} - \tau_x] and [\frac{1}{2} + \tau_x, 1], but on the middle interval (\frac{1}{2} - \tau_x, \frac{1}{2} + \tau_x) it jumps discontinuously to its maximum value. This behavior is depicted in Figure C.1. Similarly, h_q (\hat{x}) decreases linearly except on the middle interval (\frac{1}{2} - \tau_x, \frac{1}{2} + \tau_x) where it also jumps to its maximum value. Thus, neither s_q (\hat{x}) nor h_q (\hat{x}) have monotonic behavior on the interval [0, 1].

To better understand how I (\hat{x}) behaves when q_i increases given that neither s_q (\hat{x}) or h_q (\hat{x}) are monotonic, we analyze its behavior on a case by case basis. For this purpose, we refer to the three intervals [0, \frac{1}{2} - \tau_x], (\frac{1}{2} - \tau_x, \frac{1}{2} + \tau_x), and [\frac{1}{2} + \tau_x, 1] as A, B, and C, respectively. Clearly, if q_i increases but stays within the same interval, I (\hat{x}) also increases. This follows from Lemma C.2 and the fact that I (\hat{x}) will not change if q_i remains within interval B. Similarly, I (\hat{x}) also increases if q_i increases from interval A to interval C; this too follows from Lemma C.2. The only cases when I (\hat{x}) may decrease when q_i increases occur when either q_i transitions from interval A to B or q_i transitions from interval B to C, but in these cases, the behavior of I (\hat{x}) depends heavily on the scores for the other tokens in \hat{x} and the value of q_i before it increases as depicted by Figure C.2. It is also worth noting that I (\hat{x}) in fact will never decrease if \hat{x} has more than 150 tokens outside the interval (0.4, 0.6), since in this case increasing q_i either into or out of B also corresponds to either adding or removing a second token score q_j. The effect in this case is that I (\hat{x}) always increases.

The attacks against SpamBayes that we introduced in Section 5.3 ignore the fact that I (\hat{x}) may decrease when increasing some token scores. In this sense, these attacks are not truly optimal. However, determining which set of tokens will optimally increase the overall I ( \cdot ) of a set of future messages { \hat{x} } is a combinatorial problem that seems infeasible for a real-world adversary. Instead, we consider attacks that are optimal for the relaxed version of the problem that incorporates all tokens from \hat{x} in computing I (\hat{x}). Further, in Section 5.5, we show that these approximate techniques are extraordinarily effective against SpamBayes in spite of the fact some non-optimal tokens are included in the attack messages.
