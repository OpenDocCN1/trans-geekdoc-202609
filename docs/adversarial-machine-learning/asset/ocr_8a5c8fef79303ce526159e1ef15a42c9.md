Clearly, for tokens in \hat{x}, \delta (\hat{x})_i steps from 1 to 0 and back to 1 as q_i increases. This causes s_q (\hat{x}) to have two discontinuities with respect to q_i: it increases linearly on the intervals [0, \frac{1}{2} - \tau_x] and [\frac{1}{2} + \tau_x, 1], but on the middle interval (\frac{1}{2} - \tau_x, \frac{1}{2} + \tau_x) it jumps discon- tinuously to its maximum value. This behavior of is depicted in Figure C.1. Similarly, h_q (\hat{x}) decreases linearly except on the middle interval (\frac{1}{2} - \tau_x, \frac{1}{2} + \tau_x) where it also jumps to its maximum value. Thus, neither s_q (\hat{x}) or h_q (\hat{x}) have monotonic behavior on the interval [0, 1].

To better understand how I (\hat{x}) behaves when q_i increases given that neither s_q (\hat{x}) or h_q (\hat{x}) are monotonic, we analyze its behavior on a case by case basis. For this purpose, we refer to the three intervals [0, \frac{1}{2} - \tau_x], (\frac{1}{2} - \tau_x, \frac{1}{2} + \tau_x), and [\frac{1}{2} + \tau_x, 1] as A, B, and C, respectively. Clearly, if q_i increases but stays within the same interval, I (\hat{x}) also increases. This follows from Lemma C.2 and the fact that I (\hat{x}) will not change if q_i remains within interval B. Similarly, I (\hat{x}) also increases if q_i increases from interval A to interval C; this too follows from Lemma C.2. The only cases when I (\hat{x}) may decrease when q_i increases occur when either q_i transitions from interval A to B or q_i transitions from interval B to C, but in these cases, the behavior of I (\hat{x}) depends heavily on the scores for the other tokens in \hat{x} and the value of q_i before it increases as depicted by Figure C.2. It is also worth noting that I (\hat{x}) in fact will never decrease if \hat{x} has more than 150 tokens outside the interval (0.4, 0.6), since in this case increasing q_i either into or out of B also corresponds to either adding or removing a second token score q_j. The effect in this case is that I (\hat{x}) always increases.

The attacks against SpamBayes that we introduced in Section 5.3 ignore the fact that I (\hat{x}) may decrease when increasing some token scores. In this sense, these attacks are not truly optimal. However, determining which set of tokens will optimally increase the overall I ( \cdot ) of a set of future messages { \hat{x} } is a combinatorial problem that seems infeasible for a real-world adversary. Instead, we consider attacks that are optimal for the relaxed version of the problem that incorporates all tokens from \hat{x} in computing I (\hat{x}). Further, in Section 5.5, we show that these approximate techniques are extraordinarily effective against SpamBayes in spite of the fact some non-optimal tokens are included in the attack messages.
