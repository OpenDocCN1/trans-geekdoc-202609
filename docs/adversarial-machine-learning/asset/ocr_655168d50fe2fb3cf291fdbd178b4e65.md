Thus, \( D_N^*(M, \infty) \), the optimal distance achievable for any sequence in the space \( \mathcal{A}^{(M,\infty)} \), is expressed in terms of the problem's parameters \( M \) and \( N \); i.e., if \( \boldsymbol{\alpha}^* \) is an optimal strategy in \( \mathcal{A}^{(M,\infty)} \), then \( D(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*) = D_N^*(M, \infty) \). Similarly for attacks constrained to a finite duration \( T \) in the space of sequences \( \mathcal{A}^{(M,T)} \), we define the optimal achievable distance to be \( D_N^* (M, T) \), which we return to in Section 4.4.

# ## 4.2.3 Characteristics of Attack Sequences

To better understand our problem, we characterize its properties and those of (optimal) attack sequences. These properties provide the foundation for the further analysis of the problem.

# ### 4.2.3.1 Invariance to Empty Attack Iterations

We discuss the behavior of the attack distance \( D(\boldsymbol{\cdot}) \) from Equation 4.11 with respect to zero elements in the attack sequence. First we show that the attack distance \( D(\boldsymbol{\cdot}) \) is invariant to the insertion of a zero at the \( k^\text{th} \) position in the sequence (with \( k > 1 \) following Remark 4.9).

LEMMA 4.11 For any \( k > 1 \), every sequence \( \boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \mathcal{A}^{(M,\infty)} \) achieves an identical distance as the sequence \( \boldsymbol{\alpha}' \) defined as

```
\(\alpha'_t = \begin{cases} \alpha_t, & \text{if } t < k \\ 0, & \text{if } t = k \\ \alpha_{t-1}, & \text{if } t > k \end{cases}\)
```

i.e., \( D(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = D(\boldsymbol{\alpha}') \).

Proof. First, \( \delta_t(\boldsymbol{\alpha}') = \delta_t(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) \) for \( t < k \) since \( \delta_t(\boldsymbol{\cdot}) \) depends only on the first \( t \) elements of the sequence (see Equation 4.12). Second, \( \delta_k(\boldsymbol{\alpha}') = 0 \) from Equation (4.12). Third, \( \delta_t(\boldsymbol{\alpha}') = \delta_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) \) for \( t > k \) since inserting a 0 at the \( k^\text{th} \) position does not affect the denominator \( \mu_t \) in the definition of \( \delta_t(\boldsymbol{\cdot}) \) (see Equations (4.7) and (4.12)) and the numerators are shifted, accordingly. The distance achieved by the sequence \( \boldsymbol{\alpha}' \) is

```
\(\begin{aligned} D(\boldsymbol{\alpha}') &= \sum_{t=1}^{k-1} \delta_t(\boldsymbol{\alpha}') + \delta_k(\boldsymbol{\alpha}') + \sum_{t=k+1} \delta_t(\boldsymbol{\alpha}') \\ &= \sum_{t=1}^{k-1} \delta_t(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) + \sum_{t=k+1} \delta_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = D(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) . \end\alpha}\)
```

From this lemma, it follows that the insertion (or deletion) of zero elements (\( \alpha_t = 0 \)) is irrelevant to the sequence's distance, and therefore all zeros can be removed in considering our notion of optimality. Intuitively, the distance achieved by an attack is not affected by the retraining iterations in which no adversarial data is used since, in this scenario, the adversarial data is the sole source of new data.
