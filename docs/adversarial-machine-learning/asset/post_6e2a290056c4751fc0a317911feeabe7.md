
From the fact that \(\|\mathbf{a}^{(t, \ell)} - \mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}\| \le R\) for all attack points, we can use the generalized triangle inequality to obtain our first bound:

```
\(\|\mathbf{r}_t\| = \frac{1}{R \cdot \mu_t} \left\| \sum_{\ell=1}^{\alpha_t} (\mathbf{a}^{(t, \ell)} - \mathbf{c}^{(t-1)}) \right\| \le \frac{\alpha_t}{\mu_t} \le 1,\)
```

since \(\alpha_t \le \mu_t\). This leads to the following theorem and a general (albeit, weak) bound on the effort required by the adversary:

**THEOREM 4.6** *The total relative displacement between* \(\mathbf{c}^{(T)}\) *and* \(\mathbf{c}^{(0)}\) *in T retraining iterations has a norm of at most T (i.e.,* \(\|\mathbf{D}_T\| \le T\)*) and a displacement alignment of* \(\rho(\mathbf{D}_T) \le T\). *Therefore, to achieve the desired total relative displacement of* \(D_R\)*, a successful attack must have at least* \(T \ge D_R\) *attack iterations.*

*Proof.* The bound on the norm follows from the generalized triangle inequality and the fact that for all \(t\), \(\|\mathbf{r}_t\| \le 1\). Similarly, the bound on \(\rho(\cdot)\) follows from the following application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

```
\(\begin{aligned} \rho\left(\mathbf{D}_T\right) = \mathbf{D}_T^\top \frac{\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}}{\|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\|} &= \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\|} \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbf{r}_t^\top \left(\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\right) \\ \le \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\|} \sum_{t=1}^T \|\mathbf{r}_t\| \|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\| \le T \end{aligned}\)
```

Ultimately, the attacker's goal is create a sequence of attack points (i.e., a set of attack points \(\mathbb{A}^{(t)} = (\mathbf{a}^{(t, \ell)})_{\ell=1}^{\alpha_t}\) at each attack iteration) such that the attacker's goal is satisfied. The following theorem states that the attacker can accomplish this in a greedy fashion by placing all its attack points at the point where the current hypersphere boundary intersects the line segment between the current centroid \(\mathbf{c}^{(t-1)}\) and its goal point \(\mathbf{x}^4\). Further, it shows that, when this greedy strategy is executed at every iteration, the resulting centroid at the \(t^{th}\) iteration follows the line segment between the initial centroid \(\mathbf{c}^{(t-1)}\) and the attacker's goal point \(\mathbf{x}^4\), gradually shifting toward its goal.

**THEOREM 4.7** *For every attack sequence* \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_t \in \mathfrak{N}_0)\) *and for all* \(t \in \mathfrak{N}\)*, at the* \(t^{th}\) *iteration the set of attack vectors* \(\mathbb{A}^{(t)}\) *that optimize* \(\rho(\cdot)\) *according to Equation (4.5) consists of* \(\alpha_t\) *copies of the vector* \(\mathbf{c}^{(t-1)} + R \cdot \frac{\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}}{\|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\|}\) *and*

```
\(\mathbf{c}^{(t)} = \mathbf{c}^{(0)} + R \cdot \frac{\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}}{\|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\|} \cdot \sum_{\ell=1}^t \frac{\alpha_\ell}{\mu_\ell}\)
```

where \(\mu_t\) is the cumulative sum of mass for the attack given by Equation (4.7).

*Proof.* The proof appears in Appendix B.1.

This theorem shows that the optimal centroid at the \(t^{th}\) iteration can be computed in a greedy fashion only from the supplied parameters \(\mathbf{c}^{(0)}\), \(R\), \(\mathbf{x}^4\), and \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\).

COROLLARY 4.8 For every attack sequence \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_t \in \mathfrak{N}_0)\) and for all \(T \in \mathfrak{N}\), the total relative displacement achieved by an optimal attack following the attack sequence \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) after \(T\) iterations is \(\mathbf{D}_T = \frac{\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}}{\|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\|} \cdot \sum_{\ell=1}^T \frac{\alpha_\ell}{\mu_\ell}\), which achieves a displacement alignment (Equation 4.5) of

```
\(\rho (\mathbf{D}_T) = \sum_{\ell=1}^T \frac{\alpha_\ell}{\mu_\ell}\)
```

where \(\mu_t\) is the cumulative sum of mass for the attack given by Equation (4.7) and \(D_R\) is a parameter of the attack given by Equation (4.1).

Proof. The result for \(\mathbf{D}_T\) follows directly from substituting the optimal centroid given by Equation (4.9) into Equation (4.4). The resulting displacement alignment follows from \(\|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\|^2 = (\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)})^\top (\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)})\) and Equation 4.1.

Importantly, under our assumptions, this theorem shows that the attacker’s objective depends solely on the sequence \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_t)_{t=1}^T\); the actual attack vectors follow directly from its specification. The attacker can choose the elements of \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\); i.e., the number of attack points to employ at each iteration. Hence, we have reduced a multidimensional optimization problem to an optimization over a single sequence. In the next section, we formalize how the attacker can optimize this sequence based on the attack objective given in Equation (4.10).

However, before continuing, note that Equation (4.10) shows that the success of an attack at time \(t\) can be described solely as a function of this attack sequence. Moreover, since the optimal displacement vector \(\mathbf{D}_T\) is a scalar multiple of the desired attack direction, \(\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\), its projection onto that direction has no residual component, and Remark 4.3 shows us that the progress of this attack is given by

```
\(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(t)}\|}{R} = \frac{\|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(0)}\|}{R} - \sum_{\ell=1}^T \frac{\alpha_\ell}{\mu_\ell}.\)
```

The success of this attack in minimizing \(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}^4 - \mathbf{c}^{(t)}\|}{R}\) is determined completely by the attacker’s choice of \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\), which it chooses so as to maximize \(\sum_{\ell=1}^T \frac{\alpha_\ell}{\mu_\ell}\). We now proceed to formalize this setting by describing these attack sequences.

Remark 4.9 (Nontrivial Initial Attack) The astute reader will have noticed that the above results, including all derivations from Equation (4.8) onward, rely on the assumption that \(\forall t \in \{1, \dots, T\}, \mu_t > 0\). Equivalently, this requires that \(\alpha_1 > 0\), which we assume throughout the remainder of this chapter—the nontrivial initial attack assumption. In fact, if the first nonzero attack occurs at the \(k^{\text{th}}\) iteration (i.e., \(\alpha_t = 0\) for \(t < k\) and \(\alpha_k > 0\)), the first \(k-1\) iterations can be discarded from the attack, since there is no adversarial impact on the classifier during this period. Further, the attack sequence of all zeros, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \mathbf{0}\), is the trivial attack sequence and need not be considered.

## 4.2.2 Formal Description of the Attack

Having described the problem and the assumptions made under it, a formal analysis can be conducted to reveal optimal attack strategies. This formal analysis begins with a formalization of the problem setting and of the objective. We refer to the number of *attack points* used at the \( t^\text{th} \) retraining iteration as \( \alpha_t \) and the optimal (under prescribed conditions) number of attack points to be used at the \( t^\text{th} \) iteration as \( \alpha_t^* \). We use \( \aleph \) to denote the natural numbers 1, 2, 3, ..., \( \aleph_0 \) to denote the non-negative integers 0, 1, 2, 3, ..., and \( \Re_{0+} \) to denote the non-negative reals. Unless specifically mentioned otherwise, \( \alpha_t, \alpha_t^* \in \aleph_0 \) although later in the text we consider sequences in the non-negative reals, which we differentiate notationally by using \( \beta_t, \beta_t^* \in \Re_{0+} \), respectively.

Along with \( \alpha_t \) and \( \beta_t \), we also define the space of possible attack sequences. Formally, we define \( \mathcal{A} \) to be the space of all legitimate sequence of attack points; that is, \( \mathcal{A} = \{ \left( \alpha_t \right)_{t=1} \mid \forall t \, \alpha_t \in \aleph_0 \} \) (in this space, any attack of a finite span is represented by concatenating an infinite trailing sequence of zeros). Similarly, we define the space of attacks of finite duration and limited size as \( \mathcal{A}^{(M,T)} = \left\{ \left( \alpha_t \right)_{t=1}^T \mid \forall t \, \alpha_t \in \aleph_0 \, \wedge \, \sum_{t=1}^T \alpha_t \le M \right\} \); the space of attacks of a finite duration, \( T \), but unlimited size as \( \mathcal{A}^{(\infty,T)} \); and the space of attacks of a limited total size, \( M \), but unlimited duration as \( \mathcal{A}^{(M,\infty)} \). Finally, the analogous continuous versions of these spaces are denoted by \( \mathcal{B}, \mathcal{B}^{(\infty,\infty)}, \mathcal{B}^{(M,\infty)} \), and \( \mathcal{B}^{(M,T)} \) and defined by replacing \( \alpha_t \) with \( \beta_t \in \Re_{0+} \) in the corresponding definitions of \( \mathcal{A} \).

With the notion of an attack sequence, we now formalize the notion of optimal strategies by reexamining the objective of the attacker. The attacker wishes to maximize the displacement alignment \( \rho \left( \, \cdot \, \right) \) as described in Definition 4.2, which was shown in Corollary 4.8 to depend solely on the attack sequence. The objective function is defined with respect to a given attack sequence \( \boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \mathcal{A} \) according to Equation (4.10) as

\( D \left( \boldsymbol{\alpha} \right) = \sum_{t=1} \frac{\alpha_t}{\mu_t} = \sum_{t=1} \delta_t \left( \boldsymbol{\alpha} \right) \quad \text{(4.11)}\)

\( \delta_t \left( \boldsymbol{\alpha} \right) = \frac{\alpha_t}{\mu_t}, \quad \text{(4.12)}\)

where \( \mu_t = N + \sum_{\ell=1}^t \alpha_\ell \) from Equation (4.7) and the function \( \delta_t \left( \cdot \right) \) assesses the *contribution* due to the \( t^\text{th} \) iteration of the attack, which depends on only the first \( t \) elements of the attack sequence. The goal of the attacker is to maximize this objective function \( D \left( \cdot \right) \) with respect to constraints on the size and duration of the attack.

DEFINITION 4.10 *Optimality*: An attack sequence \( \boldsymbol{\alpha}^* \in \mathcal{A}^{(M,\infty)} \) against a hypersphere with \( N \) initial non-attack points is an optimal strategy that uses a total of \( M \) attack points if \( \forall \boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \mathcal{A}^{(M,\infty)}, \quad D \left( \boldsymbol{\alpha} \right) \le D \left( \boldsymbol{\alpha}^* \right) \). The optimal distance achieved by such a sequence is denoted by \( D_N^* \left( M, \infty \right) \), where \( \infty \) here represents the infinite attack duration. This optimality can be achieved for the attacker by solving the following program for \( \boldsymbol{\alpha}^* \):

\( \boldsymbol{\alpha}^* \in \underset{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{\text{argmax}} \, D \left( \boldsymbol{\alpha} \right) \quad \text{(4.13)}\)
\(\text{s.t.} \quad \boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \mathcal{A}^{(M,\infty)}\)
