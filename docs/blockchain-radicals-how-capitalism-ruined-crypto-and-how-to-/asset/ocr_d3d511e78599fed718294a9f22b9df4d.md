Your private key acts as the username and password to access the account online which you never share with anyone, and your IBAN (similar to an account and routing number if you're in the United States) that can be shared with anyone so that they can send money without needing to know anything about your username and password. This also means that people's identities on the network are a random string of numbers and letters, making them pseudo-anonymous and giving some layer of theoretical privacy protection. Because of the properties of hashes previously, it is effectively impossible to know someone's private key to access their bitcoin by just knowing their public address, making it a very secure mechanism for storing accounts.

On the Bitcoin network, and most other blockchains, in order to make a transaction, you “sign” it with your private key. This means that you prove you are the owner of the public address (sometimes referred to as a wallet) that holds the bitcoin or cryptocurrency by proving you are the owner of the associated private key. As long as you are able to prove you know the private key, you are able to access the funds held in the wallet. There are important differences with this type of system compared to normal online banking, however. For one, if you forget your username or password for your online banks, it's not difficult to go through the process of requesting a new password. The reason they're able to do this of course is because you have provided them with personal information about yourself that only you should be able to answer to prove that you are. Using public key cryptography in the case of blockchains like Bitcoin, if you lose your private key, you lose access to any of the cryptocurrency that may be in your wallet. There is no customer service phone number to call for the Bitcoin network. This inherently puts a bit more onus on the individual to store the private key in a safe spot where it can be retrieved.
