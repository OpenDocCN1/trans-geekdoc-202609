This may be fine for those who do not have any powerful enemies or fear being discriminated against, but shadow banking in the form of cryptocurrency is the only avenue for those deemed enemies of the state. It is exactly through this mechanism that Wikileaks, Sci-Hub, sex workers in the US and those living in sanctioned countries can have basic financial rights, for better or worse a necessity under our current form of capitalism.

The legality of blockchains will also make it very difficult for lawmakers to try apply regulations on the most decentralised financial protocols. The only way to do so would be to massively increase the surveillance state, something which would, in the long run, hurt progressive movements the most. Regulators are most likely to target centralised entities like CEXs, Tether Ltd and others residing within legal jurisdictions as they will be easier to reach. The applicability of the same regulations on what are essentially immutable smart contracts available to anyone will likely prove to be much more difficult as there aren’t clear centralised choke points to target. You simply can’t force a smart contract to ask its customers to provide personal information so as to comply with KYC, AML and CTF flows.

The obvious answer to surveillance would be for it to be regulated not to hurt the most vulnerable, but there’s little reason to think that this change will come about any time soon, rendering moralistic arguments against the use of cryptocurrency because it represents shadow banking weak. The legitimacy of the struggles being fought by the most vulnerable should not be subsumed into paternalistic calls for legal clarity and submission to surveillance in the name of protecting investors.
