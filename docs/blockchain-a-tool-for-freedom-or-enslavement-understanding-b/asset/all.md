# Introduction

A non-violent revolution is not a program of seizure of power. It is a program of transformation of relationships, ending in a peaceful transfer of power. 

–        M. K. Gandhi

One realizes the power in that statement when one lets it really sink in and contrasts it with the popular idea of a revolution we have been fed—the one with violent protests. Only when you understand the idea of a peaceful revolution will you be able to grasp the telltale signs of the revolution that’s happening silently right around you: the revolution that you are a part of, probably, without even realizing it. That is the blockchain revolution for you!

For the novice, the blockchain may seem like a fancy term that’s too technical and too removed from their daily life. The truth, as you will discover in the coming pages, couldn’t be farther from this notion. Sure, blockchain is a fairly complex technology to understand and execute, but that does not mean the implications don’t reach you and me.

## Blockchain Initiation for the Beginner

Since the emergence of Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies, everyone seems to be talking about blockchains. Since you have picked up this book, I am assuming you have been fascinated by this fancy word usage, and yet have ended up feeling a little lost and obtuse in the technical aspect of this technology. Don’t you worry—we have all been there. It is no secret that blockchain technology can be a little overwhelming for the uninitiated. And, that is the reason why I wish to wipe out the dubious fog from your mind with a simple analogy. No, we won’t be dumbing down the technology in any way. Rather, here, we shall try to understand the underlying principle before we dive into the technical details in the coming chapters. 

Imagine you are planning an office party. Since you are running short on time, you enlist the help of your colleague in delivering the invites to all the departments. There is a teeny-weeny problem though. Your colleague doesn’t like the head of the marketing department, and thus decides on his own to drop that invite you sent. The result is that the marketing department never receives the invite and you are left wondering why they never showed up for the party. Thus, your colleague is the middleman who has created a problem that you hadn’t anticipated. 

Now, imagine an office party blockchain that connects all the office department computers through the internet, even your colleague. Through this blockchain, every time a party invite is sent out, a copy of the invite is saved on each of these computers on the network. So, everyone has the exact same invite. 

Note that, on the blockchain, it would be impossible to know who the invite belongs to. But, when your colleague spots the time and the day of the party on the invite, he can accurately guess the invite belongs to you. He decides to goof around with it again, trying to change the time and venue of the invite. The moment he does that, however, he is cut off from the blockchain as other computers on the network realize that the data is being manipulated. Thus, the invite reaches everyone in just the form that you intended. That is what blockchain technology can do.

Remember that this is quite the simplified version and the actual technology, as you will see later in this book, is much more complex than this. But, as much as I hope that you do not have mean colleagues like the one in our example above, such interference in virtual data transactions isn’t too uncommon. And with more and more of personal data taking the virtual form, blockchain technology carries a special significance for each and every one of us, especially when it comes to privacy and control over our own data being exploited by big tech companies in Silicon Valley and overreaching governments, alike.

## The Panopticon Paradigm

Somewhere in the 18th century an English philosopher, Jeremy Bentham, designed the panopticon prison. This architectural design was meant to bring out desired behavioral changes in the prisoners by utilizing the power of mere observation. This was no innocent observation, mind you, but a sort of watchfulness that kept the prisoners in check. 

Imagine a watch tower in the center surrounded by prison cells. The observer (or the guard, in this case) sits in the tower keeping tabs on all the prisoners. The guard can freely observe the prisoners, but there isn’t a way for the prisoners to watch the guard. The result is that the prisoners can never be sure as to whether they are under the watchful eye of big brother or not. This uncertainty, combined with the fear of retribution, then brings out a peculiar behavior among the inmates. 

Unsure of being watched, the inmates are always following the rules laid down by this omnipresent guard—so much so that this discipline is internalized by the inmates, no longer needing external supervision. Almost sounds sinister, doesn’t it? It is, in a way—even more so when this concept expands beyond the prison structure and applies to something as widespread as the global economy. (Yes, the global economy.) This isn’t just a lesson in philosophy, but an economic phenomenon observed around the globe. 

The panopticon theory has a double-sided implication in the context of blockchain technology. On the one hand, the more powerful countries seem to have created a global panopticon surveilling the developing economies to the extent that the developing economies are evaluated on the basis of the standards of the developed nations. This doesn’t necessarily help the developing nations, inevitably making the rich countries richer and poor countries poorer. 

The blockchain technology, however, flips the panopticon theory on its head. Or shall we say, it emphasizes it all the more? Confused? Well, here it is. The problem with the global panopticon is that the power is centralized in the hands of the omnipotent players who dictate the rules of the game. But, what blockchain technology does is to take this power and decentralize it in a way that all stakeholders can be the watchful guards. Thus, in essence, it diffuses the influence component of the panopticon while proliferating the self-regulation aspect. 

## Makings of a Revolution

It’s no coincidence that the blockchain movement began gaining steam after the 2008 financial crash. This crash made it clearer than ever before that the trust that characterizes most of our financial transactions could dissipate overnight with the rampant corruption and scandals in the financial institutions. But, if the very medium of these financial transactions could not be trusted, then what was the common man to do? 

As the American economy was being gradually consumed by this hopelessness, a ray of hope emerged from the tiniest crack. Peter Kropotkin, a Russian historian, remarked once, "The hopeless don’t revolt, because revolution is an act of hope" (Peter Kropotkin Quote, n.d.). This time the act of hope was carried out by an entity named Satoshi Nakamoto in the form of a white paper that spoke of "trustless trust." 

To this day, we do not know who Nakamoto was, but the paper they published brought out the wonderful idea of a distributed ledger that would eliminate the need for the trust in our transactions altogether, simply through the ultra-transparent module of functioning. Soon, Bitcoin was launched and, since then, the curiosity about blockchains has only risen. This technology challenged the existing systems like never before. It sought to take the power from the powerful and distribute it in a way so as to bring even the most disenfranchised people into the system. What is a revolution, if not this?! 

Ron Paul, the former Congressman and a libertarian pundit, rightly said, "If you sacrifice liberty for security, you will lose both" (Ron Paul Quote, n.d.). The blockchain, more than anything else, is a symbol of this liberty—the liberty that refuses to be bound down by the shackles of invisible powers. We shall discuss the concepts of liberty and libertarianism in detail a little later; but, for now, understand that this revolution isn’t a struggle between the left and the right but a struggle of the people standing firm and united against government overreach, often known as statism. It is a struggle to restore things that matter the most to you in today’s world—your rights, identity, and authenticity of the information which forms the basis of so many of your life decisions. 

As someone who values freedom a great deal, I have come to respect the workings of a blockchain and I hope that you shall, too, as you finish this book. Blockchains have been aptly named as the biggest thing since the internet and have become the most vital weapons in the biggest social, political, and economic revolution of the 21st century. Don’t let the democratic and peaceful nature of this revolution fool you—blockchain technology is changing the future one community, one business, one sector at a time. But, here’s the most important thing you need to remember—the blockchain revolution doesn’t begin with the technology; it begins with you. 

## Why Blockchains?

Now that we have begun the blockchain conversation, a pertinent question in your head might be: why blockchains? What is it about this technology that makes it so unique? More importantly, why hype it as this big revolution? The truth is, and I can't stress this enough, blockchain is the first thing in a long long time that gives liberty and power in the true sense to people like you and me. But, this is not the only thing that draws me to this technology. What is truly remarkable to me is the profound influence it can have on the third-world economies around the globe. 

Whenever I talk about blockchain technology in developing economies, I am given severe frowns. People almost think of me as this first world citizen living in the clouds with no idea of the ground reality in these nations. "These countries don’t even have the basic technology; how do you plan to implement such an advanced one there?" they ask me. 

But, here’s what most people miss out on completely—the financial world of the developed nations and the developing nations are completely different. So, even though people like me may find the credit/debit card and banking systems cumbersome and time-consuming, the services are still present for us. Despite their disadvantages and risks, the majority of the people are still able to make these systems work for them. Though blockchains would result in significant improvement, the present systems are, at least, available to us. 

However, when we talk about third-world economies, most often the transactions tend to be devoid of trust due to the widespread corruption, as well as crime. So, the systems that are available to the first world citizens rarely fall in the financial spheres of the developing nations. Many of the citizens may not even have banking accounts due to the several socio-economic factors. 

But, what they do have much more often than bank accounts are their smartphones. The use of smartphones familiarizes these communities much more easily to the internet than to traditional banking. This is one of the reasons that mobile money seems to be so popular in countries like Kenya and Tanzania, much more than countries like the United States and the United Kingdom, for instance. Mobile money gives these individuals a way to safely store and carry their money without being constantly worried about theft. Mobile money, as convenient as it may be considered in these parts, still poses quite a few challenges. One of them is the clunky nature of operability in between networks, making the transactions, again, not quite convenient. Imagine the groundbreaking change a blockchain could bring in an environment like this. 

Let me explain what I mean by this. One of my friends narrated this incident to me. He was on a trip to Tanzania and was required to make a few stops before crossing the border to Kenya. He had hired a local cab driver who knew the place well enough, and arranged to pay the man in cash. Everything was going fine, until the driver began sharing the horror stories of theft, robbery, and even violence. Of course, these were horror stories to my friend, but for the driver these were quite the usual occurrences. As my friend held on to the cash in his pocket, it became more and more clear to him the dangers that these people were exposed to on a daily basis for merely carrying their own money. 

My friend’s nightmare was nowhere near over yet, though. As they reached the border, the vehicles had to be changed and my friend had to board a new car that would take him to his destination in Kenya. Now, my friend was lucky to have a kind driver who had arranged for another vehicle, but the transaction still had to be made. So, there was the car, and there were my friend and the driver negotiating a deal in the middle of the street. My friend was very aware of the bundle of cash feeling heavier by the moment in his pocket. There was no way for my friend to verify the identity or the records of this driver, and yet there was no other option that he had. Wouldn’t blockchains offer a much safer and more elegant alternative in a case like this?

Understand that these are not the issues of tourists but the issues that the citizens face more often than not. Blockchains could help create a much better, compassionate world for the people that have never been given the consideration they deserve in the traditional systems. And, that is why I say that this technology could revolutionize the world, and even more so, the third-world nations. This isn’t hype; it’s mere reality. More and more organizations are jumping on the blockchain wagon simply because they realize the impact this technology creates, as we speak. World Mobile is the first blockchain-based mobile network that is executing their motto of “Connecting the Unconnected” fabulously across Africa. We discuss this in more detail in a later section. Humankind is on the precipice of a technological mutiny that’s peaceful and non-violent. You ought to know how blockchains are changing the world for you and those around you.

Now that we have gone over the basic principle of blockchain technology, it’s time to dig deeper into the nitty-gritty of the technology itself. Without a doubt, however simply we may attempt to describe the technical aspect here, the actual working of a blockchain is a complex process. So, one fine day, around a year back, I was at my bank resolving an account situation and chatting up an old friend when I overheard this young man who was exasperated about the time it was taking for the overseas money transfer that he had made to his family back in India. ## The Philosophical Underpinnings

As mentioned earlier, the blockchain saga began in 2008 when a mysterious paper was published by a person (or a group of people) under the pseudonym of Satoshi Nakamoto. But, to what end? Why was the need felt to publish a paper that introduced a whole new system, when the well-oiled machines of traditional financial systems were already in place? But wait, was it really well-oiled, though? Turns out, it wasn’t. The financial crash brought to light some pretty serious concerns that plagued our financial world: one of them being the fraudulent nature of the so-called gatekeepers of our financial security—the banks. And mind you, these fraudulent banks were backed by both the left- and the right-wing political parties for their own vested interests. Too many people lost their life savings and were pushed to the edge of bankruptcy. 

This is when the discontent that was simmering under the surface all along came to the fore with full force. It wasn’t just that the blockchain paper was written, but more significant was the curiosity it created in the many sectors through the economy. This was majorly because this paper talked of a technology that could bypass the fraudulent financial institutions altogether. 

This was a never-before-thought-of idea because, until now, it was believed that these institutions were the apex bodies and no transactions could occur without their approval. Now, here was a group of people who rubbished this notion completely and talked of creating a parallel currency, the famous Bitcoin. This currency was thought to exist in a world where there was no state authority. Though we, till date, do not know who Satoshi Nakamoto was, we do this: Whoever invented cryptocurrency, and even those who supported it, were not just any people. These were professional coders, who knew the ins and outs of the system. In fact, the first description of the idea was proposed long before the internet boom came about. Stuart Haber and W. Scott Stornetta had already put forth this idea of generating time-stamps for digital documents so that they could not be back-dated. These, and many other cryptographers like them, didn't believe in centralized governance running interference. But, would the human race ever be able to function without the external force of authority? 

### Liberty Through Spontaneous Order

This might surprise you, but humans don’t need an external jurisdiction in order to live lives that are oriented towards general wellbeing. Don’t take my word for it but it is an idea that’s at least 2,500 years old. All those years ago, a Chinese philosopher, Zhuang Zhou, proposed the exact same thing. ## How Blockchains Work

Now that we know the underlying principle, let’s start understanding the process in a little more depth. Because of their origin, blockchains are, quite often, wrongly used interchangeably with Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies. While it cannot be denied that cryptocurrencies are the most widely known application of blockchains, it is critical to note that they are just one of the several. 

Blockchain is essentially a network where records can be stored. This hack-proof system makes data forgery next to impossible. So, rather than recording the data in only one place, it is stored in multiple places at the same time—which means that even if one source may be manipulated, the copies of the record keep the data from being corrupted. 

When you think of a database, you likely think of something similar to an Excel spreadsheet complete with rows and columns. Now, blockchains are slightly more complex than that. Previously known as chains of blocks, they store data not in rows and columns, but in blocks. These blocks become digitally connected and, as more and more transactions occur and more data is generated, blocks keep on adding to the digital chain. Think of a cabinet drawer: once the drawer is full with files, you close it and move to the next one. Similarly, in blockchains, the blocks can only hold a certain amount of data. Once this limit is reached, they are closed and linked to the previous block. 

The crucial difference between the cabinet analogy and the blockchain, however, is that in the case of the former you can always go back and shuffle the files around. In the case of the latter, as data continues to be stored in blocks, these blocks create an irreversible timeline which means that going back and changing the details of the records is impossible. Each block receives the exact unique time stamp when it becomes a part of the digital chain.

Blockchains use peer-to-peer networks to allow for the recording and distribution of digital information without giving the notorious elements the opportunity to edit or duplicate the data in any way. Simply put, this technology does not believe that secrecy equals security of data; instead, it makes the data perfectly transparent and puts it right in the view of everyone on the network, thereby eliminating the possibility of malpractice, altogether. 

To understand the importance of this system, we need to first look at how it differs from traditional transactions. Again, the impact is much more apparent in third-world countries. Blockchains tend to digitize the transactions which are tremendously regulated and, ironically, still reeking of corruption. It is said that when the Afghan soldiers started receiving their salaries through the digital medium, they actually thought they had received a raise in their pay. It turns out there was no raise; they were only receiving what was rightfully their own, only now their superiors didn’t have the opportunity to skim off their own cuts from the salaries of the subordinates—hence, the increase in the amount received. If mere digitization can bring that kind of a financial change, imagine what a fully transparent blockchain system could do, in this context.

### Networked Integrity

In the child and candy analogy, if you notice, the outcome depends on the integrity of that one child. Similarly, our traditional systems usually expect integrity to be placed within a person rather than the process itself. We expect our politicians to stay true to their word, and our bankers to be honest with the money transactions without necessarily having the right accountability system in place. Please note that I am in no way attempting to shift the blame away from corrupt individuals. The problem is that we are always living in uncertainty of the outcome. And by the time that the coin flips the other way, it’s too late to do anything about it

Answer this question with brutal honesty: If I were to tell you that you’d never be caught, would you still do the right thing? The way we, as a society, have chosen to regulate this is by imposing external rules when, in fact, the much more effective means would be to introduce the idea of networked integrity. This means that the transparency and integrity of one’s behavior is not determined by the ambiguous territory of moral values, but is incentivized by the very structure of the system. 

Think how it would be to live in a close-knit tribe, for instance. Let’s say, you live with this tribe on an island with limited food resources. One day you find the perfect kill in the form of a huge delicious fish. The whole tribe assesses this kill and puts you in charge of making sure it remains safe until they return from the hunt. In this case, you will have two options: one is that you keep the fish safe until the tribe returns and then divide it equally among the members, and the other is that you gobble up some of it and make up a story for what happened. It is very likely that, no matter how much you are starving, you’ll still choose the first option. 

The reason for this is simple—the tribe is built in such a way that their trust means a lot more to you than the immediate gratification of your hunger. If you were to lose that trust, there is a high chance that you could be outcast and left to fend for yourself. Thus, cheating would be prevented at two levels. Firstly, since the whole tribe has seen the catch, it’d require quite a bit of effort to come up with a story. Secondly, even if you did come up with one, the tribe members would never be able to see you in the same manner that they did before. All in all, sticking to the tribal code would be a much easier option. 

That is precisely what a blockchain does to the virtual data through its principle of networked integrity. Cheating, hacking, and manipulation are either made impossible or make the individual pay a heavy price, thereby minimizing the motivation to indulge in any of these practices. 

This principle of networked integrity finds its most useful implication for the problem of double-spend. Have you ever wondered why it’s possible for you to click pictures of food on your phone and send it to multiple friends, but you can’t do the same thing with your digital money? It’s obviously because money and pictures aren’t the same thing. While you can exchange whatever you wish for money, I hardly think you can do the same with your food pictures. Well, it seems like a silly question to even ask, I know. But this seems to be at the root of a huge problem with online transactions. 

As is obvious, digital currency cannot be in two places at the same time. Thus, the money would have to leave your account and go into the account of the one you have paid it to. In the case of double-spending, the digital token file that represents your digital currency may be falsified—the result being that the same token can be used twice. This works out perfectly well for the notorious elements trying to defraud the system, but can incur great losses for the party receiving these tokens. This is usually why we have third-party members like banks monitoring the digital transactions. But, as much as we may hate it, these third-party members can be equally fraudulent in nature. 

Blockchains, with their networked integrity, make this duplication impossible while also making the third-party monitoring redundant. For instance, in the case of cryptocurrencies, this is done through a consensus mechanism where everyone on the blockchain reaches the necessary agreement regarding a particular transaction with the exact cryptocurrency token. This transaction has a time stamp which cannot be duplicated. This solves the double-spend problem in a big way.

If you are wondering what ensures that this group of peers stay honest in their activities on the blockchain, then remember that all of these peers have an equal stake in the smooth functioning of the system. I am sure you have been on one of those group projects where there is one member that invariably slacks off and yet scores the same based on the group performance? What if these school projects evaluated the performance not of the group, but of the individuals themselves and how they contributed to the success or failure of the group output? This would incentivize those already working hard to improve further, knowing that their hard work pays off into meaningful grades and those not working at all to pull up their socks. 

The blockchain more or less employs the same logic with the peers involved. The fact that every individual’s contribution is important in the functioning of the blockchain, and that the peers receive their dues only if this smooth functioning continues, keeps everyone involved on their toes. This is significant, especially, in those parts of the world where major institutions have been consumed by corruption and cannot be trusted to verify the transactions. As blockchains rely on not one, but many sources of validation, this problem can be avoided to a great extent. However, it is important to note that the networked integrity cannot be allowed to run on autopilot. If that happens, then it’d be quite easy for the nefarious sections to take over a majority in the network, thereby tremendously compromising the very integrity that sets blockchains apart. ### Security

It would suffice to note that cryptographic code enables us to keep the data secure by ensuring that only the intended recipient of the data can read it. In case of cryptocurrency, for instance, the purchase and sale of the digital tokens happens with these cryptographic keys which the individual uses to access the relevant information, complete the transaction and get out. ### Common Terminology

When reading about blockchains, you’ll frequently come across certain words that may seem like Latin and Greek. But, doesn’t every child feel the same before learning the alphabet? So, here we go, learning the alphabet that makes up the blockchain world.

First off, what we need to understand is that a blockchain is a network made of several nodes. Nodes may be compared to small servers relaying data back and forth on a network. The presence of these multiple nodes is what ensures transparency, and you’ll see how that works in just a while.

![](OEBPS/Images/image00156.jpeg)

With that basic framework out of the way, let’s start with the block in the blockchain, first and foremost. These blocks have some characteristic traits and aspects that would be useful for our reference. The first block in the chain is aptly named the genesis block. Think of this as the ground zero for a blockchain; this is where it all begins. As the transaction data increases, it is bundled together in this block and then filled up to its capacity. The capacity may differ, based on the type of blockchain application. Once the genesis block is filled, the next block comes up and the transactions are recorded there. The major difference between the genesis block and the following blocks is that the latter all have a reference to the prior blocks; since the genesis block is the first one, there is obviously no prior reference. 

The blocks have three aspects which are of special significance from the perspective of the end users of blockchains. One of them is certainly the time stamp. This time stamp is decided based on the time of the transaction received from all the nodes on the blockchain. So, imagine a blockchain that’s spread across the United States, Australia, Canada, and India. Since all of these are in different time zones, the blockchain application would adjust for time from all of these nodes to get a median time stamp for the transaction. Since these time stamps are corroborated by multiple nodes, it becomes simply impossible to manipulate record keeping. 

The second aspect is the hash that’s generated with each block. Hashing is quite a mathematical process that takes varying lengths of the strings input from transaction data, and converts it into an output of a fixed length. The hash provides the transaction with added security due to the extremely mathematical algorithmic nature of the whole process. That, indeed, makes it much more difficult for the malicious hackers to tamper with the data itself. The data, thus, reaches the precise intended recipient, making manipulation that much harder. 

![](OEBPS/Images/image00157.jpeg)

Rather than go into the algorithmic detail of this hash function, I would like to talk to you about what it means to you as an end user, without ever realizing. Every block of a blockchain has a hash of the previous block embedded within its header. The brilliance of this technology truly lies in the simplicity of the logic used. What this chained embedding of previous hashes does is that it makes it practically impossible to manipulate one block without manipulating the others. And, manipulating data on that large a scale whereby all the blocks are manipulated is immensely unlikely. 

![](OEBPS/Images/image00158.jpeg)

If someone does find the massive intellectual capacity to do it, it would still be a mammoth financial undertaking to gain the physical resources to get it done. Remember the point we made earlier—cheating is either extremely difficult or extremely expensive, or both, in the case of blockchains. The hash function is one of the things that make it so. The hash function is also what makes digital signatures, that we are all familiar with, possible.

The third and last aspect that makes blockchains is what is known as nonce—a number used only once. This is a number that’s assigned to every hash of every block. This is the number that a miner would have to discover to begin the process of mining. Since finding this number isn't all that easy, nonce almost acts like a screening test to weed out the less capable miners. 

To understand this, we’ll have to dig a little deeper into the mining process. Mining is what earns the much needed rewards for those operating the nodes of the blockchains and incentivizes them to keep the integrity of the network intact. Think of these miners as auditors who are paid to keep the transaction data neat and clean. If you have the slightest interest in blockchain technology, mining is a term you have surely heard of. And, the traditional usage of this word obviously brings up the imagery of hot, humid, and dark underground tunnels housing the hardworking laborers. The truth is, blockchain miners are just as hardworking, but the difference is they can do it from the comforts of their homes as long as they have some high-power CPUs and a lot of in-depth knowledge of the mathematical computations. 

In simple words, mining is used to refer to the process of validating the transactions on blockchains. Let’s say, there are two stacks of $100 bills. One of them is legitimate, while the other is counterfeit. Since no one has checked which one is which, both the stacks continue to be used in transactions. Now, those who are fortunate enough to get the legitimate bills will face no problems, but those who have counterfeit ones run the risk of being caught unawares. However, what happens in blockchains is a little different. There are a few people here who can check the validity of those bills. These auditors take the time to look at both the stacks, verify their serial numbers, and separate the counterfeit bills, thereby stopping them from getting into circulation. For these auditors to continue to look at such counterfeit bills, however, they would need to get some kind of rewards. 

This is exactly what happens in a blockchain, but at a much more complex level. Remember that the transactions on blockchains are encrypted, and for these to be validated they would, of course, have to be decrypted. Decrypting this data is no simple task. Mining is equivalent to solving puzzles of quite an advanced difficulty level requiring massive computational resources, human effort, and intelligence. These miners then come to a consensus about the validity of these transactions. 

There is also another alternative to the above method. While what I describe above refers to proof of work (PoW). There is also the proof of stake (PoS) method, which is much more feasible for someone who wishes to mine blockchains on, let’s say, a home PC. So, if you have been thinking that blockchains and mining are out of reach for the common folks like you and me, then that’s not the case at all. Anyone and everyone with a computer can have a blockchain-based business from home producing regular blocks of data. Let’s see how this is possible.

### Consensus Mechanisms

The strength of blockchain technology lies in its decentralized nature. Consensus mechanisms have a lot to do with this strength. Depending on the area of application, the type of consensus mechanism a blockchain would employ would be one of the three used frequently—proof of work (PoW), proof of stake (PoS), and delegated proof of stake (DPoS). Though there are multiple other mechanisms that have been developed, these three seem to be the most popular.

●     Proof of Work: What we discussed in the last section is termed as PoW. This is where a miner has to prove that he has worked towards decryption and validation. The difficulty of the mathematical puzzles increases as the blocks are being mined faster. If the miner reaches the puzzle solution quicker than the others, they are rewarded the block—which acts as an incentive to work more efficiently.

They are the most expensive of all types of consensus mechanisms for the simple reason that they utilize large amounts of resources: human as well as technological. Though this is the type of consensus that was originally desired by Satoshi, it has been coming under fire often due to the sheer energy that it utilizes, and hence the development and evolution of other mechanisms.

●     Proof of stake: In PoW mechanism, every miner has an equal opportunity to solve the puzzle and find the block. Here though, the miner who has the most number of tokens gets to be the validator for block transactions. These validators can stake their tokens and, on the basis of who has had these tokens the longest or has the most staked tokens, are randomly assigned the opportunity to create the next block. These validators are then rewarded with either the transaction fees or cryptocurrency tokens. This consensus mechanism is much more energy efficient, compared to the PoW consensus. 

●     Delegated proof of stake: This is quite the democratic version of the PoS mechanism. Here, the users with stake (in the form of tokens) have the power to vote for the delegates who would get the chance to produce the next blocks. The weightage given to every vote depends on the proportion of stake those users hold. Besides being among the fastest consensus mechanisms, it also brings in a unique digital democracy to the world of blockchains.

As mentioned above, these aren’t the only consensus mechanisms out there, but they sure are the most popular. When you take a close look, you’ll ascertain that no matter how highly we talk of blockchain technology, consensus mechanisms, and the potential for security, none of it would mean a thing if people didn’t take charge. When you look at the consensus mechanisms, especially, it isn’t too hard to notice that this protocol, if given in the hands of powerful institutions, fails the point of blockchains, altogether. You might wonder, why then is this technology any different and/or revolutionary? The truth is, blockchains give people like you and me the opportunity to participate like the traditional systems never have. And, it’s up to us to make the most of this opportunity.

### The Game Theory of Decision-Making

Another aspect of this technology that we just cannot skip over is the decision-making model in this widely distributed system. While consensus mechanism is how the nodes reach a decision, why they should feel incentivized to reach there is a completely different ball game. Why, for instance, should I keep the good of everyone on the system above my own good? Why not cheat my way through it to get the best output for myself? The peers that share the network are not from another planet and are bound to share the flaws that we, as a human race, share—greed, corruption, all of it. But, the whole point of blockchains from the get-go has been to create a decentralized system where the flaws of one do not become the weakness of all. Game theory explains this perfectly. 

Game theory is nothing but the strategic decision-making model employed on blockchains that mathematically predicts the success of all strategic options available to the node. Let me try to explain this through something called "prisoners’ dilemma." Let’s say two individuals, Pete and Tom, are arrested for shoplifting. There are no other witnesses to the crime, and the case against them can be held only with a confession. Logically, there is a definite series of outcomes that can be expected in this. 

1.  One of them may confess and the other may deny the charges. 
2.  Both of them may deny the charges.
3.  Both of them may confess to the charges.

The best possible scenario in this case would be for both of them to flat out deny the charges. The worst that can happen, in that case, is they might be fined a minimal amount or receive scolding from the police officers. It’s simple, isn’t it? But, here’s the dilemma—since they are being interrogated separately, neither can be sure that the other person will deny, too. If, for instance, Pete denies and Tom testifies to become the witness, Pete will get the maximum punishment while Tom will be out free without any punishment at all. Now, this can feel like a tempting option for Tom because he gets to keep his record clean. But, in reality, this is a worse outcome; because Pete is getting a bigger punishment, he may be fined and sent to jail for five days. Go a step further and imagine that Pete also thinks the same way and rats Tom out. Tom and Pete have thus both betrayed each other and will now both be punished with fine and jail for five days. This is known as the prisoners’ dilemma, where Tom and Pete both decided to serve their own interests, when in reality it would have been much more beneficial for both to have each others’ backs. 

The game theory model simply puts a numerical value to the probability of outcomes here:

 
|   || Pete Testifies || Pete Doesn’t Testify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tom Testifies  || 5, 5 || 0, 5 |
| Tom Doesn’t Testify || 5, 0 || 0, 0 |

So, how does this relate to the game theory of decision-making on blockchains? Remember that blockchains are designed in a way that the system rewards miners only if the transactions are validated by multiple nodes. If the miners are cheating or manipulating data, then it invalidates the blocks and the miner may be ousted. That means that cheating on a blockchain to put one’s selfish interests ahead of the network’s is quite the waste of time. On the contrary, if miners keep the transaction data clean, thereby putting the welfare of the system ahead of their own interests, they can continue to earn substantial rewards. Thus, blockchains inherently encourage its nodes to do what’s best for the group. 

## Common Features of the Technology

The elements mentioned above aren’t just fancy terms, but actually result in the strength of blockchains as we know it. But, it’s not enough to just know the terms and not pay attention to the core features that these individual elements culminate into. Aspects that fulfill all those promises that I made to you about blockchains are distributed ledgers and smart contracts.

### Distributed Ledgers

We have all known the critical nature of ledgers for businesses for a long time. Distributed ledgers take those ledgers to a whole new level. They make decentralization on blockchains possible. The technical details we discussed earlier—complete with nodes, blocks, hash, and nonce—make up the distributed ledgers. These are the databases that are shared, duplicated, and synchronized across blockchains, thus accounting for a uniquely transparent system. Any read on blockchains would be incomplete without distributed ledger technology, often abbreviated as DLT. But, what most people don’t realize is distributed ledgers are not the same as blockchains. While all blockchains require DLT, not all distributed ledgers are in the form of blocks and chains. There are multiple types of DLT. For instance, Tangle is a form of DLT used in Internet of Things, or IoT, applications. 

Due to their ability to record data securely and transparently on multiple nodes without the need for third-party monitoring, these distributed ledgers have the power to drastically reduce transaction and settlement times from days to minutes. Remember the example of the distressed Indian man who wanted to send money urgently to his hometown? This is the part of the blockchain that would allow him to do it with absolute ease. These ledgers also eliminate the need to reconcile records from various sources, thereby reducing both time and effort.

As you can see, DLT is a truly remarkable innovation; but, for this to fulfill its potential for an impactful implementation, it needs the help of what are known as smart contracts. 

### Smart Contracts

Smart contracts can be thought of as programs on a blockchain that are designed to run only if and when certain prior criteria are met. Think of a combination locker. Only when the combination of the numbers is perfect will it be unlocked. Let’s say you forget even one number—you are locked out. Smart contracts are like that, but the conditions are not numbers but some sort of clauses that would either activate or deactivate the agreement between the involved parties. Again, since the process is automated, there would be no need for an intermediary to oversee the proceedings. 

Let’s look at an example of how smart contracts can make life much easier, especially for disadvantaged groups. It is a well-known fact that land registration and titling systems in Africa can play a significant role in the empowerment of the poor. However, these systems are so strained that the process can be often too tedious to be taken up by, let’s say, a poor uneducated farmer. To top this, the trust in legal institutions is likely to have suffered due to the corruption that’s rampant in the offices. Smart contracts act like a simple solution—the various steps in the registration process—like checking for registry of the land in government records, inspection of the property, and so on—can be written into the code of these blockchains, thereby minimizing the chance for errors, as well as corruption. 

The communities that are frequently disrupted by natural disasters (like hurricanes, earthquakes, etc.) can also live an anxiety-free life as they don’t need to constantly worry about the safety of their records. For example, in 2010 in Haiti, an earthquake destroyed the homes and government archives alike, leaving millions homeless physically, as well as legally. Something like this can be avoided with smart contracts on blockchains. Since the records on the blockchains are stored as multiple copies throughout the world, they can claim their rightful ownership to properties despite the catastrophic loss. 

## Types of Blockchains

A Yahoo!Finance report in February 2022 mentioned that Google CEO Sundar Pichai is "looking to help build Web3, blockchain products." If this conglomerate is catching on, then you can be sure that blockchains have arrived. But, what is this Web3? Before we get into it, let’s look at the 1 and 2 versions, shall we? After its explosive entry in 2008, blockchain technology has undergone an evolution of its own. Some would say the technology was ahead of its time and, though it was appreciated, we are seeing its implementation only in recent years. That’s probably the reason why the times we are living in today are referred to by some as the blockchain renaissance, if you will.

There’s often talk of blockchain 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0. The first generation, blockchain 1.0 was synonymous with Bitcoin. This was introduced by Satoshi and was used exclusively for cryptocurrencies. Thus, all the cryptographic currencies would fall into this category. Subsequently though, innovators began to realize the infinite potential the blockchain technology held within. If cryptocurrencies could be exchanged without external regulation and interference, there could also be smart contracts that could be implemented within these ledgers. Thus was born blockchain 2.0. But, blockchain evolution was far from over, rather it shall continue for a long time to come. Blockchain 3.0 was when this technology began to come out of its nascent stage to realize its true potential in industries and a multitude of sectors, which could not have been imagined before, like governance and healthcare. 

As would be expected, blockchains have undergone drastic change not only in the implementation, but also in its core structure. From this change emerge the four prevalent types of blockchains in use at present: 

1.  Public blockchains
2.  Private blockchains
3.  Consortium blockchains
4.  Hybrid blockchains

The type of the blockchain used will depend on the industry using it and also on the benefits it aims at deriving from it. Let’s try to understand these up close.

### Public Blockchains

The most famous type of a public blockchain would have to be Bitcoin. By public, it simply means that anyone can join the blockchain without requiring any permission from the prior members. This is why it’s also called a permissionless ledger. This blockchain typically requires a cryptocurrency as a form of token and follows the consensus methods that we discussed earlier. For instance, Bitcoin uses the Proof of Work consensus mechanism. 

On the one hand, these provide the highest form of decentralization among all four. Since there are no barriers to entry, common people can easily be accommodated on the network of nodes. All of these are likely to feel an equal sense of involvement in the functioning of such a blockchain setup. But, on the other hand, these are also heavy on the environment because of the energy intensive protocols. Moreover, not every blockchain application requires cryptocurrency. 

Another issue frequently encountered with this type of blockchains is lack of scalability. This means that these blockchains cannot support bulk transactions. It is this issue that has impeded these kinds of blockchains, especially in terms of cryptocurrency, from reaching their full potential. For instance, the number of transactions that Bitcoin can handle per second are significantly lower than those handled by its counterpart: VISA. A technique known as sharding is often used to work around this issue. So, the bigger blockchain is essentially broken down into smaller chunks, known as shards. These shards can then be processed by multiple nodes at the same time. Though this may seem like a brilliantly simple solution, the truth is incorporating such shards into the blockchain architecture is a complex task. This is why other forms of blockchains may be preferred at times, even though public blockchain platforms offer the highest decentralization possible. 

But, despite the disadvantages, public blockchains have shown great promise in bringing change in what may be seen as especially disadvantaged parts of the world. Cardano public blockchain is an example of such blockchains. This one is completely decentralized and fully open-source. This blockchain has even tried to work around the resource-intensive task by implementing the Ouroboros protocol for consensus mechanism. This belongs to the proof of stake consensus family and effectively solves the problem of requiring an increasing amount of energy to validate transactions—at least to a great degree, if not completely. This energy-efficient protocol has made it a great fit for building a blockchain-based society, as well as economy, in some parts of Africa. 

Cardano makes use of something known as stake pool operators (SPOs). These are people who are trusted with the setting up and running of the stake pool. On the Cardano blockchain, ADA is a digital currency which acts like a stake in the Cardano blockchain. A stake pool is a node on the Cardano blockchain that stores the stakes of various users. Stake pool operators, in return, are individuals that are entrusted with maintaining these stake pools, which they do by owning or renting a server. This is quite like putting money in the bank and getting back some interest. When you hold a stake in Cardano, a return of around five percent can be achieved, which is distributed among the user and the operator. What’s even more interesting is that many of these stake pools are mission driven, which means that a portion of the returns also goes to charity. Take for example the BIO pool—which is a community of ADA holders that utilize the pool income for global environmental and species protection programs. Those who delegate the ADA to this organization can also vote for which projects they would like to see executed. Thus, even saving the environment happens in a democratic manner. Another instance is SHIFT pool that works for a similar cause. Stop Monkey Shocks is one more mission driven pool that aims to stop the electrocution of monkeys due to the electrical wires for the pathways. The causes may be different, but the kind intent supported by technology is changing the ground reality one project at a time. If that’s not giving back, I don’t know what is. 

But, also remember that Cardano is not the only public blockchain that does this. Other examples that come to mind are Algorand and Harmony blockchains. I am quite certain more and more of these need to be encouraged and supported by the masses and are certainly coming up, even as we speak.

### Private Blockchains

A private blockchain, as the name suggests, allows only certain authorized individuals the access to the blockchain. This may be more suited for private businesses. The access can be gained only through a valid invitation, and the members who are allowed access need to be approved by the blockchain operators. This approval is ideally done by strict adherence to some preset criteria about the background of the individual. Even the members that are in the blockchain may have different rights and privileges. So, one person may have full access without any restrictions, whereas another may not. An additional way that private blockchains differ prominently from public blockchains is that, while the latter allows the transactions to be completely anonymous, the former does not. 

A big advantage that private blockchains carry over public ones is the transaction speed. Since the number of participants on a private blockchain are quite limited, the consensus is reached much quicker. However, as you may have guessed, private blockchains are not decentralized in the true sense, but only make use of a distributed ledger to keep multiple copies of the data in several nodes. Hyperledger and Ripple (XRP) are the most common examples of private blockchains.

When you look at these from an implementation point of view, it is clear that private blockchains cannot be used for wide community-based programs, but are only applicable to business organizations looking for a better way to organize their data. But, even there, the user would have to be vigilant because, while the small numbers give a much better transaction speed, they also make these blockchains quite susceptible to attacks from external malicious elements. Trust in transactions may also be another issue whereby the credibility of the nodes and their validation of the transactions may be open to ambiguity. 

### Consortium Blockchains

Another option that private business users may have is the blockchain that tries to bring together, at least partially, the goodness of public blockchains to the private permissioned ones. Consortium blockchains are quite similar to private blockchains, with one major difference. In this type of blockchain the participants belong not only to one organization, as in the case of private blockchains, but the participants on the network may come from several organizations. This makes hacking into the database that much more difficult. These also offer a tad higher level of decentralization due to the presence of multiple players. 

### Hybrid Blockchains

These add another layer of sophistication to the permissioned blockchains. Though they are private to an extent, the records on these blockchains can be made available to third parties, whenever necessary, through smart contracts. An example of hybrid blockchains would be Dragonchain, released by Disney. Yes, you read that right. Disney released this open-source platform way back in October 2016 (Knowles, 2017). This should tell you how prevalent blockchains are—rather, they have been for a while now. Although, now is the turning point, where this technology is not only being used to manage businesses but to transform communities. 

Another really cool feature here is that, even though these blockchains may be owned by private entities, they cannot make changes to the data, as in the case of private and consortium blockchains where network operators have the right to override the transaction data. The access granted to members is also equal and complete without restrictions on certain selected members. Even the identity of the users on this blockchain are kept secure until they participate in a transaction, in which case it would have to be divulged. To a great extent, hybrid blockchains can be thought of as bringing network integrity, transparency, and security to private blockchain structures. 

One thing that’s clear from all of the things we have discussed in this chapter is that blockchains are still evolving, and there is a wide scope for both growth and improvement of public, as well as private, blockchains. The decentralized immutability of data that blockchains offer, especially in the public sector, can help the struggling economies stand firmly on their own feet by eliminating corruption and faster settlements. You may differ on the choice of blockchain based on your area of application, but you cannot deny the life that blockchains have breathed into the underprivileged sections of society.

## Free Market Capitalism Vs. Crony Capitalism

We have often seen that, in the left-right noise of the political parties, the issue often gets lost; the result is almost always the suffering of the masses. While some elements are forever blaming capitalism for all evils in the society, others are too busy covering the backs of the corrupt capitalists indulging in luxuries, at the cost of the electorate. Since both sides are interested in upholding their own political and financial agendas, neither are really interested in what lies behind this excessively oversimplified narrative.

In 1776, Adam Smith, often called the father of modern economics, published The Wealth of Nations where he discussed what would be a contradictory topic for years to come: the theory of self-interest. Smith argued that free trade as predicated by the liberty to produce and exchange goods, without any restrictions, would drive for more prosperity by kicking in the feeling of self-interest. Many seem to misunderstand this concept to mean pure greed, but this is far from the truth.

Okay, consider this—what motivated you to go to school? What makes you hold onto your job, business, or some sort of stable income? There could be as many responses to this as there are individuals, but at the core of all of those reasons is self-interest. That’s some form of personal gain, or at least an assurance of helping you with that personal gain in the future. In An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, Smith describes it thus, “It is not from the benevolence (kindness) of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest” (Sanderson, 2008). In other words, one would say there is no such thing as a selfless good deed. As humans we are wired to think of ourselves before anything and everything else. Now, understand that this doesn’t equate to selfishness because we don’t necessarily seek this self-interest at the expense of others’ well-being. In a nutshell, the game theory that we discussed earlier is what makes these transactions possible.

How does any of this fit into the economical understanding of the markets, you ask? Well, it’s simple, indeed—when individuals are left to their own means, people produce and consume goods in an economy that serves their self-interest. As a seller wishes to earn money, they would be more likely to produce the goods that buyers would want to buy, and would produce a good quality, at that. On the other hand buyers, because they are interested in saving money for good quality items, would approach the sellers that satisfy these conditions. As a result, both sides benefit and, moreover, the whole market system functions in a positive environment. 

That’s not where the story ends, though. A crucial aspect of free markets is the competition. The obvious problem with this self-interest notion for the smooth functioning of the markets may seem like it’s too good to be true. For instance, wouldn’t this lead to corruption through blatantly inflated prices? It would, if not for competition. The fact that there are multiple sellers fighting for the attention of the buyers means that none of them could step over the line and exploit the buyers. This came to be known as the Invisible Hand Theory—the complementary forces of self-interest and competition. 

Despite the theoretical value of both these components, a market that runs all by itself still seems impossible to many. Many people look at what has been happening the last few years in the country and jump to the premise that capitalism doesn’t work. This is a fallacy, right from the beginning to the end. The reality is that the customer is the king and that is the only reason businesses, no matter how big and powerful, do everything to get customers to use their products. Without this there would really be no business, no generation of money, and no economics for us to analyze. In essence, the consumer holds the key to the direction that the economy takes. Take the example of an initiative by a company called \[Re\]Waste in the city of Edmonton, Canada. The founder, Corey Saban, after pitching a plastic recycling project to the city of Beaumont, has now landed major contracts with two companies in the cannabis industry. This shift is shaped not just by the ideas of the top management but, to a large extent, by the consumers in this industry. According to a survey of more than 15,000 consumers, 86% of the consumers under 45 years of age said that they would pay more for sustainable packaging. When consumers’ demand patterns change, the businesses have no option but to oblige by changing their products. That’s the beauty of a free market.

I know what you are thinking, “But, this is just a small company, isn’t it?! What about the all-powerful, big corporations?” The truth is these big corporations, too, are not all-powerful as we may assume them to be. Take the pizza experiment of McDonald’s in the 1990s. Even though McDonald’s has been a huge brand for a long time, the fact that consumers didn’t want to wait around for their food at a fast-food chain resulted in massive losses for the franchise company to the point that they eventually shut down the pizza ovens. Even in recent times, whenever the company gets too comfortable with its success, the consumers have aptly given the well-timed reality checks. Imagine a fast-food company giving the consumers healthy choices to keep up with the demands of the healthy millennial lifestyle—that is what consumer demand has done till now and will continue to do in future, as well!

Don’t get me wrong, I am under no illusion that capitalism in its current form is doing anyone any good. It’s obviously as dysfunctional as its socialist opponents. And, this is why we need to understand the difference between free market capitalism and crony capitalism. Free market capitalism, in itself, means giving power to the buyers and sellers; whereas crony capitalism gives it to a select section of cronies or big business houses that may be in the good graces of the government. We have seen this lobbyist crony capitalism take an ugly turn across the globe, right from China to Russia and even the United States, in the last few years. 

In many of these parts, this crony capitalism, mixed with communism, is wreaking havoc on the common man in the most subtle and gradual manner. An ideology that started with outlining the evils of capitalism eventually walked the same path, but in a very different manner. Take the example of the Naxalite movement in India—the constant war between crony capitalists and the communist militant group has only resulted in the bloodshed of the innocent, powerless villagers. And, this is just one example of how ugly it has gotten on both sides of the spectrum. 

Most people don’t realize, at all, that this is nothing like free-market capitalism. The absence of government interference, explicit as well as implicit, makes all the difference here. For a long time, people have been swung to and fro between these extreme ideologies, with no other option but to be played like pawns. For the first time in history, we have a third option. Blockchains can help us facilitate a truly free-market economy as it was always meant to be. But, the common masses have to move fast because, if we don’t, then we risk blockchains being overtaken by the very ideologies that we are trying to escape. 

## A Game of (Market) Monopoly

Greed is an inseparable part of the human condition. Socrates said, “He who is not contented with what he has, would not be contented with what he would like to have” (Socrates Quotes, n.d.). Despite this wisdom haven’t we all been found to always want just a little more—that one more bite of cake, one more minute of blissful sleep, one more promotion, etc.? We have all been greedy in our own way. Let’s face it—evil is not an uncommon thing among humankind. We all have a dark side and we all slip up. But, what happens when this little individual greed turns into an organizational one, impacting millions of lives?

Market monopolies are arguably the evilest manifestation of centralization that takes power away from people. Monopolies allow for organizational giants to influence the product prices to such an extent that they can restrict the entry of new players into the market. Markets work because they are a place of fair exchange, but monopolistic markets take that away by replacing competition with complacency. That does nothing but make the ridiculously rich even richer, while leaving the poor completely out of the equation.

Take the example of the Eskom debacle in South Africa. The power company has been a monopoly in the power sector for almost a century, but the cracks in this shining facade can no more be hidden. For a long time, the institution was quoted as a shining example of how a marriage between governments and corporations was in the best interests of the economy. Many believe that the end began its down-the-hill journey when it changed from Escom to Eskom. Of course this, as described by Frederic Bastiat in his age-old works, would be the perfect example of the logical fallacy of judging a situation by its short term results rather than the long-term consequences. 

In a nutshell, the monopolistic structure of this organization was doomed to fail right from the beginning. When you apply the principles of the Austrian school of economics, (which we will discuss in detail a little further in this chapter), you will understand exactly what I mean. Austrian economics is clear on the importance of price in the economy. Being a state-controlled monopoly with no competition, Eskom was destined to fail right from the get-go because it could never adjust prices according to the demand-supply patterns. 

Many believe that giving greater power to policing mechanisms like the judiciary and the governments can solve the issue of monopolistic markets that run at the expense of people. That, as has been evidenced multiple times and even in the Eskom case study, is not true. To understand what works in eradicating monopolies from the market, we’d have to understand what leads to the formation of these monopolies.

## Patents Vs. Open-Source Technology

Bill Gates has an interesting take on patents. He says, “If people had understood how patents would be granted when most of today’s ideas were invented, and had taken out patents, the industry would be at a complete standstill today” (Lee, 2007). If the bitcoin creators were to patent the cryptocurrency and blockchain technology, they would be swimming in a pool of money and we wouldn’t even be here talking about this. Conflicting views on patents have been around almost as long as patents have. Different people may take up different positions on this but what no one can deny is patents take up tremendous amounts of resources (that may, let’s face it, well be used for something much better, though the pro-patent individuals don’t agree). It’s useful to understand patents, especially because they may lead us to unravel the mystery of monopolies. 

The patent vs. open-source debate has come to light prominently during the recent decades as the exposure to technology and the drastic improvement it can bring in people’s lives has increased. Intellectual property has become a landmine that can blow up in your face if you misstep even slightly. So, which side of the debate do I get off on, you ask? Well, as you may have guessed by now, I am in favor of everything that increases our freedoms and innovation, and shall always oppose anything that restricts it. And, you may have also guessed where my inclination lies. But, just picking sides won’t help us here; we need to understand the role that each of these play in the context of a monopolistic market.

Patent is a fickle word the entrepreneurs and innovators are made to suffer by the system. They are said to be for the protection of the invention so that only the inventor, and no one else, can use, sell, or modify the invention—be it a product or a process—for a predetermined period. Anyone else encroaching on the inventor’s territory is punishable by law. Despite Gates’ quote, all big tech companies in Silicon Valley that produce your phones, gadgets, and whatnot are filing, and having patent suits filed against them, every single day. 

It’s true that, theoretically, patents give the inventor solid protection and ensure that the inventor gets their dues for all the efforts they have put in. However, reality might surprise many of these investors seeking these patents. The system is so broken that even the intended recipients of this benefit might not find it helpful. This is because of what are known as “patent trolls.” These people acquire patents without any real intention to develop a product. When an inventor does produce something, they find themselves caught in this trap where they have to fight to legally obtain the rights to their own products. Understand that litigation is no simple task; it takes a lot of money, time and effort. Now, who do you think has the resources needed for such drawn-out litigation—a big business house already making millions or an individual with leaner means? It is the unfortunate truth that, many times, the individual investor gets squashed out in the competition, getting either the minimum dues or none at all. What’s more, the monopolistic business houses do not just survive, but thrive.

Many would consider this a pessimistic view of the world—patents have also benefited individual inventors, after all? Yes, by no means is what I described above a blanket statement determining every case. And, thus comes my next argument. Let’s say the inventor dodges the tricky legalities and gets the patents they are looking for. While patents may be really good for the individual seeking them, they do much harm to the industry as a whole. In 2014, Tesla Motors released all of its patents for electric cars. This has acted as a major boost for the electric car industry, and the eco-friendly innovation we have seen in the last seven years in terms of technology, as well as infrastructure, is staggering. Would this have been possible if the company had scores of lawyers protecting the patents for this technology? No, at least it would not for a long time to come. 

On the other hand, consider the impact that patents can have on access to medicines in African countries. A company that may be producing a vital medicine can prevent other companies from manufacturing it, thereby critically limiting the availability of the product in the market. The company can charge any price that they wish, further limiting the access of the medicine to the poor masses. Though these patents on intellectual property of these medicines are regulated by the Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS) agreement by the World Trade Organization, there is still a lot of conflict in the area on the ground.

Patents tend to not only promote self-interest but greed, too, wherein monopolizing a certain part of the market is promoted. Especially in the field of technology, patents only slow our economic progress as a whole. Don’t get me wrong, as I said I do understand the benefits that patents carry, especially for the inventor; but, looking at the big picture, they take a lot more than they give. It is this discontent that has given rise to the open-source movement. This movement has put high quality software and technology in the public space where anyone and everyone has the liberty to use it. Creators and inventors who put welfare before profits simply come together to create something that resolves a particular need of the people and then make the resolution public, free to be used. Github is one such platform that brings together millions of developers to contribute to the open-source community. 

Simply put, the open-source movement is turning the copyright law on its head and its doing wonders for the world, especially countries that do not have the financial resources for technological innovation. In other words, the patent approach wants to protect profits over innovation and open-source allows for protection of innovation over profits. Remember the big picture here—it’s not just about profits and business. Look at all of this in the context of the free market ideology, better still, in the context of the centralization vs. decentralization debate we have been having all along. Understanding the cogs and wheels in that debate is essential to make sense of how, and at what levels, centralization has crept into our lives. 

So, what is the relevance of all of this to blockchains? Blockchain technology works on this open-source model, too, as professionals come together and pool in their expertise for the betterment of society. The fact is, regardless of the damage that monopolistic markets do behind the scenes, they still seem to have a reputation that makes it all the more easier for them to continue monopolizing the markets. This, among other factors, acts as a major barrier to entry for a new competitor. With blockchains, the factors of reputation and trust vanish because blockchain transactions promote “trustless trust” anyway. A new entrant that has a good product, for example, will receive good reviews; and, other users can be certain that the reviews (positive and negative) can be trusted and aren’t manipulated or deleted, as may be the case otherwise. Note that some blockchains have also been taking the more protective copyright approach. And, it’s important to steer clear of these, as this protective privatization approach goes against the very tenets of decentralization on blockchains in the true sense.

## The Political Debate

The logical progression of the monopoly conundrum would have to be in the political realm. The one common way monopoly thrives in an economy is the very crony capitalism we discussed earlier. Thus, the discussion on the economic implications in the next chapter would be incomplete without understanding the political ideologies that surround this issue. We have already touched upon the crony capitalism, communism spread of ideologies that we have been exposed to, each parading their own superiority at the detriment of lives they cost. Libertarianism provides a truly elegant solution to this age-old problem.

A few of my fellow political enthusiasts are quick to label libertarianism as an extreme, authoritarian ideology that only promotes a rebellion against the establishment. The truth is something very different. Walter Williams puts the ideology in the context of the government in the most succinct manner possible. He says, “Nothing in our Constitution says that the Government is a grantor of rights. Instead Government is a protector of rights” (Walter E. Williams Quotes, n.d.). Contrary to misdirected popular belief, libertarianism doesn’t nullify the position of the government; rather, it places the government in a serving position and not a ruling one. I am only one of the many, many libertarians that firmly stand by the idea that the constitution was essentially anti-centralization, rather than the propaganda that we are made to believe—that it gives all powers to the government.

Libertarianism may be a tricky concept to come to terms with, especially in struggling economies. ## Keynesians, Monetarists, and Austrians

Milton Friedman once famously said, “We are all Keynesians now” (Burman, 2007). In order to understand the fine nuances of that statement, we would have to go back and understand this theory, at least briefly. It’s only when we contrast this theory to the Austrian theory that we shall be able to understand what each one means for the future of blockchain technology.

Let’s start at the very beginning. I am sure you have heard the term macroeconomics, and the length of that word is enough to turn right around and make a run for it! In reality, though, macroeconomics would only mean the study of economic behavior at the national level, as opposed to microeconomics: which studies economic behavior at more individual, or group, levels. As you may have guessed, macroeconomic understanding is much more crucial than a microeconomic one. It’s also important to note that the Austrian school of economics doesn’t necessarily subscribe to this division of macro and micro (but more on that in a little while). For now, let’s keep this broad understanding of macro and micro variables in the background of our discussion.

So, let’s discuss macroeconomics! For hundreds of years, economists have put forth different viewpoints about how national economies function, or how they should function, for the most optimum output. George Bernard Shaw once said, in good humor, “If all economists were laid end to end, they would still not reach a conclusion” (George Bernard Shaw Quotes, n.d.). As if to prove him right, several theories have been proposed over time, each one priding itself to be superior; but here, we shall focus on the Keynesian, monetary, and Austrian models to see how they fare in the context of the current global economy. Now, I want you to understand that I don’t pretend to be an economic expert, but I have picked up a few learnings along the way which I believe are paramount to the big-picture understanding of blockchains in our economy.

Though the Keynesian, monetary, and Austrian economic debate has been raging for centuries, the recent Central Bank policies have made it more relevant than ever before. Keynesian economics, founded by John Keynes, looks at aggregate demand as the main focus of an economy. Keynesians strongly advocate government intervention in order to alleviate the impact of recessions, inflation, and other market failures. They believe that when governments alter their financial policies by increasing spending and lowering taxes, they would play a major role in stimulating demand within the masses and encourage them to increase their expenditures, thereby contributing to the growth of the economy. This is generally the way most have understood national economic growth, for ages now.

A very similar perspective is provided by the monetary economic theory, though this school claims to present quite an antagonist view. This view advocates a slightly passive approach. as compared to the Keynesian viewpoint, in that they believe that the government should only control the flow of money into the economy and the rest of the market will automatically fix itself. Thus, they are essentially pointing to the notion that the government doesn’t necessarily have to intervene to push the buyers to buy more, as the Keynesian school of thought suggests, but rather manipulate the money flow and wait for the puzzle pieces to fall together. Think of the economy as a car that’s having problems with starting up—a monetarist driver would focus on filling up the tank more than anything, whereas Keynesians would focus on revving up the engine. 

For an outsider, these two theories represent government activity of some sort, in order to artificially induce market behaviors that seem to ‘fix’ the issue at hand. The third interesting school of thought is Austrian economics. As I mentioned before, the Austrian school of economics does not subscribe to the macro/microeconomic model. Rather, Austrian economists find it strange that individual and group economic behavior would be so far removed from each other. For instance, the economists at the macro level tend to have little-to-no idea how the microeconomic elements of labor, health, etc. fall together. Wouldn’t it make more sense to have a theory that’s consistent over these two levels, and that actually joins the smaller pieces in the bigger picture rather than painfully separating them? 

Austrian economists take a rather Laissez-Faire approach to the market crisis. They swear by minimal government intervention and assume that the markets tend to go through their own life cycles, automatically adjusting themselves to deal with crises. This ideology is based on the core notion of subjective value of an object. By subjective value, they mean that every object would be valued at the subjective needs and wants it satisfies. And, the more easily or scarcely available that object is would determine how efficiently it is used. For example, an affluent family that is well-fed would be more inclined to throw away leftover bread than its poor counterpart that has to be very thrifty when it comes to portion sizes. Thus, where there is scarcity of food, the leftover bread would have much more value and would be much more efficiently used than where there is plenty of food available. 

Since the consumer behavior is directly dependent on the perceived value based on the availability of the product, there is an inherent mechanism that takes care of the demand and supply cycle. But, when an external entity like the state intervenes in this process, the whole balance goes out the window. This has been well exhibited in the unraveling of the Brazilian economy. Over the years, especially since the 2008 crisis, Brazil has seen an increasingly interventionist economic policy. Take the example of the automotive industry where ample subsidies were given by the government, which led to an artificially expanded boom-and-bust cycle with significant negative consequences for the economy, in general. The discontent with this is now leading to the Brazilian discourse taking an Austrian turn. 

There is yet another branch of monetary theory known as Modern Monetary Theory (MMT). One of its proponents, Stephanie Kelton, counters the blurring of macro/microeconomic lines in Austrian thought. She proposes that a household cannot function the same way, economically, as a nation because the household doesn’t have the authority to create money. The assumption regarding power to create money seems to be one of the many core ideological differences between MMT and the Austrian school. This assumption makes all the difference because if Uncle Sam could create more money anytime he wanted then what would it matter how much debt the national economy is in or how much of fiscal deficits the state has?

It’s a tempting notion, isn’t it? Almost too good to be true? If it seems that way, then that’s because it is. Austrian economics counters this Alice-in-Wonderland world by the MMT. If MMT were to be taken at face-value, then all those evils that have haunted nations for centuries, inflation, bankruptcy, and so on, would all be just perfectly imaginary words. The reality, though, is that these aren’t imaginary and have plagued our societies for a long time. The Austrian economics vouch for this by clarifying that MMT policy prescriptions may well lead to a stunted capital structure and an overall economic stagnation. 

## The Fiat Fiasco

Okay, so, we have just taken a tiny crash course in economics, now what? How does that help us in our blockchain discussions? The answer to that question lies in what I like to call the fiat fiasco that holds the economic, political, and blockchain ingredients of our discussion here all together. Yes, for now, it may seem like we are just discussing random topics I fancy, but you’ll soon see how fiat money is the missing link that completes the puzzle. 

Austrian economics takes the bull of the fiat money by its horns. Understand that the premise of fiat money rests on what’s called the central banking system. It’s no secret that one thing MMT sure got right is that the state holds a monopoly over money production, and the central banking system depends on the financial institutions approved by the state to propagate that monopoly. For Austrian economists, however, the fiat, or the paper, money has only one function: to act as a medium of exchange. In fact, they don’t even think fiat money is essential for a well-functioning economy. On the contrary, the Austrian economists strongly believe that fiat money brings in centralized evil that we could very well do without. 

Austrian economists have always supported a medium of exchange that’s backed by commodities. So, something like the gold standard would be a perfect fit for the Austrian viewpoint. From this vantage point, it is easy to see why the fiat money can be problematic. Let’s go back to our favorite 2008 crash example. But first, answer this question: What would you expect to have happened to the banking industry after this crash? It doesn’t take a rocket scientist to guess that the banking sector would more or less collapse as a result of all those bad loans. But, do you know what actually happened? The total assets of banks actually rose by 18.4% in 2010 (Polleit, 2010). This was because the Federal Reserve did exactly what the MMT suggests and supplied the banks with vast amounts of money just to prevent their collapse. 

While this may seem like a feasible option, such injections of expanded money supply can be catastrophic for an economy and can create the very boom-and-bust cycles we referred to earlier. Bringing the fiat economy to an equilibrium is practically impossible because it does not carry an inherent value, at all,, and is considered as money because everyone just accepts it as money. In contrast, when the currency was latched to the gold standard, the government couldn’t just wave the wand and come up with new money; they had to purchase the additional gold for it. Thus, even if the banks made fraudulent transactions, they would ultimately have to line up the claims against the available gold, and the system would eventually correct itself to come back to the equilibrium originally desired. In simple words, fiat money has left our economies to irreparable damage for a long time. 

Since the movement away from the gold standard, the Austrian proponents have been diligently hunting for an alternative that fills the “sound money” void. It turns out, when Nakamoto came up with Bitcoin, this hunt ended unexpectedly and the Austrian proponents had found their pot of gold (pun intended).

## The Sound Money Alternative

Before I talk to you about cryptocurrencies, we need to talk a little more about the concept of sound money as stated by the Austrian economists. As can be seen in the previous section, Austrian economists hate the idea of government-controlled money supply. If you pay the slightest attention to the economic news around the globe, you may be able to see the merit in this theory. For decades and decades, together we have witnessed governments around the world throwing the economies into a disarray by manipulating the money flow. When the chaos manifests into unavoidable degradation of living standards for the common man, the same governments (parties may be different) entice the masses with false promises of a better tomorrow with further manipulation of the already struggling money flow for the sole purpose of gaining votes. 

Think of it as the loop of alcohol addiction one may be caught up in. In the beginning, the alcohol makes him feel light and high. But, as the initial high fades off, what’s left behind is a disoriented body, a guilty conscience and the financial burdens. When the frustration of all these shortcomings climbs, as tempting as it may be to drown oneself in the alcohol again, it is hardly a solution. Getting through the difficult withdrawal symptoms might, indeed, do much more good for the person. 

Essentially, Austrians believe that when the money supply remains constant, even though the immediate consumption might be restricted, the long-term savings would increase. Imagine a world where the value of your currency could increase rather than decrease. If you knew this was to happen, would you want to spend all your money today, or save it for the future where it would fetch you a better value? On the other hand, money whose value decreases over time, as is the case in our current society, leads to a YOLO (You Only Live Once) kind of an attitude where reckless consumerism is encouraged, regardless of the cost. 

From the business perspective, appreciating money would also reduce malinvestments in projects which gives one an illusion of growth only during the times of inflation. If the inflation were to drop, these would become unprofitable at the drop of a hat. To put it simply, investors would only contribute to projects that would be profitable in the long run, rather than falling for the illusion of profits in the immediate future. 

In 1972, a Stanford professor, Walter Mischel, proved with mere marshmallows that children who could delay gratification tended to have healthier, happier, and more successful lives as compared to their counterparts that could not. Austrian economics worked out the same logic for the economies not years, but centuries, back. And yet, here we are trying to reinvent the wheel every chance we get and still come up with more of the wrong solution: unsound money. 

Globally, too, this unsound money has brought to the fore many issues. As it has to go through the hassle of conversion, it gives an implicit power to certain economies with higher conversion rates than others. This has led to multiple trade conflicts, inevitably leading to war and loss. Imagine if there was one global currency that you could use without the restrictions of national borders. This has the potential to root out much of the current global conflict. 

Sound money, then, would need to have the features that counter the evil brought in by unsound fiat money, the most important one of which would be stability through time. Even though sound money appreciates over time, the appreciation is not suddenly brought about by artificial elements. Another characteristic that sound money needs to have, but the current fiat money doesn’t possess, is indestructibility. Cryptocurrency serves these functions effortlessly and is thus a favorite of the contemporary Austrian economists. The feature that’s most attractive to them, however, is that this global currency is a decentralized one with no government interference in the form of restrictive policies. We shall discuss cryptocurrencies in more detail in a while but, for now, it’s necessary to understand the perfect segue that they offer, from the flawed fiat monetary system prevalent in current economies to the sound money theory that is staring us in the face from the future. 

## The Crypto Conundrum

For a long time, the term cryptocurrency remained synonymous with the famous (and infamous) Bitcoin. Somewhere in 2010, a man named Laszlo Hanyecz ran out of cash to pay for a pizza he ordered, and thus, convinced the delivery boy to take 10,000 bitcoins in exchange for the pizza. At that time, these Bitcoins were only worth $40. That was the first recorded Bitcoin transaction, and had only a few enthusiasts diligently following the Bitcoin progress. Both Bitcoin and cryptocurrency, at large, have come a long way since then. Now, not a single day goes by that has no reported news on crypto. 

Recently, a debate has been raging: Can cryptocurrency be adopted by national economies as legal tender? El Salvador has, in fact, named Bitcoin as its official currency, along with the U.S. dollar in September 2021. Multiple arguments have been made both for and against this move. But, what most people don’t realize is that cryptocurrencies have arrived, and you cannot undo them. They are gaining more and more traction, and there is no point trying to stop this movement. What would be much more useful is to spend time deliberating the means of adoption. 

A survey by YouGov shows that 27% of the people in the United States today support the adoption of Bitcoin as legal tender (Fries, 2021). Cryptocurrency, on the whole, seems to be a favorite of the younger section of the population. So, why are cryptocurrencies gaining so much popularity? The most relatable reason might be the convenient absence of all those transaction costs and all those middlemen. In short, all the features we discussed in the context of blockchains would all apply here, too. 

One aspect of cryptocurrencies that we haven’t discussed yet is the limited supply. Some cryptocurrencies, not all, have limited supply through their lifetime. For instance, Bitcoin’s supply is capped at 21 million, which means that all the bitcoins that can be mined are expected to be mined somewhere by early 2024; and, Cardano (ADA) has a limited supply of 45 billion. Once these reach their respective market caps is when we will see the true deflationary effect of these currencies. This simply means that, as time goes by, the value of these would appreciate, rather than depreciate, because there are no more tokens coming in. If it feels similar to the idea of sound money we discussed earlier, that’s because it’s exactly the same. This is one of the reasons that Austrian economists love these cryptocurrencies. 

Today, we are at a point where the user has a buffet of cryptocurrencies at their disposal. And, even within the cryptocurrency universe, we have come to see a lot of conflict and drama. You’d think that, since cryptocurrencies are all based on the solid principled foundation of decentralization, that they would be all on the same page, more or less. Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin calls these people “Bitcoin Maximalists.” Such a maximalist attitude defeats the purpose of distributing the power in the market as much as possible, and centralizes it only in the cryptocurrency of their choice. 

But, Buterin himself hasn’t had a very supportive record when it comes to differences with his own partner and co-founder of Ethereum, Charles Hoskinson. This is the same person who, due to these clashes, has gone on to create his own cryptocurrency (Cardano), which we discussed earlier. Not just Charles but Gavin Woods, the creator of Polkadot, along with six other founding members of Ethereum left the project because of the ideological clashes. The point is, it’s important when investing in cryptocurrencies to understand that not all of them follow the principles of decentralization, nor have deflationary properties. Charles Hoskinson, being a Libertarian himself, has certainly been able to create a blockchain that is the perfect amalgamation of the core principles of liberty. The Cardano founder was also an active participant in the campaign for Ron Paul’s leadership. I mention Cardano over and over but, in my research (and it’s not exhaustive, by any means, considering the vast landscape of cryptocurrencies and blockchains in general), I have found that it is certainly one that's built with those building blocks. 

## Blockchains from the Austrian Economics POV

Many believe the cryptocurrencies to be the only bridge between Austrian economics and the emerging technology of the future. But, this would have to be a limited view of the unfolding situation. While it is, of course, true that cryptocurrencies provide the most direct connection to the school of Austrian economics, blockchain as the big picture technology also doesn’t stray too far from the principles of this school of economic thought.

Somewhere around the late 1980s as the information technology (IT) emerged as the long player, many began to consider its impact on the market structures. The electronic market hypothesis was formulated stating that the IT industry would impact the markets in three ways—by making communications easier and more efficient, by helping the buyers and sellers come in direct contact with each other without the need for middlemen, and lastly by integrating valuable information in a way that helps both the sellers as well as the buyers. The result, it was predicted, would be a much greater market activity. 

It was hoped that the arguments previously made by Austrian economists like Mises and Hayek would finally be incorporated into this new age of markets that was about to boom. These economists had argued that the major problem facing any economy is not scarcity of resources, but the unavailability of relevant information. Now that IT was emerging as a savior, this information could be made available, leading to lesser hierarchies and better coordinated production activities. However, we all know that never happened. With the emergence of IT, big players like Amazon, Google, etc. took over this information and, rather than eliminating hierarchical structures, utilized it to create even more concentrated hierarchies. So, though the overall production has increased, small-time business owners are finding it harder than ever to survive, while the big tech companies are getting richer by the second. 

Today, the followers of Hayek and Mises and other Austrian economists see another opportunity in terms of blockchain technology. If you notice, blockchain technology represents everything Austrian economics stand for, even beyond cryptocurrencies. The most significant area of overlap between the two, though, would have to be their love for decentralization. As has been discussed before, blockchains allow for minimal, if any, interference from government authorities and thus cannot be used as tools for suppression and oppression. 

It is to be noted, however, that this libertarian principle would be upheld if, and only if, common people like you and me are involved with this technology. Big companies like Google, Samsung, etc. are already on the move to adopt blockchains, and if we don’t participate effectively, even this tool of liberty might soon be snatched away from us. 

Since the emergence of Satoshi’s Bitcoin, a multitude of aspects about blockchains has been put under the microscope including their safety, feasibility, adoptability, and so on. But, we need to understand that blockchains are much more than these technical details. They are an embodiment of the very economic and political values that have been defamed over decades, and even suppressed, because masses have been led to believe it’s a total scam. The truth, however, remains that keeping the masses in the dark about this emerging technology, as well as ideology, only helps those in power to attract more and more authority. 

Despite the persuasive nature of this argument, there is little to no understanding of the political and economic consequences of this technology. We may talk about blockchain disruption for as long as it may please us and, yet, the discourse would be pointless to a great extent until we incorporate the political and economic ideologies; like it or not, it is eventually these ideologies that determine whether a particular technology, no matter how revolutionary, will sustain in the long run in a given culture. 

Don’t get me wrong; I am not saying that the political and economic schools of thought we have discussed thus far have a one-way influence on the survival of blockchain technology. They don’t. Ultimately any emerging technology is in a more-or-less continuous loop of its technical, economical, and political feedback. It is impossible to understand one without the others in an appropriate cultural context. Thus, just as our economic and political philosophies can decide whether a technology will sustain its originally intended form, the technology itself also has the power to overthrow ideologies that are not consistent with the running of the systems. While the former places the power in the hands of institutions like the government, the latter can definitely be determined by the participation of the masses in technology. 

This is certainly true of blockchain technology—while most people are wasting their time fighting for or against blockchains, the wheels are already in motion backstage. You can love it, or hate it, but the reality is you cannot escape it. Blockchains are already coming into your life in more than one way. It’d be wise to participate actively in it, not just by gathering knowledge about the technical aspects but by understanding and getting personally involved in the technical, economic, and political context of this technology, as a whole. If you don’t, you might soon find your life hijacked by tech that you barely know anything about. 

Wasn't the internet the same? Not many of us really know or understand what the internet is and how it works, but you (as a user) are caught up in the surface-level leisure implications of it (like social media, for instance). But, underneath all those innocent-looking posts and funny filters, the behemoths in the tech industry are putting the internet to a much more sinister use by prompting you to allow them to access your data every time you log in, and then selling it to the advertisers for profits. Not to mention, the insurmountable power they hold to control free speech, it’s quite obvious that the internet isn’t as sovereign as it should be.

We need to wake up and smell the coffee before it’s too late and the big tech companies gain charge of the emerging blockchain technology. ## Blockchains for Small Players

Till now, we have only looked at blockchains in the context of big players (like Cardano, for instance) and how they are using it for creating the much needed social impact. We will get back to blockchains in Africa and the social impact in the next chapter; but, here, what I want you to remember is that blockchains are not a distant technology. Blockchains are just as much for you and me as they are for the big tech industries, as well as the third world countries.

### Helping the Small Entrepreneurs

Fighting against the big brands has always been a pain point for small businessmen. The accelerating consumerism often has us running behind the commercial brands rather than local businesses. Though multiple initiatives have been taken in the past to boost small businesses, none seem to have had a lasting impact. Blockchains can help the new businesses by leapfrogging to allow them to compete in the same space. 

A massive advantage of blockchains for small players is that they utilize much of existing infrastructure, rather than demanding that new setups be built from scratch. These new entrepreneurs have the option of using the pre-built blockchains on the open-source platforms like Ethereum, Corda, Hyperledger, etc. Of course, they can build these from scratch, too; but, with limited resources available to a new entrant in entrepreneurship, already functioning blockchain platforms may be particularly appealing. 

Large corporations have always had much bigger financial reserves, as well as much more sophisticated technology, as compared to local ventures. It’s no secret that e-commerce giants like Amazon have torpedoed the industry from all sides, making it extremely difficult for small business owners to even survive, let alone thrive. Blockchains have the potential to level the playing field, at least to some degree. In fact, when it comes to implementing blockchains, the freedoms afforded by both the attitudinal openness of a start-up, as well as the flexibility of setting protocols and standards, upcoming projects might just be better suited for this technology. 

Blockchains have the potential to level the playing field for small players like never before. This is especially true in the context of information asymmetry that has always hampered the growth of small industries. Since these do not have the same tools at their disposal as their big counterparts, they have always been at a disadvantage. Blockchains practically make the information gap disappear by making all data points readily available for assessments. 

Blockchains have also revitalized loyalty programs to help redirect the customers to these small-scale businesses. Traditionally, the loyalty programs have been fraught with confusion, leading to quite low redemption rates. Customers tend to be wary of these, especially because of the personal data they have to give away. Blockchains offer a way around this, as the data is all encrypted safely. Also, from the point of view of the entrepreneurs, executing these loyalty programs is much easier, and also much less expensive, because of the elimination of all middlemen and transaction costs. Moreover, the blockchain mechanism can help prevent fraud with stolen tokens or other forms of illicit use. 

There is one more implication that has not yet been widely explored, but could change the shopping experience completely. The small business owners could come together, forming a sort of local marketplace where the blockchain loyalty tokens across businesses could be interlinked, thereby allowing the customer to utilize these across the linked businesses without hassle. These could prove to be quite a turning point in community-building, as the money stays within the community and multiple businesses flourish while providing the customer with ease of shopping experience. 

### Blockchains for Small Farmers

Even as global consumption of produce is only increasing, the profits that small farmers make still remain quite slim. Ever wondered why? It’s because of the fundamental flaw in the way our systems are structured. Some may even argue, and quite rightly so, that our society and the systems that it relies on are designed only to make the rich richer and the poor poorer. Even the global supply chains in effect today centralize the market power at the top, and the stakeholders at the bottom without access to opportunities of growth. 

Take the simple example of the glamorous and ever-growing chocolate industry. Can you guess how much percentage value a small farmer, who grows his own cocoa/cacao beans, would receive in the end? Seven percent—yes, that’s the percentage of the total value of his production that he receives (Fairfood, 2020). This is because of the number of actors involved in the supply chain of this raw material that most often spreads over continents. 

Several initiatives have been brought forth to resolve this situation and make life a little easier for these small farmers in, let’s say, a place like Africa. Many boutique chocolate companies which call themselves bean-to-bar have come up, which focus their attention on creating shorter supply chains and giving a greater percentage of profits to the farmer directly. These companies sell “ethical chocolate” at more premium prices. At this point in time, however, there isn’t a way for the consumer to verify if these claims are being fulfilled in reality or not. Blockchains provide a simple, yet elegant, resolution by providing instant verification of these claims. 

This, however, would only be a part of the problem resolved. A much bigger issue is that, often, small farmers do not usually have access to finances that would allow them to engage in more calculated risk-taking. In the case of the above-mentioned farmers, for instance, since they do not have the means to purchase equipment to independently process their cocoa harvest, they end up having no leveraging power. They have to harvest whenever the crop is ready and sell immediately, for fear of wasting the produce due to over-ripening. Imagine what financial empowerment could do for a small farmer like this. 

As we already discussed before, the chances of a farmer in a remote area having access to banking services are much less, as compared to them having access to smartphones. The peer-to-peer lending blockchain services like the upcoming blockchain startup AgriChain, for instance, and transactions across such networks, can be completed with the ease of a tap on the phone screen. Moreover, blockchains are also much more efficient in storing the documents related to best practices that the farmer follows, and the various certifications that they may require to prove their special value to the customer. 

This blends into the aspect of “smart agriculture” that can help the farmer implement quite sophisticated measures of data storage, as well as analysis. This data, being immutable and distortion-proof, carries special significance. Take the case of environmental monitoring data. This is usually under the centralized control of government authorities, and is utilized by them in making policy decisions based on environmental trends. But, since the central authorities have their own interests to protect, this data may be quite susceptible to manipulations which is just not possible on a blockchain enabled network. Many projects utilizing the power of blockchains, in relation to Information Communication Technology (ICT) and Internet of Things (IoT), are currently in the works to help the farmers make the most of this technology.

Another related area in which the blockchains can, and have been, bringing significant improvement is the supply chains of produce. Issues in this arena, in addition to making things difficult for the farmers, also result in tremendous economic costs to the global society as a whole. From the perspective of the farmers alone, blockchain technology helps in building trust with the consumers, as information about every product is transparently and freely available on the blockchain. This would eliminate competition, which attempts to monopolize the market with cheap and bad-quality products. The farmers can thus supply true value to the customers. On the other hand, from the customer’s vantage point, they can now be certain about how the food that they consume is made. A company known as Ripe does exactly this by providing a blockchain platform for making the food supply chain digitally transparent.

Blockchains also address the concern of traceability, ensuring the least wastage of produce, if any. A start-up called TE-FOOD uses identification tools to follow the movement of produce—be it packaged edibles, fresh produce, or even livestock—through the supply chain. Analyzing the patterns of wastage over a period of time can bring valuable insights, which aid coming to a resolution of the issue. There are even blockchain-enabled tools that help the farmer with livestock breeding. Here, some identifying markers are tagged on to the animals, right at birth, which helps them follow the growth of these animals through their lifespan in a measured and scientific manner. Their vitals are monitored and issues, if any, are identified at an early stage. 

Even with regards to illegal activities in the food industry, blockchains have a crucial role to play. Most often the illegal elements make it quite difficult for the law-abiding producers by snatching a big share of their consumer market. A relevant example would be illegal fishing, which has been a nuisance for a long time now, for both the regulatory authorities as well as the fishermen who take the effort to abide by all the regulations. Blockchains can make it absolutely simple for the customers to check if the produce they are buying is legal or not. They can also provide reliable information to the consumer regarding where their fish comes from: a breeding farm, fresh water bodies like a lake, or the salt water of the ocean.

Though all this may seem like it’s out of some realistic sci-fi novel, I remind you again that these are the applications that are being successfully run on blockchains all around you. And, this is just the tip of the iceberg. We won’t even be close to unleashing their true potential until, and unless, each one of us participates in them.

## Blockchains for Sustainability

While we talk endlessly about the technology of it all, we cannot afford to forget the environmental component. While this is true, as has been pointed out before, eco-friendly consensus mechanisms have also been designed and are widely in use, especially for public blockchains. This is one of the reasons why we absolutely must look at blockchains as much, much bigger than its tiny subset called Bitcoin, which does use a PoW consensus mechanism. Even other cryptocurrencies, like Cardano (ADA), use the eco-friendly proof of stake (PoS) consensus mechanism of Ouroboros, as was discussed previously. And yet, the critics seem to be stuck on the irony of using blockchains, which are not environment-friendly, for sustainability projects. Even a quick glance at the functioning of these public blockchains will help you understand that this assumed irony is nothing but a figment of imagination from those that would rather oppose the change than adopt it. 

### Ethical Mining

Whenever we think of the world in terms of a duality of developed and developing economies, we have often been led to believe that the developed countries are the epicenter of affluence, and the developing economies drain the global resources. This is not only a harsh outlook, but also a grossly inaccurate one. A continent like Africa has been blessed with massive mineral reserves, and yet the local population hardly seems to benefit from the mining industry. In their book Mining in Africa: Are Local Communities Better Off? (Chuhan-Pole et al., 2017), the authors talk about the “natural resource curse.” They point out the disastrous effects that corrupt political entities, combined with such abundance of resources, can have on the local development. This is primarily because such centralization in weak and undemocratic institutions makes it easier for monopolies with vested interests to control and capture rents. Blockchains can flip these dysfunctional systems on their head and change the game for the better of the local communities.

For example, the Democratic Republic of Congo has a wide range of small-scale mining operations for cobalt and copper. While these widespread mining sites are the lifeline of millions of people, they are also the breeding ground for multiple human rights violations, including child labor, in these mining operations. The government authorities have often faced severe criticism and backlash for failing to regulate these. Blockchains can change that. IBM currently runs a collaboration blockchain pilot that makes sure that the mines in Congo fulfill the certification standards. A mine would be certified only if, let’s say, there is no child labor involved, or the working conditions and wages of the miners meet the required standards. 

While this could be a big game-changer, the human effort cannot be undermined. Remember that the information that remains on the blockchain would be just as good as the information that is entered on it. This is exactly where our involvement as the public comes into the picture. The more we are aware of the implications of this technology and what it can achieve, the more success this technology can have. 

### Waste Management

You don’t have to be an environmentalist to know that our planet is in a deep crisis. As our landfills sit taller than ever, waste management drives across the globe seem to be struggling to catch up with the increasing mounds of garbage. As the population and the commercial consumerism increases, so does the waste, especially non-eco friendly waste. Ontario, Canada was recently reported to be running out of space for its own garbage. As this waste in solid, liquid, and gaseous forms piles on, human health is more and more endangered. Waste mismanagement has been well-documented, especially in developing countries leading to major health concerns like respiratory problems, skin infections, and even prenatal and postnatal defects. The primary reason for this mismanagement in developing countries, apart from corruption, is that waste management has multiple moving parts along its logistical chain.

Have you ever wondered what happens to the trash collected from your curb? The easy and probably the less scary answer is that it goes to the landfills. But, beyond this, it may be difficult to trace the movement of this trash because of the different types of waste, the numerous collection and processing centers involved in the process, and a number of such dynamic factors which might be quite difficult to track on a large scale. Blockchains have proven extremely successful in this regard.

Argentina, despite being a developing country, has proven to be a wonderful role model for putting its garbage on blockchain. The government, in collaboration with entrepreneur Ivan Zubilewicz, have implemented a reward program for waste collection using a cryptocurrency created by Zubilewicz, called JellyCoin. This cryptocurrency is awarded to all those who are involved in the waste management process. All that anyone who wishes to participate has to do is log in to the app and register as either the producer, collector, or processor of trash. Each of these is then rewarded for following proper waste segregation. These different actors are also connected with each other, ensuring that the traceability—and the resulting accountability—are implemented for the betterment of the process. The collectors are even rewarded based on the distance they travel to collect trash. Overall, the whole process becomes incentive-based, thus making people more proactive in their work. This cryptocurrency can then be used to buy anything they wish for in the mainstream market, as it is widely accepted in the Argentine economy. What a wonderful way to resolve an age-old problem without the need of a federal government!

Another such pilot project is fueling community effort to create a better waste disposal system in Bengaluru, India. Blockchains have provided a fool-proof way to ensure that no problem in garbage collection goes unnoticed. If the garbage collection has been neglected, the citizens can go and register complaints. Before the introduction of the blockchain element, the government used a geotagging system where the collector would take a picture of the cleaned site with a time stamp and upload it. But, the problem is that there are loopholes in this technology that can allow for tampering with the past data entries and time stamps. Pairing this technology with blockchains brings in a much needed level of sophistication, while also keeping the data for all stakeholders to see. This transparency ensures accountability, and gives a clear understanding of those that are performing and those that are not.

RecycleGO is a New York-based project that uses QR code scanners to scan the plastic bottles, increasing the traceability of plastic waste management. They also have a unit in Lagos, Nigeria where they plan to use blockchains to take their plastic recycling a notch higher by putting the entire process on a blockchain.

All the projects we have discussed here are being run in the so-called developing parts of the world. I highlight this because, often, we have a skewed perception that the developing world is not as tech-savvy as the rest of its counterparts. But, what we see here in this section, as well as what we have discussed so far, completely contradicts that notion. These countries may not find the financial resources, but maybe it is this scarcity that inspires them to use the resources even more economically, as well as efficiently. And, the reason blockchains excite me is they seem to be the perfect tool for this resource utilization.

## Digital Finance Infrastructures

The digital money on blockchains has seen a widespread acceptance from people. This shouldn’t come as a surprise, considering our previous discussion about the Africans’ heartening to mobile money apps like mPesa. You might be surprised to know that a majority of global mobile money operators run from Sub-Saharan Africa. While the whole world fusses over the feasibility of cryptocurrencies, the Africans seem to be quite comfortable with trading in Bitcoin, among other things. This comfort level is easily seen in the booming manner in which the value of the crypto market has grown in this region. This is, of course, because the alternative in the form of traditional banking is much more problematic and hasn’t been able to match up to the blockchain-based financial solutions. We shall look at these in more functional detail in our section on banking; but, for now, let’s look at what’s happening in Africa, specifically.

The Appzone group, a leader in fintech, has recently launched an app called Zone which could be the first step in building the continent’s decentralized payment network. Zone utilizes a blockchain platform for local, as well as Intra-African, payment processing in fiat and digital currencies. Traditionally, the companies have used centralized Electronic Financial Transaction (EFT) switches, which are single points of contact between them and the banks. These are problematic because they carry out the settlements of transactions only at defined intervals, and have also been known to fail altogether in certain situations. As Zone uses a decentralized blockchain architecture, the settlements happen instantly; and, a failure at one point doesn’t bring the whole system to a halt. Moreover, as we discussed previously, being a blockchain solution, it saves up on transaction costs in a major way, too.

This isn’t the only player in the game, though. Another leader in the fintech arena, Interswitch Group has been conducting significant research in collaboration with Interstellar Inc., a blockchain infrastructure company. We are likely to see a big emergence of blockchain-powered solutions coming from this collaboration. The great thing about these projects is that they seem to have identified a major gap in the digital finance market in Africa—interoperability between the several regions of the continent; and, they are bringing effective resolutions to tackle this problem. This, in many ways, would prove to be a major factor in blossoming the trade in the region, and herald a new era of economic prosperity.

## Government-Partnered Blockchain Projects

It is often observed that developed nations make the most of any upcoming technology because of the resources, both human and financial, that they have at their disposal. Blockchain technology seems to partially resolve this issue by utilizing much of the available infrastructure. Despite this advantage, however, blockchain architecture would require a considerable amount of expertise if this technology were to be used at scale, especially in the public sector. You’d think that this could deter the African countries from early adoption, but what has been observed is, quite wonderfully, the exact opposite. Governments in parts of the continent have formed various collaborations with experts from both law and technology to develop infrastructure, thereby attracting investment. 

We already looked at the landmark Cardano deal with the Ethiopian government to provide digital identities to five million students. The great thing is that it is one of the projects, a massively important one, but just the one, nonetheless. There are many more collaborations like this, either in the works or have already been functioning for quite some time. 

The South African National Blockchain Alliance (SANBA) is one such collaboration. The great thing about this is that it connects government, sponsors, the research community, and the industrial community to help adopt blockchains beyond the financial and cryptocurrency domains. Since its creation, SANBA has been looking at several strategic partners for developing a smooth-running and connected blockchain ecosystem including IBM, Microsoft, Binance, Cardano, and so on. During the last year, two stand-out projects that they have undertaken are ILIMA—a farm-to-fork support program for small farmers and processors powered on a blockchain, and Credentialing Blockchain to digitally document skill development credentials for apprentices. Though both projects are in a proof of concept stage currently, it shows the increasing interest that even the government is taking in this technology.

Blockchain adoption by the Ugandan government is also noteworthy. The major issue that the government hopes to tackle is that of counterfeit pharmaceutical drugs in all of the East African community. For this, a couple of years back, it partnered with blockchain-based platform MediConnect—which records the manufacturing of pharmaceuticals on a blockchain, thereby making it much easier to trace the fake drugs and stop their distribution in the supply chain. 

Another significant blockchain adoption in the public sector in this country is the land titles registry. Uganda has a nuanced land tenure system with a variety of protection rights and modalities. Despite the fact that the law is very much designed to favor the common man, the ground reality suggests quite the opposite. The corruption and fraud has been eating away at the system by issuing multiple titles over the same property. This basically renders the title certificate, which is technically the only requirement to prove ownership, practically useless. To make matters worse, a large number of Ugandans have been evicted from their own properties due to a growth in foreign investment, without a trace of fair compensation, leaving them to the streets. Even their farming lands have been snatched from them, taking away what was previously their sole means of earning a livelihood. The government has been looking at a pilot project to implement blockchains for land registry—to uproot the rampant corruption. 

An interesting approach taken by the Ugandan government is seen in the Kampala Declaration, in which states support non-regulation of the blockchain before further research is conducted. This would allow for what’s known as “permissionless innovation,” meaning that blockchain innovators can utilize their creativity to the maximum level without worrying about government restrictions. Such legislation signifying openness can go a long way in creating acceptance among the general public.

Nigerian initiatives are also inspiring people to jump on the blockchain bandwagon in the country. Kashifu Inuwa Abdullahi, the Director-General of the National Information Technology Development Agency (NITDA) hinted at the Nigerian plan to generate $10 billion in revenue by 2030, with the adoption of blockchain technology (Paul, 2020). Though the most widespread applications of blockchains in Nigeria remain in the financial cryptocurrency sector, the government plans to utilize this technology for several projects, like national digital identity, digital literacy, startups in various sectors, and so on. The policy incentives for these projects can certainly charge the Nigerian economy into an economic boom. 

However, these projects also come with a beware sign. For instance, a national digital identity would only make sense when the platforms are truly decentralized in nature—where the individual does not have to worry about the dangerous implications of sharing their personal data. On the other hand, when centralized government systems utilize blockchains, they tend to adopt blockchains that move away from decentralization towards privatization, which we spoke of earlier. This use of private blockchains, even in the public sector, is more common than you think—many of the international organizations continue to consider blockchains to strengthen their centralized hierarchy-driven structures. Take, for instance, Blockchain4Cities: a United Nations research group collecting comprehensive data for blockchain implementation in smart-city infrastructure. Though this has accelerated blockchain growth, the masses will have little to gain from it because the power still remains with the centralized governments. The point is, though blockchain innovation in these projects may seem shiny and attractive, it’s crucial we don’t forget the decentralization theme. That, along with mass participation, is the only way to bring power back to people.

##  The Trade Boost

In 2018, a free trade area called African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) was founded, with the actual trade kick-starting in January 2021. This was a massive undertaking, and is considered to be the largest free trade area globally connecting billions of people across multiple countries. Free trade area only means that this region has entered into an agreement which would allow for minimal policy restrictions with regards to trade, and can thus help in boosting economic growth. An intriguing observation can be made here—while developed nations have been recently moving towards more governance-based solutions for their socioeconomic problems, it seems that developing nations are utilizing the capitalist tenets of trade to resolve theirs. 

As exciting as it is, there are multiple hurdles along the way. Access to processing of trade documents is one of them. Trade documents may include several crucial things: like certifications, insurance papers, and so on. These would have to be perfectly in place, even more so as the trades are happening across national borders. Another concern, high on the list, would be the currency conversion across borders, which would result in piling up of costs as well as an extension of the timeline to settlements. Blockchains have been looked at as the most appropriate platform to tackle these issues.

While the AfCFTA is still finding its way through the legislation regarding rules of origin, the associated countries remain hopeful. The COVID-19 pandemic has also had an interesting impact on this whole process. On one side, it has shaken up the economies of multiple countries, leaving the trade in these regions severely impacted. But, at the same time, it has also acted like a wake-up call for African countries that widely depend on export of commodities to start taking their internal trades, as well as trades with other countries. This might have been the much-needed impetus for the African countries to speed up the AfCFTA activities. 

It is interesting to note, however, that blockchains are already helping the trade scenario beyond AfCFTA. Though AfCFTA now seems poised to catalyze the trade boom, it is still in its early stages; this means each country could have different customs procedures requiring several documentations, as we discussed, even in AfCFTA. The difference would be that, while AfCFTA would have a uniform procedure, the formalities that need to be completed in a regular scenario may be widely different in each country and worse, still, even at each port. 

These red tape legislations have traditionally created massive barriers to entry for small-scale businesses. With blockchains, these can be reduced drastically, if not eliminated altogether. Rather than submitting separate trade documents at every point in the trade route, one could upload the information on a blockchain once, and it is there for all authorities on the chain to view. TradeLens is a platform that currently helps entrepreneurs to do exactly this. Traders can upload their information and trace the location of their shipment throughout the journey. However, with only a limited number of port operators signed up on this particular platform, there remains room for improvement.

The prosperity that these initiatives can unlock in these regions is beyond consequential. Remember that blockchain-aided trade, and especially free trade, allows the small players to enter the global markets. Whether it’s Tunisian olive oil, Zambian cassava, Rwandan coffee, or the Cote d'Ivoire cocoa that we talked about earlier, the African continent has tremendous potential. All this while, though these resources were exploited (many times unethically), the returns hardly reached the ones who deserved to be paid for their hard work. The profits were snatched by middlemen and corrupt government officials, leaving the actual producers in the lurch almost every time. Today, with blockchains, the traders have an opportunity to make profits while also giving the well-deserved share to the small farmers.

All in all, the trading scenario in the African countries is very much on the brink of a breakthrough. Blockchains have been, and will certainly in the future be, a valued ally in going the extra mile. 

## Peer-to-Peer Electricity Model

The electricity crisis is a real struggle for African countries. It would seem that there is a perpetual shortage of produced electricity in comparison with the actual needs of the population. Despite importing noisy and not-so-environment friendly power generators, an overwhelming percentage of rural African territory remains in the dark. Even though the governments across the continent have made promises of electrification, universal access seems like a distant dream, especially for the rural underprivileged sections of the continent. 

Because of the high cost of power generation, many countries have now started looking at utilizing renewable sources of energy , especially for communities that are largely off-grid. In such areas, projects to harness solar and wind energy have received great encouragement in the form of investment from several financial institutions. But, even with this development, renewable energy—as a resource to power rural territories—still does not match up to the needs of the population. The major reason for this is the focus on the number of connections and megawatts installed, rather than the actual reach of the power produced. This is where the decentralized power comes into the picture.

An important development has been the upcoming decentralized power where home solar panels have been installed on individual homes, or micro/mini grids have been installed to power small communities. The real game changer lies ahead, though. The houses that produce more energy on their solar panels than they use can sell this excess energy to those houses which have no solar panels. This way, it’s a win-win for both parties—the one installing panels in their homes can repay the loans taken for the installation while enjoying their uninterrupted power supply and the ones who cannot afford to install these panels by paying for only the energy that they use. The transactions, of course, happen through the cryptocurrency in use on the blockchain. A mobile app called Exergy, powered by blockchain, has been helping keep track of such trades across Africa. It is these peer-to-peer electricity lending initiatives that are making it possible for the small consumers, who would never be on any big power company’s radar to gain access to electricity, and also achieve financial inclusion in the process with cryptocurrency.

As the blockchain movement gains momentum, these ecosystems are mushrooming everywhere. So, let’s talk Cardano! Till now, we have looked at Cardano’s projects where it is implementing the principle of decentralization. Now, let’s take a look at how it runs its own function on the same principle, too. As I mentioned, it is useful to look at this process because it holds useful lessons for our democratic process, too. It provides us with the proof that, if we can incentivize the citizens to play an active role in the procedures while ensuring that the votes they cast matter because they are recorded immutably, then true transparent democracy is actually possible. In one of his routine YouTube videos On Governance (Part I), this process is explained succinctly by the Cardano founder (Hoskinson, 2020). 

Hoskinson identifies two parts to the governance problems: decision-making and financial resources. What happens in a centralized environment is a central authority, by default, takes care of these things without much deliberation. That would be akin to an authoritarian dictatorship. But, more common than the outright dictatorship, is the disguised democracy. As the famous quote in the book Animal Farm goes, “All animals are equal but some animals are more equal than others” (Orwell, 1945). Here, too, some voices carry greater weightage. More often than not, this weightage is implicitly determined by the amount of money each one brings into the functioning—the more the money, the bigger the voice. 

Cardano tackles this problem by creating a sort of an experimental sandbox called Voltaire. This is essentially a bundle of protocols and experiments for bringing about change and decision-making for spending the financial resources in the Cardano (ADA) blockchain. This is designed to be as fair as possible to the ADA holders, regardless of the number of tokens they hold. These experiments utilize Incentivized Testnet (ITN) to test the validity of different methodologies for a democratic voting. These are run parallel to the actual Cardano blockchain on permissioned or permissionless side-chains, with the objective of testing different methods over shorter periods of time. These simulations are based on the data received from the Cardano blockchains. From this data, certain ADA holders that are eligible to take part in the votes are chosen. The experiments would keep running until a sort of a Beta program develops, building on the most effective and standardized protocol.

Once the ADA voters are chosen, the actual votes are held on the basic concerns we talked of: how to spend funds and what changes to implement. The best way to start is with the former. The latter, on the other hand, is slightly more complicated. This is simply because, if there are any mistakes in spending funds, they are easier to rectify whereas, if wrong changes are implemented, they might actually result in the collapse of the system altogether. 

So, the voter is required to send in a ‘ballot.’ This is like a proposal for how they’d like to spend the funds—maybe start a blockchain initiative, start a business, make some improvements to the Cardano ecosystem, etc. These ballots are then sent to Submittable, a social impact platform that first screens these ballots for any malicious elements, like D-DOS attacks, and then puts them through a process called innovation management. Here, the community members and experts are incentivized so as to seriously check the validity of the ideas in the ballots. Now, at this point, none of the ballots are rejected but, instead, are given a rating. Once these ratings are completed, the ballots are put through two rounds of voting: preference vote, which decides on what sectors need to be prioritized and threshold vote, which decides on which of the ballot ideas get funding. 

The beauty about this system is that, regardless of how many ADA tokens a community member holds, their voice will receive no differential treatment as compared to others. As long as the idea is in the interest of the people and is approved, it will be implemented. Remember that what I have discussed here is just a faint outline of the voting process. ## Clinical Trials

Clinical trials are often lengthy and complex, with regulations that require strict adherence. For a long time, the questions over transparency, in many cases, have been glossed over. Any research trial has to undergo several stages, and has to abide by a different set of procedures at every stage. Without going into the depths of the nature of trials, let’s jump straight to blockchain application in this arena. Three primary areas of application can be identified in these trials—tracking the chronological order of the phases, traceability of the movement of data through these phases with a time stamp, and maintaining the integrity of the data by ensuring immutability. All of these can help with the reproducibility of the research, which is a test that many research trials fail because of the “after the fact” data manipulation.

Apart from these, there is also one extremely important function of blockchains in clinical trials: that of smart contracts. The “if, then” logic of these contracts allow for building a secure and quality-controlled way of developing workflows around a predetermined logic. Since the workflow is automatically triggered when certain conditions are generated, there isn’t a way for the bias to trickle in. Also, as the data is recorded throughout the blockchain, the question of going back and manipulating the data to prove a certain hypothesis does not arise, either. 

Blockchains, due to their innate design, automatically solve the problems of registration, informed consent, etc. The data stored on the blockchain is easily verifiable and remotely auditable. Also, as the data is not attached to names per se, the doctor-client confidentiality is maintained perfectly. The vision for the future is to be able to have a kind of archive that consists of the data and metadata of all such clinical trials in a specific, predetermined format, to make reviewing the data at a later point in time easier. A digital archive would also be helpful, and much less time-consuming, for future researchers to reference.But, remember that these ideas are still developing and, as of now, blockchains haven’t been supporting clinical trials, at least not on a large scale. But, what it could do when it does roll out is just pure brilliance.

Blockchain-supported trials have the potential to do just that. It’s worth noting, however, that even though clinical trials were not ready to be rolled out on blockchain networks yet, COVID-19 has seen a wide range of blockchain applications in the supply of vaccines, uploading the vaccine certificates on the network for the recently announced vaccine passport, and so on. You can think of this discussion as an extension of the case studies in Africa, but now let’s consider a few more scenarios. Smart grids can essentially work in three different ways. We’ll do a quick review of the first two, as we have already discussed these. So, a household that wishes to be self-sufficient with their energy production would install solar panels and quite simply utilize the electricity produced thereof. The extra energy produced can be stored in a power bank for a literal rainy day when the solar panels are not stimulated by the sunlight. This would be the primary usage of the generated electricity. Remember that, in this particular set-up, a blockchain would not be required as the use is limited to one party only.

The second use case is where you would sell the extra electricity to your neighbors. So, if the smart grid is to power a neighborhood, it will be connected to an automated aggregation service which optimizes the demand and supply for electricity. But, what happens when the panels don’t produce enough electricity to fulfill the needs of the entire community? Simple, you ask for more! The extra electricity that’s needed is negotiated for from another energy-producing community; the transactions happen much like they would if electricity was any other commodity. These are then paid for in micropayments, simultaneously, which are securely recorded on the blockchains. These can be verified to see if the transactions are actually working out for both parties. Here, the electricity-generating solar panel would act as a secondary provider of energy.

This system is smart not only because of secure transaction logging, but it also assesses the patterns of your electricity usage, thereby making an estimate of the amount you will need for a stipulated period of time. Based on your usage data, it can even schedule major loads like electric vehicle charging, heating water, and so on. 

The last case is when the microgrid would fulfill requests for temporary deficits. In this case, it would become a tertiary reserve for energy production. Now, pay attention to the sophistication of the process—a transaction is activated on the blockchain, which comes in as an offer for a certain amount of electricity. The grid then syncs this request with your online calendars to determine if you have any travel planned. Accordingly, it decides if it can spare enough energy, factoring in the electric charging of your vehicle. If it can spare the electricity, then it is sold at a much higher price, quite like the surge prices for Uber. 

Even though it may not seem like much, when looked at in the context of one house, this can and is bringing millions access to electricity. Besides, blockchains, with their distributed ledgers, can scale up these microgrids to a much larger extent, too. Remember that the payment for the electricity is through crypto tokens. The blockchain technology successfully incorporates into the microgrid model the peer-to-peer energy trade. which is a great possibility for a decentralized approach to power generation and distribution. Several platforms have been testing this concept flawlessly for quite a while now. Take, for instance, Suncontract in Slovenia, Powerledger in Perth, and LO3 Energy in Brooklyn; they are fine evidence that decentralized energy projects are possible across the globe. 

The same concept has been extended to the retail and wholesale markets for energy, where companies can fulfill their energy needs by sourcing them from such platforms rather than the traditional centralized authorities. WePower is one such platform that helps companies to fulfill their energy needs by providing them with the right amount of green and clean energy. 

This is another aspect, in addition to consumption of energy itself: the decarbonized energy that we spoke of at the beginning of this section. FlexiDAO, for instance, is blockchain-driven software aimed to boost the decarbonization and decentralization of power. This software tracks the energy production, right from its source to destination, and is vigilant against double counting. One can also receive what are known as carbon credits for adhering to the green standards, and these can then be used to either obtain services or simply be sold in the marketplace.

Many of the Ethereum-based decentralized apps (DApps) are also making their own contributions in this power revolution. Swether is one such app that provides end-to-end solutions for the internet of energy. Right from booking charging stations for your electric vehicle, to handling payments, to bidding for energy from various providers, to syncing loyalty tokens—this DApp seems to do everything, making your life much easier.

# Chapter 12: E-Commerce 

It is no secret that the last decade has seen a huge uptick in the e-commerce industries. Whether it is merchandise from your favorite TV show, books, electronics—you name it, and it’s available with a simple click or a tap. Especially with the onset of the pandemic, the e-commerce industry has boomed beyond imagination. If you are like most of the people today, you might not even remember the last time you went to a brick-and-mortar retail store. But, as much as we may love the ease of sitting in our bed, all cozy under the blanket while shopping for our favorite pair of sunglasses, the system isn’t entirely without problems. 

One of the biggest problems faced by e-commerce platforms is counterfeit products that do not match the product descriptions. The result is quite obvious; customers send these products right back. This not only results in loss of sale, but also tarnishes the brand image of the platform more than the vendor company that sent the substandard product. Moreover, the logistical costs of picking up a return are far too high, as compared to the profits. Though, usually these platforms tend to fine the vendors for low-quality products, it’s still a lot of unnecessary wastage through the supply chain. What’s more, managing the payments to the vendors is another arduous task. It’s a tedious process with multiple steps, many intermediaries, and a lot of documentation. This process, in itself, can incur heavy costs for the e-commerce platform. 

Apart from the product-related concerns, e-commerce platforms might also have to face serious functional problems. An e-commerce platform, for instance, has to stay up and running, no matter what. But, this may be endangered by virtual attacks posing grave security risks. Many cybersecurity reports suggest that e-commerce platforms are the most valuable to malicious hackers. 

As if these weren’t enough, there are also the problems that occur at the customer end, when the delivery happens. If the supply chain is inefficient and glitchy, there is no way for the customer to know exactly where the package is. Many times, when the package arrives, the customer is not home, forcing the delivery executive to either register a failed attempt to deliver or the more common route of action—leaving the package at the door, which is very likely to be lost or stolen. The customer can then file a complaint to have delivered the package again, but this whole grievance redressal protocol is more resources down the drain.

Now, before we proceed further to talk about how blockchains help, I would like you to engage in a mental exercise with me and try to think of some of the ways that they could increase the efficiency of e-commerce platforms. You will have realized, by now, that the same blockchain principles of security, immutability, traceability, and decentralization are being applied to all of the industries we have discussed so far. Those are the same principles here, as well. 

With a decentralized inventory management system, the e-commerce platforms can greatly decrease the delivery times. Also, the blockchains provide the customer with the exact location of the products to be delivered, significantly reducing the failed attempts at delivery and, also, the related costs. Blockchains also offer protection against malicious attacks and prevent any penetration in the system, making sure that the data of the customers remains safe and secure within the platform. 

Apart from these, there are two added benefits that can come from blockchains. The first, loyalty and rewards, we have already discussed earlier in relation to small entrepreneurs. The second benefit is even more curious: customer reviews. Again, the principle of immutability is at work here, but the manner in which it comes across is quite novel. On a blockchain-based e-commerce platform, when the customer leaves a review, there is no way it can be changed, for better or for worse. Thus, it is likely that the reviews are more trustworthy and reliable. 

# Chapter 13: Art and Culture

Art and culture are key cornerstones of any civilization. ## Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs)

A big use case for blockchains in the art and culture segment is the Non-Fungible Token (NFT). We already spoke about these in a very crude form earlier, but now it’s time to dig deeper. To give you a brief reminder of what NFTs are, let’s say they are blockchain-backed digital tokens. These tokens could be an audio clip, a video, a piece of digital art, and so on; and they could be tied to a cryptocurrency. Now, what makes an NFT different from cryptocurrency is that, while the latter carries the same value, the former does not. This means that, when I say Bitcoin is worth so-and-so amount, each bitcoin has the same rate; but, when I talk about NFTs, each NFT is like a differently priced commodity. So, just as two art pieces wouldn’t cost the same, two NFTs wouldn’t either, because art pieces are exactly what they are. So, just like when you buy Bitcoin, you would get a unique key—when you buy NFTs, too, you would get a unique and secure digital token. 

To a layperson, the digital file that you receive may not seem like a big deal, but to the art appreciators and collectors it is certainly worth the millions that they spend on these art pieces. It signifies more than just owning the piece; it essentially means that these people have gone down in the digital history of that piece for an eternity and beyond. With this purchase, though, art connoisseurs don’t own the copyright of the work, which still rests with the artist. That means that the artist can create more artwork just like the one you own—a little like limited edition baseball cards, if you will. A number of NFT marketplaces have come up like SuperRare, OpenSea, Nifty Gateway, MakersPlace, etc. which take a commission on each artwork sold. In return, they make sure that the artwork is advertised and the transactions run smoothly. 

Now, the disruption is this: NFTs are empowering artists and creators to make a living doing what they love. The technology that they have added to the mix can help these artists reach and engage with a much larger crowd directly, without any agents. The other benefit that these NFTs bring to the artists is the undisputed record of ownership. While, in traditional art, there are a lot of feuds about the original vs. fake, here the possibility never arises. From an artistic point of view, NFTs provide yet another medium to pour onto the canvas or rather the screen, all of the artist’s creative expression. Furthermore, the NFTs can bring about a much more unbiased and unskewed market, giving equal opportunities to artists regardless of gender, race, etc. 

NFTs also solve the problem of royalties for the artists. Consider this scenario: In the traditional art industry, though the artist holds the rights to his work, there would be no way for them to know if their painting has been sold or pirated. Thus, even though the concept of royalties exists in theory, in practicality it’d be quite difficult to enforce. However, on the blockchains, with the use of smart contracts, the artists can make sure they receive a certain portion of the consequent sales. 

NFTs will also change the landscape of art collection, so to say. The art collector will no longer have to be a rich man, sipping wine from the glass in one hand and holding a cigar in the other. Since the collection remains online, anyone with the slightest propensity towards art can purchase an NFT. The prices can start as low as $12 and go up to hundreds of millions, giving the purchaser a wide variety of choices. 

Another aspect to consider here is that NFTs don’t have to be just artwork, but could be anything from music, photos, or anything at all. Any music fan would know that, historically, recording labels have not been the most respectful toward the artists. This reality—which was, for the most part, kept under rug previously—was publicly exposed when the legendary Prince wrote ‘slave’ on his face, comparing his contract with Warner Bros. to slavery. The recording labels have continued to have that hold over musicians, more often than not handing them the shorter end of the stick. With the emergence of NFTs, however, that can change as artists are ditching the labels altogether and selling their work directly to the listeners in the form of NFTs. 

The same goes for comics selling their standups as NFTs. Jambb is a comedy collectible startup that is raising millions for building a marketplace for comedy NFTs. Apart from raising money for comedy sets, NFTs also offer the censorship-free zone to these comedians, to create satirical pieces. Comedians have often come under fire for making comedy, which is exactly what they are supposed to do. But, there seems to be a wave of the cancel culture, whereby people believe that anything they don’t agree with needs to be condemned publicly, and even abusively. This very perception seems to be fueling the raging fire of the culture wars around us today.

With the woke pseudo-liberals waiting at every turn to cancel a comedian’s right to free speech, the social climate is becoming ever more insecure. This self-proclaimed moral police hate comedians who do commentary on current events. I wonder if it is because they speak the truth—a truth so beyond pathetic that it lands itself into the hilarious realm? Blockchains and NFTs can certainly breathe new life into this artform. One thing is certainly true: The implications of NFTs run far and wide for all genres of art.

This may seem like NFTs are specifically, and only, for the artist niche; but, the truth is that, in recent times, NFTs have emerged as the hot property in the investment sector. Until 2021, hardly anyone really knew what NFTs were; but, with the metaverse talks gaining momentum, NFTs have peaked in popularity, too, as the idea of holding commodities in a virtual universe seems that much more exciting.

### Steem

Steem, in itself, is a blockchain that supports applications like Steemit and DTube. Steemit is the decentralized social media platform that rewards content creators by providing them with cryptocurrencies. This incentivization model helps keep the blockchain running, with immutability of the content as its foundation. The Steem blockchain supports a total of 324 apps, but Steemit, Utopian, and DTube remain the most popular. 

Both Steemit and DTube offer a series of news and videos based on your choice, while also offering you a source of income in the form of cryptocurrencies by contributing some form of written content. The decentralized nature of these platforms allows them to be free from the political and censorship influences, as well as the influences of the advertisers. Utopian, on the other hand, is an open-source project where crypto-tokens can be earned by contributing not content, but some kind of a fix on the open source project: like fixing a bug, for instance. 

### Sapien

While Steem was an early adopter, the decentralized social media platform has only seen growth in the last few years. It is obvious that the users are tired of the charades that the news channels and the centralized social media platforms are playing with them. They want a communication channel that's unscripted, uncensored, and just genuinely authentic. Sapien is one such platform that fulfills all of these intellectual needs.

Here, users have full control over how much personal information they choose to share, and with whom. The platform also curates the user experience based on the user’s interest and preferences. Unlike the centralized social media, here there are no restrictions on the content posted. For instance, towards the end of the last year, Facebook all of sudden declared a ban on crypto ads, but took an about turn on this after it faced a backlash from its users. It is clear that these policy flip-flops are quite politically motivated, maybe only to gauge how far they can go in curbing the freedoms and basic rights of their users. The decentralized platforms are the only way to stop treating the big players as if the free citizen is their personal pet parrot. 

The Sapien Network uses the age-old remedy for tackling information one doesn’t agree with—it’s called choosing not to engage (after a point). The users can express themselves to the absolute edge and no one can stop them. What you can do, as a user, is to pick and choose what content you take in; if you feel offended by something someone said, best not to watch it again, especially if you are only going to end up abusing the person and take away the others’ right to expression. It also has private and public mode features, which means that you can decide if you want to stay anonymous on the platform or switch your original identity. What’s attracting a significant number of people to this platform is the various social media features like creating groups, texting, voice chatting, etc. which are largely missing in Steemit.

Sapien is still only in its fetal stage. It’s built on the Ethereum blockchain and, with its token SPN backed by ERC20 and Ethereum token, it looks excitedly to the possibility of turning into a marketplace soon. This will promote various other third-party applications and, even, integrations within the network itself. 

### SoMee Social

SoMee Social is yet another Cardano gem. This is a blockchain-powered social media platform primarily designed for privacy, user control, and monetization. This was launched in 2017 and is currently putting up the third iteration of the platform. They have also been onboarding developers and marketers who have been involved with much bigger projects in the past like Google and Zillow, and are now doing this to kickstart the platform that can uphold the decentralization ideology in the true sense. 

### Indorse

Indorse can be thought of as a LinkedIn substitute for decentralization enthusiasts. It has been marketed as a social network powered by blockchains, but for professionals. This has a unique feature that allows other users to verify the personal skills listed by any user which essentially serves like testimonials. In return, the users are incentivized by a certain share of profits going back to them. The idea is simple—if you are claiming expertise in some area, then you would have to attach proof of that claim, too. A consensus then follows, deciding whether the claim is to be ‘endorsed’ or flagged. This platform has unlocked a unique way to weed fake claims without curbing free speech. Though Indorse uses it specifically for professional skills, this model might find its uses in avoiding propagation of inaccurate information in the name of free speech.

As we come to the end of the disruptions section, I would like to point out one very crucial aspect that almost always gets overlooked in the excitement of discussing blockchain applications. Let’s remember that the strength of blockchains is immutability and, not necessarily, accuracy. This means that, though the information that is entered into the system cannot be manipulated, eventually the efficiency of the systems may be determined by the information that goes into it, in the first place. So, if I, as a user, enter forged data into the system, then the data entry will still be inaccurate despite the immutability. This is the reason why blockchains, like any other technology, are only as good as the people running them. And, that is why I keep saying that more of the commonfolk need to participate in this, lest we lose the control over data that gets solidified in blockchains for an eternity.

It would also be wise to note that all the disruptions we have noted here are not the end of the blockchain potential, but only the beginning. In fact, there are many that we haven’t touched upon; for example, blockchains are being used in traffic management, vehicle leasing, management of software versions and release of documents, so on and so forth. The list is endless. 

Many of the blockchain projects in the works today have a ten-year vision, which means that the seeds that are being sown today with these disruptive use cases will bear fruit somewhere around the year 2030. That doesn’t mean that no current benefits would be reaped. All of what we have discussed are fruits of early adoption that we are already enjoying.
