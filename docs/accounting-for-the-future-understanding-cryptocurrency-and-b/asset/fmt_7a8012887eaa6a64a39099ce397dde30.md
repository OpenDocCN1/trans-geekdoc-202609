
## Example: Long-Term Capital Gain on Ethereum

Sarah buys 5 Ethereum (ETH) for $2,500 in March 2020. In April 2022, she sells all 5 ETH for $20,000. Since she held the Ethereum for more than a year, her gain is classified as a long-term capital gain. The capital gain is calculated as:

```
Capital Gain = Selling Price - Cost Basis
Capital Gain = $20,000 - $2,500 = $17,500
```

Sarah reports $17,500 as a long-term capital gain, which is taxed at a lower rate than her ordinary income.

## Tax on Crypto-to-Crypto Trades

One of the most misunderstood aspects of cryptocurrency taxation is that crypto-to-crypto trades are considered taxable events. When you trade one cryptocurrency for another, it’s as if you sold the first cryptocurrency and used the proceeds to buy the second one. You must calculate the capital gain or loss on the cryptocurrency you are selling, using the FMV of the crypto you receive in return.

### Example: Trading Bitcoin for Ethereum

Alex trades 1 Bitcoin (BTC) for 10 Ethereum (ETH) when the price of 1 BTC is $50,000 and 1 ETH is $5,000. He originally purchased the Bitcoin for $30,000. This trade is considered a sale of Bitcoin, and Alex needs to calculate his capital gain:

```
Capital Gain = FMV of Crypto Received - Cost Basis of Crypto Sold
Capital Gain = $50,000 - $30,000 = $20,000
```

Alex reports a $20,000 capital gain from this trade. The cost basis for the 10 ETH he receives is the FMV at the time of the trade, which is $50,000 ($5,000 per ETH).

### Income Tax on Cryptocurrency

Cryptocurrency received as income is taxed at the FMV of the asset at the time of receipt. This applies to income from various sources, such as:

1.  **Payment for Goods or Services**: If you receive cryptocurrency as payment for providing goods or services, you must report the FMV of the crypto as ordinary income.
2.  **Mining and Staking Rewards**: Cryptocurrencies earned through mining or staking are treated as income and are taxed when received, based on the FMV at the time.

#### Example: Receiving Bitcoin as Payment

Olivia, a graphic designer, agrees to design a website in exchange for 0.5 Bitcoin (BTC). At the time of payment, the price of Bitcoin is $40,000. Olivia must report the FMV of 0.5 BTC ($20,000) as ordinary income.

In her accounting records, Olivia records:

*   Debit: $20,000 (Cryptocurrency Asset – Bitcoin)
*   Credit: $20,000 (Revenue – Web Design Services)

When Olivia later sells the Bitcoin, she will need to calculate her capital gain or loss based on the value of Bitcoin at that time.

#### Example: Tax on Staking Rewards

Mark participates in a proof-of-stake network and earns 2 Ethereum (ETH) as staking rewards. At the time he receives the rewards, 1 ETH is worth $3,000. Mark must report $6,000 as income on his tax return.

In his accounting records, Mark records:

*   Debit: $6,000 (Cryptocurrency Asset – Ethereum)
*   Credit: $6,000 (Income – Staking Rewards)

### Tax Loss Harvesting with Cryptocurrencies

Tax loss harvesting is a strategy that allows investors to reduce their taxable income by selling underperforming assets at a loss. The capital losses generated from these sales can be used to offset capital gains from other transactions, potentially reducing tax liability.

#### Example: Tax Loss Harvesting with Bitcoin

David purchases 1 Bitcoin for $50,000. Later, the price of Bitcoin drops to $30,000, and David decides to sell the Bitcoin to lock in a capital loss. His capital loss is calculated as:

```
Capital Loss = Cost Basis - Selling Price
Capital Loss = $50,000 - $30,000 = $20,000
```

David can use this $20,000 capital loss to offset other capital gains or reduce his taxable income.

> **Note**: Some countries have a “wash sale rule,” which prohibits investors from claiming a capital loss if they repurchase the same asset within 30 days of selling it. However, this rule does not currently apply to cryptocurrencies in most jurisdictions.

## Reporting Cryptocurrency on Tax Returns

In many countries, taxpayers are required to report all cryptocurrency transactions, even if no tax is owed. It is essential to keep accurate records of all crypto activity, including:

1.  **Dates of Transactions**: The date when the cryptocurrency was bought, sold, or exchanged.
2.  **Fair Market Value**: The FMV of the cryptocurrency at the time of the transaction, in the local currency (e.g., USD, CAD).
3.  **Cost Basis**: The original purchase price of the cryptocurrency, including transaction fees.
4.  **Transaction Type**: Whether the transaction was a sale, trade, or income.

### Example: Reporting Crypto Trades on a Tax Return

Anna buys 1 Bitcoin for $10,000 in January. She later sells the Bitcoin for $30,000 in October, realizing a $20,000 capital gain. Anna must report this gain on her tax return using the appropriate form (e.g., `Form 8949` in the U.S. for reporting sales of capital assets).

Her completed form will show:

*   Date Acquired: January 1st
*   Date Sold: October 15th
*   Cost Basis: $10,000
*   Selling Price: $30,000
*   Capital Gain: $20,000

### Tax on Gifts and Donations of Cryptocurrency

Gifting or donating cryptocurrency can also trigger tax consequences. The rules vary depending on whether the recipient is an individual or a charity.

1.  **Gifts of Cryptocurrency**: In many countries, gifts of cryptocurrency are not considered taxable for the giver. However, the recipient may have to pay tax when they sell or trade the gifted cryptocurrency. The cost basis of the gift is the same as the original cost basis for the giver.
2.  **Donations to Charity**: In most countries, donating cryptocurrency to a registered charity is not a taxable event for the donor. The donor may also be eligible for a tax deduction based on the FMV of the donated crypto at the time of the donation.

#### Example: Gifting Bitcoin to a Family Member

Brian buys 1 Bitcoin for $5,000 and gifts it to his brother when the Bitcoin is worth $50,000. Brian does not owe any tax for making the gift. When his brother sells the Bitcoin, he will need to calculate his capital gain based on Brian’s original cost basis of $5,000. If his brother sells the Bitcoin for $55,000, the capital gain is:

```
Capital Gain = Selling Price - Cost Basis (Carryover Basis)
Capital Gain = $55,000 - $5,000 = $50,000
```

#### Example: Donating Ethereum to Charity

Linda donates 2 Ethereum (ETH) to a registered charity when the price of Ethereum is $3,500 per ETH. Since this is a donation to charity, Linda does not owe any capital gains tax. She is also eligible for a tax deduction of $7,000 (the FMV of the donation).

### International Variations in Cryptocurrency Taxation

Tax treatment for cryptocurrencies varies by country. While the U.S. and many Western countries treat cryptocurrencies as property, some countries have taken unique approaches:

*   **Germany**: In Germany, cryptocurrency held for more than one year is not subject to capital gains tax.
*   **Portugal**: Portugal has a favorable tax regime for crypto investors, as individuals do not pay tax on capital gains from cryptocurrency unless it is their primary source of income.
*   **India**: India treats cryptocurrency as a taxable asset, and gains are subject to income tax at the individual’s applicable rate.

> **Note**: It is essential to understand the specific tax rules in your country and seek advice from a tax professional if necessary.
