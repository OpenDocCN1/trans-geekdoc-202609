
Evan buys 0.5 Bitcoin (BTC) for $20,000 USD, with a transaction fee of $100 USD. The total cost basis is $20,100 ($20,000 purchase price + $100 transaction fee). This cost basis will be used to calculate capital gains or losses when Evan eventually sells or trades the Bitcoin.

In accounting terms, this would be recorded as:

*   Debit: $20,100 (Cryptocurrency Asset – Bitcoin)
*   Credit: $20,100 (Cash/Bank Account)

## 2. Sale of Cryptocurrency

When cryptocurrency is sold for fiat currency or exchanged for another cryptocurrency, the proceeds must be compared with the cost basis to determine whether a capital gain or loss has occurred.

*   **Capital Gain**: When the selling price is higher than the purchase price.
*   **Capital Loss**: When the selling price is lower than the purchase price.

The gain or loss is calculated as:

```
Capital Gain/Loss = Proceeds - Cost Basis
```

### Example: Selling Ethereum for USD

Sophia purchased 5 Ethereum (ETH) for $10,000 ($2,000 per ETH). Two years later, she sells the 5 ETH for $15,000 ($3,000 per ETH). Her capital gain is calculated as:

```
Capital Gain = $15,000 - $10,000 = $5,000
```

This $5,000 capital gain must be reported for tax purposes.

In accounting terms, the sale would be recorded as:

*   Debit: $15,000 (Cash/Bank Account)
*   Credit: $10,000 (Cryptocurrency Asset – Ethereum)
*   Credit: $5,000 (Capital Gain)

## Accounting for Crypto-to-Crypto Trades

One of the most significant differences between cryptocurrency and traditional accounting lies in the treatment of crypto-to-crypto trades. Unlike exchanging one fiat currency for another (e.g., USD to EUR), crypto-to-crypto trades are typically treated as taxable events. Each trade is considered a sale of one asset and the purchase of another.

### Example: Trading Bitcoin for Ethereum

Michael trades 1 Bitcoin (BTC) for 30 Ethereum (ETH). At the time of the trade, 1 BTC is worth $50,000, and 1 ETH is worth $1,666.67. Michael originally bought the 1 BTC for $10,000, so he needs to calculate the capital gain on the Bitcoin and record the purchase of Ethereum.

```
Capital Gain on BTC = $50,000 (FMV) - $10,000 (Cost Basis) = $40,000
```

Michael then records the acquisition of 30 ETH with a cost basis of $50,000.

In accounting terms, this would be recorded as:

*   Debit: $50,000 (Cryptocurrency Asset – Ethereum)
*   Credit: $10,000 (Cryptocurrency Asset – Bitcoin)
*   Credit: $40,000 (Capital Gain)

### Transfers Between Wallets and Exchanges

Unlike buying or selling crypto, transferring cryptocurrency between personal wallets or between exchanges does not generally result in a taxable event because ownership doesn’t change. However, it is essential to keep accurate records of transfers to ensure that the transaction history is clear and the cost basis is correctly maintained.

#### Example: Transferring Bitcoin Between Wallets

Anna transfers 0.2 Bitcoin from her wallet on Exchange A to her hardware wallet. The transaction fee for the transfer is 0.001 BTC. Since Anna already owns the Bitcoin, this is not a taxable event, but she must reduce her Bitcoin balance on Exchange A by 0.201 BTC to account for the transfer and transaction fee.

In accounting terms, this would be recorded as:

*   Debit: 0.2 BTC (Hardware Wallet)
*   Credit: 0.201 BTC (Exchange A Wallet)
*   Debit: 0.001 BTC (Transaction Fee)

### Recording Crypto Income

Cryptocurrency can also be received as income, whether through work, staking, or mining. In these cases, the value of the cryptocurrency at the time of receipt must be recorded as income, and any subsequent sale or trade will need to account for capital gains or losses.

#### 1. Receiving Crypto as Payment for Services

When cryptocurrency is received as payment for goods or services, the recipient must record the value of the crypto as income at the time of receipt, based on the fair market value.

##### Example: Receiving Bitcoin as Payment for Services

Julia, a web developer, agrees to build a website for a client in exchange for 0.1 Bitcoin (BTC). At the time of payment, 0.1 BTC is worth $5,000 USD. Julia records $5,000 as business income.

In accounting terms, this would be recorded as:

*   Debit: $5,000 (Cryptocurrency Asset – Bitcoin)
*   Credit: $5,000 (Revenue – Web Development Services)

#### 2. Mining/Staking Income

Cryptocurrency mining and staking are common ways of earning crypto as income. Mining refers to solving complex computational problems to verify transactions on the blockchain, while staking involves participating in a proof-of-stake network by locking up tokens to help validate transactions.

Both mining and staking rewards are considered taxable income when received, and their value must be recorded at the FMV on the date of receipt.

##### Example: Staking Ethereum

Carlos stakes his Ethereum on a proof-of-stake platform and earns 0.05 ETH in rewards after one month. At the time he receives the staking rewards, 0.05 ETH is worth $200 USD. Carlos must report $200 as staking income.

In accounting terms, this would be recorded as:

*   Debit: $200 (Cryptocurrency Asset – Ethereum)
*   Credit: $200 (Income – Staking Rewards)

## Reporting Crypto Gains and Losses

Accurately tracking gains and losses from cryptocurrency transactions is essential for both tax compliance and financial reporting. Since crypto markets are highly volatile, the value of assets can change rapidly, making it crucial to record the fair market value of assets at the time of each transaction.

### Cost Basis and Valuation Methods

The cost basis of a cryptocurrency is the original purchase price, plus any fees. For tax purposes, when selling or trading cryptocurrency, individuals must choose a valuation method to calculate their gains or losses.

Common valuation methods include:

1.  **First In, First Out (FIFO)**: The first cryptocurrency acquired is the first one to be sold. This is often the default method in many countries.
2.  **Last In, First Out (LIFO)**: The last cryptocurrency acquired is the first one to be sold. This method may result in different tax outcomes than FIFO, especially in a rising market.
3.  **Specific Identification**: This method allows the taxpayer to select which specific units of cryptocurrency were sold or traded, allowing for more flexibility in managing gains and losses.

#### Example: Using FIFO for Bitcoin Sales

David buys 1 Bitcoin (BTC) at the following intervals:

*   0.4 BTC for $8,000
*   0.3 BTC for $12,000
*   0.3 BTC for $15,000

Later, he sells 0.6 BTC when Bitcoin is valued at $20,000 per BTC. Using the FIFO method, David would calculate his capital gain as follows:

*   The first 0.4 BTC sold has a cost basis of $8,000.
*   The next 0.2 BTC sold has a cost basis of `0.2 BTC * $12,000 = $2,400`.

Total cost basis = $8,000 + $2,400 = $10,400.

David’s sale proceeds are `0.6 BTC * $20,000 = $12,000`.

```
Capital Gain = $12,000 - $10,400 = $1,600
```

David must report a capital gain of $1,600 on his taxes.
