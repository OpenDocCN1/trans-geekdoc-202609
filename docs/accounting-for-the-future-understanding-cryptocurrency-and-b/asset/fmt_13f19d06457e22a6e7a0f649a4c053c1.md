
Michael transfers 0.5 Bitcoin (BTC) from his Coinbase wallet to his Ledger Nano S cold wallet. He originally bought the Bitcoin for $10,000, and at the time of the transfer, it is worth $25,000. Since the transfer does not constitute a sale, no capital gains are realized, but Michael incurs a transaction fee of 0.0005 BTC.

Michael must record the transaction fee as an expense and ensure that the cost basis of 0.5 BTC remains $10,000. When he eventually sells the Bitcoin, he will use this cost basis to calculate his capital gain.

### 4. Custodial vs. Non-Custodial Wallets: Accounting Implications

The choice between custodial and non-custodial wallets has accounting implications, particularly regarding transaction visibility and control. Custodial wallets, provided by exchanges like Coinbase or Kraken, handle transaction records on behalf of the user. Non-custodial wallets, such as Ledger or MetaMask, require users to manage their own transaction records.

In custodial wallets, users can typically download transaction histories directly from the platform, simplifying the tracking process. However, with non-custodial wallets, users must manually track each transaction, including transfers between wallets and exchanges, which can complicate the accounting process.

#### Example: Managing Transactions in a Non-Custodial Wallet

Emma stores her Ethereum in a Ledger Nano S cold wallet. When she transfers her Ethereum to Uniswap for trading, she manually records the date, transaction amount, and transaction fees. Since Ledger is a non-custodial wallet, Emma must keep accurate records of all transfers to and from her wallet to ensure proper accounting of her crypto assets.

### 5. Crypto Staking and Income from Wallets

Many crypto wallets, especially non-custodial wallets, allow users to participate in staking programs or earn rewards through yield farming. In these cases, the rewards received must be recorded as income at the FMV on the date they are received. These staking rewards may be taxable, depending on the jurisdiction.

#### Example: Receiving Staking Rewards

Jason stakes his Cardano (ADA) in a non-custodial wallet and earns 10 ADA as staking rewards. At the time he receives the rewards, the FMV of 1 ADA is $2. Jason must record $20 as income from staking rewards and report it on his tax return.

### Best Practices for Accounting with Crypto Wallets and Exchanges

To ensure accurate and compliant accounting when using wallets and exchanges, consider the following best practices:

1.  **Consolidate Transaction Data**: Use crypto accounting software, such as Koinly, CoinTracking, or TokenTax, to import transaction histories from multiple exchanges and wallets. This helps consolidate data and provides an accurate record of all transactions.
2.  **Track Transfers and Fees**: Always record transfers between wallets or exchanges, along with any transaction fees incurred. While transfers are not taxable events, fees may impact the cost basis of the cryptocurrency.
3.  **Use Consistent Valuation Methods**: Use a consistent method (e.g., FIFO, LIFO, or Specific Identification) to track the cost basis of cryptocurrency when it is sold or traded. This ensures that capital gains and losses are calculated accurately.
4.  **Record Staking and Mining Income**: If you earn cryptocurrency through staking, mining, or yield farming, record the FMV of the rewards as income on the day they are received.
5.  **Reconcile Wallet Balances**: Periodically reconcile the balances in your wallets and exchanges to ensure that your transaction records are accurate and complete.

# Chapter 8: Reporting Crypto on Financial Statements

As cryptocurrencies become more common in business and investment portfolios, accurately reporting them on financial statements is increasingly important. However, the decentralized and volatile nature of cryptocurrencies creates unique challenges for accountants. In this chapter, we will explore how to report cryptocurrency holdings and transactions in financial statements, including how to classify, measure, and disclose these assets under common accounting frameworks such as GAAP and IFRS.

***

## Classifying Cryptocurrencies on Financial Statements

One of the first steps in reporting cryptocurrency on financial statements is determining how to classify the asset. Cryptocurrencies do not fit neatly into traditional asset categories, such as cash or cash equivalents, due to their volatility and lack of legal tender status. Instead, cryptocurrencies are often classified as:

1.  **Intangible Assets**: Many accounting standards, including IFRS, classify cryptocurrencies as intangible assets because they are not physical and lack a legal claim for conversion to cash.
2.  **Inventory**: For businesses that buy and sell cryptocurrency as part of their normal operations (e.g., cryptocurrency exchanges), cryptocurrencies may be classified as inventory.
3.  **Investments**: In some cases, cryptocurrencies held as long-term investments may be classified as investment assets.
4.  **Financial Assets**: Some argue that cryptocurrencies could be classified as financial assets, although most current accounting standards do not recognize them as such due to their decentralized nature and lack of an issuer.

#### Example: Classifying Bitcoin on a Company's Balance Sheet

Tech Innovators Inc., a company that holds 100 Bitcoin (BTC) as part of its investment portfolio, classifies the Bitcoin as intangible assets under IFRS because Bitcoin does not have a physical form, and the company does not use it as legal tender. The Bitcoin is reported under non-current assets on the balance sheet, as it is held for long-term investment purposes.

***

## Measuring Cryptocurrencies on Financial Statements

Once the classification of cryptocurrency is determined, the next step is to decide how to measure its value on the financial statements. Cryptocurrencies are highly volatile, which complicates the measurement process. Common methods for measuring cryptocurrency on financial statements include:

1.  **Cost Model**: Under the cost model, cryptocurrencies are recorded at their original purchase price (cost basis) and are not revalued unless an impairment occurs. This method is typically used for intangible assets under IFRS and some GAAP standards.
2.  **Fair Value Model**: Under the fair value model, cryptocurrencies are revalued at the end of each reporting period based on their current market price. This method is commonly used for investments and inventory in companies that actively trade cryptocurrency.
3.  **Impairment Testing**: If the value of cryptocurrency declines below its purchase price, companies may need to perform impairment testing and write down the value of the asset on the balance sheet.

#### Example: Using the Cost Model for Ethereum Holdings

Blockchain Enterprises Ltd. holds 500 Ethereum (ETH) tokens purchased at $2,000 per ETH. At the time of the balance sheet reporting, the price of Ethereum has dropped to $1,800 per token. Under the cost model, the Ethereum is recorded at its original cost basis of $1,000,000 (500 ETH × $2,000).
