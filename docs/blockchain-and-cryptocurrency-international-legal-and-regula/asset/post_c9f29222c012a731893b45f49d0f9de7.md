
## 1.37 *Introduction*

**1.37** In **Chapter 11** we compare and contrast decentralised organisations with DAOs and explain that the key difference is that DAOs are truly autonomous in that the need for human involvement is completely eradicated. A decentralised organisation and a DAO may both incorporate smart contracts but the decentralised organisation will still require some level of human intervention; the DAO, however, is human operator free and runs entirely on its code. The authors also examine the legal status of a DAO and issues that arise from this as well as the growing challenge from the expansion of IoT and smart cities. In short, as the IoT evolves and expands so will the risk as smart contracts and completely autonomous technology control a spectrum of tangibles and intangibles within our interconnected smart city environments.

## GLOBAL REGULATION

**1.38** The international, borderless nature of blockchain means that, in our view, any book limiting itself to looking at it through a local domestic prism fails its readers. Moreover, regulations and the acceptance of blockchain and cryptocurrencies vary so widely between countries and states that one should examine each in turn. This approach is particularly apposite to entrepreneurs, businesses and organisations whose services and activities straddle various parts of the globe. In an interconnected World it is becoming ever more difficult to close one's eyes to regulations that may snag activity begun in one country or region but accessible to other jurisdictions.

**1.39** A further advantage of providing the reader with a global view is that it better enables an informed choice of jurisdiction(s) where a decision can be made as to which countries offer the better location possibilities. With this in mind, we have again provided separate regional chapters for the UK and EU (**Chapter 4**), North America (**Chapter 5**) and the Rest of the World (**Chapter 6**). Each chapter contains a country-by-country (and state-by-state for the US) discussion of the regulations and challenges for the countries/states within that region. We identify the front runners and explain why those jurisdictions provide clear advantages for cryptocurrency and blockchain enterprises.

**1.40** It should be understood that when looking at a jurisdiction's potential there may be other considerations and local laws or customs that ought to influence the decision; the authors would always advise taking advice from local lawyers and expert advisers on the ground in any considered location to ensure its suitability or fitness for the intended purpose.

## INITIAL COIN OFFERINGS

**1.41** **Chapter 7** focuses on the use of initial coin offerings (‘ICOs’) an area that has generated a huge amount of controversy. $7 billion worldwide was raised via ICOs between January and June 2018, yet less than half of all ICOs survive four months after the offering.^6^ while almost half of ICOs sold in 2017 failed by February 2018.^7^ The concern around ICOs stems from their history of use as vehicles for fraud and we consequently explain their regulation and examine whether they are, on one view, an ingenious new method for raising capital away from structured funding or, on another, a method to extort money from naïve speculators.

**1.42** An ICO is essentially crowdfunding using cryptocurrencies. Although traditionally a public offering (crowdfunded), private ICOs are becoming more common especially when tech ventures seek to raise capital. In an ICO, ‘tokens’ or coins (technically also cryptocurrency) are sold to speculators or investors in exchange for legal tender or other cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin or Ethereum. The tokens sold are promoted as future functional units of currency if or when the ICO's funding goal is met and the project launches. In some cases, the tokens are required to use the system for its purposes or may give holders voting rights that can be used to determine the projects into which funds are invested.

**1.43** We examine the regulation of ICOs in the US, EU, China and compare it with the approach in the UK. In the UK there is a real danger that an ICO may fall to be classified as regulated activity that, without Financial Conduct Authority authorisation, may constitute the commission of a criminal offence. The potential trip wires and the activities that might fall within the regulatory perimeter are set out in detail.

**1.44** The US stance needs to be understood because that understanding is useful when looking to appreciate the UK and EU treatment of ICOs. In the US, ICOs or token sales may be deemed an ‘investment contract’, which falls to be treated as a security under section 2(a)(1) of the Securities Act and section 3(a)(10) of the Exchange Act.^8^ Importantly, the SEC stresses that US federal security laws will apply to an ICO if it falls to be considered as an investment contract. ## NATURAL RESOURCES INDUSTRY

**1.45** Blockchain technology will likely have an impact across almost every aspect of the natural resources industry, and **Chapter 10** is devoted to studying the potential impact on natural resources. The potential benefits range from real time data registry as gas and oil come out of the ground, or electricity is produced, to the possibility of peer-to-peer energy selling, disintermediation and the creation of a smart energy grid. These benefits are often, and first by the Energy Web Foundation, grouped under the headings of:

- decarbonisation;
- decentralisation;
- digitisation; and
- democratisation.^9^

**1.46** These four interconnected categories are often defined as the long-term aims of blockchain implementation in the natural resources industry, and we discuss the possibilities as well as the regulation that is needed to help address the world's natural resource problems. Those problems are usually grouped into the ‘trilemma’ comprising:

- security, given the World's finite resources;
- decarbonisation; and
- affordability.

The war in Ukraine has brought these concerns into sharp focus, especially the need for affordable oil and gas. **Chapter 10** examines the position through the blockchain prism and considers whether its application might be able to resolve or mitigate the concerns around our energy and natural resource needs.
