
## 12.28 The Metaverse

12.28 To the authors it seems clear that in many jurisdictions this notion of joint authorship will apply to avatars where the avatar user applies more than a minimal level of creativity and is consequently able to demonstrate an original expression of authorship that has become fixed in a tangible form of expression, namely the avatar.

12.29 In order to try and tackle these problems before they arise it will be necessary to first appreciate where there is a contract such as an end-user license that deals with the question of the avatar's copyright. It is submitted that a derivative authorship is subordinate to joint authorship with the result that a derivative work author only owns the copyright in the original material that had been added to the derivative work and does not have rights in the underlying work. If the derivative work creator should lose the right to use the underlying work or that right is revoked, then they cannot exploit the derivative work because that would then infringe the copyright in the underlying work. If an avatar were to be regarded as a derivative work, then the author of that derivative work would have rights in the avatar which might prevent others from copying it, but those rights would not allow them to use the avatar without permission from the owner of the copyright in the underlying work namely the Metaverse platform owner.

12.30 The derivative ownership/authorship situation is most likely to arrive where an avatar is based upon an existing copyrighted character or where the avatar is based upon the template provided by the platform owner and the personalisation or adaptation is not such that the degree of originality to attain the form of joint ownership has been attained. Much will then depend on the degree of adaptation that an avatar user is allowed to make and actually does make. To make matters even more complicated avatars could be considered a collective work in the same way as an anthology encyclopaedia in which various contributions which are separate and independent are collected to form a whole. On such a view an avatar might be considered to be no more than an individual contribution to the assembled collective work. In such scenario, because each contribution to the collective work is viewed as distinct from copyright in a collective work as a whole, each user would likely be the creator or author of their avatar, they would own their avatar's copyright whilst the platform owner and Metaverse provider would be considered the creator of the computer programme and any other displays and/or visuals that the programme was able to generate without significant input from that avatar user.

12.31 In terms of tips for both Metaverse platform owners and users of copyrighted works in the Metaverse, the authors suggest that all contractual agreements and licences that relate to copyrighted works should be reviewed and checked in detail so that their scope and effect is understood. Wherever possible there should be registration of copyrights in assets and software to be used in the Metaverse. In addition, technological measures to prevent unauthorised distribution of the copyrighted works and to prevent security circumvention should be implemented wherever possible. As with trademarks there should also be careful monitoring to see when a copyright infringement might have taken place and to assess the appropriate cyberspace. This is already the position in relation to the internet and the Web 2.0 version we currently experience. Indeed, in relation to the UK, the authors wrote Cybersecurity Law and Practice, 1 which sets out the framework of civil and criminal laws governing cyberspace. Cyberspace is essentially the space of the Metaverse, and it is submitted that the same legal framework, the law of computers and the online, digital environment, will apply to the Metaverse. That legal framework includes cybercrime and offences under the Computer Misuse Act 1990 and liability under the Data Protection Act 2018 and the General Data Protection Regulations 2018.

12.36 The Computer Misuse Act 1990 will remain useful for prosecuting cyber criminality in the Metaverse. A computer is not defined in the Act but the interpretation adopted by the courts is very wide and, as well as the contemporary meaning of computer, includes any device capable of storing, processing or retrieving information. Section 1 creates a hacking offence where a person interferes with data or someone else’s computer without permission for the specific data or computer. This is the core unauthorised access or what is colloquially termed the hacking offence and an additional facilitation offence is created by section 2 where the unauthorised access, or hacking, is with intent to commit another offence. Section 3 criminalises acts to impair the working of the computer, such as a ransomware, Trojan horse or denial of service attack and is so widely interpreted as to cover the situation where information that told a lie about itself was inserted into a computer; for example information that purported to derive from an authorised source when, in fact, it did not, would constitute the offence. One can imagine countless situations where such a misrepresentation and offence might occur in a Metaverse context.

12.37 In addition to these offences the Act further criminalises the secondary parties who prop up cyber criminals by making, supplying or obtaining the electronic or other materials for use in these attacks. They then will be caught by the legislation when we move in to the Metaverse and Web 3. Significantly these offences can be prosecuted extraterritorially, so beyond the borders of the UK and across the globe where a significant link to the UK can be demonstrated. This link will exist not only where the accused was a British National but also where any computer in the cyber attack chain was located in the UK. This could be the device used to launch the attack, the target or victim computer or any computer in between. In the context of the Metaverse this will enable a prosecution and UK jurisdiction where cyber crime touches a UK based computer.

12.38 The Online Safety Bill is, at the time of writing, still passing through the House of Commons and not yet law. If one assumes it is enacted in its current or very similar form, then the question is whether the regime it creates for a regulated online service will apply to the Metaverse.

The current definition of regulated service is quite wide and made up of several ingredients. So, a service is regulated and caught by the online safety regime if:

1. it is either a user-to-user service or a search service;
2. it has links with the UK; and
3. it is not exempt pursuant to Schedule 1.

If one assumes condition 2 is satisfied in that there is a link to the UK, though this be fact sensitive, the question is whether the definition will catch services in the Metaverse.

### 12.39 The definition of user-to-user service has a number of essential ingredients that must all be present:

- an internet service;
- content that is generated or uploaded or shared by a user;
- the encountering of that content by other users.

A search function within a user-to-user platform would fall to be assessed under the user-to-user provisions and a search engine which picked up the metaverse would still be caught as a general search engine. If a search engine specialised in metaverse sites, there is a question as to whether such a search engine would be caught. This depends on whether metaverse sites could be defined as websites which, in the author’s intuitive view, is a possible if not probable outcome. The authors’ expectation is that the Metaverse will be subject to the Online Safety Bill when it becomes law and consequently those providing these regulated services will be regulated by OFCOM.

### 12.40 The focus is on regulating three types of content:

1. Illegal content.
2. Content that is harmful to children.
3. Content that is legal but harmful to adults.

All regulated services would have to protect users from illegal content and will be additional duties placed on providers services likely to be accessed by children.

### 12.41 In summary the Online Services Bill imposes legal requirements on:

- (a) providers of internet services which allow users to encounter content generated, uploaded or shared by other users (‘user-to-user services’);
- (b) providers of search engines which enable users to search multiple websites and databases (‘search services’);
- (c) providers of internet services on which provider pornographic content (pornographic content that is published by a provider and is not user generated) is published or displayed.
