It is however quite clear to the authors that any acquisition of virtual assets might turn out to be no more than a virtual and not complete acquisition where the surrounding contractual scaffolding does not properly support that purchase. A further caveat to add to the very large caveat discussed above is that even where the contractual documentation appears favourable and supportive of the purchase the user and potential buyer should check to ensure there is no clause or term that allows the owner the ability to amend, extend or otherwise change their terms of use at any time. It should also be checked that any revisions to the contract must be notified to the user. The danger otherwise is that a favourable contract can be altered into an unfavourable one with at best little notice to the user and at worst no notice at all. Essentially because the terms of use are often also a code of conduct that the user must follow there is a huge onus on the user to ensure they are complaint at all times and even where the user is to be given notice of any changes to that code of conduct and terms of use problems will arise unless the contract is repeatedly reviewed so as to avoid missing a change that might be catastrophic to the user's intentions.

# 12.14 The reason for all of this is quite simply that the Metaverse's platforms are governed centrally by an owner controller who would likely be incentivised to fulfil its own optimal commercial interests. The Metaverse is often seen as synonymous with blockchain because blockchain is a fundamental technology that allows commercial transactions to take place in a virtual landscape. Unless the ownership and central control of such a landscape is addressed, however, or rectified through increased competition such that a more democratic system or form of decentralised ownership is in place then the authors fear these problems will persist. One other way forward might be that the law itself governs the Metaverse and for example implies contractual terms or otherwise imposes regulations aimed at ensuring a fair outcome for the user. At the time of writing however such legislation is yet to be in place in any jurisdiction anywhere. The authors will also pose the question of jurisdiction and which jurisdiction might apply to the Metaverse in any event given that the Metaverse is of course a virtual world and is not thus in any jurisdictional space arguably cyberspace? The conundrum of jurisdiction is examined in more detail later in this chapter.

# Intellectual Property

# 12.15 In broad terms IP refers to creations of the intellect such as inventions, designs, brands, artwork, music and other creations of the human mind. The rights to this cerebral property or IP are established, protected, enforced and promoted through various legal means. These include patents which usually protect technical inventions, trademarks which are associated with products and protect the brand, copyright which quite literally is the right to copy music, art and literature and the design rights of products and logos.
