# Trademarks

**12.16**  A trademark is essentially a logo or other mark such as a word, a design or representation that serves to show the source of those goods; in effect a trademark can be regarded as a brand logo or mark. It follows that trademarks are important to businesses in establishing and retaining their brands because use of that trademark by another party might lead consumers to believe that the goods or services were being provided by the true owner of the trademark. Trademarks are commonplace and very useful in protecting a businesses’ brand. The same would doubtless be true in the Metaverse. The question that will frequently arise in the Metaverse however will be whether a trademark can take effect in the same way as it would in the real world. Can a trademark be enforced by its owner in a virtual world in the same way as it could in the real world? If one looks at the gaming world it can be seen that there is currently no definitive answer to this as much will be on the circumstances of each particular case and in particular the use to which the trademark is being put by any unauthorised user.

**12.17**  In *E.S.S. Entertainment 2000 v Inc v Rock Star Videos, Inc* (2008) (Ninth Circuit) the question was whether a depiction of a strip club in Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas was a trademark breach of the logo’s owner who operated a strip club in the real world. On this occasion the Court found the use of the strip club in the gaming scenario or virtual world was not as such to be actionable because it did not infringe the owner’s trademark and rights because on balance they found it be an artistic expression that was protected and it was decided that it was unlikely to cause confusion as such that a consumer might believe the video game was produced by the strip club. Here then the protection afforded to artistic expression married with the determination that a consumer would likely not think that the strip club was actually responsible for the depiction of this gaming world strip club was the reasoning that defeated the claim.

**12.18**  In the case of *Leo Pellegrino v Epic Games, Inc* (ED 2020) a claim was brought by a saxophonist that alleged that a game called Fortnite included an avatar playing a saxophone that had copied his particular dance moves and thus was an infringement of his trademark. The claim failed because it was found, perhaps not unsurprisingly, that the claim would better to have been founded on copyright law rather than trademark but in the event the claimant withdrew his case. Nonetheless what these cases show is that trademark protection in the Metaverse is far from guaranteed and steps will need to be taken by trademark owners to try and optimise their chances of having trademark protection in this virtual Metaverse world.
