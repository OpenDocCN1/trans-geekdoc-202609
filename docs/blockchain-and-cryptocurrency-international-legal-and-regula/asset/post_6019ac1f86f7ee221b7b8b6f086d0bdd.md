12.11 The Metaverse is effectively the digital land or digital platform on which the virtual asset such as a bar or other structure sits and many contractual terms will additionally allow the Metaverse’s owner to evict the user or delete or remove ‘its’ virtual assets using its discretion. Whilst the contractual terms will vary from Metaverse to Metaverse it is hard to envisage a Metaverse owner not granting themselves overwhelming influence and power in respect of the Metaverse’s use. This would at least be a position until a Metaverse’s user has a bargaining position that might mean the Metaverse has to accommodate and provide other rights and entitlements. The authors have already noted that in a Metaverse such as the sandbox, the owning company reserves the right at its sole discretion to remove, delete, use or have access to a user’s digital assets. One can certainly argue that as the Metaverse arena becomes more competitive commercial considerations may dilute these sorts of terms but the position will still be that the legal framework for the Metaverse will be decided by its owner controller in the terms of use that are imposed at the point of entry. There will doubtless be numerous arguments in courts and through lawyers’ correspondence in that certain terms are unfair or enforceable and the like but in the absence of a successful challenge the starting position will be the contractual one in many situations.

12.12 The other key point that must be emphasised here is that when one looks at the Metaverse many of the key features such as the visuals and buildings and immersive world of augmented reality exists outside of the blockchain. The characters, avatars and all the other wonderful things that go to make up the Metaverse surroundings are of course sitting on the owning company’s private servers and are inaccessible to the user. The user may have a NFT that sits on a blockchain and shouts to their purchase of a virtual asset but that virtual asset resides outside of blockchain in the private, coded owning company database and even those NFTs may not be safe from the owning company if there is a contractual term that allows it to interfere with or forfeit your NFT in a situation where for example you have been expelled from the Metaverse or otherwise deemed guilty of a breach of contract. In practical and indeed technological terms this may be difficult for the owning company to achieve but the authors can see a situation where a case might be brought against the NFT owner and relief might be sought by way of an order of mandamus enforcing the contractual forfeiture.

12.13 The authors will spare specific Metaverse owners from embarrassment but, safe to say, these very provisions have already been spotted in current Metaverse terms of use. When it comes to ‘buying’ virtual property in the Metaverse the proverb to have in mind might be ‘buyer beware’ or perhaps more accurately, ‘not all the Metaverse’s streets and assets are paved with gold’. This is not of course to say that there may not be a good commercial or other reason for acquiring virtual assets in the Metaverse or that in all instances such acquisition will be problematic. It is however quite clear to the authors that any acquisition of virtual assets might turn out to be no more than a virtual and not complete acquisition where the surrounding contractual scaffolding does not properly support that purchase. A further caveat to add to the very large caveat discussed above is that even where the contractual documentation appears favourable and supportive of the purchase the user and potential buyer should check to ensure there is no clause or term that allows the owner the ability to amend, extend or otherwise change their terms of use at any time. It should also be checked that any revisions to the contract must be notified to the user. The danger otherwise is that a favourable contract can be altered into an unfavourable one with at best little notice to the user and at worst no notice at all. Essentially because the terms of use are often also a code of conduct that the user must follow there is a huge onus on the user to ensure they are compliant at all times and even where the user is to be given notice of any changes to that code of conduct and terms of use problems will arise unless the contract is repeatedly reviewed so as to avoid missing a change that might be catastrophic to the user's intentions.

12.14 The reason for all of this is quite simply that the Metaverse's platforms are governed centrally by an owner controller who would likely be incentivised to fulfil its own optimal commercial interests. The Metaverse is often seen as synonymous with blockchain because blockchain is a fundamental technology that allows commercial transactions to take place in a virtual landscape. Unless the ownership and central control of such a landscape is addressed, however, or rectified through increased competition such that a more democratic system or form of decentralised ownership is in place then the authors fear these problems will persist. One other way forward might be that the law itself governs the Metaverse and for example implies contractual terms or otherwise imposes regulations aimed at ensuring a fair outcome for the user. At the time of writing however such legislation is yet to be in place in any jurisdiction anywhere. The authors will also pose the question of jurisdiction and which jurisdiction might apply to the Metaverse in any event given that the Metaverse is of course a virtual world and is not thus in any jurisdictional space arguably cyberspace? The conundrum of jurisdiction is examined in more detail later in this chapter.

## Intellectual Property

12.15 In broad terms IP refers to creations of the intellect such as inventions, designs, brands, artwork, music and other creations of the human mind. The rights to this cerebral property or IP are established, protected, enforced and promoted through various legal means. These include patents which usually protect technical inventions, trademarks which are associated with products and protect the brand, copyright which quite literally is the right to copy music, art and literature and the design rights of products and logos. The advent of the internet brought with it fresh challenges to IP as laws designed and enacted in an analogue age struggled to keep pace with the new context and situations that resulted from digitisation. It was the global, borderless nature of the internet that caused particular difficulties as well as the fragmented and often inadequate framework of laws within each of the myriad, connected jurisdictions. The metaverse will prove even more challenging and the problems currently posed by the one-dimensional internet will be cubed by Web 3 and the metaverse.

### Trademarks

**12.16**  A trademark is essentially a logo or other mark such as a word, a design or representation that serves to show the source of those goods; in effect a trademark can be regarded as a brand logo or mark. It follows that trademarks are important to businesses in establishing and retaining their brands because use of that trademark by another party might lead consumers to believe that the goods or services were being provided by the true owner of the trademark. Trademarks are commonplace and very useful in protecting a businesses’ brand. The same would doubtless be true in the Metaverse. The question that will frequently arise in the Metaverse however will be whether a trademark can take effect in the same way as it would in the real world. Can a trademark be enforced by its owner in a virtual world in the same way as it could in the real world? If one looks at the gaming world it can be seen that there is currently no definitive answer to this as much will be on the circumstances of each particular case and in particular the use to which the trademark is being put by any unauthorised user.

**12.17**  In *E.S.S. Entertainment 2000 v Inc v Rock Star Videos, Inc* (2008) (Ninth Circuit) the question was whether a depiction of a strip club in Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas was a trademark breach of the logo’s owner who operated a strip club in the real world. On this occasion the Court found the use of the strip club in the gaming scenario or virtual world was not as such to be actionable because it did not infringe the owner’s trademark and rights because on balance they found it be an artistic expression that was protected and it was decided that it was unlikely to cause confusion as such that a consumer might believe the video game was produced by the strip club. Here then the protection afforded to artistic expression married with the determination that a consumer would likely not think that the strip club was actually responsible for the depiction of this gaming world strip club was the reasoning that defeated the claim.

**12.18**  In the case of *Leo Pellegrino v Epic Games, Inc* (ED 2020) a claim was brought by a saxophonist that alleged that a game called Fortnite included an avatar playing a saxophone that had copied his particular dance moves and thus was an infringement of his trademark. The claim failed because it was found, perhaps not unsurprisingly, that the claim would better to have been founded on copyright law rather than trademark but in the event the claimant withdrew his case. Nonetheless what these cases show is that trademark protection in the Metaverse is far from guaranteed and steps will need to be taken by trademark owners to try and optimise their chances of having trademark protection in this virtual Metaverse world.
