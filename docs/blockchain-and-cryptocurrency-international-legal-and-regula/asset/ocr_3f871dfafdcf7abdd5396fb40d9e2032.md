6.27 This approach can be demonstrated by the ‘Administrative Provisions for Managing Blockchain Information’ Regulations issued by the Cyberspace Administration in 2019. This stipulates that blockchain platforms must collect their user’s data and then share it with the state; in essence then anything that is said or done through the platform will be subject to state scrutiny and the access by the state means there is no anonymity or privacy for users. Moreover, EOS, the state’s preferred blockchain is configured so that certain representatives must validate transactions and all decisions are approved by main nodes of which the majority are in China such that the state can more easily assert control. Add to this that in China, the government version of blockchain allows transactions to be changed and the system brought to a standstill and one might question whether this is blockchain at all. What is not in doubt is that blockchain has been applied across diverse industries where it can be seen to add value, examples include policing where its immutability suits the preservation of state gathered evidence and the secure management of data on health, dissidents and soldiers’ performance.

6.28 The Blockchain Service Network, whilst striving to facilitate the development, deployment and maintenance of blockchain in China made clear in its White Paper (2020) that there are distinctions to be drawn between a permission-less blockchain which operates in a transparent decentralised manner and an owner designed permissioned blockchain which does not. The permission-less framework would be difficult if not impossible to achieve in China under its current regulations.

# Gibraltar

6.29 In comparison, Gibraltar is a leading light that has actively legislated to regulate the operation of cryptocurrencies within its jurisdiction. It currently requires the registration of firms that use distributed ledger technology (DLT) to store or transmit value belonging to others. The registration process involves the Gibraltar Financial Services Commission (‘GFSC’) reviewing the application and, if satisfied that certain criteria are met, a licence may be granted, enabling the holder to operate a business using DLT.

6.30 On 1 January 2018, the government of Gibraltar introduced the Financial Services (Distributed Ledger Technology Providers) Regulations 2017, under the Financial Services (Investment and Fiduciary Services) Act. The aim of the legislation is to protect consumers, protect the reputation of Gibraltar as a well-regulated and safe environment for firms that use DLT, and enable Gibraltar to prosper from the use and growth of new financial technology.¹
