6.45 ICOs will be outside the regulatory perimeter where the tokens are viewed as unregulated tokens such as utility tokens. Accordingly, utility tokens which confer few rights save to access a platform’s product either now or at some future point will be outside the scope of Hong Kong’s securities legislation. This is in line with SFC guidance though it has not provided further guidance on those features or rights of an ICO token that are likely to render it a security.

6.46 In the years from 2019 the ICO activity in Hong Kong has ground to a virtual standstill as a result of vociferous warnings from the SFC combined with the market confusion as to when a token might be considered a security. This has apparently led to the perception of risk outweighing the perceived benefits.

6.47 Securities Token Offerings (STOs) unlike ICOs are generally regarded as falling within the regulatory perimeter and consequently have more regulatory certainty in some jurisdictions but it is still uncertain how they are treated in Hong Kong due to the lack of clarity as to what constitutes a security. The SFC’s view is that security tokens are digital assets which, akin to traditional securities, bestow economic rights on the token holder or provide other rights such as ownership. Conversely, where a token passes a debt owed by its issuer the token will probably fall to be a debenture token. Consideration will also need to be given to when any rights under the token will arise, if they are future rights then is a form of collective investment scheme or other regulated activity being created that shifts the token within the regulatory perimeter.

# India

6.48 In 2018 the Reserve Bank of India banned banks and any regulated financial institutions from ‘dealing with or settling virtual currencies’ and regulation prohibited the trade of cryptocurrencies on Indian exchanges which were given a deadline of 6 July 2018, to cease their business. In 2020, a landmark Supreme Court decision declared the ban unconstitutional, removed the prohibition of cryptocurrency and allowed the exchanges to reopen. Further to this the Reserve Bank of India has issued numerous warning circulars as to the risks posed by users, holders and traders of cryptocurrencies but, as at the date of writing, cryptocurrencies are neither expressly prohibited nor expressly regulated. It is plain, however, that in India cryptocurrencies are not viewed as acceptable tender and the government is resistant to their being part of a payments system. Indeed, the official view from government has repeatedly been to emphasise that cryptocurrency is not legal tender or coin, that it will do all it can to eliminate it from the payments system and from its use in financing illicit activities.
