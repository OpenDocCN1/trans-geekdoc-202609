
## 12.3 The Metaverse

One could argue that spatial-temporal consistency is the single most important characteristic of a Metaverse. In relation to the Metaverse this is sometimes referred to as hyper-spatial temporality, in effect a world that, whilst parallel and virtual to the real world, is able to defeat the constraints of physical space, time and nature and consequently offer an experience that is not only immersive but supernatural in the experience and opportunities it provides. To return to the internet extension analogy, if the internet currently provides a one-dimensional experience the Metaverse would be a 3D, immersive, augmented reality experience.

12.3 It is important to note that the Metaverse as it exists now in 2022 will likely not be the Metaverse landscape that will be possible in a decade or decades. Because the creation of a Metaverse requires a confluence of technologies it follows that what is possible is constantly evolving and the augmented parallel, virtual hyper-spatial, temporal landscape achievable today is far short of what will be possible in years to come. One major flaw in what is possible today is the need for the interoperability. Interoperability means the ability for one Metaverse platform or world to be fully integrated with another so that it is accessible for the user. There are barriers to interoperability not least that some companies for example, Apple, arguably design their products so that they are not interoperable. This is not meant as any criticism because one can argue that the current business model of many technology companies is such that interoperability defeats or at least damages their business model which relies on a consumer following that is not only devoted to but to some extent at least restricted to its brand.

## THE METAVERSE'S REGULATORY FRAMEWORK

### Contract

12.7 When one looks at the laws and regulations that will likely govern any Metaverse the starting point has to be contract law. The reason for this is quite simple; there are already a number of Metaverses and each is owned and controlled by its company or organisational owner. It is clear to the authors that whatever Metaverse one seeks to enter the first proceeding step must be to agree to its terms. Those can be terms of service or terms of use or end user licence agreement but whatever badge is given to that document it essentially sets out the contractual position between the user and the Metaverse's owner. These contracts are intended to be legally binding documents that set out in detail the rights, duties and expectations of those who use the Metaverse and the terms and conditions will govern, in some fine detail, exactly what is and what is not possible. Those contractual terms may not only deal with the concept of ownership in the Metaverse but may also extend to other aspects such as Intellectual Property rights and the powers and decisions of the owners in the context of the Metaverse.

12.8 We will look at this in a little more detail later in this chapter but, safe to say, the usual and expected position is that these Metaverse terms of use will put the owner in a position of strength over the user. Moreover, a user not only has very little bargaining position, if any bargaining position at all at the point of entry but most users will not even read the contractual documentation before scrolling down and clicking their acceptance. These detailed and usually lengthy contractual documents will contain many legal terms, legal terms of art and other intricacies that the non-lawyer user may simply be unaccustomed to or uninterested in because the tacit belief, however misplaced, will generally be that this is no more than a mere formality in order to enable entry into the particular Metaverse in question. It is perhaps ironic that because Metaverse as they currently stand are owned by companies, they are subject to central ownership and control in a manner that is the opposite of decentralisation and the aims of blockchain. However, it is not just the fact that blockchain purists may be offended by this central control position but that in terms of virtual property ownership the Metaverse will present some significant challenges due to the overwhelming influence on control of a single company owner. It is these terms of service, these contractual documents that set out the legal landscape and in the experience of the authors from examining current examples, the ownership of virtual property may actually be nothing of the sort and, as we will amplify later, ownership is a very challenging notion within this contractual framework.

12.9 The notion of virtual ownership in the mind of many users is that it is possible to buy virtual property in the Metaverse through the use of a non-fungible token (NFT). The notion runs that you acquire virtual property and in doing so you assign or purchase a non-fungible token which is immutable proof to the world of your ownership. That NFT records the transaction on a blockchain which due to its decentralised digital ledger is beyond revision or deletion. Further reassurance is brought by a NFT being kept in a wallet and that wallet cannot be accessed in any effective way without the private key. The theme of decentralisation then brings many users to believe that this is a very safe way of acquiring and owning virtual property. In addition, many users profess that interoperability, the idea that the technological feature of the Metaverse will be the ability to use property acquired across a wider, universal interlinked Metaverse, is yet another technological feature of the Metaverse that is currently more mythical than actual. In short, the problem with acquiring or believing one has acquired virtual property through an NFT is that the NFT and the virtual property whether it be a bar, restaurant, fashion item or anything else are not one and the same.

12.10 The conclusion to be drawn then is that one can acquire and legally own a NFT which immutably signifies the virtual asset purchase but you may not legally own or possess in any meaningful sense the virtual asset itself because that virtual asset can effectively be taken away from you or given away by the platform owner should the terms of service or other contractual use documentation allow it to do so. By way of example one might buy a Mona Lisa in the Metaverse and be provided with a NFT evidencing that purchase only to discover subsequently that the Mona Lisa had disappeared from the Metaverse and had apparently been deleted from the platform's database such that all one was left with was the NFT itself. In the author's view this misunderstanding and problem with purchasing virtual property in the Metaverse has arisen from a conflation with real property. Virtual property is not the same as real property or land. If one purchases land for example and is given title deeds those title deeds reflect the ownership of the land but in addition to that the land that is owned, solid bricks and mortar for example if a building, have also been acquired and purchased assuming the conveyance was properly concluded.
