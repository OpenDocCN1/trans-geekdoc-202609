# 7.10 Initial coin offerings

+   (i) No-cap: project without any limits regarding financing. The ICO will continue selling tokens to an indefinite number. No-cap may be appropriate when the venture has multiple projects planned all of which require funding.
+   (ii) Soft-cap: minimum limit of capital achieved in order to proceed with the project.
+   (iii) Hard-cap: maximum amount of capital accepted with a limited number of tokens.
+   (iv) Collect and return: a hard cap is defined and, if surpassed, the tokens are distributed with respect to the ratio of the hard cap to the total funds received.
+   (v) Dynamic ceiling: several hard-cap limits are defined and kept secret.

It is also conceivable that a combination of these could be used within a more complex ICO.

# 7.10 The funding generated may only be considered one aspect of a successful ICO. Coins or tokens are designed as a digital medium of value exchange based on the blockchain, which can operate independently and should be traded between investors.16 Consequently, if there is no secondary market for the tokens generated in an ICO can it be considered a success?

# 7.11 Having a secondary market and a tradable token is an essential characteristic for an ICO. This is a predominant characteristic of ICO projects and the main distinguishing point from crowdfunding.17 Tokens from an ICO must be listed on at least one crypto exchange. Arguably the measure of success is the tradability of the token on the secondary market rather than the funds generated.

# 7.12 The first ICOs fell outside existing regulations;18 however, since these initial offerings, state actors have been quick to either interpret existing regulatory provisions to include ICOs or, in some jurisdictions, such as China, they have created new legislative provisions to prohibit ICOs.
