7.113 Perhaps in response to debate, the ban was then extended to security token offerings and airdrops, in December 2018; and on 15 February 2019 China blocked online access to offshore ICOs and crypto exchanges. China’s internet regulator, the Cyberspace Administration of China, announced new regulations which apply to blockchain information service providers. This are, in broad terms, entities using blockchain technology to provide online information services to the public. The new regulations imposed restrictions on blockchain anonymity and required users to provide their real names and national ID card numbers or phone numbers when registering for a blockchain service.

7.114 Blockchain service providers need to register with the government and are required to censor content ‘deemed to pose a threat to national security’.57 They must also keep a record of information published by users and disclose this information to the government. Arguably these regulations are an attempt to target the use of blockchain technology to bypass China’s censorship of the internet with individuals using the Ethereum blockchain to escape censorship.

7.115 In a controlled economy, ICOs, or any crowdfunding, may represent an unacceptable risk to the state’s power. China have sought to prevent ICOs ‘democratising’ venture financing through prohibition and regulation but China has now launched its own digital currency: the Digital Currency Electronic Payment (‘DCEP’), or the ‘digital yuan’. The digital yuan is issued by the People’s Bank of China (‘PBOC’), and is essentially a digital version of the RMB. Initially the digital yuan was on trial in four major cities (Shenzhen, Suzhou, Chengdu and Xiong’an) but since April 2021, testing has been expanded to six additional regions: Shanghai, Hainan, Changsha, Xi’an, Qingdao and Dalian. Once fully tested the digital yuan would be used for both domestic and international transactions.

7.116 Alibaba’s Alipay and Tencent’s WeChat Pay are common within China, which is growing towards a cashless society, and the introduction of the digital yuan suggests China will become the first country in the world to put a central bank digital currency into use.

7.117 Critics have suggested that rather than democratising, the digital yuan is the ultimate surveillance able to capture every economic transaction on an immutable blockchain. This may be of little concern to legitimate investors who could see the digital yuan as a way to access the lucrative Chinese market. But beware. The regulatory landscape in China poses a potential risk of retrospective legislative oversight.
