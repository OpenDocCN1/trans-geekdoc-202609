# *Status as Property*

**3.4** Cryptocurrencies are a species of property which it is possible to declare trusts over, obtain proprietary remedies in respect of, and follow and trace in asset recovery exercises. It would have been deeply concerning had that not been the case, given cryptocurrencies’ other attraction as an instrument of fraud.

**3.5** In *AA v Persons Unknown* [2019] EWHC 3556 (Comm),⁹ is a significant case where an insurer was granted a proprietary injunction as part of its strategy to recover a ransomware payment which had been negotiated and paid in Bitcoin. In order to regain access to the IT systems (that had been encrypted with malware), the hacker(s) demanded that the insured company transfer 109.25 Bitcoins (equivalent at that time to $950,000) to a specified account in exchange for the decryption software. The company was insured against certain cyber-related incidents by an UK English insurance company. After the ransom had been paid, the insurer hired consultants who tracked the Bitcoin payments to a specific address linked to the Bitfinex exchange. The insurer sought a proprietary injunction to recover the Bitcoins during which the Court gave considerable weight to the then recent UK Jurisdictional Task Force (‘UKJT 2019’) Legal Statement on Cryptoassets and Smart Contracts and decided that Bitcoin could be considered property under English law.

**3.6** Bryan J granted proprietary injunctions over Bitcoin paid by the claimant to hackers in response to a ransom attack. Bryan J noted that it had the characteristics of property and was thus subject to proprietary orders (albeit being neither a chose in possession nor a chose in action). (Used and affirmed in *Ion Science Limited & Anr v Persons Unknown* (21 December 2020, Butcher J), *Fetch.ai Ltd v Persons Unknown* [2021] 2254 (Comm), and *Wang v Darby* [2021] EWHC 3054 (Comm)).

# *Proprietary Challenges*

**3.7** Cryptocurrencies as business assets are subject to interlocutory applications. Analysis requires an understanding of cryptocurrencies as distinct from traditional assets and a careful consideration of how they might be deployed, or such deployment be made acceptable to the court. It certainly seems that they will not suffice as security for costs and parties may need to consider whether a court will need specialist evidence, or even fortification, on an application for a proprietary injunction over such assets.

+   1. In *Toma v Murray* [2020] 2295 (Ch), the Court discharged a proprietary injunction over Bitcoin assets held through an online account on the basis that damages were not an adequate remedy in light of the volatility of Bitcoin and the undefined damages which could result if a defendant were not free to liquidate it as necessary.
