# **1.17** *Introduction*

**1.17** New content in **Chapter 2** has been added to this Second Edition to enlighten as to the possible risks as to ‘Pump and Dump’ schemes and we examine the demise of Meta’s (formerly Facebook) Libra Coin to examine why it failed.

**1.18** **Chapter 3** then looks at how cryptoassets are regulated, their legal status and current and future challenges; this in many respects is the beating heart of the book and essential reading for those interested in the regulatory approach. The topics covered are various and include the different categories of cryptoassets, which broadly fall to be categorised as tokens of exchange, security or utility and, dependent on the categorisation, the regulatory treatment that will apply. Legal challenges are also discussed, and these legal issues arise from the broad spectrum of laws, from criminal to cybersecurity, data protection to property and contract to jurisdiction. It includes commentary on recent case law such as *AA v Persons Unknown* [2020] 4 WLR 35 a significant case where an insurer was granted a proprietary injunction as part of its strategy to recover a ransomware payment which had been negotiated and paid in Bitcoin.

**1.19** It should be noted with caution that, in the context of any given use case, much will be decided on a case by case basis and regulatory attention must be given not only to blockchain and cryptocurrency but all the moving parts of the business that might rub up against regulation. A prime example of this would be financial promotions in the UK where any advert or marketing that encourages or induces investment or claims management activity might fall to be regulated. It can be seen then that even where a business model incorporating blockchain or cryptoassets apparently operates so as not to regulated it might cross into regulation through the manner of its marketing.

**1.21** The much-publicised criminal aspects of cryptocurrency and the future policing of blockchain are of particular importance and deserve attention. It should first be understood that blockchain has benefits as well as disadvantages, from a crime enforcement perspective. The downside for enforcement is that, by its very nature and construction, blockchain cuts out intermediaries (such as banks and financial institutions) out of the loop.

**1.22** Cryptocurrencies were developed on a blockchain platform that operates on a peer-to-peer basis, sending encrypted payments direct from the sender to the beneficiary. The direct transmission method means that banks are not involved. The end-to-end encryption means that the parties to the transaction and the transactions themselves can be shrouded and invisible to outsiders.
