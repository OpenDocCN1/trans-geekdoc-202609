Virtual currency-related business activities are illegal financial activities. Carrying out legal currency and virtual currency exchange business, exchange business between virtual currencies, buying and selling virtual currency as a central counterparty, providing information intermediary and pricing services for virtual currency transactions, token issuance financing, and virtual currency derivatives transactions and other virtual currency related transactions. Business activities suspected of illegal financial activities such as illegal sale of tokens, unauthorized public issuance of securities, illegal futures business, illegal fundraising, etc. are strictly prohibited and resolutely banned in accordance with the law. If the conduct of relevant illegal financial activities constitutes a crime, criminal responsibility shall be investigated according to law.

The provision of services by overseas virtual currency exchanges to residents in the country through the Internet is also an illegal financial activity. For domestic staff of relevant overseas virtual currency exchanges, as well as legal persons, unincorporated organizations and natural persons who still provide marketing promotion, payment settlement, technical support and other services for them knowingly or should have known that they are engaged in virtual currency-related business, the relevant legal persons shall be investigated according to law.

There are legal risks in participating in virtual currency investment and trading activities. If any legal person, unincorporated organization or natural person invests in virtual currency and related derivatives and violates public order and good customs, the relevant civil legal act is invalid, and the loss caused by it shall be borne by itself; if it is suspected of disrupting the financial order and endangering financial security, the relevant departments shall investigate according to law.

Financial institutions and non-bank payment institutions shall not provide services for virtual currency-related business activities. Financial institutions and non-bank payment institutions shall not provide services such as account opening, fund transfer, clearing and settlement for virtual currency-related business activities, shall not include virtual currency as collateral, and shall not carry out virtual currency-related insurance business or transfer virtual currency. Currency is included in the scope of insurance liability, and clues to violations of laws and regulations should be reported to relevant departments in a timely manner.

- 6.18 This notice was timed to coincide with a circular that was issued by the National Development and Reform Commission and ten other authorities directing local governments on winding down cryptocurrency mining activities within their jurisdictions. In China cryptocurrency trading activities are not just banned but declared illegal and criminal activities that if undertaken would result in investigation and possible prosecution as a crime.

- 6.19 In addition to the reasons cited in the notice there are other potential reasons as to why the ban was introduced. For example, the decentralised nature of cryptocurrency runs counter to the state’s desire to control and centralise power; cryptocurrency can evade state control and lead to capital flight from China’s borders. According to the Chainalysis, in excess of $50 billion worth of cryptocurrency left East Asian accounts to areas outside the region in the period between 2019 and 2020. China had a significant, dominant presence in those East Asian cryptocurrency exchanges and it might be concluded that a major portion of this cryptocurrency flow represented capital flight from China.

- 6.20 This is a complete reversal from the earlier period, around 2013, when China was regarded as a fertile and pioneering jurisdiction for cryptocurrency. We suspect that China's current view is also formed because it lacks faith in anything other than its own currency and, given the Chinese approach to state sovereignty, one would expect any digital currency to be solely under the power and jurisdiction of the state. Cryptocurrencies pose a threat to that philosophy and China has actively closed the lid on cryptocurrencies.

- 6.21 To be clear there is additionally a complete ban on ICOs that use cryptocurrencies, and any attempt to do so would be illegal. The state has issued a set of ICO Rules and these impose restrictions not just on the ICOs themselves but also on the permission of cryptocurrency trading platforms. In effect, these prevent the sale, purchase or other related services wrapped around cryptocurrencies and consequently China has stamped out not only cryptocurrency trading but ancillary services.

- 6.22 Ironically, despite its attempts to prevent cryptocurrency trading, the People's Bank of China has launched its own cryptocurrency the digital yuan and established the Institute of Digital Money which sits within the People's Bank of China. The contradiction does, however, make sense when one takes account of the Chinese approach to data sovereignty, and sovereignty of the state in general, in relation to all matters fiscal and political. Cryptocurrency may well flourish in China but, if it does so, it will be as a mainstream government-backed currency and will be regarded as legal tender if it is issued only by the Central Bank and not by any other non-government-backed entity.

- 6.23 Notwithstanding virtual currency-related business activities are illegal financial activities, as is cryptocurrency trading, China appears to have made an exception for non-fungible tokens ('NFTs'). Xinhua news agency was recently able to declare and proceed with its intention to issue a digital media photo collection via NFTs. In addition, Alibaba and Tencent have made significant investments in NFTs. Tencent developed Huanhe, a NFT trading platform and in June 2021 Alipay created and launched NFTs replicating ancient cave art. So are NFTs, described as a possible fraud by the state media People's Daily in early 2022, somehow outside the ban and the accepted face of cryptoasset business within China? NFTs are cryptographic assets on a blockchain though, unlike cryptocurrencies, they have unique identification codes and metadata and cannot be traded at equivalency. As a matter of law however the authors are hesitant as to how, as a cryptoasset, an NFT can be deemed legal business with any certainty.

- 6.24 Indeed in China, because of the illegality and state opposition to cryptocurrencies, NFTs are referred to as digital collectibles rather than tokens or any other term that might emphasise or infer their cryptoasset status. Moreover, there is, at the time of writing, no secondary market for NFTs within China and Alibaba has expressly prohibited the re-sale of NFTs on its own secondary market Xianyu. It remains to be seen whether a secondary market for NFTs is permitted over time, but a constraining factor may be the state’s fear that NFTs, if granted more freedom, might develop into financial tradeable assets that fly in the face of the cryptocurrency ban.

## 6.25 Global regulation: the rest of the world

- 6.25 It is important to understand that in China the state has an appetite for blockchain, the underlying technology, despite its comprehensive ban on cryptocurrency. NFTs can consequently sit on the alliance chain, a version of blockchain developed in China and be collected. Here NFTs are created only by commissioned artists and can be collected but trading, such as a commercial re-sale, is not permitted through this type of platform. Here then there is controlled and limited NFT or digital collectible marketplace. There are, however, other more versatile platforms for trading NFTs; they allow NFT creators including individuals, rather than just commissioned artists, to display their NFT creations, though there is a limit on the number of copies they can upload to the platform, and these can be sold either directly from the platform or via an external platform in another jurisdiction. This enables a sale through OpenSea or another overseas marketplace. These digital art platforms can be based on the Ethereum public chain which is currently the decentralised NFT trading platform in China. One of the best known of these platforms NFTCN, operated by Bigverse, is attracting subscribers not only in China but worldwide.

- 6.26 The appetite for blockchain is driven by the state network, Blockchain Services Network, which seeks to keep separation between blockchain, NFTs and cryptocurrency. The authors' view is that NFTs might well be able to exist and thrive in China for so long as they are controlled by the state such that they lack the financial ingredient that would render them a cryptocurrency and are kept separate from cryptocurrencies and their economy. Blockchain does not seem an obvious technology for China to adopt and foster, its attributes of relative anonymity and immutability do not play well with censorship or surveillance and its decentralisation runs counter to the state’s apparent commitment to data sovereignty, so ultimate state ownership of data that is within its borders, and the centralised control that underlies China’s system of governance. Accordingly, in China, the regulatory landscape and development of blockchain have ensured that in practice it cannot operate in a pure, decentralised way and state control is able to guide its application. The purist might argue that this is not blockchain at all but China evidently sees great value in blockchain and aims to become a technological leader and, as declared by Xi Jinping at a study session for the Politburo in October 2019, a blockchain ‘rule-maker’. The irony is that this rule-making approach threatens to undermine the very features that render blockchain such a useful technology for ensuring transparency, immutability and access that are beyond the influence of a central authority. China’s vision of blockchain then is a blockchain that is tempered so as to disable or dilute decentralisation.
