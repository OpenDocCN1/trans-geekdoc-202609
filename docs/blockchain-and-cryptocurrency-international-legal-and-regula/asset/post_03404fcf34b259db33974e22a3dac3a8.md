Generally, the presentation is expected to cover the following areas:

- background on the key individuals driving the business including their relevant skills and experience;
- business plan, including structure of the company, products and services, the target market, and strategy;

## Hong Kong

6.40 The blanket ban on cryptocurrency trading and ICOs bypassed Hong Kong, due to their separate political system from China, therefore resulting in many crypto-related businesses initially moving to Hong Kong. However, like South Korea, Hong Kong is very aware of the risks that come with developing technology and plans to ‘intervene when appropriate’.

Blockchain remittances were successfully tested in Hong Kong through Ant Financial, a subsidiary of Alibaba.

They urged market professionals to play their role in ensuring propriety over dealings in such financial instruments. They took active steps by sending letters to several crypto exchanges, warning them of the consequences of trading without a licence.

6.42 On 21 May 2021, Hong Kong’s Financial Services and Treasury Bureau (FSTB) published its consultation conclusions on legislative proposals to improve AML/CTF regulations in Hong Kong in relation to Hong Kong virtual asset exchange operators (the Consultation Conclusions). These conclusions follow its November 2020 consultation paper which aims to create a new licensing regime for certain virtual asset service providers (VASPs) that are currently not required to be licensed under the SFO because they only trade cryptocurrencies that are neither securities nor futures contracts.

6.43 Under the Consultation Conclusions, the FSTB will now require virtual asset exchanges operators to:

- (i) be licensed by the SFC, and consequently be required to comply with various licensing requirements;
- (ii) appoint responsible officers who satisfy the fit and proper test; and
- (iii) comply with all due diligence, recording, anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing obligations pursuant to the Anti-Money Laundering Order, Schedule 2. The compliance by virtual asset exchanges of these requirements would be monitored and enforced.

6.44 Hong Kong’s regulators decide on a case by case basis whether a cryptocurrency is regulated but, as a general rule, cryptocurrencies are viewed as unregulated ‘virtual commodities’. That said, a cryptocurrency that exhibits the characteristics of a security or provides rights similar to a debenture or share or are otherwise deemed to constitute regulated activity such as a collective investment scheme may fall to be regulated. This rather mirrors the position in the UK though there is less clarity in Hong Kong as to the definition of a collective investment scheme. The fundamental features of such a scheme are:

- it is an arrangement in respect of property of any kind (property is widely defined);
- the participants do not have day-to-day control over the management of the property;
- the property must be managed as a whole by or on behalf of the person operating the arrangements;
- the participants’ financial contributions and the profits or income are pooled;
- the purpose of the arrangement should be to provide participants with profits, income or other returns from the use, acquisition or management of the property in question.

6.45 ICOs will be outside the regulatory perimeter where the tokens are viewed as unregulated tokens such as utility tokens. Accordingly, utility tokens which confer few rights save to access a platform’s product either now or at some future point will be outside the scope of Hong Kong’s securities legislation. This is in line with SFC guidance though it has not provided further guidance on those features or rights of an ICO token that are likely to render it a security.

6.46 In the years from 2019 the ICO activity in Hong Kong has ground to a virtual standstill as a result of vociferous warnings from the SFC combined with the market confusion as to when a token might be considered a security. This has apparently led to the perception of risk outweighing the perceived benefits.

6.47 Securities Token Offerings (STOs) unlike ICOs are generally regarded as falling within the regulatory perimeter and consequently have more regulatory certainty in some jurisdictions but it is still uncertain how they are treated in Hong Kong due to the lack of clarity as to what constitutes a security. The SFC’s view is that security tokens are digital assets which, akin to traditional securities, bestow economic rights on the token holder or provide other rights such as ownership. Conversely, where a token passes a debt owed by its issuer the token will probably fall to be a debenture token. Consideration will also need to be given to when any rights under the token will arise, if they are future rights then is a form of collective investment scheme or other regulated activity being created that shifts the token within the regulatory perimeter.

## India

6.48 In 2018 the Reserve Bank of India banned banks and any regulated financial institutions from ‘dealing with or settling virtual currencies’ and regulation prohibited the trade of cryptocurrencies on Indian exchanges which were given a deadline of 6 July 2018, to cease their business. In 2020, a landmark Supreme Court decision declared the ban unconstitutional, removed the prohibition of cryptocurrency and allowed the exchanges to reopen. Further to this the Reserve Bank of India has issued numerous warning circulars as to the risks posed by users, holders and traders of cryptocurrencies but, as at the date of writing, cryptocurrencies are neither expressly prohibited nor expressly regulated. It is plain, however, that in India cryptocurrencies are not viewed as acceptable tender and the government is resistant to their being part of a payments system. Indeed, the official view from government has repeatedly been to emphasise that cryptocurrency is not legal tender or coin, that it will do all it can to eliminate it from the payments system and from its use in financing illicit activities.
