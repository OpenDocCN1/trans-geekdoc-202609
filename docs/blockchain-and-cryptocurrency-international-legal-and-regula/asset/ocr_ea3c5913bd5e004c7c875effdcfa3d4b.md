There can be no doubt that licensing will be key in the Metaverse and there may well be collisions of contractual rights asserted under licences with trademark laws that seek to protect the creation of originality.

**12.26** The creation of originality will be a hotly contested space in the Metaverse where avatars, for example, will be used and a number of tricky legal questions will arise. Those questions will include who owns the avatar, whose copyright is within that avatar and is an avatar a work of authorship that may be registered or is protected separately from the Metaverse in which it appears? Avatars are already in use in gaming and once this is extended to the Metaverse there will be a profound expansion of their use and, given the immersive and commercial nature of the Metaverse, the likelihood of increased disputes as to who owns that avatar’s copyright and consequently the value that goes with it. In order to understand how the problem may arrive one should first understand how an avatar may be developed. Whilst a platform may create or develop an avatar it will sometimes be the case, as in gaming, that platform owners will offer their users the possibility to adapt, create or personalise their avatars such that they have unique or distinctive traits, appearances and characteristics. Where the avatar’s user has exercised their creative influence a legal question will arise in a number of jurisdictions as to whether they have made significant creative decisions and can consequently be regarded as the author and copyright owner of what has become their unique and personalised avatar.

**12.27** The difficulty with the application of copyright law as it exists in the major jurisdictions is that it is not well suited to the Metaverse and, in particular the creation and adaption of avatars. The users who customise and creatively personalise their avatars surely deserve to be rewarded and in creating their own avatars ought to be considered the legal author of that work. Conversely the platform’s owners who will create the contractual documentation around the Metaverse and provide licences which may extend to the use of the avatars can properly argue that they set up the Metaverse landscape and that the avatar was brought to life by them and was indeed their creation. One might argue that where the revisions effected by the avatar user are slight or lack the requisite originality then the position is more straightforward, and the avatar remains the creation of the platform owner. That situation will however become much more challenging where the user has invested time, intellect and creativity to produce unique traits which extend beyond its distinctive appearance and include its manner of movement, its particular behaviours and style of communication and ‘dialect’. There are too many variations on an avatar to delve into at this juncture, however, it should be appreciated that the range of adaptations and personalisations are significant and a user could craft an avatar that is undoubtedly their creation. In such a scenario the authors suggest that there may be a form of collaborative ownership such that the avatar constitutes a work of joint ownership between the platform owner and the user.
