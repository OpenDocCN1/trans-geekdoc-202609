12.19 The defence of artistic licence does seem to be particularly effective in defeating claims as is also the outcome in AM General LLC v Activision Blizzard, Inc (S.D.N.Y. 2020) where AM General brought a claim against Activision Blizzard for infringing their trademark by using what they alleged was their trademark protected truck in a videogame of Call of Duty. The Court similarly found for Activision on the basis that the use of the truck had artistic licence and further they found that the use of an actual real-world vehicle in the context of the game helped provide a sense of realism and lifelikeness in what was supposed to be a game of modern warfare. This is not to say that trademark claims will always fail in the Metaverse because they have failed in some of the gaming claims but it is a signal that great care would have to be exercised. One feature that might cause a claim to succeed will be where the use of the trademark is evidently commercial and where it is held to be capable or likely of confusing the consumer into believing that the infringer is in fact the trademark owner and that the logo has been used or created by them.

12.20 Conversely users in the Metaverse will need to tread with caution to ensure they do not infringe or potentially infringe a trademark. This can be tricky given the number and proliferation of trademarks but the same caution that might be exercised in the real world should be exercised in the Metaverse if the claim is to be avoided. In the same way that a user might tread carefully as to what it represents, trademark owners will need to keep a lookout for infringements of their trademarks and they might consider using an expert trademark monitoring or watch company to keep a lookout and to closely monitor potential infringements in the Metaverse.

12.21 The platform ought also to monitor and be aware of activity on its platform and act when it fears there may have been infringing activity. It is also expected that where a potential trademark infringement is identified by a trademark owner the platform would be notified of this so that it can consider whether it can take action under its terms and conditions and applicable licences. Indeed, one advantage of the Metaverse might be the ability of the trademark owner to contact the platform and try to use this as a means to bring an end to the infringing activity. It was mentioned earlier that there might be circumstances where a user could be expelled from the Metaverse and his property ‘deleted’; this might be one of those situations, if the infringement is considered significantly material, grave and intentional. Again, much would depend on the user agreement and licence terms that were applicable in that situation.

12.22 The moral of the tale is that trademark owners should ensure that their trademarks are registered so that they have the best chance of being enforced in the Metaverse. Trademarks can be registered with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) or with equivalent organisations. In the author’s experience with their clients the registration with the USPTO in the US appear to be the preferred route in the hope of ensuring that the owner is able to maintain exclusive rights to the trademark in the Metaverse.
