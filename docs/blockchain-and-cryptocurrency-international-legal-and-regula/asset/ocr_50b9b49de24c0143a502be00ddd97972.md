11.30 The way through this uncertainty might be to create a form of standard legal entity that wraps around the DAO, what can be termed a 'legal wrapper'. This would be agreed upon in advance and a more conventional legal structure engineered, such that membership and shareholdings were clearly stipulated and the legal status of the wrapper was certain and safer than, say, a general partnership. To be clear, in a general partnership, its members (effectively, its partners) are deemed to jointly represent the DAO and are responsible and liable for its obligations, actions and omissions. In this way, members might build a shield against personal liability and have more certainty with less risk.

11.31 Given the importance of the choice of jurisdiction, one might also create a separate, traditional 'off chain' contract that stipulates which jurisdiction and law are exclusively applicable. This approach is examined in more detail in Chapter 3 in relation to smart contracts. The contract here might also address cryptocurrency and cryptoassets under the control of the DAO, to ensure that they are subject to multiple-signature escrow wallets that the DAO members are better able to control. When one appreciates that DAOs are essentially a combination of smart contracts (or, if you prefer, a super smart contract), it follows that all the precautions required to circumvent smart contract issues will be requisite.

11.32 It will be interesting to watch the DAO space, especially in the wake of the recent Wyoming DAO law (see 11.17 above) that came into force on 1 July 2021 and enabled DAOs to have formal legal status as a Limited Liability Company. Malta might argue that it was the first to pass legislation in 2019 that paved the way for DAOs to have legal status by enacting that an Innovative Technology Arrangement ('ITA') could be an organisation 'with or without legal personality' however, for many purists, the Maltese law placed too much emphasis on the responsibility of managers. In the authors' view neither the Malta initiative nor the Wyoming DAO law will be conclusive as to the future of the DAO which, despite new use cases springing up daily, particularly in the crypto sector, remains shrouded in legal uncertainty. Wyoming's impact will be tempered by it being a relatively small State in terms of populous and that, in the US it is plain that federal securities regulation has primacy and will certainly trump local law in any contest. For so long as the characterisation of DAO tokens is in doubt any DAO venture will face an uncertain regulatory future. Given the rise and rise of DAOs on the back of NFTs and DeFi something will need to give; the authors sincere hope is that commercial and technological sense prevails so that regulation doesn't strangle the DAO's undoubted potential.
