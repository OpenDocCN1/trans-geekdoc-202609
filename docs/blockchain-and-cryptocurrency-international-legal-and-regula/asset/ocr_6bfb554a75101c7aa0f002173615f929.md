Traditionally, the Form 8300 has related to in-person transactions due to the method of payment. The Infrastructure Act has now extended this provision to include $10,000 of digital assets. Consequently, a person receiving $10,000 worth of Bitcoin would need to file a Form 8300.

**5.27**  Digital Assets are defined as:

> ‘Except as otherwise provided by the Secretary, the term “digital asset” means any digital representation of value which is recorded on a cryptographically secured distributed ledger or any similar technology as specified by the Secretary.’³

The definition is sufficiently broad to encompass traditional cryptocurrencies and even non-fungible tokens (‘NFTs’). However, the provision also allows for the Treasury Secretary to exempt specific digital assets or scenarios.

**5.28**  The relevant section of the Infrastructure Act⁴ does not apply to traceable electronic transactions involving credit cards, debit cards, or peer-to-peer payment services like PayPal; nor does it apply to financial institutions which are subject to similar requirements under the Bank Secrecy Act. The Treasury Secretary and the IRS have a discretion to clarify how the Form 8300 will apply to digital assets, which will be important as a failure to report, due to an ‘intentional disregard’, is a criminal (federal) offence with a maximum sentence of five-years imprisonment, and a fine of up to $100,000. Civil penalties for failing to file a Form 8300 can be up to $3 million a year. These reporting requirements will not come into effect until 2024.

**5.29**  Further, the Infrastructure Act⁵ has amended the definition of a ‘broker’ for the purposes of filing an IRS Form 1099. Under the new expanded definition, a broker is now a business ‘responsible for regularly providing any service effectuating transfers of digital assets on behalf of another person’. This definition would include: cryptocurrency exchanges, peer-to-peer money transmission services, and financial institutions that support cryptocurrency transactions.
