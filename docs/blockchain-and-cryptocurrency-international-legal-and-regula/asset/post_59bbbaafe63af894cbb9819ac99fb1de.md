The AIFM and the depositary of an authorised AIF must be independent of each other; however, the AIFM and the depositary of an unauthorised AIF can be separate yet connected entities in the same group, provided there is proper management and disclosure of potential conflicts of interest.

7.69 A full depositary must be one of the following:

- an EEA credit institution
- an authorised MiFID investment firm which provides the services of safe-keeping and administration of financial instruments and which has own funds of not less than €730,000 under Article 28 of the CRD (2013/36/EU) (for a UK firm this would be an IFPRU 730k firm with permission to safeguard and administer investments)

- another type of authorised person or an unauthorised person which will, if authorised as a depositary of AIF, only:
  - act as trustee or depositary for AIFs of the kind defined in FUND 3.11.12R (a PE AIF depositary), and/or
  - provide one or more depositary services to non-EEA AIFs.

7.70 An authorised AIF can only use the firms or institutions listed at 1–3 above as a depositary. An unauthorised AIF, into which ICOs (if regulated as an AIF) would likely fall, can have any of 1–4 as a depositary. As an unauthorised AIF, an ICO could also have a 'separate yet connected entity' as a depositary. An authorised person connected to the ICO could act as a trustee or depositary if sufficient separation could be demonstrated.

7.71 A full-scope AIFM who wishes to market an AIF in the UK must apply to the FCA for permission. This permission can allow marketing to retail and/or professional investors in the UK.

7.72 Companies seeking to market an ICO to UK investors must apply to the FCA and meet domestic rules. These are currently analogous to the passporting provisions of the AIFMD if they are based within the EEA and are authorised by their home state. Otherwise, the FSMA and RAO provisions have supranational effect, over foreign companies, if marketed towards or sold to a person within the UK.

## RESTRICTIONS OF MARKETING AN ICO WHICH IS A CIS OR AN AIF

7.73 Section 21 of FSMA states that: ‘a person must not, in the course of business, communicate an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity unless the promotion has been made or approved by an authorised person or it is directed at a person who falls into one of the exempt categories of recipient and meets a series of tests’. Consequently, without authorisation, or an applicable exemption, marketing the regulated ICO is prohibited.

7.74 Further, sections 89 and 90 of the Financial Services Act 2012 (‘2012 Act’) prohibit misleading statements (s 89) and misleading impressions (s 90) with respect to a regulated ICO. Under section 89 of the 2012 Act, a token issuer would be committing a criminal offence if it knowingly or recklessly made a false or misleading statement, or dishonestly concealed any material facts, with the intention of inducing, or it was reckless as to whether it might induce, another person to enter into, or refrain from entering into, a relevant agreement (for example, an agreement to subscribe for tokens).

7.75 Similarly, a token issuer would commit an offence under section 90 of the 2012 Act if a false or misleading impression was created as to the market in, or the price or value of, a relevant investment, in order to induce another person to acquire or subscribe for investments such as tokens.

7.76 A person found guilty of either offence in the Crown Court could be sentenced to seven years’ imprisonment or an unlimited fine, or both.

## E-MONEY TOKENS

7.77 E-money is defined within the UK as electronically stored monetary value as represented by a claim on the electronic money issuer which is:

- issued on receipt of funds for the purpose of making payment transactions;
- accepted by a person other than the electronic money issuer; and
- not excluded by Regulation 3 of the Electronic Money Regulations 2011 (EMR).

7.78 The EMR specifies the issuance of e-money as a regulated activity under Article 9B of the RAO when carried on by credit institutions, credit unions and municipal banks. The firms that issue e-money must ensure that they are appropriately authorised or registered.

7.79 In order to be considered as e-money, a virtual asset must enable the user to make payment transactions with third parties. The e-money must be accepted by more parties than only the issuer. Fiat balances in online wallets or prepaid cards constitute e-money.

7.80 Exchange tokens such as Bitcoin and Ether are unlikely to constitute e-money as these are not centrally issued on the receipt of funds, nor do they represent a claim against the issuer.

7.81 Distributed ledger technology (“DLT”) and cryptographically secured tokens can be used to represent fiat funds; however, the FCA have suggested that virtual assets that establish a new type of unit (i.e. a new cryptocurrency) rather than representing fiat funds are unlikely to constitute e-money unless the value of the unit is pegged to a fiat currency.43 Even then, it will still depend on the facts of the case.

It is worth noting that StableCoins may be regulated as e-money but could also fall to be regulated as a security token.

## UNREGULATED TOKENS

7.82 Exchange tokens and Utility tokens are considered to be ‘unregulated tokens’ by the FCA. However, anti-money laundering (AML) provisions will still apply for those trading in these tokens, and in that context fall within the remit of the FCA.

7.83 From 10 January 2020, the UK’s Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing and Transfer of Funds (Information on the Payer) Regulations 2017 (the ‘MLRs’) extended AML and Counter Terrorist Financing (‘CTF’) regulation to ‘cryptoasset exchange providers’ (‘CEPs’) and ‘custodian wallet providers’ (‘CWPs’) within the UK. These businesses and providers are now ‘relevant persons’ with the same AML and CTF obligations to carry out customer due-diligence and report suspicious transactions as other financial institutions and money service businesses. Cryptoasset exchange providers and custodian wallet providers are required to register for AML supervision with the FCA.

## FACILITATING REGULATED PAYMENT SYSTEMS

7.84 The execution of payment transactions, card issuing, merchant acquiring and money remittance are the provision of payment services which are regulated in the UK under the Payment Services Regulations (‘PSR’). The PSR apply to ‘funds’ which are defined as ‘banknotes and coins, scriptural money and electronic money’.44 Generally virtual assets do not fall within the definition; therefore, the PSR does not apply.

7.85 However, if a cryptocurrency is used to facilitate a money transfer, for example, a fiat currency is converted into a virtual asset and back to a fiat currency before being transferred to a recipient, then it will fall to be regulated under the PSR. Individuals or companies undertaking the provision of payment services will need to consider their methodology and seek specific advice if there is a concern.

## THE LIMITED APPROACH OF THE EUROPEAN UNION

7.86 There is currently no explicit ICO regulation which provides overarching governance within the EU; however, the EU proposal, announced on 24 September 2020 as part of the European Commission’s Digital Finance Package, is to provide a Comprehensive Framework for Digital Assets.45

7.87 The centrepiece of the framework is the proposed regulation: Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA). A ‘Crypto-asset’ is defined within MiCA as ‘a digital representation of value or rights which may be transferred and stored electronically, using distributed ledger technology or similar technology’ and will apply to ‘any legal person who offers to the public any type of crypto assets or seeks the admission of crypto assets to a trading platform for crypto assets’.46
