It is perhaps ironic that because Metaverse as they currently stand are owned by companies, they are subject to central ownership and control in a manner that is the opposite of decentralisation and the aims of blockchain. However, it is not just the fact that blockchain purists may be offended by this central control position but that in terms of virtual property ownership the Metaverse will present some significant challenges due to the overwhelming influence on control of a single company owner. It is these terms of service, these contractual documents that set out the legal landscape and in the experience of the authors from examining current examples, the ownership of virtual property may actually be nothing of the sort and, as we will amplify later, ownership is a very challenging notion within this contractual framework.

12.9 The notion of virtual ownership in the mind of many users is that it is possible to buy virtual property in the Metaverse through the use of a non-fungible token (NFT). The notion runs that you acquire virtual property and in doing so you assign or purchase a non-fungible token which is immutable proof to the world of your ownership. That NFT records the transaction on a blockchain which due to its decentralised digital ledger is beyond revision or deletion. Further reassurance is brought by a NFT being kept in a wallet and that wallet cannot be accessed in any effective way without the private key. The theme of decentralisation then brings many users to believe that this is a very safe way of acquiring and owning virtual property. In addition, many users profess that interoperability, the idea that the technological feature of the Metaverse will be the ability to use property acquired across a wider, universal interlinked Metaverse, is yet another technological feature of the Metaverse that is currently more mythical than actual. In short, the problem with acquiring or believing one has acquired virtual property through an NFT is that the NFT and the virtual property whether it be a bar, restaurant, fashion item or anything else are not one and the same.

12.10 The conclusion to be drawn then is that one can acquire and legally own a NFT which immutably signifies the virtual asset purchase but you may not legally own or possess in any meaningful sense the virtual asset itself because that virtual asset can effectively be taken away from you or given away by the platform owner should the terms of service or other contractual use documentation allow it to do so. By way of example one might buy a Mona Lisa in the Metaverse and be provided with a NFT evidencing that purchase only to discover subsequently that the Mona Lisa had disappeared from the Metaverse and had apparently been deleted from the platform's database such that all one was left with was the NFT itself. In the author's view this misunderstanding and problem with purchasing virtual property in the Metaverse has arisen from a conflation with real property. Virtual property is not the same as real property or land. If one purchases land for example and is given title deeds those title deeds reflect the ownership of the land but in addition to that the land that is owned, solid bricks and mortar for example if a building, have also been acquired and purchased assuming the conveyance was properly concluded.
