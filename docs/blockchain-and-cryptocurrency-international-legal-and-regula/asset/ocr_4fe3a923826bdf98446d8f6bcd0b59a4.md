# Introduction

**2.1** The rise of decentralised ledger technologies (‘DLTs’), the best known of these being blockchain, is part of the technological revolution that is proceeding apace. There are potentially far-reaching issues with the ability of DLTs and blockchain to be compliant with recent data regulation; however, the use of blockchain is growing by the day, not least in the area of cryptocurrencies.

**2.2** Bitcoin is a decentralised virtual cryptocurrency. In November 2021 Bitcoin prices flirted with a valuation of $69,000, although its value fell to $37,917.27 by the end of January 2022.¹ This is despite the words of Jamie Dimon, the Chairman and CEO of JP Morgan Chase (and more importantly previously on the board of directors of the Federal Reserve), describing the currency as a fraud that would blow up, and was only useful ‘if you were a drug dealer’.² Similar sentiments were repeated by Christine Lagarde, President of the European Central Bank. What is it that the blockchain, and the creation of cryptocurrencies, has the potential to create huge profits for those willing to speculate on the technology, but also considered by many as a ‘bubble’ or a scam which requires further regulation. This is a statement also espoused by many central banks and central bankers. As the means to control a decentralised currency are not within their remit they call for regulation under the guise of customer protection. Meanwhile each stakeholder is experimenting with its own crypto currency offering centralised control by controlling the underlying architecture and blockchain. Their other distraction is to say that such currencies are more liable to criminal activity. As a proportion of all currency crime they are far dwarfed by the conventional means of cash and cashless transfer. In fact, cryptocurrencies are significant less prone to abuse than simple fiat currency which much harder to trace.
