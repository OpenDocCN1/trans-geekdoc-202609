## THE LEGAL STATUS AND OTHER ISSUES AROUND DAOs

11.16 The DAO is a difficult concept for the law and it does not appear to have consistent recognised legal status across global jurisdictions. If we look at decentralised organisations and DAOs as generic groups, it seems clear that law has not kept pace with technology and jurisdictions do not seem able to define or address what their legal status is. There are consequently many unsolved problems with DAOs, particularly around issues involving legal compliance, benefits, recruiting, talent retention, income volatility, and governance. How can a DAO interact with third parties or take steps to conduct its business. Without the status as a formal legal entity, DAOs do not have a legal persona. This means that a DAO cannot enter into a contract for itself and property ownership becomes problematic. Whilst not new, DAOs are still a relatively novel concept and their formal legal status in the UK is not yet well defined or settled. This remains the case globally though notably in the US, in the State of Wyoming, DAOs can be incorporated as Limited Liability Companies.

11.17 On 1 July 2021, Wyoming's Decentralized Autonomous Organization ('DAO') law came into force and Wyoming became the first US state to settle the DAOs legal status by defining it as a Wyoming Limited Liability Company ('LLC'). This is subject to certain requirements to include in its articles of association that it is a DAO that is either member or algorithmically managed and incorporate a publicly available identifier of any smart contract directly used to manage, facilitate or operate the DAO. Where the management is algorithmic, smart contracts must be capable of being modified, updated and improved.

11.18 Despite the DAO being granted legal status at state level in Wyoming the big question that hangs over Wyoming DAOs is whether regulatory problems will arise in future at a federal level where the regulator, the SEC, deems the tokens that are essential for conferring voting rights and delivering benefits are classified as securities. It can be seen then that even in the US where state law assists the DAO to become an LLC it would still need to comply with federal securities regulation.

11.19 Similarly in the UK, the regulatory treatment of DAOs is currently based on the framework that governs their underlying tokens. The classification of Ethereum-based tokens is key because, so long as they can be defined as exchange tokens or utility tokens then, in the UK, they fall outside the Financial Conduct Authority’s regulatory perimeter and are beyond the scope of regulation. But what might happen in the UK if, as in Wyoming, DAOs were granted formal legal status? Whilst there is good argument that Ethereum-based tokens are currently viewed as unregulated the authors believe that this might alter in the minds of the FCA upon DAOs having formal legal status. In that changed situation, the tokens, which represent the formal membership and voting interests in a DAO, might effectively become securities and thus fall within the security token definition. That would bring the tokens and consequently the DAOs within the FCA’s regulatory perimeter.

11.20 There is concern that, as technology advances, the capabilities of DAOs will increase and the combinations of smart contracts at the heart of the DAO will enable all manner of decisions to be made without human intervention. Evidently, so far as UK law is concerned, a DAO cannot be a natural person, for that would be a human. Equally, it would not be a person such as a company or partnership or any other legal entity, because it does not fulfil the conventional notions of what a legal entity is. A combination of smart contracts that effectively form the brain of an artificially intelligent construction is well beyond the current legal concept of what a legally recognised person or entity is.

11.21 It is essential to clarify and define the legal status of DAOs (and, indeed, decentralised organisations) because, without legal recognition, any investors whose investments were determined by a DAO would not have any protection under limited liability laws (ie those laws that apply to companies). If decentralised organisations continue to have no recognised legal status, individuals may be subject to personal liability because of their investments but will have no recourse against a DAO or decisions taken by it (that is, decisions in the sense of the operation of the smart contracts) because there is no person who can be sued or complained to.

11.22 The other question, of course, will be how a DAO is defined. For example, if it is deemed that a DAO is a form of general partnership, and there is some support for this view, those investors to whom tokens have been issued might owe one another fiduciary duties as partners that were not contemplated when they invested in the scheme. That risk might operate to reduce the likelihood of any investment in the first place, making decentralised organisations seem a risky bet.

11.23 In addition to DAOs making ever more sophisticated decisions due to their increased capability, there would also be the difficulty of jurisdiction. The use of smart contracts would allow DAOs to collaborate in worldwide activities but, without any recognition of their legal status or the rights of the investor, they become high risk and not a prudent investment.

11.24 DAOs are also subject to significant disadvantages, notably their lack of legal persona which, as mentioned, can hamper them from entering into contracts or owning property in many jurisdictions. These drawbacks can be addressed but that might require the addition of some of the traditional governance structures that the DAO was designed to avoid. Indeed, if one brings in a range of traditional enabling structures to onboard real world gains, such as capacity for traditional contracts, then not only may the objectives and purity of a DAO be defeated but the directors or management may find themselves conflicted with the aims of the DAO.

## RISKS

11.25 An example of the risks inherent in a DAO can be demonstrated from The DAO, a crowdfunded venture that is the best-known example of a DAO in action. The DAO was a venture capital business operated by a network of machines operating on basic principles equivalent to those that drive Bitcoin digital currency. It was designed to raise money for projects running on Ethereum blockchain, handing out funds dependent on the votes of the members. Ethereum is a form of decentralised platform with no centralised control or controller and is visible to anyone that has an internet address. It is similar to Bitcoin but arguably provides more functionality. In this instance, The DAO had success and this attracted multimillions of investment from around the world, but this balloon was seemingly burst when, on 12 June 2016, it was realised that an infection had found its way into the software. It was declared that, notwithstanding this, no funds were at risk.

11.26 In the background, some 50 proposals were awaiting votes as to whether to fund projects and, while attention turned to correcting the bug in the software, a hacker began to siphon The DAO of Ether. Ether is a form of payment made by clients of an Ethereum platform to those machines executing the requested operations. In less than a week, the hacker managed to transfer more than $3.6 million of Ether collected from token sales. It appears that the Ether was taken from The DAO and dropped into software with the same structure known as a ‘child DAO’. This manoeuvre hit the price of Ether, causing it to fall from $20 to less than $13. The hacker was able to exploit a weakness in the computer code to siphon, in multiple withdrawal visits, some $50 million.
