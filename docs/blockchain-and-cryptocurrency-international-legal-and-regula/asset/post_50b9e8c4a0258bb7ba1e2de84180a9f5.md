International standards published by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) provide technical information which can be applied in this context. ## So who owns the data?

8.88 Regulation of the IoT is haphazard but is growing. The US approach is to rely upon existing legislation and new security breach notification laws, whereas the EU and UK are seeking to create new methods of regulation. The EU and the UK have created new statutory instruments (GDPR/DPA) to ensure compliance with massive incentives (fines). Voluntary guidance and codes have been published to compliment the legislation.

Consequently, the answer to the question, ‘Who owns the data?’, varies depending on your location.

8.89 In the US a ‘data subject’ is provided with the same rights as a consumer. At a federal level, there is currently nothing more to protect their personal data than existing consumer law. At a state level, the company is required to notify a data breach. However, it would be difficult to say that data is ‘owned’ by the data subject.

## 8.90 In the EU, GDPR has created a strong set of ‘rights’ for a data subject. These apply within the UK through the Data Protection Act 2018. These rights include the right:

- of access (Article 15);
- of rectification (Article 16);
- to erasure (Article 17);
- to restriction of processing (Article 18);
- to data portability (Article 20); and
- to object to data processing (Article 21).

In this context, it is far easier to suggest that, within the EU, the data subject is the owner of their personal data.

## 8.91 The distinction between the US and the EU is highlighted by the requirement for the EU/US ‘Privacy Shield’ and ‘Umbrella Agreement’. The Privacy Shield replaced the International Safe Harbour Privacy Principles and was intended to facilitate the transfer of information from the EU to the US. The Privacy Shield allows EU citizens to rely upon seven major principles:

- 1. **Notice:** Individuals must be informed that their data is being collected and how it will be used. The organisation must provide information about how individuals can contact the organisation with any inquiries or complaints.
- 2. **Choice:** Individuals must have the option to opt out of the collection and forward transfer of the data to third parties.
- 3. **Accountability for Onward Transfer:** Transfers of data to third parties may only occur if these third parties apply adequate data protection principles.
- 4. **Security:** Reasonable efforts must be made to prevent loss of collected information.
- 5. **Data Integrity and Purpose Limitation:** Data must be relevant and reliable for the purpose it was collected.
- 6. **Access:** Individuals must be able to access information, and correct or delete information in relation to them if it is inaccurate.
- 7. **Resources, Enforcement and Liability:** There must be effective means of enforcing these rules.

## Right to be forgotten and right to erasure

## INTRODUCTION

9.1 The primary benefit of cryptoassets stem from being open, transparent and immutable. Blockchain-based web apps are necessarily different than the multibillion-dollar ‘walled gardens’ that currently dominate the internet. Privacy laws were written with the old web in mind, the web of Facebook and Google.

- GDPR is the exemplar of privacy frameworks, governing how individuals’ personal data can be used across technology and other industry sectors within the EU’s jurisdiction. It covers all businesses that maintain records of users, regardless of whether or not they are based in the EU. It means that companies cannot act wantonly or recklessly with private, personal information ranging from someone’s Google search history to a Twitter user’s social graph.

- This is called privacy ‘by design’ or ‘by default’, and applies to both software and hardware. It has major implications for blockchains that are, generally speaking, publicly auditable protocols. As per UK GDPR, blockchains should be ‘privacy-preserving by design’, meaning developers must consider user privacy while designing and developing crypto platforms, as well as crypto-based products and services. The immutability and broad availability of data on public and permissionless blockchains is an obvious challenge for developers. It is a balancing act between ensuring users only provide as much personal data as actually needed to get the job done, and the core tenets of this novel technology.

9.2 On 13 May 2014, the Court of Justice of the European Union (‘CJEU’) legally solidified that the ‘right to be forgotten’ is a human right when they ruled against Google in the Costeja case.1 The Court observed that not only do individuals have a right to rectify, erase or block data that is inaccurate or incomplete, but they also have a right to ensure that their personal data used by controllers2 is adequate, relevant, and not excessive3. The Court concluded that the links to articles about Costeja González’s satisfied debts were inadequate, no longer relevant, or excessive, and therefore had to be removed by Google, even though the news articles themselves could remain online.
