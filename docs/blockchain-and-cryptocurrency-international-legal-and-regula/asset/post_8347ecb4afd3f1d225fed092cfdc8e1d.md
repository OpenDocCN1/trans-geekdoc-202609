
12.51 The preceding paragraphs should serve to demonstrate that the question as to which jurisdiction and whose laws will be a valid and searching question for the Metaverse. Jurisdiction is complex; it is made more complex by an increasing willingness of countries to enact laws that are expressed as being extra-territorial by design; that is to say, a nation state is unilaterally declaring its laws as being applicable in the event of breach and/or a prescribed connection to that country. The UK, US, China and Russia have all passed laws with extra-territorial design and these laws include cyber and/or data protection legislation. That is particularly relevant in the context of the Metaverse given it is essentially cyberspace. The advent of these extra-territorial cyber laws and regulations will increasingly lead to a conflict of jurisdiction and geo-political sparring. The internet has already stress-tested our global laws and exposed not only the lack of harmony but the tensions that a borderless cyberspace can bring to laws conceived in an analogue, bordered world. Geo-politics and divergent political and cultural philosophies mean that there is no easy or likely solution in sight.

12.52 The hope must be that universal jurisdiction or something akin to that doctrine can be developed so that there can be certainty of outcome and the avoidance of conflict. Certainly, Metaverse owners can assist by creating user agreements and licences that incorporate standard terms governing conduct, but that approach also requires cooperation and external policing. Sadly, history tells us that international cooperation in this area is unlikely. The GDPR was expected to herald a brave new age in data protection with Europe leading the way in defending an individual’s personal information rights. And yet this laudable aim did not come to pass; Russia and China passed laws which ultimately gave the state sovereignty over data, and the world has a fractured, dysfunctional set of cyber security laws. The authors nonetheless posit the possibility, however novel and remote, that the Metaverse might evolve to become truly interoperable in every sense and enable a harmonisation of regulation and jurisdiction as never witnessed before.

The scale of the collection and sharing of personal data has increased significantly. Technology allows both private companies and public authorities to make use of personal data on an unprecedented scale in order to pursue their activities. Those differences may therefore constitute an obstacle to the pursuit of economic activities at the level of the Union, distort competition and impede authorities in the discharge of their responsibilities under Union law. - (10) In order to ensure a consistent and high level of protection of natural persons and to remove the obstacles to flows of personal data within the Union, the level of protection of the rights and freedoms of natural persons with regard to the processing of such data should be equivalent in all Member States. This Regulation also provides a margin of manoeuvre for Member States to specify its rules, including for the processing of special categories of personal data (‘sensitive data’). To that extent, this Regulation does not exclude Member State law that sets out the circumstances for specific processing situations, including determining more precisely the conditions under which the processing of personal data is lawful.
