Moreover, there is, at the time of writing, no secondary market for NFTs within China and Alibaba has expressly prohibited the re-sale of NFTs on its own secondary market Xianyu. It remains to be seen whether a secondary market for NFTs is permitted over time, but a constraining factor may be the state’s fear that NFTs, if granted more freedom, might develop into financial tradeable assets that fly in the face of the cryptocurrency ban.

6.25 It is important to understand that in China the state has an appetite for blockchain, the underlying technology, despite its comprehensive ban on cryptocurrency. NFTs can consequently sit on the alliance chain, a version of blockchain developed in China and be collected. Here NFTs are created only by commissioned artists and can be collected but trading, such as a commercial re-sale, is not permitted through this type of platform. Here then there is controlled and limited NFT or digital collectible marketplace. There are, however, other more versatile platforms for trading NFTs; they allow NFT creators including individuals, rather than just commissioned artists, to display their NFT creations, though there is a limit on the number of copies they can upload to the platform, and these can be sold either directly from the platform or via an external platform in another jurisdiction. This enables a sale through OpenSea or another overseas marketplace. These digital art platforms can be based on the Ethereum public chain which is currently the decentralised NFT trading platform in China. One of the best known of these platforms NFTCN, operated by Bigverse, is attracting subscribers not only in China but worldwide.

6.26 The appetite for blockchain is driven by the state network, Blockchain Services Network, which seeks to keep separation between blockchain, NFTs and cryptocurrency. The authors' view is that NFTs might well be able to exist and thrive in China for so long as they are controlled by the state such that they lack the financial ingredient that would render them a cryptocurrency and are kept separate from cryptocurrencies and their economy. Blockchain does not seem an obvious technology for China to adopt and foster, its attributes of relative anonymity and immutability do not play well with censorship or surveillance and its decentralisation runs counter to the state’s apparent commitment to data sovereignty, so ultimate state ownership of data that is within its borders, and the centralised control that underlies China’s system of governance. Accordingly, in China, the regulatory landscape and development of blockchain have ensured that in practice it cannot operate in a pure, decentralised way and state control is able to guide its application. The purist might argue that this is not blockchain at all but China evidently sees great value in blockchain and aims to become a technological leader and, as declared by Xi Jinping at a study session for the Politburo in October 2019, a blockchain ‘rule-maker’. The irony is that this rule-making approach threatens to undermine the very features that render blockchain such a useful technology for ensuring transparency, immutability and access that are beyond the influence of a central authority. China’s vision of blockchain then is a blockchain that is tempered so as to disable or dilute decentralisation.
