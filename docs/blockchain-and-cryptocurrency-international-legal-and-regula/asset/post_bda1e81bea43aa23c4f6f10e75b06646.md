
12.19 The defence of artistic licence does seem to be particularly effective in defeating claims as is also the outcome in AM General LLC v Activision Blizzard, Inc (S.D.N.Y. 2020) where AM General brought a claim against Activision Blizzard for infringing their trademark by using what they alleged was their trademark protected truck in a videogame of Call of Duty. The Court similarly found for Activision on the basis that the use of the truck had artistic licence and further they found that the use of an actual real-world vehicle in the context of the game helped provide a sense of realism and lifelikeness in what was supposed to be a game of modern warfare. This is not to say that trademark claims will always fail in the Metaverse because they have failed in some of the gaming claims but it is a signal that great care would have to be exercised. One feature that might cause a claim to succeed will be where the use of the trademark is evidently commercial and where it is held to be capable or likely of confusing the consumer into believing that the infringer is in fact the trademark owner and that the logo has been used or created by them.

12.20 Conversely users in the Metaverse will need to tread with caution to ensure they do not infringe or potentially infringe a trademark. This can be tricky given the number and proliferation of trademarks but the same caution that might be exercised in the real world should be exercised in the Metaverse if the claim is to be avoided. In the same way that a user might tread carefully as to what it represents, trademark owners will need to keep a lookout for infringements of their trademarks and they might consider using an expert trademark monitoring or watch company to keep a lookout and to closely monitor potential infringements in the Metaverse.

12.21 The platform ought also to monitor and be aware of activity on its platform and act when it fears there may have been infringing activity. It is also expected that where a potential trademark infringement is identified by a trademark owner the platform would be notified of this so that it can consider whether it can take action under its terms and conditions and applicable licences. Indeed, one advantage of the Metaverse might be the ability of the trademark owner to contact the platform and try to use this as a means to bring an end to the infringing activity. It was mentioned earlier that there might be circumstances where a user could be expelled from the Metaverse and his property ‘deleted’; this might be one of those situations, if the infringement is considered significantly material, grave and intentional. Again, much would depend on the user agreement and licence terms that were applicable in that situation.

12.22 The moral of the tale is that trademark owners should ensure that their trademarks are registered so that they have the best chance of being enforced in the Metaverse. Trademarks can be registered with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) or with equivalent organisations. In the author’s experience with their clients the registration with the USPTO in the US appear to be the preferred route in the hope of ensuring that the owner is able to maintain exclusive rights to the trademark in the Metaverse.

## 12.23 The Metaverse

12.23 It should also be noted that given the possibilities of challenges being brought under various laws in various jurisdictions that there can be different outcomes to claims brought in the Metaverse and this will equally apply to trademarks. That said it still makes sense to take steps to protect a trademark in the major jurisdictions of the world and that way seek to optimise protection. The author's expectation is that due to the commercial nature of the Metaverse and the scope for commercial advantage through the use of a brand or trademark that there will be lucrative opportunities for those who seek to infringe. This may also make it more likely for successful claims against infringements where it can be demonstrated that there was a design to profit from the deliberate, unauthorised, commercial application of another brand's logo. In the realm of games there is often not the same level of commerciality as will exist in the Metaverse and this could prove decisive in future decisions.

## Copyrights

12.24 Another area that will be a fierce battleground in the Metaverse is copyright. Whilst there are differences in copyright law and its protection around the world there are certainly some common themes and the author's view is that copyright will be severely stress tested given the immersive nature of the Metaverse and potential tension it will likely cause between the platform owners and users especially in the commercial war that will likely wage between participants to generate influence and win business. Copyright protection in the major global jurisdictions extends to protect original works of authorship that are fixed in a tangible form of expression. Copyrights, which are literally the right to copy, are exclusive rights granted to the author(s) or creator(s) of an original work including the right to copy, distribute and adapt that work. Copyright essentially protects the expression of what may be termed the fixation of an idea and not the idea itself. It is thus the transformation of the idea into a creation that is afforded protection. Copyright laws also set out those categories of works that are copyrightable, i.e. those that are capable of being afforded copyright protection and there can be no doubt that the Metaverse will produce many works that are copyrightable. This is not to say that every work created in the Metaverse will be copyrightable but certainly works that include artistic images or graphics or software or music or other creative form, for example an avatar, will be copyrightable.

12.25 If one looks to gaming and the type of disputes that have arisen, it seems apparent that these and more will arise in the Metaverse; this is especially likely given its commercial nature and the jockeying that will go on to sell goods and services. In the same way that challenges in the gaming arena can prove difficult so it will likely be the case with the Metaverse where a copyright owner will first need to identify the misuse of the copyright and then be able to demonstrate that its infringement is sufficient to form an actionable breach. Add to this the contractual layer, namely that user agreements and various licences will also need to be examined to ensure, for example, that the copyrighted work was a permitted use within the Metaverse or that a creation was permitted in accordance with the terms of use or applicable licence. There can be no doubt that licensing will be key in the Metaverse and there may well be collisions of contractual rights asserted under licences with trademark laws that seek to protect the creation of originality.

12.26 The creation of originality will be a hotly contested space in the Metaverse where avatars, for example, will be used and a number of tricky legal questions will arise. Those questions will include who owns the avatar, whose copyright is within that avatar and is an avatar a work of authorship that may be registered or is protected separately from the Metaverse in which it appears? Avatars are already in use in gaming and once this is extended to the Metaverse there will be a profound expansion of their use and, given the immersive and commercial nature of the Metaverse, the likelihood of increased disputes as to who owns that avatar’s copyright and consequently the value that goes with it. In order to understand how the problem may arrive one should first understand how an avatar may be developed. Whilst a platform may create or develop an avatar it will sometimes be the case, as in gaming, that platform owners will offer their users the possibility to adapt, create or personalise their avatars such that they have unique or distinctive traits, appearances and characteristics. Where the avatar’s user has exercised their creative influence a legal question will arise in a number of jurisdictions as to whether they have made significant creative decisions and can consequently be regarded as the author and copyright owner of what has become their unique and personalised avatar.

12.27 The difficulty with the application of copyright law as it exists in the major jurisdictions is that it is not well suited to the Metaverse and, in particular the creation and adaptation of avatars. The users who customise and creatively personalise their avatars surely deserve to be rewarded and in creating their own avatars ought to be considered the legal author of that work. Conversely the platform’s owners who will create the contractual documentation around the Metaverse and provide licences which may extend to the use of the avatars can properly argue that they set up the Metaverse landscape and that the avatar was brought to life by them and was indeed their creation. One might argue that where the revisions effected by the avatar user are slight or lack the requisite originality then the position is more straightforward, and the avatar remains the creation of the platform owner. That situation will however become much more challenging where the user has invested time, intellect and creativity to produce unique traits which extend beyond its distinctive appearance and include its manner of movement, its particular behaviours and style of communication and ‘dialect’. There are too many variations on an avatar to delve into at this juncture, however, it should be appreciated that the range of adaptations and personalisations are significant and a user could craft an avatar that is undoubtedly their creation. In such a scenario the authors suggest that there may be a form of collaborative ownership such that the avatar constitutes a work of joint ownership between the platform owner and the user.
