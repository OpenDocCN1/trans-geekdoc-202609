And yet that met with resistance; the Ethereum community had vocal members who railed at the notion of interfering with *The DAO*'s autonomy, viewing the hack as the use of agreed contractual terms to the hacker's advantage. ### 11.27
It should be apparent from this case study that any DAO is not beyond compromise and, in the event of a hack or malfunction, any participants in a DAO could find themselves exposed to liability as well as unable to effect any remedy or redress. The DAO is an autonomous organisation that is decentralised. There may be no contractual terms or conditions or any regulatory regime that automatically attaches, such that a situation can arise where a hacker might be able to use funds without any criminal or civil sanctions; whilst a perpetrator of an attack may then be free to act with impunity, the same cannot be said for investors who may face liability in relation to responsibilities that flow from the cyber-attack. The attack, for example, might of itself cause attention to be focused on the DAO and, if there have been breaches of laws and regulations in countries that the blockchain engages, the investors could find themselves facing actions for responsibilities they did not contemplate at the time of participating in the DAO.

### 11.28
Another concern is that, as the Internet of Things evolves, there will ever greater risk as smart contracts and autonomous technology controls all manner of tangibles and intangibles within our interconnected smart environment: *the car* that races away, doors locked, passengers trapped; or *the house* that is hacked, with all its interconnected content applied to the hacker's advantage. The risk that arises is not merely from the intelligence that accrues from smart contracts and code, but from the autonomy that is granted to the technology. If that autonomy is regarded as legally binding, as some argued when called upon to vary *The DAO*'s underlying code, the victims of malfunction or exploitation suffer the double indignity of a loss that is proclaimed as lawful. Smart contracts can have vulnerabilities and imperfections; it is beyond the wit of man to produce perfection on every occasion. It is beyond common sense to assert that *The DAO* was unique when it failed.

### 11.29
As has been noted earlier in this chapter, a DAO itself is arguably not an entity with any recognised legal status, or at any rate it does not have certainty in that regard. There are some lines of argument that maintain that it is likely classified as a general partnership or, if it lacks the pre-requisite partnership elements, a joint venture. Again, this will depend on the jurisdiction that one is considering and the position may likely not be fixed, given that it will be sensitive to the laws particular to each jurisdiction. There needs to be legal certainty not only as to the legal status of the DAO but also as to the Ether tokens: are they investment contracts, and consequently subject to securities regulation, or not? This appears to be the likely approach if one takes the view that investors in the DAO had a reasonable expectation of profits as a result of its management by others, but there are counter-arguments that hold interests in a partnership are not securities and, again, it will depend on which jurisdictional prism we apply to form our view of what a DAO, the tokens on the Ethereum blockchain and the tokens in a DAO are as a matter of law.

### 11.30
The way through this uncertainty might be to create a form of standard legal entity that wraps around the DAO, what can be termed a 'legal wrapper'. This would be agreed upon in advance and a more conventional legal structure engineered, such that membership and shareholdings were clearly stipulated and the legal status of the wrapper was certain and safer than, say, a general partnership. To be clear, in a general partnership, its members (effectively, its partners) are deemed to jointly represent the DAO and are responsible and liable for its obligations, actions and omissions. In this way, members might build a shield against personal liability and have more certainty with less risk.

### 11.31
Given the importance of the choice of jurisdiction, one might also create a separate, traditional 'off chain' contract that stipulates which jurisdiction and law are exclusively applicable. This approach is examined in more detail in Chapter 3 in relation to smart contracts. The contract here might also address cryptocurrency and cryptoassets under the control of the DAO, to ensure that they are subject to multiple-signature escrow wallets that the DAO members are better able to control. When one appreciates that DAOs are essentially a combination of smart contracts (or, if you prefer, a super smart contract), it follows that all the precautions required to circumvent smart contract issues will be requisite.

### 11.32
It will be interesting to watch the DAO space, especially in the wake of the recent Wyoming DAO law (see 11.17 above) that came into force on 1 July 2021 and enabled DAOs to have formal legal status as a Limited Liability Company. Malta might argue that it was the first to pass legislation in 2019 that paved the way for DAOs to have legal status by enacting that an Innovative Technology Arrangement ('ITA') could be an organisation 'with or without legal personality' however, for many purists, the Maltese law placed too much emphasis on the responsibility of managers. In the authors' view neither the Malta initiative nor the Wyoming DAO law will be conclusive as to the future of the DAO which, despite new use cases springing up daily, particularly in the crypto sector, remains shrouded in legal uncertainty. Wyoming's impact will be tempered by it being a relatively small State in terms of populous and that, in the US it is plain that federal securities regulation has primacy and will certainly trump local law in any contest. For so long as the characterisation of DAO tokens is in doubt any DAO venture will face an uncertain regulatory future. Given the rise and rise of DAOs on the back of NFTs and DeFi something will need to give; the authors sincere hope is that commercial and technological sense prevails so that regulation doesn't strangle the DAO's undoubted potential.

## The Metaverse

### 12.1
There is a great deal of confusion about what the Metaverse is and what it can actually do. In order to properly understand the meaning of a Metaverse, it will assist if one first appreciates its technological characteristics. The Metaverse is a form of internet application, what some might term an extension of our existing internet version Web 2.0 and it combines a number of new or emerging technologies. This combination of technology, that might be termed multi-technology, sees a convergence of artificial intelligence, blockchain, spatial-temporal consistency, cybersecurity and privacy. There are in fact a multitude of technological combinations and possibilities but the fundamental common technologies or common denominators of the Metaverse comprise of at least the above. It is important to note that Artificial Intelligence (AI) Algorithms, for example machine learning, deep learning, reinforcement learning and the like, are essential to connect a virtual world and real world. AI’s components including information, algorithms and computing power must play an important role in establishing and developing a Metaverse. It is by using AI that a Metaverse can create social and other economic activities that are further than the boundaries of what we experience in our actual, real world. Using computer vision, intelligent voice, language processing and other technologies users will be given the same visual and auditory sensations as in the real world.
