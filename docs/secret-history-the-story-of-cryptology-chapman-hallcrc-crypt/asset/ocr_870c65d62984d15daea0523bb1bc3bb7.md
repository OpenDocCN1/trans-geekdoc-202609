So, a purely investigatory line of thought, not backed by any theory, pointed us to an interesting feature of DES that we had not anticipated. Though the result was not new, I think it serves as a good illustration of how research may go. Even failures may prove interesting and useful.

Several DES variants have been offered to address attacks. The most notable (and simplest) is Triple DES, but there is also DES with Independent Subkeys, DESX, CRYPT(3), Generalized DES (GDES), DES with Alternate *S*-Boxes, RDES, *n*DES (a number of variants, with *n* taking the values 2, 3, 4, and 5), DES with Key-Dependent *S*-Boxes, and NEWDES.[56]

# 13.5.1 Cryptologic Humor

In the Vol. 0, No. 0, issue of *Journal of Cryptology*, the paper “Terms Used in the disciplines of Cryptography, IT Security and Risk Analysis” offered the following definition:

> **DES.** *n.* *Abbr.* Cryptographic algorithm with key size chosen so that it can be Decrypted Easily by Security agencies.

The Electronic Frontier Foundation included the following claim in their book on the EFF’s DES cracker.

> If a civil liberties group can build a DES Cracker for $200,000, it’s pretty likely that governments can do the same thing for under a million dollars.[57]
