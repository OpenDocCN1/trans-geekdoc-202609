Elliptic curves don’t resemble ellipses. The name arises from their connection with elliptic integrals, which arose in the 19th century, as mathematicians attempted to find formulas for arc lengths of ellipses. Examples are given by $\int_c^d \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^3+ax+b}}$ and $\int_c^d \frac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{x^3+ax+b}}$.

Setting the denominator of either integrand above equal to $y$, then gives us $y^2=x^3+ax+b$, an elliptic curve.

We define addition of points on an elliptic curve in a strange way. To add points $P_1$ and $P_2$, we draw a line through them and observe that this line passes through a third point on the curve, $I$. No, $I$ is not the sum! $I$ is merely an intermediate point. We reflect the point $I$ about the $x$-axis to get a new point $P_3$. Then we say $P_1+P_2=P_3$. This is illustrated in **Figure 20.5**.
