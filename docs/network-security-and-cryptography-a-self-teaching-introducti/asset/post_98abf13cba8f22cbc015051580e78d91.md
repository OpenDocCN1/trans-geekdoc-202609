# 4.11.1 Primitive Roots

- -By Euler's Theorem, $g^{\phi(n)}=1 \pmod n$ holds if $g$ and $n$ are relatively prime, where $\phi(n)$ is Euler's Totient Function of $n$, which is defined as the number of non-negative integers less than $n$ and relatively prime to $n$.

- -In addition, there may exist some integers $m < \phi(n)$ that satisfy $gm \equiv 1 \pmod n$.

- -The lowest value of $m$ that satisfies $g^m \equiv 1 \pmod n$ is called the “Order of $g \pmod n$”.

- -If the “Order of $g \pmod n$” equals $\phi(n)$, then integer $g$ is called a “Primitive Root of $n$”.

# 4.11.1.1 Significance of Primitive Roots

If $g(1 < g < \phi(n))$ is a primitive of positive integer $n$, then its powers $\{g^2, g^3, \dots, g^{\phi(n)}\pmod n\}$ are all distinct relatively prime to $n$.

Conversely, we can say that a positive integer $g$ is called a primitive root of positive integer $n$, if its powers $\{g, g^2, \dots, g^{\phi(n)}\pmod n\}$ are all distinct and relatively prime to $n$.

If $p$ is a prime number and $g$ is a primitive root of $p$, then the powers $g^2, g^3, \dots, g^{p-1}(\pmod p)$ will all be distinct and relatively prime to $p$. The set of these values will be a permutation of $\mathbb{Z}_p^*=\{1, 2, 3, \dots, (p-1)\}$.

# 4.12 DISCRETE LOGITHMS

Let $p$ be a prime number and $g$ be one of its primitive roots, and $y=g^x \pmod p$ will be an integer $(0 < y < p)$.
In addition, $x=\log_g y$ is called a Discrete logarithm of $y$ to the base $g$. Sometimes $x$ is also denoted as $ind_{g,p}(y)$, that is index of $y$ to base $g$.

# 4.12.1 Difficulty of Computing Logarithms

Given $g, y$ and $p$, it is considered easy to compute $y=g^x \pmod p$. However, given $y, g$ and $p$, when $p$ is large, it is considered extremely difficult to compute $x$, that is, the discrete logarithm of $y$ to the base $g$.

# 4.12.2 Algorithm to Determine the Primitive Roots of a Number $n$

- Step 1: Determine $\phi(n)$
- Step 2: Determine the prime factors of $\phi(n)$. Let these be $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k$.
- Step 3: For each $m \in \{2, 3, \dots, n-1\}$ do $\{(m)^{\phi(n)/p_1}, (m)^{\phi(n)/p_2}, \dots, (m)^{\phi(n)/p_k} \pmod n\}$
If none of the $k$ values determined previously is 1, then $m$ is a primitive root of $n$, else it is not.

- Step 1: Determine integer $g(1 < g < \phi(n))$ such that $g$ is one of the primitive roots of $n$.
- Step 2: Let the set of primitive roots be $\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k\}$.
- Step 3: For $i=1$ to $k$ do $g^i \pmod n$
Thus, the primitive roots of $n$.
