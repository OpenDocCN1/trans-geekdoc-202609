# 4.9.1 General Formula for Computation of Totient Function $\phi(n)$

Let $n$ be any positive integer $> 1$.

It can be expressed as a product of prime numbers as follows:

```
$n=p_1^{a_1}\times p_2^{a_2}\times...\times p_k^{a_k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_i^{a_i}$, where $p_1\cdot p_2\cdots p_k$ are prime numbers and $a_1,a_2,...,a_k$ are positive integers.
```

Then Euler's Totient Function of $n$ will be:

```
$\phi(n)=p_1^{k_1-1}\phi(p_1)p_2^{k_2-1}\phi(p_2)\dots p_k^{k_k-1}\phi(p_k)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(p_i^{k_i-1}\phi(p_i)) \quad (4.11)$
```

# Example 4.26

Determine $\phi(n)$ for the following values of $n$:

- **A.** 735
- **B.** 400
- **C.** 3375

# Solution

- **A.** $735=7^2 \times 5 \times 3$
$\phi(735)=\phi(7^2) \times\phi(5) \times\phi(3)$
$=7 \times\phi(7) \times 4 \times 2$
$=7 \times 6 \times 4 \times 2$
$=336$

- **B.** $400=2^4 \times 5^2$
$\phi(400)=\phi(2^4) \times\phi(5^2)$
$=2^3 \times\phi(2) \times 5 \times\phi(5)$
$=8 \times 1 \times 5 \times 4$
$=160$

- **C.** $3375=5^3 \times 3^3$
$\phi(3375)=\phi(5^3) \times\phi(3^3)$
$=5^2 \times\phi(5) \times 3^2 \times\phi(3)$
$=25 \times 4 \times 9 \times 2$
$=1800$

# 4.10 EULER'S THEOREM

If $a$ and $n$ are two integers relatively prime to each other, then $a^{\phi(n)}\equiv 1 \pmod{n}$.

Proof:

By definition of Euler's Totient Function, an integer $n$ will have precisely $\phi(n)$ integers less than $n$ and relatively prime to $n$. The set of those $\phi(n)$ integers be:

```
$X = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{\phi(n)}\}$
```

Multiplying the elements of $X$ by $(a \bmod n)$, we get set $Y$:
$Y = \{ax \bmod n, ax \bmod n, \dots, ax_{\phi(n)} \bmod n\}$

Since $x_i (i=1..\phi(n))$ are relatively prime to $n$ and also $a$ is relatively prime to $n$, all elements of set $Y$ will be less than $n$ and relatively prime to $n$.

Also, since $x_i(i=1..\phi(n))$ are distinct and $a$ is relatively prime to $n$, all elements of set $Y$ will be distinct.

Therefore $Y$ is a set of $\phi(n)$ distinct integers less than $n$ and relatively prime to $n$. This is the precise definition of set $X$. Thus, $Y$ is a permutation of set $X$.

Therefore, the product of all elements of set $Y$ will be congruent to the product of all elements of set $X$.

```
$Y = [(ax \bmod n)(ax \bmod n)\dots\times(ax_{\phi(n)} \bmod n)] \equiv (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{\phi(n)}) \bmod n$
```

Using the identity $a \bmod p \cdot b \bmod p = (A \times B) \bmod p$, we get:

```
$(x_1)^{a}(x_2)^{a}\dots\times(x_{\phi(n)})^{a} \equiv (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{\phi(n)}) \bmod n$
```
```
$a^{\phi(n)} = \prod_{i=1}^{\phi(n)} x_i \equiv \prod_{i=1}^{\phi(n)} x_i \bmod n$
```
Since $\prod_{i=1}^{\phi(n)} x_i$ is the product of integers relatively prime to $n$, $\prod_{i=1}^{\phi(n)} x_i$ will also be relatively prime to $n$. Therefore we can divide the above congruence by:

```
$a^{\phi(n)} = 1 \bmod n$
```
Thus, Euler's Theorem stands.

# 4.10.1 Corollary of Euler's Theorem

Let $n = pq$, where $p$ and $q$ are prime numbers. Let $M$ be any integer such that $0 < M < n$. Then $M^{\phi(n)}\equiv M \pmod n$ will hold for $M$ that may not be relatively prime to $n$.

Proof: There are two cases:

- **Case I:** $M$ is relatively prime to $n$, that is $GCD(M, n) = 1$.
Then, by Euler's Theorem:
```
$M^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$
```
Multiplying both sides of the above congruence by $M$:
```
$M^{\phi(n)+1} \equiv M \pmod n$
```

- **Case II:** $M$ is not relatively prime to $n$, that is $GCD(M, n) > 1$.
Since $0 < M < n$ and $n$ are products of $p$ and $q$, it is not possible for $M$ to have both $p$ and $q$ as factors; there are possibilities:

- **A.** $M$ is a multiple of $p$, but relatively prime to $q$, $GCD(M, q) = 1$.
- **B.** $M$ is a multiple of $q$, but relatively prime to $p$, $GCD(M, p) = 1$.

Case II (a): $M$ is a multiple of $p$. Let $M = cp$ where $c$ is an integer $> 0$. $M$ will not have $q$ as factor; therefore, by Euler's Theorem:
```
$M^{\phi(q)} \equiv 1 \pmod q$
```
Raising both sides of the above congruence to power $\phi(p)$:
```
$[M^{\phi(q)}]^{\phi(p)} \equiv (1)^{\phi(p)} \pmod q$
```
```
$M^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod q$
```
Thus there will be some integer $k$ such that:
```
$M^{\phi(n)} = kq + 1$
```
Multiplying both sides by $M = cp$:
```
$M^{\phi(n)+1} \equiv kpq + M = kn + M$
```
$M^{\phi(n)+1} \equiv M \pmod n$, which holds when $p$ is a factor of $M$.

Case II (b): $M$ is a multiple of $q$ and $GCD(M, p) = 1$. The proof is similar to case II(a). Thus, $M^{\phi(n)+1} \equiv M \pmod n$, with $n=pq$, where $p$ and $q$ are prime numbers and $0 < M < n$.

# Example 4.27

Suppose $G$ is a finite cyclic group of order $|G|$. Prove that it will have exactly $\phi(|G|)$ generators.

**Solution**

Let $G = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ..., (n-1)\}$ be a finite cyclic group of order $|G|=n$. With respect to Addition Operation $(\mod n)$, any integer $a \in G$ will be a generator of $G$ provided $a is relatively prime to $n$, that is $GCD(a,n) = 1$.

Now, the number of integers in $G$ that are relatively prime to $n$ is $\phi(n)=\phi(|G|)$.

# Example 4.28

Let $G=\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ be a cyclic group of order 6. Determine the generators of $G$.

# Solution

$|G|=6$. The group is called cyclic, since all its elements can be generated as a power $a^k \mod 6$, where $a^k \mod 6=(a+a+\dots+a)\mod 6=(k\times a)\mod 6$ $k$-times. Where $a^0=0$ and $a^{-k}=(-a)^k$.

Let us now check for generators of $G$. As obvious, 0 is not a generator of $G$. Let us check 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5.

- $1^0 \mod 6=0$
- $1^1 \mod 6=1\mod 6=1$
- $1^2 \mod 6=(1+1)\mod 6=2$
- $1^3 \mod 6=(1+1+1)\mod 6=3$
- $1^4 \mod 6=(1+1+1+1)\mod 6=4$
- $1^5 \mod 6=(1+1+1+1+1)\mod 6=5$
- $1^6 \mod 6=(1+1+1+1+1+1)\mod 6=0$

The cycle repeats after $1^5 \mod 6$, and all elements of $G$ appear in the cycle.

- 1 is a generator of $G$.

Let us now check for 2.

- $2^0 \mod 6=0$
- $2^1 \mod 6=2$
- $2^2 \mod 6=4$
- $2^3 \mod 6=0$
- $2^4 \mod 6=2$
- $2^5 \mod 6=4$
- $2^6 \mod 6=0$

The cycle repeats after $2^2 \mod 6$, and not elements of $G$ appear in the cycle.

Let us now check for 3.

- $3^0 \mod 6=0$
- $3^1 \mod 6=3$
- $3^2 \mod 6=0$

The cycle repeats after $3^1 \mod 6$, and not elements of $G$ appear in the cycle.

Let us now check for 4.

- $4^0 \mod 6=0$
- $4^1 \mod 6=4$
- $4^2 \mod 6=4$
- $4^3 \mod 6=4$

The cycle repeats after $4^1 \mod 6$, and not elements of $G$ appear in the cycle.

Let us now check for 5.

- $5^0 \mod 6=0$
- $5^1 \mod 6=5$
- $5^2 \mod 6=1$
- $5^3 \mod 6=5$
- $5^4 \mod 6=1$
- $5^5 \mod 6=5$
- $5^6 \mod 6=0$

The cycle repeats after $5^5 \mod 6$, and all elements of $G$ appear in the cycle.

- 5 is a generator of $G$.

Thus the generators of $G$ are $\{1,5\}$.
