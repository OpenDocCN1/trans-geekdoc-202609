
We say that $\{X_k\}$, $k=0, 1, 2, \dots, n$ is an independent process if

$F_X(x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n; t_1, \dots, t_n) = F_{X_0}(x_0; t_0)F_{X_1}(x_1; t_1) \dots F_{X_n}(x_n; t_n)$

In addition, if random variables are drawn from the same distribution, the process is characterized by a single CDF, $F_X(x; t_k)$, $k=0, 1, 2, \dots, n$. In this case, we call $\{X_k\}$ a sequence of independent and identically distributed (IID) random variables.

Having defined the CDF and PDF for a random process $X(t)$, we are now prepared to define the statistical (or ensemble) averages—the mean, variance, autocorrelation, and autocovariance of $X(t)$. In general, for random variables, these statistics play an important role in practical applications.

The mean or expected value of the random process $X(t)$ is

$m_X(t) = E[X(t)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f_X(x;t)dx \quad (2.88)$

where $E[.]$ denotes ensemble average, $f_X(x;t)$ is the PDF of $X(t)$, and $X(t)$ is regarded as a random variable for a fixed value of $t$. In general, $m_X(t)$ is a function of time.

The variance of a random process $X(t)$ is given by

$Var(X) = \sigma_X^2 = E[(X(t)-m_X(t))^2] = E[X^2]-m_X^2 \quad (2.89)$

The autocorrelation of a random process $X(t)$ is the joint moment of $X(t_1)$ and $X(t_2)$, that is,

$R_X(t_1,t_2) = E[X(t_1)X(t_2)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1x_2 f_X(x_1,x_2;t_1,t_2)dx_1dx_2 \quad (2.90)$

where $f_X(x_1,x_2;t_1,t_2)$ is the second-order PDF of $X(t)$. In general, $R_X(t_1,t_2)$ is a deterministic function of two variables $t_1$ and $t_2$. The autocorrelation function is important because it describes the power-spectral density of a random process.

The covariance or autocovariance of a random process $X(t)$ is the covariance of $X(t_1)$ and $X(t_2)$, that is,

$Cov[X(t_1),X(t_2)] = C_X(t_1,t_2) = E[\{X(t_1)-m_X(t_1)\}\{X(t_2)-m_X(t_2)\}] \quad (2.91a)$

or

$Cov[X(t_1),X(t_2)] = R_X(t_1,t_2)-m_X(t_1)m_X(t_2) \quad (2.91b)$

indicating that the autocovariance can be expressed in terms of the autocorrelation and the means. Note that the variance of $X(t)$ can be expressed in terms of its autocovariance, that is,

$Var(X(t)) = C_X(t,t) \quad (2.92)$

The correlation coefficient of a random process $X(t)$ is the correlation coefficient of $X(t_1)$ and $X(t_2)$, that is,

$$\rho_X(t_1,t_2) = \frac{C_X(t_1,t_2)}{\sqrt{C_X(t_1,t_1)C_X(t_2,t_2)}} \quad (2.93)$$

where $|\rho_X(t_1,t_2)| \le 1$.

Finally, we define the nth joint moment of $X(t)$ as

$E[X(t_1)X(t_2)...X(t_n)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1x_2...x_nf_X(x_1,...,x_n,t_1,...,t_n)dx_1dx_2...dx_n \quad (2.94)$

We should keep in mind that the mean, variance, autocorrelation, autocovariance, and nth joint moment are good indicators of the behavior of a random process but only partial characterizations of the process. In terms of these statistics, a random process may be classified as follows:

-1. A random process is wide-sense stationary (WSS) or weakly stationary if its mean is constant, that is,

$E[X(t_1)] = E[X(t_2)] = m_x = \text{constant} \quad (2.95)$

and its autocorrelation depends only on the absolute difference $\tau = |t_1-t_2|$, that is,

$E[X(t)X(t+\tau)] = R_X(\tau) \quad (2.96)$

Note that the autocovariance of a WSS process depends only on the time difference $\tau$

$C_X(\tau) = R_X(\tau) - m_x^2 \quad (2.97)$

and by setting $\tau=0$ in (2.96), we get

$E[X^2(t)] = R_X(0) \quad (2.98)$

indicating that the power of a WSS process $X(t)$ does not depend on $t$. The autocorrelation function has its maximum value when $\tau=0$ so we can write

$-R_X(0) \le R_X(\tau) \le R_X(0) \quad (2.99)$

-2. A random process is said to be strict-stationary (SSS) if its statistics are invariant to a shift in time. Hence,

$F_X(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n; t_1+\tau, t_2+\tau, \dots, t_n+\tau) = F_X(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n; t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) \quad (2.100)$

An SSS random process is also WSS, but the converse is not generally true. In general terms, a random process is stationary if all its statistical properties do not vary with time.

## 2.11 TIME AVERAGES OF RANDOM PROCESSES AND ERGODITY

For a random process $X(t)$, we can define two types of averages: ensemble and time. The ensemble averages (or statistical averages) of a random process $X(t)$ may be regarded as “averages across the process” because they involve all sample functions of the process observed at a particular instant of time. The time averages of a random process $X(t)$ may be regarded as “averages along the process” because they involve long-term sampling of the process.

To define the time averages, consider the sample function $x(t)$ of random process $X(t)$, which is observed within the time interval $-T \le t \le T$. The time average (or time mean) of the sample function is

$$\bar{x}=x(t)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}x(t)dt$$

where $\langle \cdot \rangle$ denotes a time-averaging operation. Similarly, the time autocorrelation of the sample function $x(t)$ is given by

$$\bar{R}_x(\tau)=x(t)x(t+\tau)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}x(t)x(t+\tau)$$

Note that $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{R}_x(\tau)$ are random variables, whose values depend on the observation interval and on the sample function $x(t)$ used.

If all time averages are equal to their corresponding ensemble averages, then the stationary process is ergodic, that

$$\bar{x}=x(t)=E[X(t)]=m_x$$

$$\bar{R}_x(\tau)=x(t)x(t+\tau)=E[X(t)X(t+\tau)]=R_x(\tau)$$

An ergodic process is one for which time and ensemble averages are interchangeable.

The concept of ergodicity is a very powerful tool and it is always assumed in many engineering applications. This is due to the fact that it is impractical to have a large number of sample functions to work with. Ergodicity suggests that if a random process is ergodic, only one sample function is necessary to determine the ensemble averages. This seems reasonable because in infinite time each sample function of a random process would take on, at one or another, all the possible values of the process. We will use throughout this that the random processes we will encounter are ergodic and WSS.

Basic quantities such as dc, rms value, and average power can be defined in terms of time averages of an ergodic process as follows:

-1. $\bar{x}=m_x$ is the dc value of $x(t)$.
2. $[\bar{x}^2]=m_x^2$ is the normalized dc power.
3. $\bar{R}_x(0)=R_X(0)$ is the average power.
4. $\sigma_X^2$ is the ac-varying component of the signal.
5. $\sqrt{\sigma_X^2}$ is the rms value of $x(t)$.

## 2.12 MULTIPLE RANDOM PROCESSES

The joint behavior of two or more random processes is dictated by their joint distributions. For example, two processes $X(t)$ and $Y(t)$ are said to be independent if for $t_1$ and $t_2$, the random variables $X(t_1)$ and $Y(t_2)$ are independent. That means that their $n$th order joint PDF factors,

$F_{XY}(x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2, \dots, x_n, y_n; t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) = F_X(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n; t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) F_Y(y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n; t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) \quad (2.105)$

The crosscorrelation between two random processes $X(t)$ and $Y(t)$ is defined as

$R_{XY}(t_1, t_2) = E[X(t_1)Y(t_2)] \quad (2.106)$

Note that

$R_{XY}(t_1, t_2) = R_{YX}(t_2, t_1) \quad (2.107)$

The processes $X(t)$ and $Y(t)$ are said to be orthogonal if

$R_{XY}(t_1, t_2) = 0 \quad \text{for all } t_1 \text{ and } t_2 \quad (2.108)$

If $X(t)$ and $Y(t)$ are jointly stationary, then their crosscorrelation function becomes

$R_{XY}(t_1, t_2) = R_{XY}(\tau)$

$\tau = t_2 - t_1$. Other properties of thecorrelation of jointly stationary processes are:

-1. $R_{XY}(-\tau) = R_{XY}(\tau)$, that is, it is symmetric.

-2. $|R_{XY}(\tau)| \le \sqrt{R_X(0)R_Y(0)}$, that is, it is bounded.
