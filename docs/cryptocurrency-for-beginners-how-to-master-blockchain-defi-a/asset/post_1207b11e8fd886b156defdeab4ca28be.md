Commodity money has addressed these issues. Money from goods is a form of good that serves as a currency. For instance, American colonists made use of dried corn and beaver pelts in transactions in the early part of the 17th and 18th centuries. These goods, having generally recognized values, were used to purchase and sell other items. There were some features of the goods that were used for trade: they were highly sought-after goods and, thus, expensive, but they were also compact, durable and easily stored.

A precious metal like gold is another more refined type of commodity money. For decades, before the 1970s, gold was used to significantly boost paper money. For example, in the case of the U.S. dollar, this means that foreign countries were willing to take their currencies and trade them with the United States at a specified rate for gold. This exchange was executed with the United States Federal Reserve. What's fascinating is that gold is valuable simply because people like it, unlike dried corn and beaver pelts (which can be used for food and clothes, respectively). You can't consume gold because it won't hold you warm at night; however, most people think it's wonderful, and they realize others think it's magnificent, too. It's not particularly helpful. Yes, gold has value. Gold acts as a tangible symbol of wealth because of the perceptions of people. As a symbol of something with utility, this association between money and gold gives insight into how money acquires its worth.

## 2.2 Fiat or Physical Money

Fiat money, which typically does not need support from a physical commodity, is the second form of money. Instead, supply and demand and the confidence of people in its worth set the value of fiat currencies. A fiat currency is a currency that does not have any inherent worth and is usually established by government legislation as a legal tender. Currencies have historically been dependent on physical resources such as gold and silver, but fiat currency is dependent on the economy's credit. The value of fiat money relies on supply and demand, and as an alternative to consumer money and representative money, it was adopted. Commodity money is derived from precious metals like silver and gold, while representative money is a claim that can be redeemed for a commodity. Fiat money evolved because gold was a finite resource, and fast-growing economies could not always mine enough gold to support their demands for currency supply. The need for gold to offer money value is extremely inefficient for a booming economy, particularly when its value is really generated by the perceptions of people. Fiat money is the token of the perception of value by people, the cornerstone for the creation of money. A growing economy is evidently succeeding in generating other useful items for it and other economies. The bigger the economy, the more perceived (and increasingly sought after) its capital would be and vice versa. The perceptions of people, though, must be backed by an economy that can deliver the goods and services that individuals require. In 1971, for instance, the U.S. dollar was withdrawn from the gold standard, the dollar was no more redeemable in gold, and the gold price was no longer set against any dollar value. This suggested that it was now possible to produce more paper money than the gold that was available to back it. The U.S. dollar's value would decrease both domestically through inflation and globally through currency exchange rates if the economy slows. The U.S. economy's implosion would send the globe into a bleak financial age, but many other nations and institutions are striving relentlessly to make sure it never does. Still, as determined by inflation, the value of a currency (not only the dollar but most currencies) is decided simply by its buying power. That's why merely printing new money won't produce a country's income. Money is generated through a sort of constant relationship between objects that are true, concrete, our appetite for them and our abstract belief in what has worth. Money is good because we like it, but we just want it because it will get us a product or service that we like. China, around 1000 AD, was the first nation to use fiat money, and the currency eventually spread to other countries around the world. When U.S. President Richard Nixon enacted a bill that prevented the converting of the U.S. dollar into gold, it became famous in the 20th century. Many nations still use paper-based fiat currencies that function only as a mode of payment. Unlike standard currencies dependent on commodities, it is not possible to exchange or reclaim fiat currencies. It is inherently worthless and is used by the order of the state. The government must safeguard it against counterfeiting and maintain the money supply responsibly in order for a fiat currency to be effective.

### 2.2.1 History of Fiat Money

In the 10th century, fiat money emerged from China, primarily in the dynasties of the Yuan, Tang, Song, and Ming. There was a strong demand for metallic currency in the Tang Dynasty (618-907) that surpassed the availability of precious metals. The individuals were acquainted with the use of credit slips, and paper or paper drafts were readily accepted by them. A lack of coins caused people to turn from coins to notes. There was a thriving industry in the Tchetchuan area during the Song Dynasty (960-1276), which led to a lack of copper money. Traders began issuing private notes backed by a cash reserve, and the first legal tender was thus issued. In the Yuan Dynasty (1276-1367), paper money became the only legal tender, and the issuance of notes was granted to the Ministry of Finance during the Ming Dynasty (1368-1644). In the 18th century, the West began using paper currency. The American colonies, the Continental Congress and France, continued to issue bills of credit that were used to make payments. Notes were issued by the regional governments that the holders would use to pay the authorities' taxes. Because of the risks of inflation, the issuance of so many credit bills produced some controversy. The bills depreciated significantly in some countries, such as New England and the Carolinas, and as the bills lost value, there was an increase in commodity prices. Countries shift to fiat currencies during conflicts to preserve the worth of precious metals such as gold and silver. For instance, during the American Civil War, the United States Federal Government switched to a form of fiat currency referred to as "Greenbacks." During this war, the government suspended the convertibility of its paper money to gold or silver. The government and banks had pledged in the early 20th century to facilitate the conversion of coins and notes into their nominal commodity. The high expenses of the American Civil War and the need to restore the economy, however, forced the government to terminate the redemption. The value of one troy ounce of gold was set, by the Bretton Woods Agreement, at 35 United States dollars. In 1971, however, the President of the United States, Richard Nixon, launched a variety of economic measures, including the cancellation of the direct convertibility of dollars into gold owing to the decline in gold reserves. Since then, fiat currencies that are exchangeable between major currencies have been adopted by most countries.
