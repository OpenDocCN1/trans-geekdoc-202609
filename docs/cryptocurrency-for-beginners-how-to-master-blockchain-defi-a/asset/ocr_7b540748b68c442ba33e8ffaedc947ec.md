# Money was created for social purposes

The second theory says that for social reasons, money was created, such as
fixing a bride's price or blood money for someone killed or wounded by
another tribe.

# Money was created for religious purposes

The third theory says that money was created for religious purposes. In his
book Holy Gold, Bernard Lam notes that the root of money was in the
Eastern temples. It was used as the mandated sacrifice to the gods and for
the priests' payment.

# The monetization of gold

Around 1500 BC and 1000 BC, by weight standard, the medium of
exchange changed from a cattle standard to gold. In transforming gold into
money, the temples played a major role. Gold and silver have accumulated
in the temples over the years. For decorative purposes, so much was
needed. The fact that so much gold was collected by the temples must have
been a key factor behind the decision to turn it into money or monetize it.
The theory is that by monetizing it, the priests would have wanted to use up
some of this excess gold: they may have estimated, for example, that 1 cow
was worth 130 grams of gold. The priests must have been paid for their
services overtime, such as guidance on where to grow crops, and would
have come up with a standard fee for these services. The theory that by
using arbitrary decisions, the priests calculated the value of gold contradicts
the origin of money trading, which suggests that the value of gold was
determined by the tasks performed in mining it and shaping it into a regular
unit of money.
