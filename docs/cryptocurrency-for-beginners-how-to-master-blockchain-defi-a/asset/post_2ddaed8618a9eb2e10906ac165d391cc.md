## 1.2.11 Evolution of gold

Gold has evolved over time as a consequence of three primary transitions: first, it became the global standard as a medium of exchange; second, an international banking system was built around it, with paper money fully convertible into gold; and third, convertibility into gold ceased in 1971, and gold was replaced by a pure fiat money system. The benefits of gold, on the other hand, have not altered in the 40 years since the world switched to a fiat money system. Governments discourage the use of gold as money for their citizens, but they trade in it through their central banks and treasuries, according to the financial world. There are confirmed claims that the US Treasury holds 261 million ounces of gold. In 2012, the Fed's assets were projected to reach 13.5 million ounces. India and China, through their central banks, have been buying gold at an alarming rate in recent months. As a result, in less than a year, they have boosted their gold holdings from 156 metric tons to more than 400 metric tons. This is a significant departure from the government's long-term dumping of gold equities during the last four or five decades. Even the International Monetary Fund (IMF) has lately overturned a long-standing policy of gold liquidation by purchasing 44 metric tons. Of course, gold ownership is a worldwide phenomenon. As a result, gold prices are influenced not just by the Federal Reserve of the United States but also by the actions of other central banks across the world. The ECB is widely expected to have to "print" as a last resort in order to rescue the euro. If this is the case, the impact of E.U. printing will exacerbate the current currency wars, as no country wants a strong currency. Until now, the European Union has been wary about printing money. If they become a major player in the currency wars, they will just add gasoline to the fire by speeding up global money issuance and lowering gold's put value. Gold, unlike many other assets, does not produce income or even promises to provide money at any point in the future. As a result, any profit from gold is solely based on its price fluctuation.

## Chapter 2: Money as Medium of Exchange

![](img/9e732599f379d4f1dd55a1fd64d01531_144_0.png)

In modern times, paper currency is the most widely used medium of exchange. Fiat money is made up of paper notes. To put it another way, their face and intrinsic values aren't the same. Fiat money is defined as a government-issued currency that is not backed by physical assets such as silver or gold. It is in contrast to commodity money, which has its own value. There are several advantages to using paper currency. The government can issue paper currency notes at a low cost because their intrinsic value is much lower than their face value. They are also simple to store due to their light weight, durability, and portability. They are perfectly safe to carry and store than gold coins. Paper currencies, unlike coins, are not good or bad; they are all the same. By regulating the supply of paper currency, the issuing authority can keep its value stable. The regulating authority has the power to increase and decrease its quantity. As a result, its supply can be described as elastic. The disadvantages of paper currency, on the other hand, cannot be overlooked. Governments issue these currencies, which are controlled by a central bank. Legitimate investors lose their money deposited in banks during liquidity crises and bank runs. The government issuing of too many currencies can lead to inflationary pressures. Inflation occurs when the overall price level rises, resulting in a decrease in the value of money. Paper money is not indestructible; it can be harmed by floods, fires, insects, and other natural disasters.
