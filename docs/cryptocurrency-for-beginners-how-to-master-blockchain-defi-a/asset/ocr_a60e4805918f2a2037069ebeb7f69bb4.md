Fiat money, which typically does not need support from a physical commodity, is the second form of money. Instead, supply and demand and the confidence of people in its worth set the value of fiat currencies. A Fiat currency is a currency that does not have any inherent worth and is usually established by government legislation as a legal tender. Currencies have historically been dependent on physical resources such as gold and silver, but fiat currency is dependent on the economy's credit. The value of fiat money relies on supply and demand, and as an alternative to consumer money and representative money, it was adopted. Commodity money is derived from precious metals like silver and gold, while representative money is a claim that can be redeemed for a commodity. Fiat money evolved because gold was a finite resource, and fast-growing economies could not always mine enough gold to support their demands for currency supply. The need for gold to offer money value is extremely inefficient for a booming economy, particularly when its value is really generated by the perceptions of people. Fiat money is the token of the perception of value by people, the cornerstone for the creation of money. A growing economy is evidently succeeding in generating other useful items for it and other economies. The bigger the economy, the more perceived (and increasingly sought after) its capital would be and vice versa. The perceptions of people, though, must be backed by an economy that can deliver the goods and services that individuals require. In 1971, for instance, the U.S. dollar was withdrawn from the gold standard, the dollar was no more redeemable in gold, and the gold price was no longer set against any dollar value. This suggested that it was now possible to produce more paper money than the gold that was available to back it.
