In the 18th Century, the West began using paper currency. The American colonies, the Continental Congress and France, continued to issue bills of credit that were used to make payments. Notes were issued by the regional governments that the holders would use to pay the authorities' taxes. Because of the risks of inflation, the issuance of so many credit bills produced some controversy. The bills depreciated significantly in some countries, such as New England and the Carolinas, and as the bills lost value, there was an increase in commodity prices. Countries shift to fiat currencies during conflicts to preserve the worth of precious metals such as gold and silver. For instance, during the American Civil War, the United States Federal Government switched to a form of fiat currency referred to as "Greenbacks." During this war, the government suspended the convertibility of its paper money to gold or silver. The government and banks had pledged in the early 20th century to facilitate the conversion of coins and notes into their nominal commodity. The high expenses of the American Civil War and the need to restore the economy, however, forced the government to terminate the redemption. The value of one troy ounce of gold was set, by the Bretten Woods Deal, at 35 United States dollars. In 1971, however, the President of the United States, Richard Nixon, launched a variety of economic measures, including the cancellation of the direct convertibility of dollars into gold owing to the decline in gold reserves. Since then, fiat currencies that are exchangeable between major currencies have been adopted by most countries.
