But that's not where the complications end; even if the person meets someone to exchange beef for bananas with, they might not think a whole cow is worth a bunch of bananas. Such a transaction involves an agreement to be made and a way to decide how many bananas would be enough to buy a certain portion of the cow.

Commodity capital has addressed these issues. Money from goods is a form of good that serves as a currency. For instance, American colonists made use of dried corn and beaver pelts in transactions in the early part of the 17th and 18th centuries. These goods, having generally recognized values, were used to purchase and sell other items. There were some features of the goods that were used for trade: they were highly sought-after goods and, thus, expensive, but they were also compact, durable and easily stored.

A precious metal like gold is another more refined type of commodity money. For decades, before the 1970s, gold was used to significantly boost paper money. For example, in the case of the U.S. dollar, this means that foreign countries were willing to take their currencies and trade them with the United States at a specified rate for gold. This exchange was executed with the United States Federal Reserve. What's fascinating is that gold is valuable simply because people like it, unlike dried corn and beaver pelts (which can be used for food and clothes, respectively). You can't consume gold because it won't hold you warm at night; however, most people think it's wonderful, and they realize others think it's magnificent, too. It's not particularly helpful. Yes, gold has value. Gold acts as a tangible symbol of wealth because of the perceptions of people. As a symbol of something with utility, this association between money and gold gives insight into how money acquires its worth.
