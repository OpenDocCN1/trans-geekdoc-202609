## 1.2 Evolution phases

The concept of a medium of exchange has been around for a long time. Now we will describe in detail how the instrument has evolved over time.

### 1.2.1 Barter System

The barter system is the oldest system with regard to a medium of exchange. Mesopotamians are thought to have introduced it around 6,000 BC. Services and goods were exchanged in this system. As a medium, there was no separate tool. Rather, goods and services served as the instruments. Despite the fact that this system was quite straightforward, it had a number of flaws. To begin with, the barter system necessitated locating a party with products of equal value, which was not always possible. Second, obtaining an exchange rate for all of the commodities was nearly impossible. Third, it was unable to develop the economy due to the scarcity of products. Fourth, because the comparable value of products was not always available, this system was virtually impossible to follow. Because of these flaws, the barter system fell out of favor, and the concept of currency was born.

### 1.2.2 Introduction of cowry shells as a medium of exchange

Cowry shells were used as coins in 1,200 BC to overcome the shortcomings of the barter system. These shells were small, convenient, and long-lasting, and they couldn't be forged. However, they were too readily available, making them too easy to obtain and eroding their value.

### 1.2.3 Gold metals as a medium of exchange

To address this issue, it was suggested that a medium be discovered that was both unique and hard to forge. Because metals met this requirement, metal coins were created. To serve as a medium, small gold coins were minted. The weights of these coins represented their value. The use of precious metals had the disadvantage of not being readily available in every location. There was also an increased risk of theft.

### 1.2.5 Gold's brief history

One cannot fully comprehend gold’s actual purpose without looking back at the onset of the gold market. The history of gold began in 3000 B.C. It was the time when the early Egyptians started making jewelry. This wasn't till 560 B.C. that gold began to be used as a medium of exchange. Merchants at the period were seeking a means to create a standardized, readily transportable form of money that would make the trade process easier and more efficient. Although gold jewelry was generally acknowledged and recognized at the time, the invention of a gold coin stamped with a seal looked to be the answer. Gold's prominence grew rapidly following its introduction as a form of money in Europe and the United Kingdom, with artifacts from the Greek and Roman empires prominently displayed in museums all over the world. In 1066, the United Kingdom was likewise establishing its metal-based coinage. The value of all types of British currency, such as the pound, shillings, and pence, was based on the quantity of gold (or silver) they represented. Gold eventually became a symbol of riches across Europe, Asia, Africa, and the Americas.

### 1.2.6 Bimetallic standard of the US

In 1792, the United States government maintained its gold legacy by creating a bimetallic standard. Every monetary unit in the United States had to be supported by either gold or silver, according to the bimetallic standard. For example, one dollar was worth 24.75 grains of gold at the time. This meant that the coins used as money were just representations of the central bank’s gold (or silver) holdings. This gold standard, however, did not continue indefinitely. Several important events in the early 1900s contributed to gold’s demise from the monetary system. The issue of promissory notes began in 1913, following the formation of the Federal Reserve (the present-day mirror copy of paper money). On-demand, these promissory notes could be easily redeemed in gold. The United States government received the right to all gold coins in circulation as a result of the Gold Reserve Act of 1934, allowing it to stop minting new gold coins. In a nutshell, this legislation established the notion that gold or gold coins were no longer required to serve as money. As a result, the United States abandoned the gold standard in 1971. It was at this time that its currency lost its gold backing.

### 1.2.7 Modern economy and gold

Although gold is no longer used to underpin the US dollar (or any other global currency), it still has value in today’s culture. It is crucial to the global economy at all times. By focusing just on the balance sheets of central banks and other financial institutions, such as the International Monetary Fund, we may readily verify this argument. Approximately one-fifth of the world's supply of above-ground gold is held by these organizations as of today. Moreover, some central banks have prudently increased their gold reserves. This underscores their fears about the global economy's long-term viability.

### 1.2.8 Gold helps to preserve wealth

The primary reason for gold's relevance in the modern economy is that it has demonstrated exceptional performance in the successful preservation of wealth over a lengthy period of time spanning many generations. Paper-denominated currencies, on the other hand, cannot make the same claim. Let's look at an example to assist us to comprehend what we're talking about: In the early 1970s, one ounce of gold was worth $35. Assume you had the option of keeping either an ounce of gold or merely the $35 at the moment. Both gold and the dollar, in fact, would purchase you the same items, such as a brand new tailor-made business suit or a sporty bicycle. In contrast, if you had an ounce of gold today and converted it to today's pricing, you'd be able to buy a whole new suit with it, but the same cannot be said for the $35. In reality, if you had elected to keep the $35 instead of the one ounce of gold, you would have lost a significant sum of money. This is mostly due to the fact that the value of gold increased while the value of the dollar decreased due to inflation.
