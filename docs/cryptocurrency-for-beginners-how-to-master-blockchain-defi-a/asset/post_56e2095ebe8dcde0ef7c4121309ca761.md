### 3.5.1 SmartCash

SmartCash is a zero-cash-based cryptocurrency that is community-driven. With the vision of attaining merchant adoption, it prioritizes privacy and quick transaction times. The project's defining feature is its aversion to a central authority, which has led to the project's foregoing of a core team. The currency is equivalent to Dash in that it aims to gain widespread adoption through a fast, private currency. Because of its use of master nodes and dependence on the Dash Foundation, Dash is perceived as a somewhat centralized currency. Foundations, core teams, and centralized mining are all concepts that SmartCash hopes to avoid. SmartCash, on the other hand, incorporates a number of decentralized governance as well as development features. It makes significant investments in community-based development. As a consequence, SmartCash has grown in popularity. SmartCash, while not as well-known as Dash as well as other private payment platforms like Zcash and Monero, offers a unique perspective on anonymous, untraceable, and usable cryptocurrency. Traditional cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin, incentivize only the miners while ignoring the other stakeholders who contribute to the project's upkeep, development, and promotion. SmartCash is a community-driven cryptocurrency that prioritizes development and community. A total of 29% of the block reward has been set aside to support SmartHive community proposals as well as the Hive Teams. SmartMining is a new feature in SmartCash that inhibits mining attacks. Anyone with a computer and one or more graphics cards can participate in mining. ASICs for the Keccak mining algorithm has yet to be developed, and it's safe to assume that they won't be for a long time.

### 3.5.2 SmartHive & SmartVoting

![](img/9e732599f379d4f1dd55a1fd64d01531_164_0.png)

The SmartCash community says that a cryptocurrency with a "core" team is inefficient and corrupt in the long run. The fear is that the founders will place a higher priority on making money than creating an open and low-cost platform. With the rise of ICOs and increasing token allocations for founding teams, we've seen this play out. These central organizations can become bureaucratic, hard to react to, and prioritize conflicting goals. Conversely, SmartCash recommends a governance model based on the self-organization of ants and bees. The goal is to create a hive where people can work on specific projects in small groups. There's a case to be made that this disjointed approach will end up being less efficient than the Foundation/core team approach it seeks to replace. Self-governing teams, on the other hand, are thought to be more agile and productive than guided development, according to the community. Voting rights will be granted to all SMART token holders. One token equals one vote. Anyone can submit proposals to the hive for evaluation and voting using this so-called SmartVoting system. The goal is to create a bottom-up management system in which projects are funded by the SmartHive fund. Moreover, the hive members are compensated for completing proposals. SmartHive is the project's lifeblood, and it's designed to keep the project running indefinitely. This structure has sparked grassroots support from a group of die-hard fans who are passionate about the project. Every block reward from SmartCash is used to fund future development projects. To date, the fund has amassed a value of more than $250 million.

### 3.5.3 Hive Structuring Teams

![](img/9e732599f379d4f1dd55a1fd64d01531_166_0.png)

A team will take on a proposed project after the hive votes to endorse it. These teams are straightforward at first: development, outreach, and support. New teams form as the number of improvement proposals and the community grows. A team consists of up to eight people who work together as a small group. Aside from that, you’ll need to form a new project team. This is known as Hive Structuring Teams, and it divides the contributing community into smaller subgroups, with people joining and leaving teams as needed. So every team has its own budget and a team coordinator appointed by the community. The team is self-contained once it has been formed. Aside from a community-wide SmartVoting referendum, there is no control structure in place. The Hive Structuring Teams are a group of dedicated community members who work on the project on a daily basis. Individuals will be employed by the teams. If community members are unable to commit to joining a team, there will be less formal ways for them to contribute to the SmartHive.

### 3.5.4 Flooz

![](img/9e732599f379d4f1dd55a1fd64d01531_167_0.png)

Flooz.com was a poster child for the late-nineties tech boom, which petered out by 2001. Flooz was compared to coupons or airline miles that could be accumulated through promotional campaigns or purchased directly from Flooz's central platform as a digital tool for merchants accepting payment on the still relatively young Internet. Flooz raised up to $35 million in venture capital over the course of its three years in business, betting on merchant demand for new and exciting payment mechanisms. Flooz spent no money on its advertising campaign, even enlisting the help of comedienne Whoopi Goldberg for a series of TV commercials. Despite the momentum, Flooz reportedly ended in failure to attract a large number of users and merchants, and the company's platform's criminal activities would ultimately prove too much for it. By August 2001, the company's demise had been hastened by rapidly declining investor support as well as allegations of criminal activity on its platform. According to a 2001 New York Times report, Russian criminal groups are suspected of using stolen credit cards to buy online currency and launder illicit funds, prompting a federal investigation. An approximate $300,000 was stolen, according to a Flooz source. Shortly after the fraud allegations surfaced, Flooz shut down after failing to find a buyer.

### 3.5.5 DigiCash

![](img/9e732599f379d4f1dd55a1fd64d01531_168_0.png)

DigiCash, a digital currency company founded in the 1990s, came close to becoming a global success. DigiCash, on the other hand, failed due to a combination of internal strife and a lack of ignition, which prevented it from becoming a widely used online payment system. David Chaum, the creator of the ecash system, is a pioneer in cryptographic protocols. DigiCash, which was formed in 1990, supported cyberbucks, a digital currency that provided anonymity to users and security to merchants who accepted it. Libertarians and others backed an international online currency that could transcend government control backed DigiCash, according to a 2003 report by The Guardian. DigiCash was notable for allowing a diverse range of payment sizes, particularly micropayments. For currency trading, the system used an email mailing system, and reports suggest that traders engaged in off-market transactions. According to reports from the time, a lack of cash flow, as well as friction between the company's employees and leadership, were among the reasons for DigiCash's demise.
