# ORPHANS WITHOUT GOVERNANCE

At the heart of the cyber challenge for connected security devices, we have a gov- ernance gap that leaves them orphaned from the day of installation. Customers are often unaware that their equipment requires firmware updates. Many secu- integrators install and never look back. Your IT server won't touch it unless covered by standard patch management software (aren't). The IoT land- is littered with stragglers what is ultimately a problem of distributed governance: if everyone is responsible, then no one is responsible.

Let's break this down.

Start with the customer. At work and at home, we are all customers of any num- ber electronic companies. We have many devices from many manufacturers in both places. Quick, make a list of every piece of electronics you own and its firmware is up to date. Beyond your smartphone and your laptop, you probably can't do it. Neither can I. And we it not for the nagware on my phone and computer, I would probably fail at both of those. We need to assume that the things we've purchased as they are supposed to be the day we bought them with need no for further attention. Nothing could be from the future. The threat environment is always changing, and defense strategies must change along with it.

Now, for IT departments. They don't have tools for IoT devices because they don't fit the pattern of manageable equipment. They don't follow any sort of standard model, like Windows or Linux servers. It prefers standard gear, where patch management comes as a boxed solution that can (more or less) run on its own. When they see an amalgam of network cameras from different manuf- turers, door controllers of different generations and designs, and wireless sens- connecting back through proprietary gateways, they throw their hands up. None of these devices are supported by off-the-shelf patch management systems- teams, and every manufacturer has a different set of policies and procedures for updates—if they provide them at all.
