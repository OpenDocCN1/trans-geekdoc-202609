# ANTHOMETRICS—WEB METRICS IRL

Anthropometrics—derived from the Greek word anthro (“man” or “mankind”) and the Latin word metricus (“measure”)—is the science of measuring the behavior of large groups of people in a specific context, literally “the measure of man.”

Anthropometrics have become part of the fabric of modern society. It drives our consumer economy with Nielsen ratings, focus groups, surveys, ad tracking, and in sum, by measure of gross domestic product (GDP). It is at the center of understanding modern democracies by vote of polls, opinion surveys, political contributions, and the very act of voting itself. It’s the foundation of the Internet economy, with detailed analytics on the minutia of online behavior, from unique daily visitors to click-through rates and dwell time on Web pages.

Such analytics provide an adaptable model that points to the useful insights in the physical security domain. This type of analytical approach can also turn security data into operational data that we across the entire enterprise. Visitor traffic patterns are directly relevant to building services and energy consumption, for example. Retailers can get enough data to illuminate behavior, and security can again gain value with passive surveillance data filtered and enriched through analytics.

The parallels between traffic analysis and physical site analysis are remarkable. Take traffic statistics. Unique visitors, first-time visitors, and repeat visitors—all core elements of online analytics—map from the virtual world to the physical world. Presence data make this mapping possible. The number of unique visitors tells us many things, like the number of people we need to plan for. That useful information to a security organization because greater numbers of people often signal greater risk. Being able to see new patterns sooner time to respond by reallocating resources, redirecting staff, or provides the opportunity to recognize and anticipate periodic or seasonal patterns. Knowing about these patterns means security organizations [sic: original text says security organizations] can plan for handling them differently in the future. At the very least, knowledge of these traffic patterns will have operational value for every other part of the business.

The second major analytic we can carry over to the physical world is the number of first-time visitors in the overall population. This metric tells us what percentage of the population has never been observed. For almost any type of institution open to the public, this is useful information on how your customers behave, another example of turning security data into operational data.
