Since then, we have seen a bigger and scarier real-world hack that hits much closer to home for the entire physical security industry. Ground zero, you could say.

The incident in question is the 21 October 2016, distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack that created a botnet army of 140,000 surveillance cameras deployed in businesses around the United States. Under the control of the malware known as Mirai, this army of cameras was directed to conduct a massive attack on major swaths of the Internet. The crush of traffic from these infected cameras hobbled every day, from ordinary users to big-name Internet companies like Twitter, Tumblr, and even the likes of Amazon and Netflix. Delays stretched out, websites became unreachable, and conducting business became impossible for hours on. It all took a single strain of malware and a large population of poorly protected IP cameras to bring a significant chunk of the Internet to its knees. There is no clearer example of security devices being turned into security liabilities.

Billions of IoT devices—security-related and otherwise—will be added to our environment over the coming years. Many of these will be deployed on networks shared with the security fabric of our facilities, our infrastructure, and—literally—our way of life. While they will no doubt make many positive contributions to our lives, they will also open numerous attack surfaces for cybercrime, identity theft, invasions of privacy, and ever terrorism.

These threats rising out of the nexus between the physical and digital worlds have been dubbed denial of safety attacks. They are expected to become more commonplace as facilities are outfitted with IoT devices for both security and building automation. Many, in the technology community openly wonder whether this Pandora’s box of cyber trouble can be closed. At the very least, we need standards to contain the morass of special cases and one-off device profiles that are so burdensome and vulnerable today.

The cyber risks presented by this new generation of connected devices have the potential to determine the core mission of security organizations if not properly managed. These circumstances have given rise to the point of view that all security is now cybersecurity.

## A Global, Residential, Commercial, and Even Universal Risk

More to the point, since we are already effectively operating as an IoT industry, we are already under threat. In one way or another, all of us manufacture, install, or purchase electronic systems connected to the Internet. Our physical security infrastructure is open to crippling attacks.

Our situation calls for a highly nuanced tableau of the world of video gaming. Not long before these cyber events wreaked havoc, the video game publisher Ubisoft released an ominous new titled Watch Dogs. The game depicts a dystopian world in which IoT devices manage so much of the modern urban landscape that hacking becomes a principal tool not only of cyber threats but also of real-world theft, vandalism, and dominance. The leader in this first-person hacker adventurer is “an antihero who can hack into the physical infrastructure to obtain and control information or to destroy such devices.* The heart of this disturbing vision is a paradoxical world in which the more electronic devices we deploy, the less safe we are from those who can hack. Hacking vehicles, traffic signals, bridges, security systems, doors, cell phones, and anything else with an electronic pulse; it's all in a day's work for the busy antihero.

No one is beyond reach.

## GLOBAL, RESIDENTIAL, COMMERCIAL, AND EVEN UNIVERSAL RISK

The common thread through all of these stories and their possibly dystopian conclusion is that the physical world and the cyber world are now inextricably united. The billions of miniature computers we continue to manufacture and install may be the greatest gift ever to the hacker community. They may also create the single largest or terrorist vulnerability of all time—thought that can't be from anyone's mind in a year Russian hacking is thought to have played a significant role in the most recent presidential election.

In Lights Out, Ted Koppel's excellent book on cyber attacks against our electric grid, he painstakingly traces the vulnerabilities and implications of a major hack to this vital infrastructure. In a phrase, our society is not equipped to operate without electricity, nor is our government prepared to do very much about it. the sheer logistics of responding to a disruption of more than a few days or weeks are unimaginable and undoable at our current level of preparation. Even a small electric outage like that caused by Hurricane Sandy required the importing of resources and skilled workers from all over the country to fix just a single electric system in Manhattan and New Jersey. The entire country would quickly degenerate into a preelectric society with a population that does not know how to live without it. Chaos.

With this single example—massive electric grid failure—it is easy to see how the promise of IoT will remain a promise unless this security problem can be solved. But we don't do well as a species planning for scenarios we've never seen before. We don't believe they can really happen. In all likelihood, we will simply move forward and not think about it. Such inaction will be disastrous.
