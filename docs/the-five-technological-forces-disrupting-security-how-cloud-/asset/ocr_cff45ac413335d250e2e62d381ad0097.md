Designed to benefit both the subjects and the security organization, passive surveillance perhaps a less ominous and more accurate term would be “passive sensing” provides reciprocal for the observer and the observed: forensics, and real-time information all of us safer. Much of passive electronic surveillance has involved anonymous data collection, although the equation is changing due to ubiquitous and pervasive computing. If you haven't heard those terms, they refer to the idea that computing can now place in any device, in any form, or in location.

Skynet's in your coffee maker or toothbrush.

Before pervasive computing gave cameras and related security systems the power to run good analytics, video surveillance captured images with little or identifying metadata besides a time stamp; you had to use eyeballs to figure everything else. Faces and other details might make it onto tape (if you'll forget the anachronism) but painfully identified only at a later time with untold hours of human labor. The unmasking of the London subway bombers in 2005 or the identification of the Boston Marathon bombers in 2013 required analysts reviewing the footage, frame by frame. Imagine if minutes after the attacks, police had a list of suspects. Instead, it took 3 days to identify the two Boston bombers and another 2 days of an intensive manhunt and eventually a shoot-out. Passive surveillance would have been mitigated or avoided altogether.
