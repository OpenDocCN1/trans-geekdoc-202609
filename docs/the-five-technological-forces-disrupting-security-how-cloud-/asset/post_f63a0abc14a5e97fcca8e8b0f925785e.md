Being able to track visitors may provide valuable forensics for many types of incident resolution. With presence data collected in a retail setting, for example, it would be easy to correlate losses with the presence of identities repeatedly present across multiple incidents of theft. The more data available on both, the better the correlation calculations would be. The preventative measure of this information comes from mining it and alerting when those same identities walking through the door next time (the new millennium's version of the "bad check wall of shame" many restaurants and gas stations used to hang behind the cash register.)

Dwell time is another staple of web analytics carries over into the physical realm. In the online world, it refers to the time someone spends on a Web page or the entire site. In the physical world, it takes on the same meaning for the amount of time a visitor spends in a building or campus. Here again, security data can become operational for the rest of the organization. How long are people visiting your building, airport, or store? Why are some people here for over 12 hours? Why do they show up and leave after 10 minutes? Today, we don't have the information to even ask these questions, let alone answer and understand their implications for risk and security.

Path analysis—the route a user takes while clicking through a website—is of paramount interest to web designers and marketing teams. They want to provide a satisfying user experience while deriving the greatest benefit for their company. Here again, we find a close to the physical world of presence data where the path through a physical space is a direct mapping of the path through a virtual space like a website.

Security organizations don't have a great handle on the people that take through our spaces, except when they are highly constrained. In commercial properties and public spaces, however, we have an opportunity to increase both security and operational knowledge.

# A JEWELRY CASE IN POINT

One of the first times I presented the concepts of presence and web analytics was to a group of chief security officers (CSOs) and security directors from Fortune 500 companies. Needless to say, this group is well acquainted with risk and criminality. They are always on the lookout for new technologies that will reduce the incidence of security events or, failing that, to assist in the prosecution.

After my speech, I was approached by the head of security for a luxury watch company currently involved in tracking down a series of thefts at retail stores that carry their brand. The basic thefts MO was that the group of thieves would enter a store, smash and grab the watches, and then leave. That part of what gave the crime a unique signature; they didn’t steal any other jewelry—just watches. That made it my new friend’s problem.

Working with law enforcement, their hypothesis was that it was the same group of thieves, traveling from city to city and carrying out the same basic pattern anywhere this watch was sold. The director of security wanted to know whether there was a way to ID the perpetrators as being the same people each time.

Seeing the power of presence and knowing that many thieves aren’t thinning far enough to turn off their cells before pulling off a robbery, they wanted to know whether this data could be collected across multiple locations and compared to see if the same known unknowns showed up at each one.

Why!

Last I spoke with them, they were figuring out how to use this technology across multiple facilities—a great example of what is possible in electronic security. While it might sound like another expensive layer of security, it would become a bonus for the next generation of access control and building management systems. These systems will use Bluetooth technology mobile credentials rather than the outdated RFID proximity cards or even short-range smart card protocols.

# BREATHING THE DATA EXHAUST

Presence detection will become a free data-product of security and sensor networks that transition to Bluetooth. We won’t even have to ask for it. It will be a built-in part of the systems we install for other purposes—a secondary effect that may rival and outweigh the original intent. Deep analytics will run on multiple clouds as machine learning algorithms to tell the difference between normal and abnormal and then tell us about it. Graphic representations of results will appear on next-generation dashboards or be read to aloud as human-machine interfaces continue their evolution from vision and gesture to the spoken word.

This free data by-product is known as “data exhaust”—an information stream inadvertently produced in the process of doing something else. The concept of data exhaust originated as an observation on the electronic ephemera as a side effect of our online activities: cookies, logs, timestamps, temporary files, download fragments, and all the other digital froth we leave in our wake. The concept has since been generalized to include any data by-products we leave behind when moving through the world. Estimates are that connected cars, for example, will send 25 Gb of data per hour up to the cloud. Aircraft will generate half a terabyte of data with each flight. Closer to home, global smart building production is already measured in zettabytes (21 zeros).

The exhaust from a Bluetooth reader on an entryway is one example of security devices produce a data stream that can be mined for secondary purposes beyond the original intent of authorizing access to a door. Lucky for us, these data streams are produced for free time a smartphone comes within range of a Bluetooth reader (or any other Bluetooth reader, for the matter).

These secondary uses of exhaust from smartphones will more than double the value of the Bluetooth readers that support mobile credential exchange. Consider the economics. A Bluetooth reader is no more expensive than a conventional proximity reader, and they are on their way to being an order of magnitude less expensive. Bluetooth chipsets are being added to other electronics like door controllers for about five dollars, displacing stand-alone readers entirely. That makes them almost free.

People counting is a primary example of mining data for secondary value. Nearly every retail business is interested in the number of people who come through their door. In the past, this was handled with simple technology light beam sensors or expensive video analytics. Both have limited accuracy, and both require additional components, installation, and software to collect and analyze the data. The cost of using a Bluetooth reader at each entry point, on the other hand, is already accounted for in the access control system and produce unique visitor data for free (at roughly the same degree of accuracy).

Another example of mining data exhaust is extracting value from the metadata produced by simple video motion detection. Every modern surveillance camera produces this information, but the data stream is so massive, not to mention ambiguous, that it is usually ignored. Instead, it is paid up by billions of rows per minute in databases across the world. But this low-grade information has a great deal of value in the aggregate for producing heat maps of building utilization and anomalous pattern detection. Machine learning can figure out what "normal" is and departures from normal almost immediately. This entire process is an example of video content analysis (VCA), and it's about to become orders of magnitude more useful as machine learning accomplishes what humans have until now been plodingly coding from first principles.

As the saying goes, one man's trash is another man's treasure.
