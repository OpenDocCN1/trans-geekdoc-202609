## Social Spaces

There is an emerging group of physical objects known as the Social Internet of Things (SIoT). This new class represents the convergence of IoT and social networks. In particular, SIoT refers to physical objects that form social relationships with other physical objects without the intervention of people.

I will say again—without the intervention of people.

Social objects also connect to the familiar social networks, which means that we can interact with them in the same way we interact with people on those same networks.

Standing at the intersection of the physical and the social, SIoT devices are squarely in the domain of physical security. Whether or not we choose to use them for our electronic security systems, they will be a part of the environments for which we are responsible. There is really no ignoring them any more than we can choose to ignore other technology that might literally walk into our buildings.

At the simplest level, social things will be able to mimic social interactions that look like human activity through texting, group updates, checking in, posting photos, e-mails, and anything else humans can do. To get a glimpse of how this future is taking shape, you need no more than IFTTT, the web service that provides a nexus between hundreds of other web services and social networks through a simple visual programming mechanism they call applets. An app might link a connected thermometer with Facebook, for example, that every time it's above 90°F it says "hot enough for ya?” or it might do something more useful, like opening the garage when your car enters the driveway.

Social things will gradually exhibit more and more of the characteristics we currently label as human or intelligent. This phrasing only serves to beg the question of what is uniquely human and what can be simulated or emulated by machines. The exploration of this possibility—that machines can be social and intelligent—has a long and illustrious history as the central subject of many works of science fiction literature and cinema (think HAL 9000 and Skynet).

One of the most recent entries in this genre, the British-American TV series, Humans, which premiered in the United States in 2015 on AMC, illustrates the profound quandaries that arise from interactions with social machines. But it also illustrates the incredible utility of machines that are capable of thought and respect (most) human conventions.

In fiction, social machines look a lot like human beings, but they don't have to. It just makes them creepier. They can just well be disembodied bodies with a limited range of social interactions skills. Or they can be much larger things, like buildings.

What if the physical spaces we manage connected through social devices and became social themselves? What if our publics knew who we were and why we were there? What if public places could talk about their security concerns? Would would “social spaces,” to coin a phrase, change security around public places?

Even though the security industry is usually late to the party for technology adoption, it seems inevitable that social applications will eventually find their way into the industry. When they do, they have the potential to introduce some of the most sweeping changes we've seen since the introduction of IP technology.

Several of the likely outcomes are the following:

- Enabling a participatory security process
- Blending the security experience into business processes
- Improved situational awareness
- More intuitive and less obstructive interactions

## WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO BE A SOCIAL SPACE?

Three converging sets of innovations have made social spaces possible: the small, powerful embedded devices capable of machine learning, the growing web of connections between physical spaces and the cloud, and the acceptance of social identity as a legitimate form of identity credential.

Amid all discussion about social networks, social media, and social applications, you rarely find a definition of what it means to be social. We know that people are social. Animals certainly appear to be social. But can a building or a physical space be social?

## What Does It Mean to be a Social Space?

When it comes right down to it, being social is mostly about how something interacts with the rest of the world. In the context of this book, there are four key behaviors that define social spaces.

First, they know who you are. Social spaces will be able to identify their inhabitants, visitors, guests, caretakers, administrators, and all the other people who interact with them. This knowledge is a fundamental building block because social relations are built on the concept of a stable personal identity over time. We find that social spaces will need a way to identify people uniquely in a way that doesn't change randomly, preferably by referring to an external identity provider. Social spaces will also need to understand the attributes and roles that are meaningful in the context of their interactions with us. The way we're greeted, where we're allowed to go, and whatever assistance we might be given, for example, will all depend on who we are and why we're there.

Second, they can talk to you. Being social is all about communicating. No communication, no social relations. Social spaces will communicate with us through social applications, just like our friends and colleagues today. The more natural this communication is, the better. Instead of having to learn a new application for every place we go, it is beside possible to communicate with a social space as if it were a person, using text or voice exchanges. You could ask a social building to let you in, lock the door, or perhaps allow a guest to visit on a particular date, all without having to learn a new application or switch out of the applications you're already using.

Third, they can be part of a trust relationship. Social relations are all based on trust and is based on familiarity. That's why your dog barks at strangers and not at yours. A social building wouldn't bark at your friends, either. For social spaces, trust would also be built up over time, the same as it is between people and animals. Trust frameworks to support this type of learned trust are a hot topic in identity research. Automating them is a thorny problem. We will need automated frameworks for public spaces as the number of security concerns continues to grow. It is not possible to manage all of them through explicit rules. At some point, things, buildings, and spaces will need the ability to operate more like we do, and learning trust is a big part of it.

Fourth, they can take action on who they interact with and what their past choices have been. Ultimately, the whole point of creating social spaces is empowering them on the ability to know, talk, and build trust. For example, a social space could remember its past interactions with me and perhaps allow someone else to enter, whereas a complete stranger couldn't.

## IT'S ALL ABOUT IDENTITY

For social spaces to be a reality, they need to be able to identify people and need to share an identity of their own in return. Today, we don't really have any agreed upon to assert identity with any uniqueness. The fact is, there are too many ways, they're all bound up in the small world of whichever organization issued us a token. When we go to work, we use an access card from our employer. When we go to the gym, we use a card from the health club. When we use an automated garage, we have another identity on the fob. This means we're always identifying ourselves as someone different when we go to different places. None of these identities has anything to do with the others. We no wonder our buildings don't know who we are.

There have been attempts to find an identity provider everyone can agree on, but so far none of them has stuck. The security industry, for example, has many ways of identifying people, but they suffer from a lack of uniqueness and scope. People outside the industry are horrified to hear that proximity cards aren't necessarily unique. Smart cards, but the market has seen to shift to the new technology.

The federal government has made several slabs at this as well, but the closest thing to a national identity initiative is the NSTIC program run by NIST. As a government initiative, however, it faces many adoption hurdles, especially since the NSA spying fiasco.

Against this backdrop, a social space still needs to have its own unique identity to interact with social applications on a social network. It's likely they will go in the same direction as the rest of us: using the available commercial identity providers that have become de facto way by which we identify ourselves online.

## COMMUNICATION AND CROWDSOURCE

One of the main benefits of having social spaces with social identities on the same networks as the user community is that everyone can communicate with each other using identities and applications already part of their daily lives. These communications could be one-on-one, for example, such as a request or command to a social space. They could also be group messages, as we commonly experience in social media, where a community of interest is all privy to the same stream of information about a particular space or building. Or they might be messages about special events, like the arrival of a guest or a change of venue.
