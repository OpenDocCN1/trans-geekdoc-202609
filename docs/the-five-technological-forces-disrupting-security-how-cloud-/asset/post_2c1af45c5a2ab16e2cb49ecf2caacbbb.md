When it proved to be the best commerce platform mankind ever invented, many of the original users lamented its devolution into commercialism. That same commercialization and the ad revenue behind it, however, made cost-free social networks accessible to all.

Sometimes it goes the other direction. A technology for identifying coeds at Harvard evolves to support many missions around the world. It even excites them better than existing approaches, despite that these new use cases were never part of what it was built to. As Mark Zuckerberg puts it, sometimes, “we don’t realize how useful things are going to be.”^4

We know that social media networks have created entirely new types of communications modalities, but they have also proved better at many older types of communication than anything preceded them. It is the intersection at which we now see companies like Facebook providing personal check-in features that easily outdo the clunky forbears invented in security software. Given that the social networks control how these new systems work, they are in a position to find exactly what the best security features are, who and how they can be improved. They are able to do research at an unprecedented scale, receiving real-time input from millions of users, all at once. But this is about more than being in the driver’s seat for product management. It’s about who controls the information and how it can be used.

New technologies have always changed the power dynamic among existing providers. That’s the nature of the disruption. This will be jarring for the industry, which tends to operate on a fairly insular basis, but it will not be to do so forever. Just as the new breed of IoT devices is creating connected devices and networks displacing categories of security equipment and services, so will social networks change the dynamics of mass notification, monitoring, and emergency response.

At a much higher level, social networks now offer resilient, multipath routing by instantly sharing information with all the subscribers of a particular feed. Contrast with the callous process that has been standard operating procedure for most emergency escalation scenarios. A call center operator dials one person after another over a voice line until they find someone who is available to take responsibility. If we were holding a race around the world on this old callous process and social networks, the news would be half-way around the world on social media before the call center operator had even dialed the third digit.

## NOT YOUR KID'S SOCIAL MEDIA

The use of social networks Facebook has made security about people, rather the building they happen to occupy. We've seen the same insight in innovation from traditional providers like ADT; Canopy service has reoriented security around personal mobile devices. All these innovations share the same obvious-in-hindsight principle: security is about people, not places.

The difference is that a billion people already use Facebook, which dwarfs the subscriber base of every monitoring company. This is not to suggest that the services are comparable. They do, however, have an overlap, and such overlaps are often the first sign of disruption. If we project forward and imagine a world where Facebook becomes the preferred medium for sharing security information, what does that do to the rest of the industry?

Given the compelling case for harnessing this power, why haven't we seen more use of social networks already within our industry? Social networks have become an essential part of many other spheres of human activity, but not security. As an industry, we do not regard social networks as a serious tool for anything but marketing. Many cite concerns about privacy, public exposure, and now fake news as reasons they cannot be trusted for the weighty business of security management. The fact is that social networks are where people increasingly live their digital lives. If we want to communicate with the masses—a fundamental principle of effective security strategy—then we need to reach where they live: in their own neighborhoods.

Slack is a collaboration tool that has many characteristics of a more or less closed social network for teams. It is best known for promoting collaboration among software developers, and anyone working on projects together. It has quickly displaced many other internal corporate communications tools like email and instant messaging because it provides both community and selectivity over topics you want to be involved in. With teams who use Slack, it has become a 24/7 window onto their world.

When Slack began using my company's cloud-based access control application, they immediately connected to our web API. They wanted to interact with their new security system without ever leaving their primary work environment, aka their own digital neighborhood. By interconnecting the two systems, they were able to bring the functionality they wanted into their preferred user experience without having to switch to a different user interface. Now, they can open doors for visitors right from the Slack application without having to get up from their desks.

You can take the kid out of the neighborhood, but you can't take the neighborhood of the kid.
