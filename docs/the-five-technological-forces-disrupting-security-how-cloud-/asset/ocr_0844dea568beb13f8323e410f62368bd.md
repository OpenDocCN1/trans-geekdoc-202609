# GATEWAYS TO THE FUTURE

A “gateway” is simply a device that connects one network to another. The name derives from their similarity to physical gateways, because they both act as a connector between two different domains. What makes them interesting and useful is that they can connect networks that use different rules or speak different languages.

Imagine connecting two road networks. If the rules of the road are the same on both sides, it's trivial to connect them. Just lay down some pavement, and let people drive from one to another. But what if the rules are different on the left and another where they drive on the right. If we just pave the space between them, cars moving from one to the other will have head-collisions. So, we need a transition zone (or gateway) where the left and right lanes are switched over and to provide drivers with new rules (or protocols) about how to navigate the other side.

But that's not all they do.

If the data expressed on the two networks are different, the gateway will modify it while passing it from one to the other. In this context, gateways can be thought of as translators that connect groups of people who speak different languages. Looking at the other way around, translators are gateways who switch between groups who would not otherwise be able to communicate with each other. Of course, a translator doesn't just listen to the words used and repeat them to the other. That would be pointless. A good translator substitutes new words, syntax, and rules of grammar to accurately expressing the underlying message.

Technically, this capability provides a method for connecting devices across similar networks, that is, networks without the same underlying protocols. For example, something that does not use IP protocols can be connected to something that does, like cloud services.
