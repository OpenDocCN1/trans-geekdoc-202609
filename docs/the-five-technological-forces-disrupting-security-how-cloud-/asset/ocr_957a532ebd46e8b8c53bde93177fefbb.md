# ADOPTION SCORECARD

I used to joke that everyone in the industry must be from Missouri, because it's
like living in one giant show-me state. Early adopters are hard to come by, no
wants to go first. This makes the problem of crossing the chasm, to revisit
geoffrey Moore's metaphor, especially acute.

One of Moore's basic tenets is that in the early stages of technology adoption,
people don't follow the examples of leaders outside their own vertical indus-
tries. People in health care want-health examples, people in retail want
retail examples, and people in security want security examples. That's why it
d'nt particularmente matter to most security practitioners that all of Silicon Valley\had gone over Web technology by the late 1990s. They needed to hear it
from someone else. Someone they already knew.

The stereotype of the early adopter is that they work in a small, maverick com-\many breaking tradition across many dimensions, including the way they
acquire and use technology. Startups today don't even consider the old enter-
prise application approach for their computing needs. They go to the
cloud, straight to open source, straight to social, and straight to a subscription
revenue models.

Sometimes, though, it's the industry giants that come out of the gates. ADT
is a case in point. They adopted my company cloud technology by way in
2003, less than 2 years after we brought it to market. Jay Haunn, VP of
product management, was responsible for moving the biggest name in the secu-
ity into the cloud. He still tells the story of how the former CEO of
ADT's parent, Tycho International, tapped him on the shoulder day one and\asked him to check out this startup with a cloud subscription model for access
control. As a dyed-in-the-wool recurring revenue company, it fit with ADT's
worldview to incorporate this new model as another line of business. Early
adopters sometimes up where you expect them.

Anecdototes make for memorable stories, but what we're really interested in
how the industry as a whole is being transformed by each of the five forces
trace through this book. How much of an impact has each of them really
made? How much more potential do they have to improve or disrupt?
