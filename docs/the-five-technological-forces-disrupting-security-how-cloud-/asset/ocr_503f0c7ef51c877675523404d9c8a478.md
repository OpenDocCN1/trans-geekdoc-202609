Now, what if we look at the second half of the guard equation? Physical presence. Guards in many cases provide physical enforcement, interdiction, restraint, and physical custody during emergencies and crimes, among many other life and safety services. They also carry guns, Tasers, and batons that do a lot of the work of convincing someone to do what they want to do. Robots are now capable of carrying and using all of these deterrents and provide the mobile eyes and ears we need for ongoing situational intelligence—I mean, data. No, it's not legal to send a gun-tuning robot out into the night, at least not in any civilian setting, it's not hard to make that many duties could be transitioned to airborne robots and drones, their airborne counterparts. Such tasks are transitioning from people to machines in industry for industry for jobs that are far more complex than the majority of the hours logged by guards. There is no reason to think the superior economics of automation won't eventually push into this job description in the same way that it has many others.

What will make this transition possible? More speed.

Speed and data are intertwined. They need each other. A fast processor without a lot of data is the computing equivalent of all dressed up with no place to go. Data without fast processing is just excess baggage. The two are now coming together in physical security systems, at both the edge and the center of the computing model. By “edge,” we mean the processing that happens locally, inside a sensor, a camera, a smart lock, or any other computing device. The term derives from viewing networks as having a central hub of some sort (computer, cloud, and switch) plus many connected things at the “edge.” The center was classically a large computing device like a mainframe or big server that has now become virtualized along with everything in the cloud. The edge has become faster and smarter and able to do more on its own, it can also produce more data than ever before and send it back to the center. increase in the data velocity at the cloud-computing center on which big data is converging.
