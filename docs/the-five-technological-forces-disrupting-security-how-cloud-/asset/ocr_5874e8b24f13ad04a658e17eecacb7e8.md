A look at public safety, however, reveals that they are emerging as a conduit for messaging and dissemination of emergency preparedness information. Though understood as a tool in commercial security, these early public applications have made them a formalized part of emergency response procedures at local, state, federal, and international levels.

It's easy to pigeonhole social networks with so much Facebook frippery, a public airing of the trivial and banal. At a deeper level, however, social networks are persistent digital identity—one that spans many online institutions and relationships. Social was not yet a “thing” when it became part of how we viewed identity. Now, it is an inescapable capable of daily life for anyone who goes online. Many of us use and create digital identities, unaware of the implications. Your digital identity is a prime example. Once you start identifying yourself with this identity, it becomes a thread through all your online interactions. It is your ID on third-party websites whenever you choose the convenience of signing on using Facebook, Google, Twitter, or LinkedIn.

Social networks want to be your identity provider, the official instrument to use your identity—both online and offline.

The use of social sign-on, previously confined to accessing virtual spaces as websites, may become a tool for accessing real physical spaces. *Network World has already reported on Facebook’s “future plans” to issue your identity cards as well.* ²2

In this way, your social identity can serve as a unique identifier that replaces the redundant identities issued to you for every physical space you interact with. Our use of a single identity for diverse physical authentication requirements—such as unlocking your home, letting into secure areas of a facility, or starting your car—could mirror the way we log onto websites or use a single payment app for financial transaction.

These identities and the social applications that billions of people already could quickly sow a valuable communication channel for physical security interactions. These broad social interactions could in turn support a type of crowdsourcing that could harness the power of the people for participatory security. There is no reason why physical identities and IoT devices can’t get in the act with their own social identities.

Together, these developments could form the basis of what I call “social spaces.”
