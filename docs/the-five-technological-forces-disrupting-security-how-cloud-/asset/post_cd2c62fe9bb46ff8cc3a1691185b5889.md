After, all had invested tens of millions of dollars over dozens of years perfecting a feature set for an on-premise client-server solution. Surely, that couldn't be obsolete now, could it?

Few executives understood the not-so-subtle distinction between operating a multitude of virtual machine instances—each a complete environment itself for a single customer—and a true multitenant software architecture that supported all customers concurrently on a single platform.

They tried anyway.

These early attempts to simulate the outward appearance of cloud computing with scads of individual virtual machines ignored the real work that goes behind scenes. As such, they came to be known as “stack-a-box” systems because their economics and operational requirements were no different than stacking piles of servers, or “boxes.” They achieved no economies of scale at the hardware level, no economies of scale at the IT administration layer, and no economies of scale at the development and deployment layer. While the customer no longer had a solution residing in their own data center, they still paid the same price (or more).

This imitation cloud model broke down for several reasons. First, software providers failed to hide the underlying complexity from both channel partners and end users. The rapid provisioning and elasticity buyers wanted simply couldn't be delivered on the back of a thousand independent servers. The cost advantage of the cloud couldn't be realized. Either customers had to pay more or service providers had to settle for low margins, neither one sustainable. It tried and failed to circumvent the fundamental economics of the cloud: it takes economies of scale to drive unit costs down to the point where efficiency gains off.

Finally, the leverage cloud services for mobile and API services couldn't be replicated. The stack-a-box approach required a multiplicity of endpoints and network management to supply these services, creating a crushing burden too great to bear.

## ARE WE MORE CONSERVATIVE THAN OUR CUSTOMERS?

The integrator channel itself presented the third major hurdle. While several leading national integrators started using cloud solutions as early as 2003, others regarded it as a sideshow to the main business of security. The majority believed their customers would not endorse or pay for an off-premise, recurring revenue version of systems had long purchased and installed in their own buildings for a onetime fee.

Contrary to expectations, it turned out their customers already used the cloud for many other business applications. By the mid-2000s, major cloud companies like Salesforce.com had won over many customers who liked the simplicity and pricing model of the cloud. Thanks to good track records, customers had come to grips with the fact that—maybe, just—cloud companies were better at managing and protecting data than they were themselves. By 2010, analysts agreed that the vast majority of organizations expected to grow their use of SaaS. However, many within the industry remained convinced that these customers would not buy cloud solutions. Adoption rates for as-a-service offerings hovered in the low single digits.

Many observers have pointed out a generational effect in this lagging adoption dynamic. Operating with the assumption that the cloud would be unacceptable to their customers, many industry grayboards would not present the option, validating the self-fulfilling prophecy that no one will buy it. Meanwhile, a new success pattern emerged at integrators who recruited new sales staff out of college. The youngest members of their sales forces—millennials who had grown up with the cloud—had no trouble accepting the cloud value proposition and presenting it convincingly to customers. Ironically, this resulted in sales offices with an old cloud naysayer in one corner and a younger colleague counting cloud commission in the other.

## A FOOTHOLD FOR CLOUD

During all this, a handful of manufacturers and integrators kept moving the ball forward—not at the rate of the software industry as a whole, but forward nonetheless. Many early users viewed the cloud as a niche to pull out of the tool kit when standard solutions failed. From small or remote sites, for example, with limited access control and videos were some of the first equipped with cloud-based security systems. In many instances, locations could not support the expense and complexity of a full-site server. Cloud solutions came in a lower price point and provided built-in connectivity to corporate headquarters, which kept satisfied. Unfortunately, this by deploying cloud kept it pigeonholed as a small system solution during its early years: it's nice for a little job, but not really up to the task of securing a large enterprise.

From this, however, cloud solutions began their climb up the enterprise ladder by overtaking larger numbers of small locations. This way, each location appeared to live in the small system comfort zone. In the aggregate, cloud solutions grew to handle enterprise systems as large as any legacy platform. Retail branch locations were among the earliest to join. National retailers, banks, pharmacies, and other chain operations have massive of individual locations, often well into the thousand-plus range. Each location, however, has only a few doors or entrances that need to be secured. Local technicians working at any one of these locations, each installation looks exactly like other. None of them needs to take into account how the overall system fits together at the corporate level. The cloud takes care of that. For integrators, technical installation complexity flat regardless of system size. For customers, this meant that they could leverage the strengths of the cloud rather than paying for complex network engineering and system sizing exercises (with the expensive software to go along them).

The visibility gained by these supposedly niche applications provided a path for forward for cloud use. It wasn't huge for a security director presiding over hundreds of individual locations to think that the same system could also handle their corporate headquarters. Now it was a big leap for integrators to go from systems with 5 or 10 entry points.

## THE BARRIERS ARE DOWN

Many of the barriers present in the first decade have now come down. Concerns about data retention and audit compliance have become a routine checklist item. The cable and telephone companies have blanketed nearly every business in the United States with high-quality connectivity. Most significantly, many customers now have an expectation that all of their applications will be available as cloud services.

Does anyone really think that the security system will be the last server inside their data centers?

## EASIER FOR NEW ENTRANTS

With all these changes in the milieu surrounding cloud adoption, the advances in technology have made it easier for someone with a business plan and some funding to launch a new offering. In the year 2000, a budding SaaS company still had to purchase its own servers, database and operating system licenses, configure and install all hardware, and drag it off to a dedicated data center or collocation facility. The sheer number of staff hours this process took—not to mention significant capital or extremely good credit—dwarfs the investment in time and effort required by today's.

Today, literally anyone with a credit card can now launch a SaaS business by accounting in an AWS or the other many SaaS players in the market. Buying and provisioning an IaaS environment where a start-up can launch its applications—while presenting a technically ratified set of purchases—no more difficult than any other ecommerce activity at the level of pointing and clicking on items to put in one's shopping cart checkout.

Of course, the challenge of writing application that meets users need and expectations remains a challenge, as having a ready IaaS environment has nothing to change the need for market research and product strategy. That said, the tools for creating web applications have evolved since the first decade of SaaS application came onto the scene. Dozens of languages, frameworks, collaborating tools, evolved HTML, and API standards have all contributed to a high-productivity workplace for software developers in physical security (and every other vertical market).

Regardless of all these developments, the biggest barrier for entrants to our industry remains: we are irreducibly, inescapably physical. Creating or adapting electronic hardware to the purposes of physical security. The big strides in simplifying and enabling cloud computing development have done little to make hardware development easier. It is slower, more difficult, more specialized, and much riskier due to the cost of getting the engineering wrong in hardware.
