
# The New Playing Field

The $200 billion global security manufacturing and service industry changes at a glacial pace. In many segments, the established players have been accustomed to keeping the playing field to themselves or at least sharing it with only a small coterie of familiar competitors. Barriers to entry were higher than in many other industries, and new entrants have no choice but to play along with the existing reseller and distribution models—both well-worn silk roads negotiated with a Sherpa who knew the pitfalls.

The rise of IoT and the renaissance of hardware innovation are heating things up a bit—call it a taste of global warming that gets the glaciers tad more quickly. Companies never before on the industry radar now show up at major trade shows. Customers want to know whether their old-line security platforms integrate with these newcomers' products. Search results that once turned up, the only usual show new names at the title.

IoT is making its own waves, to be sure, but it is also magnifying the amplitude of those introduced by the cloud. In the physics of wave' mechanics, we can make this constructive interference. One waveform is superimposed on another, and if they share resonant frequencies, they amplify each other. Electronic security is one of the primary places where cloud and IoT have synergy.

IoT also resonates with the cloud in a very general sense because connected devices aren’t much good if no one is listening—a tree falling in the woods unheard—and the cloud provide an audience of applications and users. Together, they’re shining a light on previous generations of technology to make them look plain outdated.

Apologies for aging technology were in full evidence at a recent conference where I heard someone taking great pains to defend the notion that on-premise servers will always have a place in security: “the cloud will not displace them!” He compared the preference for on-premise computing to hotel guests feeling safer with mechanical latches on their rooms, even though they know that the electronic lock is secured.

Whatever psychological truths this analogy may hold, its representation of technology (i.e., mechanical latches) makes the client-server literally the exact opposite of the intended point. The smart locks on hotel doors are programmable control devices that modernized the hospitality industry many years ago. With the recent introduction of phone-based keys (aka mobile credentials), they have only become smarter, more convenient, and data-rich. The metal latch, by contrast, is as dumb as it ever was.

As the saying goes, you can't fix stupid.

# WHAT'S OLD IS NEW—AT LEAST FOR STARTUPS

Hardware companies—by which I mean connected device companies produce both a widget and the software web services to run it—are cool again. They are back in vogue and have enjoyed an investment resurgence since became a marketable reality. Many new investments are in the automation space, and many of those are related to physical security. This level of new, outside interest is different, and I am not sure the industry is used to it (yet).

The expansion of the connected hardware universe is pushing the boundaries of the security market along with it. Higher adoption rates are the availability of newer and better—and, frankly, more interesting-types of connected hardware that provide convenience and peace of mind in a more attractive package. Ease of installation drives high growth rates in the DIY sector of the residential market in particular, and it's spreading into the low end of the SMB market.

This point was explained to me sometime around 2009 and has only become more poignant since. My company had just introduced a cloud-based video surveillance service, and we were extrolling its benefits to one of our security integrator customers. I was pressing the virtue of our upfront installation cost due to the elimination of servers and setup labor. "Customers will love it," I said.

After letting me go on for a while, one of the executives said, "That's great, Steve, but we're not competing against traditional video record recorders. We're competing against Costco."

What he meant was that many buyers were willing to purchase cheap DIY systems off the shelf and install them on their. In the nearly eight years since then, the trip to Costco has been replaced with a trip to the Internet, and the tangle of wires and cheap cameras has been replaced with sleek new products like Dropcam.

Connected hardware innovation and cloud computing are producing this kind of constructive interference in many areas, most conscientiously in residential. The new companies feel nothing like the companies of old.

I won't forget the cheesy advertisements from a 2014 security start-up that lampooned so-called cavalry providers with their blue uniforms and hexagonal signs. The start-up boasted an attractive set of consumer-friendly security modules—IoT devices—that can be self-installed by homeowners in a matter of minutes. They claimed "ridiculously easy" setup that required no tools, no staying home from work for an appointment, and no coking holes in the walls. This ease of use is the power of IoT devices, using low-power chips, low-power wireless, and long-life batteries, all wrapped in contemporary industrial design that appeals to millennial sensibilities.

All of this is a radical departure from the traditional pitch for home security. Traditional security providers aren't known for trying to appeal to hipster or DIY sensibilities. The time-tested message has been exactly the opposite: "Dear homeowner, don't worry your pretty little home. This is too hard for you to do on your own." That has been true enough for the entire history of physical security.

Up until now.

Innovation of this sort isn't confined to the residential customer. Many IoT and cloud-based services appeal to the small and medium business market, which represents approximately 80% of the business addresses in the United States, according to the Census Bureau. While large enterprises often get more than their fair share of the attention in the security press, small business is the largest demographic by number of locations. That's why it has gained the attention of investors. Many small business owners will carry the lessons they've learned from their DIY home systems, which will further fuel this part of the IoT-based security market.

The new generation of IoT-based products is an obvious break with the past, and that's only scratching the surface of what makes them so different. What's most remarkable is the interaction capability they provide with other cloud services and families of IoT devices. We scan the websites of new entrants, and it's clear that they interact with a long list of web services and devices (nowhere in evidence against traditional providers).

The service that stands out in this regard is IFTTT, a cloud-based service whose name stands for if this then, a to the most fundamental conditional logic construct in all of computer programming. IFTTT has integrated with hundreds of online services and products, ranging from social networks to photo sharing to smart home, from security systems and mobile phone operating systems. Its users can form relationships among these services by creating or reusing applets with zero programming skills.

For example, I can have IFTTT automatically upload security clips to Google Drive for long-term archiving and distribution. They could simultaneously be mailed to three colleagues, tweeted, posted to Instagram, and cause my building to go into a lockdown state. The net effect of all these integrations is that one of the participating services can leverage the benefits of extensive product integrations without having to do each one directly with all the service providers. In this regard, IFTTT acts like a giant hub with value-added logic, connecting all the partners. The network effect adds more value to all because each new partner in the ecosystem adds value to all the others.

Contrast with the painstaking partner-by-partner integration prevailing among commercial security systems. It is a slow and arduous process that limits the number of options customers have for combining best-of-breed products. From an algorithmic point of view, the number of integrations N products grows in proportion to N2 when every partner must integrate directly with every other. In contrast, a hub-and-spoke topology is completely linear: N products require N integrations. In a market where security organizations might wish to choose between hundreds and thousands of products, the difference between N and N2 is a huge cost factor for all concerned.

That's the power of IoT plus cloud.

# LARGE TECH & TELECOM EYE PHYSICAL SECURITY—AGAIN

The combination of IoT and cloud—with perhaps a bit of DIY on the side—lower some of the physical challenges of the security business while keeping its attractive business model intact. As a result, many large and communication companies and start-ups and their VC investors are taking a renewed interest in products and services that address physical security problems.

The business model attracting these newcomers is called recurring revenue. It began as a staple of alarm companies and tracked with the value proposition of ongoing monitoring services. It spread to additional services like access control and video surveillance as SaaS brought the same ongoing value proposition to new areas of the security equation.
