Motion detectors have been used as dedicated security devices for many years. They progressed from bulky devices with high failure rates to small battery-operated gizmos that you can literally stick to the wall with double-sided tape. They use several different technologies and have become sophisticated enough that they can distinguish people from pets.

Now, even inexpensive IoT devices are equipped with accelerometers next to no cost. If we could aggregate the motion detection data from all devices in a home of the future, we would have a virtual motion detector network installing a single physical security device.

Is this what homes and businesses will look like?

If we take stock of the actual products that are being classified as IoT, what we encounter is a dog's breakfast of consumer and industrial devices. They range from essential products such as thermostats, lighting controls and connected cars, motion sensors, fitness trackers, smart watches, smart toothbrushes, and—my personal favorite—the PetChatz Gree & Treat Videophone.

The objects, protocols, data streams, and control operations of IoT systems in these vertical markets are vastly different: as gas cameras for capsule endoscopy are from temperature sensors for monitoring food processing compliance. They are all taking advantage of the expansion of connectivity options now available to compact, power-putting devices that consume very little energy.

As different these applications are, there is an underlying continuity across the connected devices that power them. This makes sense of our tendency to lump IoT devices together as a category: they connect to the Internet and are smarter than the first generations of things that could do. They also often run on batteries rather than being plugged into the wall, but that line is rapidly blurring.

The truth is that ubiquitous computing is springing so rapidly in many different contexts that the sheer scale of this landscape warrants we call it by its own name. To say otherwise would be saying in 1980 that personal computers were nothing new—just computers—when in fact the low price, accessibility, wealth of consumer applications, and the absolute magnitude of the PC population made one of the largest social and economic events since the Industrial Revolution.

The big difference is that device connectivity used to be a special feature—one that you had to select or perhaps guided your product choices in the first place. What's new in the IoT era is that every electronic device is a connected device. Every new television is a connected television. Every new car is a connected car. All the new coffee makers are connected coffee makers. Every security device is a connected security device. It's no longer a choice. It's part of the fabric of our products. Even the people who don't actively choose it will have it anyway. Connectivity will become embedded, inescapable part of anything that uses electricity and many that never did before.

# THE INDUSTRIAL INTERNET OF THINGS

The Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) is the name for a grab bag of nonconsumer applications of IoT technology—health care, transportation, energy, smart cities, smart highways, factory floor, supply chain, infrastructure, mining, dozens of heavy industries, and drumroll—building automation and security. It has its own associations, consortia, conferences, reference architectures, cyber manifests, consulting practices, software platforms, and its own lobbyists.

The term was introduced in 2011 and has become a rallying point for many corporate applications of IoT. Word is it's going to be huge. Thirty-nine percent of the total population of IoT devices over the next 5 years will be in an enterprise or industrial setting. It is predicted to become a multillion dollar market all on its own.

We're paying attention to it here because the use of IoT in commercial security shares many of the same concerns and goals with the IoT community. We are both more concerned with system architecture and standards. We are both more concerned with device authentication and cyber hardening. We are both concerned with availability and network recovery. This is not to suggest that consumer product companies are heedless of these issues; it's just that IIoT products operate in more complex ecosystems and face additional challenges that simply aren't present in a home environment.

The Industrial Internet Consortium (IIC) is a trade group chartered to bring together companies and technologies that can accelerate the growth of IIoT through standards, best practices, and education. They describe IIoT as a convergence that *transforms industry through intelligent, interconnected objects that dramatically improve performance, lower operating costs and increase reliability.*114 Membership in the IIC currently includes over 200 companies across the sectors of information technology, manufacturing, smart devices, automotive, information security, mining, petroleum, and, yes, even physical security.

Commercial security applications of IoT technologies fit squarely into this industrial refinement of the basic IoT premise:

- **Intelligent**: Device intelligence in electronic security has been growing steadily. Complex cryptographic and public key infrastructure (PKI) functions are now performed routinely in small, smart devices like card readers used in high-assurance applications.
- **Interconnected**: The adoption of IP by the electronic security has driven much higher connectivity. While these interfaces were initially used over LAN, they paved the way for the Internet. Later, suppliers leveraged IP connectivity with the requisite firmware for connecting to cloud services.
- **Performance**: Performance increases have enabled new behaviors in intelligent devices, most notably in the field of video analytics, where complex algorithms and high-bandwidth data streams demand a certain minimum level of intelligence before they are actually useful.
- **Operating costs**: Cloud-connected intelligent devices are a striking example of reducing operating costs. They are essential to SaaS services that manage enterprise security.

In short, yes, IoT matters a lot.

# All Security Is Now Cybersecurity

The 2015 annual ASIS security conference in Anaheim was graced by southern California's usual sunshine and the radiance of nearby Disneyland. Wandering the conference grounds, knowing that Mickey and Goofy were just down the street, I found it difficult to feel the weight of the world's security problems that day.

Inside the conference halls, however, a much darker mood prevailed. The topic that dominated nearly every conversation was the cybersecurity of electronic devices and indeed the entire realm of IoT. The publicized July 2015 hacking of a Jeep Cherokee—while driving down the road, no less—was fresh in everyone's mind. The hack was conducted under controlled circumstances and performed as something of a stunt, but its relevance could not be ignored. It was a clear case of a cyber attack causing physical damage. The occupants of the vehicle were at risk, but so was anything and anyone in its path. The ability to remotely pilot suicide vehicles was chilling. The event made it too clear that physical security will become impossible unless we figure out how to defend against cyber attacks.

Cyber attacks against connected devices can have much larger physical targets.
