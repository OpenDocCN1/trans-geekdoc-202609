## WILL IoT BE DISRUPTIVE?

IoT may prove to be disruptive in one of two ways: data analytics and the broader success of DIY products.

There is a big opportunity to harvest and add value to IoT sensor and device data streams. Manufacturing and deploying connected devices is already moving forward at a nice clip, but it has yet to see how data analytics will shape up on the back end. Large-scale analytics of sensor data streams will be slow to emerge within security software platforms by slow migration to the cloud, development of consensus analytics useful to a broad swath of the market, and pricing models that fit within security budgets. It's one thing to display pretty graphs and another to provide insights that change decision-making.

IoT will also be disruptive if it enables products that make DIY as good as professionally installed systems. I am not saying this is even possible, but that there is a continuum and many would feel the impact of a shift toward DIY IoT substitutes.

Changes to the vendor landscape will track the consumerization theme we have developed across all the major sections of this book. The nature of the disruption is that what once could been accomplished with only highly specialized products can now be accomplished inexpensive with consumer IoT platforms.

For example, it wasn't long ago that something as simple as a water sensor was a specialty device, not generally available to consumers, and usually wired into a central control panel with correspondingly high installation costs. Today, it's an inexpensive wireless device that you can place wherever you want it and begin using in less than 5 minutes. What's more is today's water sensor is probably also a temperature, vibration, and air quality sensor—all for a lower price than the dedicated, hardwired water sensor of 5 years ago.

## ADOPTION

Like cloud adoption rates, IoT adoption rates in security vary greatly between commercial and residential markets. In residential markets, IoT is pretty much a done deal. Every home security offering is now a smart home offering, which is another way of saying that it includes IoT devices. There is universal agreement among analysts and market studies that this is a growing trend for the foreseeable future. What this means is that we'll continue to see improvements in both cost and capabilities as more types of devices reach mass production and more competitors enter the market.

Commercial security systems will move more slowly, in part because they are larger, more complex, and require more time to integrate new features in a coherent way. They are also subject to different regulatory requirements, especially for anything that concerns safety of life or fire alarm systems. That said, they comprise of the so-called Industrial IoT, by which most measures are anticipated to be even larger than the residential market.

## STRATEGY

How should security organizations approach IoT? Start by watching the residential space to see what kinds of capabilities are relevant to your commercial setting. Residential systems are a predictor of what will become available in commercial systems, and the adoption of IoT is certainly one of those proof points.

A need-based approach might begin with the asking what's missing in your information set today that additional sensors could feel. What visibility would you like to have that you don't have today, and it is possible that the right IoT device could provide it?

To help with your thinking, start with the family. IoT devices often don't provide a qualitative type of data or situational awareness. Often, they just make the same types of data more affordable at more locations. Or they may make it possible to collect information at locations infeasible before, thanks to wireless connectivity, lower power consumption, and improved battery life.

## CODA: IS IoT REALLY A CATEGORY?

As a closing thought, I owe you a slightly contrarian perspective on the IoT category as a whole.

From a historical perspective, the meaning of IoT as a product category is roughly on par with the meaning of "electric appliances" at the beginning of the last century. The first electric toaster and the first commercial electric vacuum cleaner were both introduced in 1905. Then, the first was patented electric milking machine (1910), and the earliest commercialized electric washing machines (1930). At that time, there was no product coherence to these machines, and the same holds for IoT today. There are hundreds of IoT products for hundreds of uses, but they are no more alike than a toaster and a milking machine. Many of these turn-of-the-century technological marvels were retrospectively lumped together as “electric appliances” only because they had one thing in common: they were powered by electricity channeled through an electric motor.

Moreover, they were manufactured by different companies, sold in different markets, and purchased by completely different buyers. In manufacturing, we have the following: the Hoover Company for vacuum cleaners; the Ford Motor Company for industrialized conveyor systems; and the Babson Bros Co. for the highly regarded “surge milker,” one of the first in its category to overcome objections to efficiency, sanitation, and cow safety. Markets for these products were, respectively, mail order catalogs, business-to-business industrial sales, and agricultural fairs and the proved farm-to-farm traveling salesman. In short, there was no reason to think of them as a category until later, when mergers and acquisitions consolidated electric manufacturers and multi-retailers consolidated the end-user marketplace. General Electric stands out as an early aggregator in this regard, producing light bulbs, electric motors, and finished appliances—not to mention their biggest hit, “electricity as a service”—otherwise known as the power that comes out of wall sockets.
