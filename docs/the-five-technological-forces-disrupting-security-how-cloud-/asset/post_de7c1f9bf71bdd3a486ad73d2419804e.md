
# DOT-COM REDUX IN PHYSICAL SECURITY

The transformation of IT and the US economy as a whole to the cloud took place quickly and chaotically during the dot-com era that it was hard to observe. Even for those of us in the middle of it, there was more going on than anyone could follow all on. I recall the breathless coverage of The Industry Standard, a weekly that called itself the news magazine of Internet. New companies sprang up and died before they could celebrate their first birthday. The “new economy” had hard cash, and “changed all the rules,” and “eyeballs” are counted more valuable than cold. Exorbitant rates were a badge of honor. Suddenly, they weren’t, and the party was over.

I expect to see a replay of certain dynamics of that era in the physical security industry over the next 5 years—without that awful crash part at the end. It will not be quite so encompassing, but it will be every bit as transformative and beneficial for the security consumer. The “new economy” will see the steady growth of market share for cloud companies at the expense of those who lagged behind a bit too. It will emphasize customer acquisition, as the original land grab for market share in each subset of the new economy. It will emphasize innovation, new paths to the market, and online purchasing, as we saw in the first shift from bricks and mortar to bricks and clicks.

Security companies are increasingly threading cloud language through their marketing messages for fear of being behind. Meanwhile, product teams with multillion dollar investments in huge legacy code bases struggle to deliver a cloud-like experience without sacrificing their company’s cash cow. New entrants and nontraditional suppliers have moved into the cloud space at a faster pace than legacy providers, making for a territorial squabble with far more players than anyone ever expected.

Because of these dynamics, the security industry represents an echo of the first wave of the new economy. It gives us another chance to look into the petri dish and watch a new life form emerge. This time, though, we know how the experiment will turn out and where to place our bets.

# TECHNOLOGY ADOPTION IN PHYSICAL SECURITY

The technology adoption life cycle is a well-known sociological framework for describing the acceptance of a new technology or innovation across one or more markets. It was originally developed in Everett Rogers’ landmark Diffusion of Innovations³ and forms the theoretical backdrop for Geoffrey Moore’s Crossing the Chasm.⁴

It has become so much a part of how we look at the evolution of technology that many will recognize the language—early adopter, early majority, late majority, and laggard—without even knowing where these terms originated.

As a rule, the physical security industry falls somewhere around the “late majority” in this framework. As an illustration, I cite the state of industry in the early 2000s, just as I was getting to know this market. There was a debate raging whether Internet technology could be safe and reliable enough for security applications. People actually took up the “con” side of this argument—in print—and predicted that fat serial cables would be routed forever because Internet Protocol (IP) was not trustworthy.³ Fifteen years later, with the whole world using the Internet for everything, we can safely say that the industry got the memo and shifted to technology that is now merely 30 years old.

How has the industry afforded the luxury of being a late adopter of these-changing innovations? First, the market dynamics are somewhat sheltered by the way that end users consume these products. Small and medium businesses—the largest single market segment—purchase infrequently, know little about the brands and features, and therefore ever exert pressure on the channel to change the ways things have always been done. Myriad requirements across thousands of overlapping jurisdictions make it challenging to introduce new products that are substantially outside the envelope of what went before. And, finally, there is a natural conservatism that justifiably shies away from the perils of the bleeding edge.

A recurring theme we will see in this framework is while the physical industry is usually a late adopter of new technology, it always catches up in the end.

How will the sum of these forces affect the future of the physical industry over the course of the next 5 years? Many of these forces amplify one another, and the sum of the parts makes the current transformations compelling. We will see the biggest shift toward cloud-based services that incorporate all four other forces. In particular, this will shift the market away from those unable to make the leap and in favor of those more agile. The sum of these technologies could also be described as not only exhibiting a consumerization of commercial security.
