This makes it difficult for suppliers to come up with product offerings that satisfy a wide enough swath of their customer base to make them worthwhile. A useful model for thinking about big analytics is the model we currently use for video analytics, where there are specific analytic algorithms in use for each types of physical security problem: license plate recognition, facial recognition, flow analysis, and abandoned object recognition, name a few, are all designed to answer very specific questions. Big data analytics develop the same.

A great illustration of divergent data goals is the requests I see from customers about how and when they would like to use information. Some of them are glued to screens every minute of the day and place a high value on real-time data feeds. Others only log in once week or less to create reports about activity, which they file away for compliance or HR purposes. Others are more interested in using security data as an employee management tool. My favorite example of weird information science is the guy who monitors the smokers' door to their defined smoking area in a courtyard. True story, others have highly unique needs for downstream uses of data, and they choose to consume their data via an API.

The lesson is that information needs of each customer can be quite different. The needs of a small- medium-size business could be entirely different from the needs of an enterprise security director at a multisite corporation. A small business owner might be concerned with knowing that her stores have all opened on time and want only exception reporting in the event of an unopened location. An enterprise security director at a public company will be interested in compliance and a real-time view of any potential risks to his organization.

Software designers and engineers have been dealing with divergent needs like this for generations. It's one of the major challenges in creating a software system or service with broad appeal to multiple types of users. It should come as no surprise that these differences in data requirements would be a part of any big data solutions that emerge in the market. They will be one-size-fits

There are, however, some commonalities of categories that everyone shares. Rare or anomalous events are of interest to nearly everyone. They stand out, but only if you can find them in the sea of data you are confronted with every day. Confronted after the fact with these anomalies, the usual question is “how did you miss that?” Indeed, how did you miss the fact that on three successive Tuesdays, John Doe came into the office past midnight and left only after 15 minutes?
