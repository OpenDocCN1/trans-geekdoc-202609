# DISRUPTION

Social networks are disruptive. They have displaced older communications methods and increased the frequency of our interactions with other people and groups. They have altered the way we express ourselves and hastened the shift from voice to digital. They enable us to communicate with far greater ease and simplicity than anything that came before. Social channels have pulled us into closer groups over common interests and made us closer to people we already know. For many, they become a primary means of communication, eclipsing earlier technologies like voice calls by a wide margin.

They have redirected not only how we communicate but also with whom and services. Social log-in has simplified the way we interact with new websites and services, providing millions with a new way of identifying themselves online. They have decreased our password burden and increased our ability to collect and share information across multiple channels whose only common thread is my interests, my online identity, and me. They have spun off thousands of derivative Web businesses that would have been unthinkable without social networks.

So far, however, they have made few inroads into physical security other than through marketing, promotion, and mass communications, and that mostly by governmental agencies. This means that they have yet to provide any significant disruptive effect on the way we practice security or the way we develop products for this market.

The disruptive opportunity for social would be the widespread use of social identities in core security applications. Recall the discussion of Apple Pay for spaces. To win a disruption race in physical security, social identities would need to gain or at least hold ground against other forms of identity and authentication.

# STRATEGY

Security organizations can begin leveraging the power of social networks on their own, even before they show up in commercial security software platforms.

The communication efficiencies of social networks can be applied to many commercial and residential security needs. They are a low-cost, readily available complement to other ways that security organizations already communicate with their constituents. The larger and more diverse, the more useful they will be compared with other digital techniques e-mail lists and mass notifications via text.

What you will likely find a better fit to the task than any of the wide-open public networks, however, is one of the many internal collaboration platforms that started as software development tools (e.g., Slack) but are quickly turning into enterprise-wide messaging platforms. Security communications and topics can easily be adapted to these. The leverage in this approach is to use the same platform. Your strategy will depend a great deal on what type of constituency you are protecting. If you are working with a predominantly group such as employees, an e-mail address or some other internal identifier that is only valid within your organization. If, on the other hand, your constituency is more open and members are able to self-register, social sign-on will become more useful. Think of applications like admission privileges to movie theaters or sporting events, for example.

When I first entered the security industry at the height of the dot-com bubble, it struck me as having a steampunk quality compared with the business consulting and Web design world I had just left.

Analog CCTV cameras used Leave It Beaver era television signal formats and sent them down coaxial wires. Electronic security systems exchanged data over heavyweight cables that would have looked at a Teletype machine. The last disruptive advance for intrusion systems was the digital alarm communicator, a invention that roused operators at central stations via copper lines.

IP had barely shown its face in the industry and was far from being a shoo-in as the next, industry-wide arguments raged over whether it was reliable enough or remotely secure enough for the important business of physical security. (Hint, it is.)

Against this backdrop, I naively thought that broke all the rules might be greeted as a liberator. To many ears, our message sounded more like "we are from the dot-comb and we're here to help." Skepticism towards outsiders runs deep, especially if they arrive with the smarter-than-you swagger that characterizes many startups. In the first decade of my own experience with this market, I saw a number of would-be cloud companies make a run at the industry, only to run out of gas before they could get enough traction to sustain themselves.

It's not the industry appreciate new technology. I've never seen a group of people who love a good discussion more than a gathering of security pros. But it needs to pass muster with tough-minded integrators. It needs to make sense to the CSO who is quite literally making a career decision with every selection of new technology. It needs to make sense financially to the small business owner who needs to protect his business.
