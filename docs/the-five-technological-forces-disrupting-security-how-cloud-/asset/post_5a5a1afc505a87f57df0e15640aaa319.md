Security is not for the faint of heart. With some of these barriers lowering, there is new outside interest.

Silicon Valley comes to mind immediately. Large technology companies are creating or investing in security, access management, and video surveillance products for the residential and SMB markets. Google's acquisition of Dropcam (by way of Nest) is a prominent example. Apple is pushing its Home-Kit architecture that promises to stitch together diversity and automation products and unify the user experience. Most of the early marketing around these initiatives is consumer-oriented; it's not a huge to see them moving into the SMB space. After all, small business owners are as much consumers at work as they are at home. The technology solutions they choose at the office are heavily influenced by the technology they use at home.

Over the years, there has been an on-again, off-again relationship between the security industry and large telcos and cable companies, at least in the residential market. The logic was fairly simple. When intrusion systems were connected by plain old telephone service (POTS), the tel thought it made sense to expand into alarm services because they already provided the communication link and already had the customer relationship. Cable companies followed this same logic after they became Internet service providers (ISPs). They provided broadband into the home and saw alarm services as a natural expansion area because they already had the communication link and the all-important customer-relationship.

The security industry has eased these incursions with a combination of wariness and disdain.

Circumpection is warranted because many of the tech companies are too large and influential to ignore. Apple—a computer company—would be able to make a phone and compete with device makers like Motorola and Nokia. I guess we know how that turned out. By the same token, it's not crazy to think that someone outside the industry could make such a home automation and security system that they could take food off the table of many existing suppliers. thermostat guys probably didn't see themselves being disrupted until Nest showed up.

Cable companies have been floating home security solutions for at least a decade, and more recently, crossing over with small business packages. With cloud becoming more affordable and popular, the cable companies are in a privileged position, at least with their own customer base, because they own the broadband circuits and have a natural cost advantage over anyone else.

## CONSUMERIZATION AND IoT

These trends add up to an increasing consumerization of security that follows the consumerization of IT taking place over the past decade (or more). As discussed in the opening chapter, consumerization is a process whereby technologies originally introduced as consumer products subsequently transition into professional use. Employees often drive this when they acquire new technologies in their personal lives and bring them into the workplace. Personal smart phones, tablets, and employee-owned laptops serve as several of the most common examples, frequently lumped together as the bring-your-own-device (BYOD) movement.

Consumerization already had profound impacts on enterprise IT, leading over 80% of organizations to make changes to accommodate the new paradigm. Unplanned data retention on personal devices and requirement for mobile device management (MDM) services are two highly visible side effects. The coexistence of both personal and professional data on a single device makes for strange bedfellows, but the cost and convenience factors are too compelling for most businesses to ignore. These factors will be equally compelling to buyers in the physical security market.

For both consumer and commercial contexts, consumerization will be driven not only by the availability of mobile platforms but also more powerfully by the confluence of cloud, mobility, and IoT. The first outcome will be accelerated displacement of older technologies that do not offer the same convenience and ease of use found in consumer products. As reported by the same ID study, as early as March 2014, 46% of enterprises were already expecting to use IoT devices to make business decisions.¹ These solutions will be fully cloud-based and mobile, making any earlier technology solutions that do not look old, tired, and for replacement.

A second major effect of consumerization is to raise the bar for user experience (UX) expectations. Most of us now take for granted the UX improvements of the web browsers that replaced clunky and idiosyncratic desktop clients and the even older "green screen" terminals hanging off mainframes. However, until the advent of IoT, this improvement largely stopped changing the way we interact with physical things. There were a few products that had fought through the configuration process better than others. But under the old paradigm, most embedded electronic devices had horrible manual interfaces and physical switches and jumpers that needed to be configured with pins and twezers for proper operation. Consider the difference between programming even the best modern thermostat on its own interface versus doing that same task via a web app for a connected thermostat.

As recently as the early 2010s, audiovisual and home automation systems, for example, were still complex, available only through professional channels, required trained installers to make them work. A consumerization home theater system could easily cost much as $10,000 for parts and labor, not including the AV equipment itself. Today, in the consumerization marketplace, the same degree of control can be purchased for a little over $300 and 15 minutes of your time configuring the system with a cloud application.

Similarly, in the residential space, the cost of a fully equipped, preconsumerized home security system was typically upward by $3000 by the time parts and labor were factored in (not including professional monitoring fees). Today, in the consumerization marketplace, there are many products that offer nearly equivalent or good enough alternatives and are installed by the consumer or business owner.

The one facet of both residential and commercial space consumerization has not been able to address is the question of 24/7 professional monitoring. Central alarm stations provide a vital link between electronic security systems, police dispatch, and other forms of alarm escalation.
