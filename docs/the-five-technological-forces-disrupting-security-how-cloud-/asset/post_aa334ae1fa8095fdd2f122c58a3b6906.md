For the security industry, these standards rightly caution about adopting any new product categories that appear half-baked. We can't afford to deploy security solutions still being debugged or subject to obsolescence within a handful of years. In terms of technology adoption cycles, this cautious attitude is a typical early majority posture. Most of industries cannot act as innovators or early adopters (except in specialized cases where a vendor can vet a complete solution and guarantee some measure of stability to their own ecosystem.

How do we proceed?

First, we need to accept the premise that in the absence of standards, nothing can be integrated or secured, except one device at a time. That's neither scalable nor would leave every network unmanageable but defenseless as worst. Standards have benefits beyond basic interoperability. One of the most important is making devices conform to certain technical and behavioral patterns so that they can be treated as a class, not as individual instances. This is the core of making them usable and secure in any mass deployment.

Second, we need to recognize that most standards for IoT devices will be developed outside the security industry. Most of the action is coming from large technology companies and consumer electronics manufacturers. This should be familiar, because it's a case of history repeating itself. The security industry's wholesale shift to IP technology, for example, represents a migration to mainstream standards that originated with the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF). Similarly, the standards in every modern surveillance system originated from Moving Pictures Expert Group (MPEG), a multi-industry consortium predominantly composed of experts from the entertainment industry. We can expect the same adoption of broader standards to be the mainly of the discussion in security circles.

Finally, we need to understand that for IoT devices exist at many levels of the protocol stack and across many types of functional interactions among the components. There is no expectation that all IoT devices need to use a single standard, even at the most rudimentary level. IoT device capabilities and purposes are so widely divergent that there will need to be multiple standards for various industries and applications. A light bulb, a connected toothbrush, and a security camera have very little in common and probably don't need to interact with each other, at least directly. You certainly wouldn't expect all of them to adhere to the same standards.

## CURRENT STATE OF PLAY

Dozens of organizations and consortia work in the field of IoT standards. They each have their own distinct mission and focus on different aspects of standardization to varying degrees. Some are in competition, and some are complementary. Some focus on local networking interoperability, for example, while others refine communication standards for wide area networks. Some focus on device identity and authentication, while others work toward the adoption of standardized metadata to create “platform of platforms.” Some focus on radio protocols, and others work toward the adoption of common semantics so that everything gets the same way every time.

In short, the world of IoT has grown large—quickly. It is difficult to navigate and difficult to even know what's relevant to your own interests. Comp matters whatever you know might be outdated by years from now. However, there have been some gains in areas that will have lasting impacts.

Security organizations need not pay attention to all of the standards. Many lower-level standards that you never interact with directly. Many are bundled into products that don't always manifest at the level of user experience. Some of them, however, will matter a great deal as your organization begins to leverage IoT.

A number of the major IoT standards organizations and consortia have joined forces and begun toward common specifications, collectively recognizing the short-term pain of unification and harmonization is better than the ongoing chaos of fragmentation. The members of these groups are some of the most powerful holdnames in electronics, chip manufacturing, networking, and telecommunications, so they have the weight to make these standards successful.

In a similar spirit of unification, some large technology companies have published or open-sourced their own proprietary frameworks. This is essentially the old IBM or Microsoft playbook: we’re big enough that we can create an ecosystem of our own with enough benefits that people will gladly play our game.

Such propetary frameworks are not all bad when it comes to building consensus. Compared with the yearlong process it takes for groups of competing companies to develop technical standards from scratch, it can be a blessing for a single organization to hand down a fully realized vision to implement.

## STANDARDS ORGANIZATIONS AND CONSORTIA

Open-standards organizations are defined by their inclusiveness (anyone can join) and adherence to a published set of procedural rules governing how standards are proposed, created, and subsequently modified. They are operated under the auspices of large standards bodies such as the American National Standards Institute (ANSI), the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), and the International Telecommunication Union (ITU). They are generally open to any organization or individual who wishes to join, attend proceedings, or submit comments. There are no membership fees and no pay to play.

Standards consortia, though often mistaken for open standards organizations, are closed-membership groups that usually charge a fee and are free to determine their own procedural norms. They look very similar from the outside because their membership lists and scope of activities. They are nevertheless an important part of the standards landscape and can often move more quickly than formal standards organizations.

What follows is a brief overview of a number of open-standards organizations and consortia that are currently working on IoT standards to reveal the physical security industry.

### Security Industry Association Standards Committee

The open-standards organization closest to the interests of most security organizations is the Security Industry Association Standards Committee. (Full disclosure, I have served as the chairman of the SIA Standards Committee for the past 6 years.) The committee is an active ANSI Standards Development Organization (SDO) and so can have its specifications ratified as formal standards with both national and international organizations. The purpose of the group is to develop, discover, influence, and provide education on standards relevant to security applications. It is predominantly composed of members of the Security Industry Association, although its membership is open to any-one who wishes to participate.

The SIA Committee recently established the cloud, mobility, and IoT subcommittee to examine interoperability, authentication, identity, and cyber-security issues for the adoption of IoT in the physical security. The rationale for developing this subcommittee was based on the following observations:

- IoT devices will be the growing source of data in physical security systems.
- Security systems can harvest this value only if there are norms to build secure IoT deployments that operate with security management tools.
- IoT devices will introduce new communication protocols that need to be integrated into security systems and services.
- IoT can enhance physical security only if cybersecurity risks are properly mitigated.

The committee is also examining standardization of mobile credentials, specifically the use of digital credentials with access control readers via Bluetooth low energy (BLE). In broad terms, these standards would play a similar role to the Wieand and card-to-reader standards that have been part of the industry for the past several decades. In the absence of standards for mobile credentials, the industry is at risk of having multiple proprietary protocols, which retard adoption, add cost, and ultimately not serve the interests of end users. The standards created by this project could form the basis of mobile-to-reader interaction for the next 20–30 years.

### Industrial Internet Consortium (IIC)

The Industrial Internet Consortium was founded in 2014 as a not-profit partnership dedicated to advancing the growth of IoT for commercial and industrial applications. Their goals are to drive innovation with industry-specific use cases, develop a reference architecture for interoperability, influence the global standards process, and facilitate innovative approaches to IoT security. The IIC has published a number of IoT reference frameworks that are relevant to both manufacturers and system integrators (and, therefore, to security organizations).

The IIC has organized its activities around five vertical industry groups: health care, energy, transportation, smart cities, and manufacturing. All of these verticals intersect with security, particularly at the framework level where the IIC has created two publications to be influential in end-user organizations in all of these market segments.

The Industrial Internet Reference Architecture² is a standard-based approach to guide system architects building IoT systems in common framework.
