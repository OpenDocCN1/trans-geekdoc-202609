# Authentication Standards 201

All of the management functions of a modern security or building automation system are now controlled by software. Whoever can gain access to the software can gain access to the building and manipulate the security infrastructure. Preventing this from happening requires that these systems use the best authentication standards we can practically use.

When you think about it, the electronic security systems for buildings are just computer systems with different peripherals than the ones we use on desktops. Input of printers and scanners, they have card readers, door sensors, and electric strikes. Beyond all of that, they're just computer systems. Gaining access to a building is no different than logging into a computer or online service. Enabling better authentication standards at the point of entry is as important as it is for any other computer system.

There are many methods for authenticating people to computer systems, starting with the oldest and simplest, log-in and password. This model has been strained to the breaking point because of the sheer number of log-ins and passwords that most people must now manage. Recent surveys put this number somewhere in the 20s to 30s (I wish), and for many, it is far more. Newer authentication schemes from organizations like the FIDO Alliance and the Kant Initiative aim to replace this model with methods that are both scalable and trustworthy.

What models does not address the problem of device authentication for things that need to be on the network but have real user interface of their own. This is generally known as end-authentication. When something is plugged into a local network or attached to a Wi-Fi hotspot, how do other devices on the network know whether to trust it or not? Nearly all of the IoT standards we covered earlier attempt to deal with this issue.

Unfortunately, most control systems purchased today still use proximity cards as the primary form of authenticating people. This is known as "one-factor authentication" based on an object in your possession. The truth is it's not really authentication at all. Not only are they insecure, but also they can be lost or stolen by an unauthorized person as if they were a physical key. System administrators must be aware of the loss and delete that card from the system in order to maintain security.

At this point, I have to confess that I am part of the problem. My company sells good old-fashioned proximity cards in far greater quantities than the more smart cards that have been available for years. Why do we do this, you ask? Because our customers continue to order them out of habit and familiarity, even though they know they are less secure.
