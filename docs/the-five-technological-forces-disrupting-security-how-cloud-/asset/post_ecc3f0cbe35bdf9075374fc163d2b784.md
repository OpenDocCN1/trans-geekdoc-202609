In the public sphere, for example, NIST collects and publishes such data in a National Vulnerability Database (NVD) that provides a centralized resource for security researchers and practitioners. And there are numerous private resources, such as IBM’s X-Force Exchange, which is a library of shared security intelligence data, including 700 terabytes of raw aggregated security information for researchers. The service includes real-time data that can be useful in preventing or limiting damage from current or ongoing attacks. At an ingestion rate of 15 billion security events per day, X-Force provides a cloud-based platform that allows organizations to collaborate on security incidents and responses.

## ADOPTION, TRANSFORMATION, AND STRATEGY

Adoption of big data within commercially available security systems is virtually zero out of a few pioneering features in smart home products. I wish it were otherwise, but big data will not transform much of anything in the commercial security market for least the next 3–5 years. It won’t be in the market for least that long.

Over time, big data will eventually follow the path of many other technologies. What once differentiators become commoditized features that every vendor is expected to include. At point, there will be those who have kept pace an incorporate a big data strategy into their product design and those who haven’t. The change will be similar to the much earlier falling away of vendors who do not make the transition to the cloud.

For business owners and corporate security organizations, this inevitable change should be familiar by now. It’s about picking a horse, so to speak, that has some staying power. Vendor and product obsolescence are one of the largest among buyers when purchasing core IT systems that must depend on for many years—and we've said repeatedly, electronic security systems are just like any other IT system in that regard.

How does all of this lead you to a strategy for benefiting from big data?

Your big data strategy is based on your cloud strategy because you are unlikely to see big data features emerge on on-premise systems at reasonable price points. Even if price weren a factor, it's unlikely that your business alone will generate enough data for you to take advantage of big data while you are still living in a data puddle. You need to jump into the lake if you want to swim.

What can you do? Ask vendors about their product road maps. Find out how far they are looking into the future. See if they are moving in this direction. Make decision based on their track record of providing frequent software updates with significant feature enhancements.

When people ask how to position themselves for big data, I tell them that if you want big data in 3–5 years, put yourself in the cloud today. That's where it will happen.

Your answer to that question depends a great deal on who is asking and why. When Pete Townshend famously asked it of a police officer in Soho on a dark night in the early 1970s, it was the question of an incoherent man trying to get his bearings. It was also the birth of one of the greatest rock songs of that era.

When we ask it in the security field, we expect an answer that links back to a trusted identity source, like a passport, an employee ID, or perhaps nothing more than a referral from another person we know. Or at least that's how things worked.

Trust in the document confers trust on the person bearing it. We need this information to decide whether someone will be allowed into our property, allowed to become an employee, or allowed to control data or physical resources that affect the security of others in our charge. In this sense, identity is fundamental to security, and the way people identify themselves determines how we will interact with them going forward.

We citizens of the digital world have lacked an agreed way to uniquely assert our identities, but it's not for trying. We've actually come up with far many ways. The problem is that each of them is contained within the identity field of wherever we happen to create them: at the office, inside e-commerce sites, watching media outlets, in the pursuit of online dating, and thousands of other Internet destinations. Each of these is a classic information silo. When we go to the gym, we use an identity from our employer. When we use an auto-managed garage, we have another identity based on the fob assigned to our car. In each of these cases, instead of identifying ourselves as the same person each time, we present digital versions, with none having to do with the others.

You might be thinking that your name is unique, but it's not. In each of the day-to-day scenarios described above, the thing (or token) that identifies you is actually different.

## WHAT'S IN A TOKEN?

Physical documents face a challenge in the digital era. How do you use them to reliably verify someone's identity? That's why theft is such a problem. In the physical world, physical documents serve us well most of the time. We can examine them and have a pretty good idea of whether they are real or forged. We can also be fairly assured that there is only one copy of each credential, because the issuers do a pretty good job of following a one-person, one-document protocol. In the digital world, these start to fall apart. All everyone needs the data from your identity documentation, not the documents themselves, to assume your identity online.

The same is also true of the digital credentials we commonly use, log-in and password. It is no secret that passwords are fundamentally broken. If you do not notice soon enough, they can compromise your online accounts, and if you do not they can compromise your cash, your reputation, and your place of business. With security systems moving irreversibly to the cloud and a state of constant connection, the industry has joined the rest of the online world and begun to take seriously the issues of online identity and authentication. But it's a journey of a thousand miles, and we've just taken the first steps.

Never before in physical security have the issues of identity and authentication been intertwined as they are now. For many years, our internal software systems were on landlocked corporate networks with no connection to other networks. The ability to log was limited by access to the network and, more likely than not, the need to have a proprietary client installed on your computer. Sure, we all worried about the possibility of rogue employees and the tiny cracks in our armor posed by dial-up modems, but those threats were minor in comparison with the authentication challenge before us now.
