But this won't happen all at once.

As an extension of our eyes and hands, IoT devices can perform many tasks we could previously accomplish only by being physically present. In many cases, they can do it faster and better than we can and without the associated risks. A connected thermostat does a much better job of warning about temperatures than we do. A connected garage door that remembers to close itself is much better than relying on human memory. A Bluetooth listener is always known when personal electronics are in use. And a camera with object detection can do a much better job of observing and alerting suspicious situations- than a team of human guards.

But what do we want IoT to do for us? How do we decide where we get the most value? What are our biggest problems? In the science of risk management, the most relevant information we have, the more we can reduce risk—assuming that it is timely, accurate, and actionable. This is clearly a need IoT devices fill. The dramatic cost reductions in sensor technology by IoT allows us to affordably deploy networks at a fraction of the cost they commanded a few years ago. This lower cost structure introduces not only a quantitative change in sensor deployment but also a qualitative one. Sensors can now be deployed at low cost that vary every cubic foot of building or public space can be one or more forms of surveillance at all times.

Who knew Big Brother would be so affordable?

# IT'S ALL ABOUT THE DATA

Security has steadily progressed from rear-facing forensic research (aka closing the door after the horse has bolted) to forward-facing prediction and prevention (aka an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure).

IoT helps, but it's far from the first tool we leveraged for better data. Ever since we have lived in caves, a single human could detect only his own eyes and ears could tell him. Needing better tools, people added dogs to the mix as a four-legged multiplier and data collection system excellence. Their hearing and tendency to bark at nearly audible sounds perfectly equipped them to act as one of the earliest motion detectors. Much later, modern electronic security produced these canine capabilities. Electronic sensors gave the security organization eyes and ears. All of these enhancements, whether biological or electronic, have been moving us in the direction of more than bark through better data.
