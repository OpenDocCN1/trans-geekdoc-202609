It is also a barrier to trust for any thing of digital ID, because you can't trust what you don't understand. Finally, complexity also introduces vulnerability because the management challenges. That's one of the reasons most of us still middle along with passwords; anything more is a bridge too far.

# WHAT IS AN IDENTITY STANDARD?

There are standards for nearly everything on the Internet, and identity is no exception. That's a good thing, because without standards the web would not have evolve to what it is today. Everyone who uses a browser has become familiar with least a few standards like HTTP and SSL and possibly HTML. These are surface layer standards that pushed their way into everyday banter in web-speak. But there are many more standards beneath the surface—tens of thousands—that keep the whole Internet glued.

Identity sits in the category of those hidden from sight but doing their job every time you log anything. Some deal with the technical mechanics of identity validation, but many are more abstract. Like all standards, their purpose is to make things together that wouldn't otherwise. It all goes back to answering the question "who are you?” in a ways other understand, whether they are a person, a computer, or a building.

Two dogs can both wear collars that say "Spike," but that does not mean they are the same dog. That situation is possible because there are no standards for how dogs are named or how collars are monogrammed, and no rules against two of them being the same. That is the absence of standards. It's really inconvenient anyone going in the way of anything, at least not until one of the Spikes gets lost and needs to be reunited with its owner.

Fortunately, for Spike, the RFID chips implanted in most dogs provide standardization for canine identity. By agreement among chip manufacturers, breeders, veterinarians, and owners, canine chips are unique and centrally registered so that anyone can look up if a dog is unfortunate enough to lose or stolen. That is a standard in action.

RFID chips are a simple example because they do only one thing: emit a number upon request. Most identity standards are far more complex.
