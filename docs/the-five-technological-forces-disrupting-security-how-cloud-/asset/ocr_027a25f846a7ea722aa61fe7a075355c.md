# WHAT'S OLD IS NEW—AT LEAST FOR STARTUPS

Hardware companies—by which I mean connected device companies produce both a widget and the software web services to run it—are cool again. They are back in vogue and have enjoyed an investment resurgence since became a marketable reality. Many new investments are in the automation space, and many of those are related to physical security. This level of new, outside interest is different, and I am not sure the industry is used to it (yet).

The expansion of the connected hardware universe is pushing the boundaries of the security market along with it. Higher adoption rates are the availability of newer and better—and, frankly, more interesting-types of connected hardware that provide convenience and peace of mind in a more attractive package. Ease of installation drives high growth rates in the DIY sector of the residential market in particular, and it's spreading into the low end of the SMB market.

This point was explained to me sometime around 2009 and has only become more poignant since. My company had just introduced a cloud-based video surveillance service, and we were extrolling its benefits to one of our security integrator customers. I was pressing the virtue of our upfront installation cost due to the elimination of servers and setup labor. "Customers will love it," I said.

After letting me go on for a while, one of the executives said, "That's great, Steve, but we're not competing against traditional video record recorders. We're competing against Costco."

What he meant was that many buyers were willing to purchase cheap DIY systems off the shelf and install them on their. In the nearly eight years since then, the trip to Costco has been replaced with a trip to the Internet, and the tangle of wires and cheap cameras has been replaced with sleek new products like Dropcam.
