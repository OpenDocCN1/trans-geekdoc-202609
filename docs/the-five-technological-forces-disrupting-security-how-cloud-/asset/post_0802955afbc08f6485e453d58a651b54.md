
# One Key to Rule Them All

But there were several important differences between the work of credit cards and the world of electronic access. First, the credit card industry long ago agreed on technical standards so that every credit card reader works with every credit card. This interoperability implies many layers of agreement between bankers, merchants, manufacturers, and payment processors: identically, standardized data encoding, interconnected networks, and—perhaps most importantly—agreement on who you are.¹

The world of electronic access control, for residential and commercial, lacks this degree of standardization. Users need different apps, have different user experiences, and need to master different programming procedures for every brand of equipment they encounter. Imagine if your Ford needed to go to a different gas station than your Chevrolet—to say nothing of Tesla—and you had to memorize which cars fueled up at which stations. Maddening. In fact, we’re seeing this friction with electric vehicles, played out as an excruciating chicken-or-the-egg dance between manufacturers, consumers, and infrastructure providers. We are at the same point with technical standards for smart locks, except there is barely a chicken or egg in sight.

The forging of technical standards among competing companies has always been something of a mud wrestling contest. Everyone enters the ring hoping that technology will win, and they all leave dirty and dissatisfied. But technical standards are actually the easiest part of this problem. The bigger issue is identity.

Identity has emerged as industry analysts like to call “the new perimeter.” The metaphor is apt because your identity now forms a boundary around your assets, both physical and digital. Your identity lets you in—to your bank account, for example—and keeps everyone out, unless someone steals your identity. In case, they’ve breached your perimeter and have access to your world.

In the case of buildings with access control or smart locks, your identity lets you in and tells the building owner you’ve been there. Imagine the damage and havoc someone could wreak if they stole your electronic identity and could come and go in your apartment as they pleased. That’s why each institution issues a token to each person who uses their facilities, making our key chains heavier with fobs, our wallets thicker with plastic, and—soon—our smart-phones more cluttered with apps.

> ¹Who you are and how you prove it is at the heart of the current rise in identity theft, which is a topic for another chapter.

# APPLEPAY FOR SPACES

If there was something like PayPay for spaces—a single app that would let you present your credentials at any access control system anywhere, the same way that PayPay or AndroidPay could be used at any credit card terminal. Your mobile credential app could be used at any managed access point.

If there were such an app, the user experience would be nearly identical wherever you went. This is a big deal, because today every app has a different set of gestures to make it work at any given door or facility. For some, it's a swipe at a reader, for others a twist and turn of the smartphone, and for others a (nearly) frictionless experience of just having doors magically in front of you, like on Star Trek. Variety of gestures, the goal is to use a single verified identity (or at least a handful), with credit cards entered into your digital wallet. You would present yourself at any participating point and have the unique privileges that come with you.

The biggest impediment to this vision is the physical security industry lacks the kind of technical standards that the credit card and payments industry, of necessity, had to adopt in order for the whole system of credit cards to work in the first place. PayPay and AndroidPay have the luxury of leveraging the entire preexisting infrastructure of the credit card industry. Most obviously, they have access to all the credit card terminals installed in retailers around the world. But it doesn't end. Perhaps even more significantly than the physical terminals themselves, they also leverage the back-end processing of the giant financial payment networks like Visa and Mastercard.

Bane of my existence, physical security doesn't enjoy the luxury.

# DON'T GET ANY STANDARDS OUT OF THIS?

As the chairman of the Standards Committee of the Security Industry Association, a trade association of security manufacturers and system integrators, my colleagues and I are desperately trying to catch this train before it leaves the station for good. The Cloud, Mobility, and IoT subcommittee of the SIA Standards Committee has a project underway to standardize Bluetooth and other mobile credential exchanges for physical access control. The group was formed after many individual member companies had already developed their ad hoc standards for credential exchanges between mobile devices and readers. Unfortunately, once private companies have invested in their own proprietary solutions, they don't want to invest even more to comply with a standard, especially when that new level of compliance won't directly result in higher sales.

Standards have always been a matter of “build it and they will come.” They require faith that everyone will see the benefits and do the right thing for the industry, the customer, and the world at large. Today, with mobile credentials, it’s still every man for himself. However, I see early signs of supplier interest in creating standards before things are completely out of control. Hopefully, they’ll continue in this vein, cutting down on the need for dozens of apps that do the same thing and moving us closer to the one key to rule them all.

# Altered Presence

*In Being There*, the 1979 movie adapted from Jerzy Kosinski's novel of the same name, a simple-minded gardener is mistaken for a savant with oracular powers of insight about such complex subjects as international finance and world politics. The wealthy sophisticates around him hang on his every word and, paradoxically, derive great and unintended value from his dim-witted utterances. Just being there is all that Chauncey Gardiner needed to do to enlighten those around him.

For everyone who carries a smartphone, simply *being there*—in a building or a public space or roaming the open roads—is now enough to provide valuable security data and services. It is also the basis of widespread concerns about privacy, incidental surveillance and the wholesale monetization of every digital interaction we have with the rest of the world. While just sitting in our pockets, our mobile devices gather and create information derived from security technology. As Chauncey Gardiner might say, "this is like television, only you can see much further"

But it's not all doom and gloom.

# PRESENCE

One of the first elements of our ecosystem is what I call “presence,” the sum total of all the interactions we have by virtue of having a smartphone or wearable device. Today, this is nearly everyone, with the number of mobile devices surpassing the number of human beings on the planet. Admittedly, some of us skew this by having more than one device (e.g., a smartphone and a smartwatch), but the numbers continue to grow.

If Herbert Hoover were alive, he wouldn't talk about a chicken in every pot and a car in every garage; he'd sell the idea of a smartphone in every pocket. But then again, he wouldn't have to, because we're nearly there. As of 2017, the number of mobile subscriptions is on par with the global population; nearly half are smartphones.

# CHAPTER 6: Altered Presence

The concept of presence begins with our smartphones sensing where we are. This isn't just a matter of convenience. Presence applications provide automated or background interactions with our environments. They require a user to download an app, register under their identity, and be authenticated at one or more levels depending on the services in question. In access control, for example, presence might mean having your smartphone in your pocket is enough to activate and unlock doors, elevators, and other service points. Presence services contrast with other security and mobile credentials without requiring the user to take any action. They run in the background on your smartphone, always active. Most importantly, because your identity has been registered in all these cases, movements and interactions create an identifiable individual history of your presence within a given environment. Presence is simply a matter of being. We produce value and create interactions by showing up.

# PASSIVE SURVEILLANCE

The flip side of presence is passive surveillance, often perceived as the evil twin of all those positive services that come with the ambulatory radio beacon our phones have become. The very networks of wireless sensors that provide conveniences for everyone uses public spaces can also keep track of everything we do. In the real world, as it is online, surveillance comes at the price of a good user experience. You want to be relevant while surfing the web? Let me tracking cookies in your browser. You want a physical ecosystem that responds to you as an individual? Let me install a mobile identifier that tells me who you are. You want to know who is in your building and where they are? Convince them of the benefits of your magical app.

Passive surveillance, as its name implies, requires no knowledge or participation from the user. It does not require an app download or user registration.
