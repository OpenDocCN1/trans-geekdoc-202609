## Going Mobile

Recent surveys indicate that most of us check our smartphones around 85 times per day and touch them astonishing 2,617 times. These compulsive habits have elevated mobile usage to the dominant share of time we spend online, with smartphones now driving over half of Internet traffic. Much of our consumption is personal, but an increasing amount is for business and commercial transactions. With the blurring of lines between the personal and professional, we do both whenever we light up the screen.

As recently as five years ago, few of these use cases concerned physical security. Most security organizations were still bound to desktops. The earliest mobile apps had just made the scene and provided only a fraction of the features available on their full-strength desktop counterparts. Video surveillance apps were early favorite, certainly far better than static rows could ever achieve. Next were administrative applications for access control and building systems, followed by freestanding dashboards. Last were mobile credentials that let tenants, employees, and other occupants of properties open doors with their smartphones for the first time in history.

These mobile apps have gone through several evolutionary cycles over the past few years, they have begun to cluster around three distinct user groups:

- Administrative applications designed for members of the security team.
- Occupant applications using a very broad definition that includes tenants, employees, customers, and anyone else within the organization's purview.
- Presence applications that provide two-way information and services in the background.

Fourth, smartphones will stand in the role of passive surveillance in physical security. Unlike the previous three, this does not require the use of an app. can be tracked passively by receivers installed anywhere. Indoor location applications in retail and stadium settings, for example, already use this technique to create heat maps that show crowd flow and concentration around points of interest, bottlenecks, and disturbances. As the cost of these receivers come down—more portable—they will become an important part of our infrastructure and provide rich data streams for analytics.

## THE DESK IN OUR POCKET

Many of the apps we use every day had no prior life as desktop apps. They were born as mobile applications and provide uniquely mobile experiences. Think of real-time mapping or social messaging apps. They are not interesting as desktop applications because the whole point is to use them in real time, all the time, everywhere. They are incredibly mobile and that is their appeal.

Many of the first-generation mobile apps in security, on the other hand, are reincarnations of legacy desktop applications in new (and more portable) flasks. There is not anything wrong with that. The desktop needed to be put out to the curb, even when scrolled across it remain instructional. Mature applications embody a decade-long distillation of user requirements and valuable domain expertise. With this providence, the mobile versions could start far ahead and give most people what they want, all the time. Mobility delivers information where and when it's needed. As the “mobile-first” design philosophy continues to penetrate the security industry, these applications will evolve into something mobile.

An increasing number of new security apps are natively mobile and bring more to the table than their forebears. Mobile credentials are the clear example of a new wave of applications that fully leverage the power of the platform. More than just an incremental improvement, they introduce a qualitatively new user experience and convenience for the security ecosystem. They modernize access control authentication and pave the avenue for participatory security where all stakeholders have a role in both intelligence and response. Many technology analysts say that for an innovation to be truly disruptive, it needs to be ten times better than whatever it replaces.

Personal safety provides another example of native mobile use case that extends the eyes, ears, and arms of security organizations. Campus safety applications, for example, accompany students with a virtual security escort as they walk pathways through libraries, dormitories, and parking lots. Fascinating examples in the consumer space include services that provide personal protection on demand—a digital bodyguard that follows you wherever you go. At a corporate scale, managed applications provide global protection for top executives and employees traveling around the world.

## MOBILE FIRST

All of these examples show that the “mobile-first” design strategy is changing development priorities for security products in nearly every vertical market. The mobile-first approach nerfs the old order of software development priorities, where desktop versions of applications were the primary design consideration and subsequent adaptation to mobile platforms was often less than an afterthought. Instead, mobile first says that new features should originate on the small screen and then migrate to larger devices. Residential security applications have been mobile first for a number of years already, and commercial applications are not far behind

To see why mobile first is so important, consider the way that new features were traditionally developed and released. The primary design thinking focused on the desktop browser experience. Mobile apps then started their lives as an attempt to shrink down the user experience, using responsive design techniques as best they could. This process variably resulted in compromises and the removal of features. As mobile apps matured, the feature sets grew until they reached some approximation of the full-size version, but it never felt like a good fit. This strategy ultimately recreates the desktop on a smaller screen, but it does not capture the unique advantages of the mobile platform.

Mobile platforms have many capabilities that desktops do not, and they dramatically change the user experience and utility that can be delivered. As a result, the mobile design operates from a completely different viewpoint than the desktop frame of reference. Geolocation—the use of GPS and other positioning technologies—is the biggest differentiator between mobile and fixed frames of reference. Location is clearly a relevant piece of information for security applications, usually leveraged with geofencing or the ability to detect whether a smartphone is within a certain geographical boundary. Most residential apps, for example, provide a geofencing option to automatically arm or disarm an alarm system. In commercial security, mobile credentials can be position-aware, so that they provide the added layer of security being activated when they are near the property for which they are valid.

Given that we spend over half of our time on mobile devices, desktop usage has declined at roughly 10% per year for the last several years. I would be remiss not to observe that much of the impetus for mobile first is dependent on the prior experience of cloud computing. Without the availability of ubiquitous cloud services, there would be no mobile phenomenon as we know it.

## THE INTERACTIVE PARIGM

In the first generation of the web, we had one kind of content: static Web pages—little more than digital billboards displaying the same message every time you did drive-by. Usually, these websites described their products, solutions, and the company's profile, even when e-commerce existed as an essentially one-way exchange.(Like Henry Ford said,"any customer can have a car painted any color he wants so long as it is black.”) Despite we now view as the Stone Age, the Internet became much more interesting, though, when the second stage of evolution took off. The year 2004 saw the term Web 2.0 coined to describe the evolution toward user-generated content and interactive social networks. Think of YouTube, SoundCloud, and millions of blog sites that all packed with created content. Think of review sites like Yelp and TripAdvisor or person-to-person transaction sites like Match.com and Instagram that exist only because people are willing to upload every detail of their lives. Think of all the forums on health care and products and every topic of interest to mankind. All user-generated. All social.

The web was no longer a billboard with an occasional cash register. It became an interactive medium for everyone.
