## WHAT ABOUT RESIDENTIAL?

The residential market has always led the industry in creating innovative apps for security and automation systems. The leaders in this market have strong mobile offerings since at least the late 2000s, right on the heels of the iPhone introduction and all the other smartphone platforms that followed. These residential systems pioneered the era and are still the model for many commercial products. At that time, they displaced the old clunky, wall-mounted alarm panel. They boasted new capabilities like remote video and kids alerts told parents when their children arrived home school. Some of this innovation was by necessity. As a consumer product, residential security faces higher expectations for convenience and engagement than what commercial users demand.

That said, residential applications are necessarily different from commercial applications. Commercial applications face a different set of requirements and design challenges than residential, and many of these differences are beneath the surface. Each residential account, for example, has a much smaller number of users who can log in. This reduced scale has broad impacts on the design of the user interface and complex subjects like role-based permissions and authentication. Residential accounts also naturally limit the number of locations, cameras, doors, and sensors compared with enterprise-class systems. Designing for security assets that live under hundreds of roofs is different than designing for dozens or even hundreds that live under a single root.

Commercial systems must also support a much greater diversity of third-party products than residential systems, in some respects we have the luxury of a "walled garden" approach. Nowhere is this more evident than in support for literally thousands of models of high-quality video cameras, in the case of commercial systems, versus the small set of web cams that suffice for most homeowners. Commercial users also demand video retention periods of as much as 1 year, for compliance reasons, whereas homeowners are generally content with a few weeks. A similar story plays out with video quality, where commercial uses require high resolution, high frame rates, and demanding low-light performance, while residential systems relax most of the requirements to fit market pricing and homeowner budgets.

We see the same difference in access control features. For example, mobile apps for commercial properties need to support complex rules and user hierarchies, while residential systems need only manage a handful of people who may have

## BIOMETRICS & MULTIFACTOR AUTHENTICATION

Mobile platforms have solved a problem that has vexed the industry for years: how to get users to adopt the higher quality authentication biometrics provide. For as long as I have been in the industry, experts have been predicting that biometrics was “just about to take off.” The promise has come sound like airline announcements in a plane stuck on the tarmac: 15 minutes say that the plane is about to take off while we wait for hours.

Sometimes we have to switch planes before we can take off, that’s not a bad analogy for what’s happening with biometrics right now. Since ever technologies like fingerprint and retinal scanning have become effective and practical in a small electronic package, they’ve intrigued customers. On the surface, they appear high-tech, convenient, and trustworthy. They eliminate the problem of lost access cards. I can’t count the number of times customers have asked for the same biometric technology they see on TV and would like to implement for their bank, their condo association, their office building, or even the refrigerator in the break room, until they hear the price. At that point, the smile leaves their face, and they decide to settle for conventional card access.

The big uptick in biometric adoption occurred when phone-based fingerprint recognition was introduced during the early 2010s. These platforms provided a clear path toward the widespread use of biometrics—just not on the dedicated fingerprint scanners the industry had expected.

Smartphone biometrics cover two important use cases: logging into a security management application and using a mobile credential to enter a restricted area. Until biometrics became part of our mobile platforms, logging into a security management app faced all of the same password management and cybersecurity considerations as any other online log-in. Lost, stolen, or overly simple passwords created a gap that compromised our assets. The issue is even more pronounced in the case of mobile credential authentication because of the high usage frequency we see for most access control settings. The choice for app developers was to keep the credential logged in, in which case it’s no more secure than a card, or else antagonize the user with an authentication request every time she wanted to enter a restricted area (which now includes bathrooms in modern offices or corporate environments).

So far, mobile technologies have been more transformative than disruptive. The split between disruption versus transformation falls along the dividing line between administrative applications, which will be transformative, and occupant-based applications, which will be disruptive.

The administrative mobile apps introduced in commercial security over the past 5 years do the same things as their desktop forebears and are proved by the same vendors. They've moved a bunch of administrative features onto the portable platform that Internet users now treat as primary. So, it's old wine in a new flask.

The disruptive effects of mobile applications come from what I occupant applications. Today, the most common occupant application is mobile credentialing, as we've talked about extensively. As more features get bundled into basic credentialing, these occupant apps will drive strong and preferences for the “haves” versus the “have-nots.” Chief among the occupant features interactive security in which civilians (users) play a role by exchanging information with the security team.

First, they can act as casual informants. That's not nefarious as it sounds. In this case, being an informant simply means supplying ambient information about what's going on in a particular place. “The see something, say something” aspects of these applications also provide a ready avenue for channeling noteworthy- or worrisome information to the right people.
