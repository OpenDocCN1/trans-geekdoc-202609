AI has made impressive strides in recent years: medicine, finance, robotics, science, photography, and almost any subject area where problems can be represented as data (which is almost everything). The term AI is used in many ways, some more precise than others. The classic concept of AI is that it's synonymous with conscious machines, like HAL from 2001: A Space Odyssey—computers that think, feel, create, and in some sense exert their own free will. Back in the 1970s, it meant building a software model of the world or a particular domain that could be used to reason about things in that domain. If we fast-forward to the present, the notion of building an all-knowing AI system has been supplanted with the idea that it's better to create many micro intelligences that are each good at one specific task. The software that animates them includes hundreds of methods like machine learning, neural networks, natural language processing, image recognition, data mining, deep learning, and (still) cognitive computing. But those are the subject of another book.

For now, let's consider AI as a way of dealing with incoming data and making sense of them on our behalf, without getting too up on exactly what's going behind the scenes. Regardless of the specifics, it is clear that a new generation of analytic capability or intelligence will be needed to handle the growing volumes of data streaming into our security systems. Without such capabilities, the hacks will continue to grow faster than our ability to find the needles. Frankly, there are many indications that this will within reach of almost any software system over the course of the next decade.

Let's step back for a bit of contrast. In the early days, a company needed its own team of AI researchers to make any progress within their own domain. The notion of a generalized intelligence that simulated the human brain was proving difficult due to the complexities of knowledge representation. As the science progressed, the approach changed to solving very particular problems or teaching computers to execute specific tasks. For that type of research, every problem was so narrowly defined that unless else was working on the exact thing you wanted to know about, you had to roll your own. For most companies, having this level of expertise in-house was completely out of reach. The expertise could only be found in academia and organizations with large R&D budgets and long-term horizons.

Back to the present, AI and related algorithms are now available to almost anyone, and simply using them requires less expertise than ever before. For software developers and product companies, there are now “app stores for algorithms” that condense all the complex data science and programming into one easily consumable base. You no longer need to have Marvin Minsky in the basement.¹ The same capabilities are also available in the even easier-to-digest form of AI as a service from major cloud providers like Amazon, Google, and IBM, to name a few. This is remarkable, considering that as the dot-com era, AI was still considered to be out of reach for most products and many professional disciplines. What we are witnessing is the consumerization of AI, just as we witnessed the consumerization of IT and security making the effects of a perfect storm in the preface of rising IoT data, security applications in the cloud, and AI as a service.

The term artificial intelligence is used in many different ways, some more precise than others. In its most general sense, it includes methods like machine learning, neural networks, decision trees, principal component analysis, k-means clustering, support vector machines, and numerous other techniques too arcane to mention here. But it also has a very specific meaning. The original sense of the terms, back in the 1970s, it meant building (software) a model of the world or a particular domain that could then be used to reason about things in that domain.

To oversimplify, classic AI would be good at classic syllogisms as 'all men are mortal; Bob is a man; therefore, Bob is mortal.' While this type of AI indeed sort sorts from mortals in any data set, it's very different from machine learning in which an algorithm deduces all men on a training set in which everyone meets the demise. Classic AI did not move forward as quickly as anticipated over the years. It proved extremely difficult to build conceptual models of sufficient complexity to provide any useful value in real-time settings. It's not that the AI community has given up on this type of approach; it's that other approaches have been more fruitful.

# MACHINE LEARNING

Most of the problems we encounter or most of the data sets we are interested in are much more amenable to machine learning than they are to classic AI. There is some selection bias here. Machine learning has turned out to be good at solving problems that have business value, from medicine to self-driving cars to cybersecurity. Early success in these areas attracted additional investment and research, and the virtuous circle of positive results and reinforcements have continued to fuel growth.

We've mentioned machine learning in passing earlier chapters of this book, but have not focused on a definition. Usually defined as a way for computers to gain new knowledge without being explicitly programmed, machine learning sounds like the holy grail of artificial intelligence: machines that can think for themselves. That's the part that excites the popular imagination, but there's a bit more to it than that. Machine needs to be exposed to data in order to learn, and there are multiple ways to accomplish that goal.

There isn't just one type of machine learning. There are many types of machine learning algorithms, and they excel at solving different kinds of problems. It is not a one-size-fits-all intelligence (which, by way, is the pretty cool things about our own brains). The algorithms are usually categorized as one of the three general types: supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning. They are all relevant to physical security analytics, but in different ways.
