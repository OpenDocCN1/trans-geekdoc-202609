# What Does It Mean to be a Social Space?

When it comes right down to it, being social is mostly about how something interacts with the rest of the world. In the context of this book, there are four key behaviors that define social spaces.

First, they know who you are. Social spaces will be able to identify their inhabitants, visitors, guests, caretakers, administrators, and all the other people who interact with them. This knowledge is a fundamental building block because social relations are built on the concept of a stable personal identity over time. We find that social spaces will need a way to identify people uniquely in a way that doesn't change randomly, preferably by referring to an external identity provider. Social spaces will also need to understand the attributes and roles that are meaningful in the context of their interactions with us. The way we're greeted, where we're allowed to go, and whatever assistance we might be given, for example, will all depend on who we are and why we're there.

Second, they can talk to you. Being social is all about communicating. No communication, no social relations. Social spaces will communicate with us through social applications, just like our friends and colleagues today. The more natural this communication is, the better. Instead of having to learn a new application for every place we go, it beside possible to communicate with a social space as if it were a person, using text or voice exchanges. You could ask a social building to let you in, lock the door, or perhaps allow a guest to visit on a particular date, all without having to learn a new application or switch out of the applications you're already using.

Third, they can be part of a trust relationship. Social relations are all based on trust and is based on familiarity. That's why your dog barks at strangers and not at yours. A social building wouldn't bark at your friends, either. For social spaces, trust would also be built up over time, the same as it is between people and animals. Trust frameworks to support this type of learned trust are a hot topic in identity research. Automating them is a thorny problem. We will need automated frameworks for public spaces as the number of security concerns continues to grow. It is not possible to manage all of them through explicit rules. At some point, things, buildings, and spaces will need the ability to operate more like we do, and learning trust is a big part of it.

Fourth, they can take action on who they interact with and what their past choices have been. Ultimately, the whole point of creating social spaces is empowering them on the ability to know, talk, and build trust. For example, a social space could remember its past interactions with me and change its way of dealing with me as it gets to know me better. A social building will know if someone is a friend of someone else and perhaps allow them to enter, whereas a complete stranger couldn't.
