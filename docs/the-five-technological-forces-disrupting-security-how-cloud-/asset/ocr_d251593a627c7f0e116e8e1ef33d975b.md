Both of these radio protocols require the smartphone to periodically transmit small amounts of data to announce themselves and coordinate with other radios in the area. These radio transmissions from the smartphone (or other connected device) allow the device (although not necessarily the user) to be identified and present within range of the base. In networks with multiple access points, data can be combined to triangulate the smartphone’s position on a floor plan. This data can then be fed into spatial display systems or analyzed to understand movement paths, dwell times, and whether particular device has been seen in the area before.

It is projected by 2018, 90% of smartphones will be equipped with Bluetooth capabilities and that 45% are already using it in North America.¹10 Smart watches, car audio systems, free-headphones, Internet tethering, and many Bluetooth-enabled home devices drive more smartphone users to turn on Bluetooth and forget about it. Smartphone makers like Apple are ever surpassing the choice by automatically turning Bluetooth with each iOS update.

Even for users who turn off Bluetooth, Wi-Fi has become an essential tool to avoiding expensive cellular data plans with streaming video and bandwidth-intensive mobile applications. A recent survey, for example, found 76% of consumers connect to Wi-Fi outside the home on a regular basis.¹¹ Every time a Wi-Fi device connects to one of these networks, its unique MAC address¹² can be recorded. In a centralized monitoring environment, all of these MAC addresses can be collected and analyzed.

Analysis might be focused on consumer behavior questions like “has this person ever been in our coffee shop before, and do they visit any others?” These data could also be used to answer risk management questions or as forensic evidence after an incident: “who was there when the murder took place?”
