Of these questions, the view of the security mission is undoubtedly the most important. To revisit our earlier examples, if your security mission is limited to loss prevention in a retail setting, your parameters for spending on data collection and analysis are pretty well established. They must be less than the value of your losses (likely after subtracting the rest of the loss prevention budget).

If the security mission is to prevent corporate espionage, where billions of dollars worth of intellectual property may be at risk, the budget will look very different but will also be tilted toward cyber defenses, because most intellectual property now exists in digital form. If the security mission is to prevent loss of life in a public or municipal setting, budget considerations and risk tolerance will be evaluated by a completely different set of criteria and will also be limited by budget processes that may not reflect the risk versus reward equation. If the security mission is to protect critical infrastructure against terrorist attacks, then political processes will establish the budget, and the solutions will be engineered to gather data about the world, not just the sensor system within the infrastructure itself.

Finally, if you are a small business (or delivering security services to a small business audience), the security mission is going to be highly productized; that is, the features of standardized security products and services will have a large role in defining the security mission, because few business owners will carve out their own bespoke security program. They will instead choose from the usual three-legged stool of access control, video surveillance, and alarm monitoring.

# THE LACK OF STANDARDS FOR SECURITY EVENTS

One of the biggest challenges facing any big data implementation in the physical security domain is the lack of data standards. Today, nearly every security manufacturer and software developer store data in their own proprietary formats. For your security team, this means you're effectively on your own unless your security mission is cooperating with you or provides documentation and access to either a file system, SDK, or API. Some do, and some don't. Many consider data models to be proprietary information, not for public release, or even sharing with customers.
