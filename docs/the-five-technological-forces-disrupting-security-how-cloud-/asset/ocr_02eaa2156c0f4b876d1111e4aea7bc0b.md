IoT devices afford no such conveniences.

They are installed in wildly different environments with vastly different cyber-security practices. Unlike cloud services, they enjoy no common perimeter protection. They may be built behind a well-managed commercial firewall or just plunked down behind a home router to factory defaults. Unlike cloud services, they may not be patchable or manageable under centralized control. They can easily languish with year-old firmware that allows them to be compromised. Finally unlike unlike cloud services, the very notion of a perimeter is irrelevant for many IoT deployments. They are out in the wild.

Even if there were never a single IoT device used in a security system, their existence poses a cyber threat. That's because they will be installed on the same networks with security systems. A problem with any of these devices presents a problem for all.

# DEVICE AUTHENTICATION: DO I KNOW YOU?

Device authentication—think of it as log-ins and passwords for devices to get onto your network or access cards and employee badges for inanimate objects. Unfortunate, in the age-old context between innovation and security, this is one area where old safety is losing to sexy new inventions.

It's hard to provide effective, convenient authentication for relatively powerful computing devices like laptops, tablets, and mobile phones. They have more power than sophisticated encryption, secure storage, and certificates. They can also be remotely updated with patches that address the invisible security holes in every piece of software ever written. Perhaps, as important as all these factors is that they support man-machine interaction in which humans can respond to challenges, manage multifactor authentication, and provide crucial “what-you-know” factors in multifactor authentication protocols.

But do we provide similar levels of cybersecurity for small embedded devices with limited computing capabilities, no man-machine interface, and so low that the payoff may not be for anything but the most rudimentary methods? Most IoT devices do not identify themselves in any trustworthy way. This might seem manageable in a home network where you can (almost) count or recognize the devices that should or shouldn't be connected (although days are quickly coming to a close).
