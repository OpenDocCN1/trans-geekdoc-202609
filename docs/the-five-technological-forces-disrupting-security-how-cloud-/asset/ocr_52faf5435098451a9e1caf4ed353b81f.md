# The Secrets of the Universe

When I first entered the security industry at the height of the dot-com bubble, it struck me as having a steampunk quality compared with the business consulting- and Web design world I had just left behind.

Analog CCTV cameras used Leave It to Beaver era television signal formats and sent them down hose hose braided coaxial wires. Electronic security systems exchanged data over heavyweight cables that would have looked at on a Teletype machine. The last disruptive advance for intrusion systems was the digital alarm communicator, a Disco invention that roused operators at central stations via copper lines.

IP had barely shown its face in the industry and was far from being a shoo-in as the next reigning, industry-wide arguments raged over whether it was reliable enough or remotely secure enough for the important business of physical security. (Hint, it is.)

Against this backdrop, I naively thought that that broke all the rules might be greeted as a liberator. To many ears, our message sounded more like "we are from the dot-comb and we're here to help." Skepticism towards outsiders runs deep, especially if they arrive with the smarter-than-you swagger that characterizes many startups. In the first decade of my own experience with this market, I saw a number of would-be cloud companies make a run at the industry, only to run out of gas before they could get enough traction to sustain themselves.

It's not the industry appreciate new technology. I've never seen a group of people who love a good discussion more than a gathering of security pros. But it needs to pass muster with tough-minded integrators. It needs to make sense to the CSO who is quite literally making a career decision with every selection of new technology. It needs to make sense financially to the small business owner who needs to protect his business.
