# IT'S ALL ABOUT IDENTITY

For social spaces to be a reality, they need to be able to identify people and need to share an identity of their own in return. Today, we don't really have any agreed upon to assert identity with any universality or uniqueness. The fact is, there are too many ways, they're all bound up in the small world of whichever organization issued us a particular token. When we go to work, we use an access card from our employer. When we go to the gym, we use a card from the health club. When we use an automated garage, we have yet another identity on the fob assigned to our car. This means we're always identifying ourselves as someone different when we go to different places. None of these identities has anything to do with the others. We no wonder our buildings don't know who we are.

There have been attempts to find an identity provider everyone can agree on, but so far none of them has stuck. The security industry, for example, has many of ways of identifying people, but they suffer from a lack of uniqueness and scope. People outside the industry are horrified to hear that proximity cards aren't necessarily unique. Smart cards, but the market has seen to shift to the new technology.

The federal government has made several slabs at this as well, but the closest thing to a national identity initiative is the NSTIC program run by NIST. As a government initiative, however, it faces many adoption hurdles, especially since the NSA spying fiasco.

Against this backdrop, a social space still needs to have its own unique identity to interact with social applications on a social network. It's likely they will go in the same direction as the rest of us: using the available commercial identity providers that have become de facto way by which we identify ourselves online.

# COMMUNICATION AND CROWDSOURC

One of the main benefits of having social spaces with social identities on the same networks as the user community is that everyone can communicate with each other using identities and applications already part of their daily lives. These communications could be one-on-one, for example, such as a request or command to a social space. They could also be group messages, as we commonly experience in social media, where a community of interest is all privy to the same stream of information about a particular space or building. Or they might be messages about special events, like the arrival of a guest or a change of venue.
