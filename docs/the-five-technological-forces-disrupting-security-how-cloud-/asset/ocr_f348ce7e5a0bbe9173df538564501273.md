# CHAPTER 10: IoT Technology and Standards

Track gauges in many countries and regions were standardized based on the common efficiencies of doing so. Ultimately, the Pacific Railway Act of 1863 sets standards for the transcontinental railroad, and others followed suit. Now, trains roll from coast to coast without problem.

Today, the world of IoT devices is roughly where the railroads were before the arrival of standard gauge. The protocols they use to communicate are not the same gauge. This prevents them from interoperating with one another directly. It is also directly responsible for the proliferation of gateways, hubs, and base stations, plus all the authentication procedures needed to make them work together. In short, the jungle of wireless devices in a modern IoT implementation can easily rival the mid-PC era fumble of specialized cables for serial, parallel, SCSI, USB, Ethernet, and other peripherals that would only communicate with a specific type of wire.

The real issue is here one of interoperability. Ideally we would like for all IoT devices to talk to each other in order to accomplish some greater task, like alert occupants to danger. But there is no guarantee that any two IoT devices can work together without a lot of custom integration. That approach doesn't scale.

For the security industry, this lack of standardization poses a huge problem for IoT adoption. Forgetting the mess for a moment, the more serious problem is what it means to be on a single protocol family, only to find a few years later that it lost in the standards war and is no longer manufactured. If you are a buyer, you are now stuck with a system difficult to expand. If you are a system integrator, you will have to be the bearer of this bad news to your customers. And if you are a manufacturer, you have to place a bet that's as long as your product life cycle (or several bets).

This also has major implications for your workforce. Training is a constant treadmill exercise for most companies; technology obsolescence only makes it much worse. No sooner do your technicians know how to install one type of IoT network than something else comes along to replace it—with no guarantee of backward compatibility. As quick as your operations staff understand the quirks and foibles of one deployment, another must be placed alongside to build out your next layer of infrastructure.

This rolling stew of proposed standards from an evolving ensemble of too many chefs in the kitchen forces many companies to change their strategy over and over. In the earlier days of home and industrial automation, handful of radio protocols like ZigBee and Z-Wave seemed enough to put together a network of sensors and control devices.
