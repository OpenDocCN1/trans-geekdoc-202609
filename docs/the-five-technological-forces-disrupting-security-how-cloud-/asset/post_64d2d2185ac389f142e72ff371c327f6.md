
# CAN BIG DATA SAVE YOUR LIFE?

That was the question posed by Dr. David Agus in the January 2016 issue of Fortune. If your doctors practice data-driven medicine, the answer, happily, is yes. Because their medical decisions are informed by large sets of data about many patients rather than just yourself. In the data-driven enterprise, analytics provide the doctor with diagnoses and prognoses that can't be derived from individual cases. Machine learning adds even more to this equation, with trained algorithms now better able to identify disease conditions than a specialist with a lifetime of education and experience. Not knocking docs, the issue is that there is just too much data and not enough for any one to make decisions as quickly as they need to be. Big data to the rescue—it's a lifesaver.

Contrast this model of clinical practice with that of bleary-eyed staring into video monitor. This is the unfortunate child of traditional surveillance in our buildings and public spaces. The hope is that big data will spot anomalies and take action to prevent harm to people or property. But this scenario is actually a poor way to catch anything in real time. It is in the same sense that Churchill said, "democracy is the worst form of government, except for all others." So it is with event monitoring by humans: they are the worst form of vigilance, except for all others.

Limits on human attention make these models ineffective. Numerous studies show that attention diminishes from 100% to near zero within approximately 20 minutes. This means that over 90% of the time, we are expecting to be vigilant; they are actually doing a terrible job at it. Not knocking guards; it's just that traditional row-by-row display of events and alarms has been rendered ineffective by the volume of data in modern security systems. Reams of data scroll the human eye, but the brain can't begin to process or recognize signs of potential trouble. Only after the fact do dashboards and digits have become de rigueur for taming all this data and adapting them to our cognitive strengths. As the amount of underlying data has grown, however, the number of widgets has grown so large that not even the screen has space to show them all. This is the problem.

## BIG DATA DEFINED: THE FOUR VS

Too much data to deal with is pretty close to the working definition of big data: information sets are so large and complex that conventional data processing applications are inadequate. In this world, the familiar relational databases we grew up with break down the strain of more information than they can handle. Queries take long times to return the requested data, if they return at all. Disk arrays that once seemed to fill are now too small. Distributed file systems can grow horizontally to practically infinite dimensions. Software that allowed us to make sense has become useless against new ways of organizing underlying data. Even the statistical concepts we once took at the height of mathematical sophistication are now passé.

There is more to big data than simply being big. The four Vs, as they are known, provide a mnemonic framework that expands our definition:

- Volume
- Velocity
- Variety
- Veracity

Volume is the defining characteristic of big data. It's right there in the name itself. But how big is big? That depends. The threshold is not a particular number of bytes, but rather a size that demands new technologies. This is a moving target, of course. Conventional relational databases and business intelligence tools, for example, can manage data sets into billions of rows. But they can't necessarily do it enough to be useful if the data sets change rapidly or aren't structured.

Velocity is the rate at which data stream into an information system. Think of the rate of trades flooding the financial exchanges or the rate of data flowing through a global security operations center. Stable or slowly changing data sets are usually amenable to traditional database and analytic technologies. That's because standard indexing and segmentation techniques can perform an analysis within the timeframe in which answers are required. High information rates crash batch-oriented analytics because the answer to every question changes from nanosecond to nanosecond. This is where data won't stay still.

Variety refers to the proliferation of data types that are now part of our analytic universe. In the beginning of computing, there were numbers and numbers represented all things and without them there were calculations. Since then there has been an explosion of data species. Now we have a garden of data types that includes strings, video, unstructured text, enumerations, links, objects, pointers, functions, sets, graphs, arrays, and good old numbers—real and integer. This vast array of data types has changed the kinds of analytics that are both possible and required to make any sense of it.

Veracity the fourth V might be called a sometimes V because not everyone accepts it or even agrees on its meaning. For security, it matters, and it can be expressed as one of the bedrock principles of our profession: trust. Whether your data are big or small, do you trust them? Surveys indicate that roughly one-third of business leaders don't trust the information they receive from their data systems. Veracity of data sources and believability of analytic outputs are crucial to our big data future in security. We can't afford to be wrong a third of the time.

## THE CASE OF THE MISSING DATA

Large security organizations with huge budgets have access to powerful big data resources and analytic software. I am thinking of state agencies like the CIA or FBI, international organizations like Interpol or NATO, or many of the offices in the Department of Defense. They have teams of trained data scientists who can follow the wisps of data left behind by international terrorists and string them together to find a needle in a haystack. The successes of these intelligence, law enforcement, and military teams are legendary. They seem almost omniscient, but a lot of the credit goes to the data resources and analytics at their disposal.

The commercial security systems available to most businesses, unfortunately, provide nowhere near this level of data insight. Given the property value and lives at stake, better data analytics could make a significant difference in the effectiveness of our products and people. Without it, there are limitations to our ability to provide early threat detection and advanced risk assessment. Many organizations buy security systems and then never look at the data. We've made huge investments in data collection but less in its analysis. The crux of the problem with the state of the art: we've done a great job of deploying systems and collecting data but less so at applying data science in a way that reveals what we want to know.
