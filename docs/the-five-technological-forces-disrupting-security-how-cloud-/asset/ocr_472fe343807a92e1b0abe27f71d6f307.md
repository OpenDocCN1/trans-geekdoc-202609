The proceedings for some of them are conducted in full public view through standards organizations, while others are growing up inside caustic consortia where pay to play is the law of the land. Still, others are put forth by industry giants or preemptively distributed through open-source projects that encourage adoption among powerful communities.

For the security industry, these standards rightly caution about adopting any new product categories that appear half-baked. We can't afford to deploy security solutions still being debugged or subject to obsolescence within a handful of years. In terms of technology adoption cycles, this cautious attitude is a typical early majority posture. Most of industries cannot act as innovators or early adopters (except in specialized cases where a vendor can vet a complete solution and guarantee some measure of stability to their own ecosystem.

How do we proceed?

First, we need to accept the premise that in the absence of standards, nothing can be integrated or secured, except one device at a time. That's neither scalable nor would leave every network unmanageable but defenseless as worst. Standards have benefits beyond basic interoperability. One of the most important is making devices conform to certain technical and behavioral patterns so that they can be treated as a class, not as individual instances. This is the core of making them usable and secure in any mass deployment.

Second, we need to recognize that most standards for IoT devices will be developed outside the security industry. Most of the action is coming from large technology companies and consumer electronics manufacturers. This should be familiar, because it's a case of history repeating itself. The security industry's wholesale shift to IP technology, for example, represents a migration to mainstream standards that originated with the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF). Similarly, the standards in every modern surveillance system originated from Moving Moving Pictures Expert Group (MPEG), a multi-industry consortium predominantly composed of experts from the entertainment industry. We can expect the same adoption of broader standards to be the mainly of the discussion in security circles.

Finally, we need to understand that for IoT devices exist at many levels of the protocol stack and across many types of functional interactions among the components. There is no expectation that all IoT devices need to use a single standard, even at the most rudimentary level.
