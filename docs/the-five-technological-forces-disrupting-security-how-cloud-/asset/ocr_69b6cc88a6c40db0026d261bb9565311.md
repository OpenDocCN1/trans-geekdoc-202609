Since then, we have seen a bigger and scarier real-world hack that hits much closer to home for the entire physical security industry. Ground zero, you could say.

The incident in question is the 21 October 2016, distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack that created a botnet army of 140,000 surveillance cameras deployed in businesses around the United States. Under the control of the malware known as Mirai, this army of cameras was directed to conduct a massive attack on major swaths of the Internet. The crush of traffic from these infected cameras hobbled every day, from ordinary users to big-name Internet companies like Twitter, Tumblr, and even the likes of Amazon and Netflix. Delays stretched out, websites became unreachable, and conducting business became impossible for hours on. It all took a single strain of malware and a large population of poorly protected IP cameras to bring a significant chunk of the Internet to its knees. There is no clearer example of security devices being turned into security liabilities.

Billions of IoT devices—security-related and otherwise—will be added to our environment over the coming years. Many of these will be deployed on networks shared with the security fabric of our facilities, our infrastructure, and—literally—our way of life. While they will no doubt make many positive contributions to our lives, they will also open numerous attack surfaces for cybercrime, identity theft, invasions of privacy, and ever terrorism.

These threats rising out of the nexus between the physical and digital worlds have been dubbed denial of safety attacks. They are expected to become more commonplace as facilities are outfitted with IoT devices for both security and building automation. Many, in the technology community openly wonder whether this Pandora’s box of cyber trouble can be closed. At the very least, we need standards to contain the morass of special cases and one-off device profiles that are so burdensome and vulnerable today.

The cyber risks presented by this new generation of connected devices have the potential to determine the core mission of security organizations if not properly managed. These circumstances have given rise to the point of view that all security is now cybersecurity.
