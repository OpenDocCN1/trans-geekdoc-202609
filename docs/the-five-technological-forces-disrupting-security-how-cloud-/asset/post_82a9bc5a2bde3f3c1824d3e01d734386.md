# ORPHANS WITHOUT GOVERNANCE

At the heart of the cyber challenge for connected security devices, we have a governance gap that leaves them orphaned from the day of installation. Customers are often unaware that their equipment requires firmware updates. Many security integrators install and never look back. Your IT server won't touch it unless covered by standard patch management software (they aren't). The IoT land is littered with stragglers what is ultimately a problem of distributed governance: if everyone is responsible, then no one is responsible.

Let's break this down.

Start with the customer. At work and at home, we are all customers of any number electronic companies. We have many devices from many manufacturers in both places. Quick, make a list of every piece of electronics you own and its firmware is up to date. Beyond your smartphone and your laptop, you probably can't do it. Neither can I. And if it were not for the nagware on my phone and computer, I would probably fail at both of those. We need to assume that the things we've purchased as they are supposed to be the day we bought them with need no for further attention. Nothing could be from the future. The threat environment is always changing, and defense strategies must change along with it.

Now, for IT departments. They don't have tools for IoT devices because they don't fit the pattern of manageable equipment. They don't follow any sort of standard model, like Windows or Linux servers. It prefers standard gear, where patch management comes as a boxed solution that can (more or less) run on its own. When they see an amalgam of network cameras from different manufacturers, door controllers of different generations and designs, and wireless sensors connecting back through proprietary gateways, they throw their hands up. None of these devices are supported by off-the-shelf patch management systems teams, and every manufacturer has a different set of policies and procedures for updates—if they provide them at all.

# The Update Treadmill

Next come security integrators. Unfortunately, many aren't in a good position to manage the cyber updates of connected devices. First, it's not usually written into maintenance agreements, and it certainly can't be done for free. Second, even if it were, many integrators do not have enough cyber expertise on their staff. Their technicians are trained to perform installations, not keep track of the latest threats to equipment from dozens or hundreds of manufacturers. Third, it would not be cost-effective for them to apply software and configuration patches across hundreds of individual customer sites. There'd have to be some centralized system to do it.

What about manufacturers? Many vaguely believe that manufacturers really ought to be providing these updates. The ideal is appealing in theory but falls apart logically because manufacturers don't typically know where their devices are installed and they cannot reach into a network to make changes to them—unless they are already cloud-connected devices in the first place. Manufacturers can provide automated updates, but many customers won't want them because they have seen updates fail. The best we can do is to provide tools and choices and hope customers and integrators use them.

# THE UPDATE TREADMILL

So, let's imagine that in some perfect future, we've overcome the knowledge and governance gaps that get in the way of keeping our connected devices up to date. You may also feel free to imagine that you've won the lottery. It's about likely as not.

IoT devices in physical security applications likely share the same network with numerous other IoT devices deployed for different purposes. This is always the case in home and small businesses, where it is prohibitive to set up a secondary network for physical security devices. This means that the new normal for security equipment will be network coexistence with an unknown number of random devices from mystery manufacturers.

Writing in the a16z blog, Benedict Evans makes an insightful comparison between this aspect of IoT proliferation and the earlier rise of electric motors. Our grandparents knew how many electric motors owned because they were newfangled curiosities and few in number. We used to be in this position with respect to connected devices in our homes, but it's quickly escaping our grasp and will be beyond the reach of our children. At that point, we will have forgotten them and any need they may have.

The fly in the ointment for IoT is that the sheer number of devices in need of software updates will become unmanageable—at least with current software updates. Many of us would say that we're already at that point. The reality is it's difficult to make it through an entire week, if not a day, without an update request from one or more phones, laptops, tablets, televisions, watches, refrigerators, thermostats, or other household gadgets. To say nothing of the mobile apps that control them, all of which seem to be monthly release cycles that we use on the treadmill.

That's without us ever leaving our home. A frequent response to being on this treadmill is to ignore updates altogether. And why not? If the thing you bought did you wanted the day you bought it, can't you leave it that way? In the preconnected era, the answer might have been yes. But that's no longer true: a vast number of software patches fix serious security holes. A security flaw in a single IoT device could be the crack through which hackers enter your home or business network. From there, they can compromise other devices.

The only conclusion is that everything needs to be automatic (or least coordinated) update schedules. The problem is simply thousands of IoT devices becoming ubiquitous and invisible, security integrators may not know where or how they are in any network setting. Fortunately, there are products and standards emerging within some domains that aim to make these updates efficient at the device level and manageable at the manufacturing or enterprise level.

For any given security organization or integrator, however, it will be a long time before they see adoption or uniformity across the suite of products that may be using for security isolation. Aside the logistics of managing software updates, security providers also have a well-established need to check new firmware releases for behavioral changes and security flaws before they are deployed. This is already common practice for every device installed in critical networks in financial institutions and other high-risk/high-value computing environments. It should also be common practice in any industrial setting where compromise of industrial controls could pose harm to human life or large economic losses.
