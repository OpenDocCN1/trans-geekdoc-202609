# THE ROAD TO VOLUME

Electronic sensors have been around for a long time in many industries. Initially, they could accomplish a great deal with simple technology. Think of the humble switch that detects whether a door is open or a $5 motion sensor that detects movement in a room. Each of these introduced a night-and-day difference in remote surveillance. Video surveillance systems are benefiting from the same type of analytic capability that is enabling computer vision for self-driving cars. These are now capable of tracking dozens of objects simultaneously in a single scene. But all of these, individually and collectively, are still only a fraction of the deep intelligence we could have at our disposal within a few short years.

Don't get me wrong. I am not saying that today's technologies are ineffective. They are foundational data collection systems. We would be blind without them. What is missing is the additional layer of data analysis already transforming other industries. As a simple example, I could cite my own anticipation of the comparative energy report I receive each e-mail from my local power and utility. It tells not only how I compare with my energy usage before but also how my energy consumption stacks against several efficient and inefficient neighbors. It also (painstakingly) reminds me of what I could be doing better.

If data can tell us how energy-efficient we are, couldn't they tell how secure we are?

One reason is the structural difference between the electricity and security services are typically delivered to their respective customers. Electric distribution is centralized; most commercial security is not, with the exception of alarm services, which are only one leg of the security stool. Centralization of energy consumption data provides not only for large, regional data sets but also for an economical way to generate and distribute the higher intelligence about usage patterns. In commercial security, the economics of advanced analytics has placed these capabilities beyond security budgets. Even a single security organization could obtain reference data sets (a big problem in itself), the traditional on-premise computing model would be quite prohibitive to run the analytics software. Expensive enterprise software licenses keep ahead intelligence out of reach for most.

In the case of access control, video surveillance, and related security information management systems, the prevailing deployment scenario has been to one or more or mid-class servers inside a protected property. The cost of these servers plus software licenses for operating systems, databases, and applications totals up to a pretty penny, considering that they are only used by a single organization and, even then, usually only by a small number of users.
