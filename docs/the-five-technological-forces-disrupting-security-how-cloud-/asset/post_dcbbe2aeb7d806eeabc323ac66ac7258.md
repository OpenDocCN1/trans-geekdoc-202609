## SOCIAL IS THE NEW ME

Social identities have become a growing part of how we identify ourselves on the Internet. It's almost unavoidable. As I am writing on a document in Google Docs, for example, I am logged in with the same Google+ identity that I use across other websites and services that have nothing to do with Google. These other online services have simply chosen to use Google as an identity provider for the sake of convenience, security, and improved user experience. In many of these applications, I could just as well use my Facebook identity or my LinkedIn or a one-off log-in based on my favorite e-mail account.

All of these examples are commonly known as social sign-on—using your identity from a social network provider or your identity on an unrelated website. The social network takes care of authenticating when you first log in and then lets secondary sites know that you are who you say you are and that you've authorized access to resources on that secondary site.

Part of the reason they have gained acceptance among users is that our social network identities are at least tenuously connected to who we are in real life. We begin to form a web of connections with our real Facebook pages, our real Apple IDs, our real Google+ playlists on YouTube, and our real e-mail addresses that tie our online lives together. All of these connections back to the real world are verifiable to one degree or another. That's why it's usually easy to spot fake Facebook pages or fake reviewers or anything that doesn't connect back to what we can sense, is literally, a full-blooded human being at least of the time.

For those who are still a bit uneasy about using social networks for serious business, there are pure-play identity providers that don't come with the bare-all baggage of the most popular social platforms. OpenID, for example, is a stand-alone identity provider that uses many of the behind-the-scenes identity federation techniques as the social networks. Arcane protocols like "OAuth" and "SAML" and "LDAP" (of which many later) make possible the convenience and security we derive from making the link to a third-party identity management platforms.

The billions of us who use the Internet have implicitly appointed this small handful of companies to be custodians of a significant part of our digital identities. Anyone who has created a new account at a website will be familiar with the option to use one's own e-mail address or choose to leverage an existing identity at one of the providers just listed. The attraction of using, say, your Facebook ID is that you avoid creating another log-in and password combination that you will soon lose and/or forget. By reusing an existing identity from a social network, you can also now weave a single identity across many services provided on many websites and enable data sharing and transactions between them.

In this respect, the public has already voted with its keyboards and thumbs about who will be its favorite identity providers. Facebook lately has the largest user base adopting their credentials to register and log into third-party websites. Google has been pushing their Google+ credentials for the same purpose. We're even seeing B2B companies like Salesforce.com acting as identity providers for enterprise customers.

## THE HOLY GRAIL OF UNIVERSAL IDENTITY

Let's return to the problem of centralized or national identity programs. The basic problem is that they are often proposed and operated by institutions that people don't trust—at least not with their entire identities. That level of trust would be a big ask, to be sure, but trust is the central principle in identity management and secure digital transactions.

Burecrats, social planners, computer programmers, and dictators alike all imagine a more perfect future in which every human being is identified by a single, unique “handle” centrally issued and managed. Civil libertarians have worried about the same eventuality, characterizing it more as a “mark of the beast” than a coolly efficient management technique. Against this backdrop, the history of a single universal identifier is a tug-of-war between the will to organize and the will to maintain privacy.

In the United States, social security cards famously brandish the words “not for identification.” First issued in the wake of the Social Security Act of 1935, the cards were originally intended as account numbers for tracking contributions and benefits due under the new legislation. Some seem to be for many other purposes and over the years they have become a de facto universal ID for establishing bank accounts, credit cards, health insurance, and many others unrelated to social security. This widespread use has come at a price, giving identity thieves a single key to unlock access to your financial persona and health records, among other things.

Oracle CEO Larry Ellison was widely derided in the early 2000s for his role in advocating a national ID card program that would have required all Americans to carry a new form of universal identification that included a photograph and biometric identifier. Regarded as a transparent attempt to insert Oracle products into the center of virtually every financial, commercial, and legal transaction, the initiative failed because advocates of stripes that would erode fundamental freedoms and paved the path for widespread surveillance of innocent citizens. Mind you, this was before Edward Snowden revealed the full ramifications of the Patriot Act and the extensive use of the NSA’s PRISM system, which demonstrated that our identities are easily reconstructed from many digital breadcrumbs. In 2011, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) launched a program known as NSTIC, the National Strategy for Trusted Identities in Cyberspace, to improve privacy, security, and convenience of online transactions. NIST ultimately awarded $9 million to fund pilot programs that demonstrated these principles. The timing of the Snowden leaks in 2013 could not have helped. As with most initiatives, the program faced criticism from privacy watchdogs. In fact, India's Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) that was established in 2009, enrolled over one billion people in its Aadhaar database. Despite the successes of such programs, the digital privacy watchdog Electronic Frontier Foundation is blunt in its assessment of the impact and true purpose of national ID cards in any form: "national ID cards and the databases behind them comprise the cornerstone of government surveillance systems that creates risks to privacy and anonymity." These were certainly not the first or only efforts by national governments to establish such centralized identity platforms. The list alone includes the Ottoman Empire, the United Kingdom, Germany, and Hong Kong. The world's largest identity program, India's Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) that was established in 2009, enrolled over one billion people in its Aadhaar database. Notwithstanding many of the same privacy concerns as other identity programs, Aadhaar has nevertheless been a boon to providing services to the rural poor and as an ecosystem for innovation. I believe that this sense of unease will persist and ultimately thwart the establishment of universal identity programs, unless they are voluntary and user controlled, and that's my quest for greater universality in smart apps brought me to the concept of social.

## IDENTITY IS THE NEW PERIMETER

This has become a catchphrase in both the cyber and physical security worlds. What this means is that the edges of our properties and networks are no longer clearly demarcated. They are porous; what passes through them depends on who or what it is. We used to look at solid barriers—doors, walls, and borders or their digital equivalents, VPNs, firewalls, and gateways—we now look at the rules, credentials, and procedures required to go from the unprotected to the (theoretically) protected.

This whole model presents that the concepts of outside and inside still make sense. If you have an enterprise that is scattered around the city, the country or the planet, where is your perimeter? What is inside? If you have a corporate network composed of a dozen local office networks that connect to each other through the public Internet, where is your perimeter? If you have mission security electronics on the corporate network where your employees daily defend against phishing attacks, where is your perimeter? If your employees use cell phones and web APIs to open doors in your facilities, where is your perimeter?
