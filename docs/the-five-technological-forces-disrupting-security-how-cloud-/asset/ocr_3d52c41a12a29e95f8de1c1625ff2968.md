# CHAPTER 10: IoT Technology and Standards

To realize the full promise of secure IoT deployments, we need better protocols with better authentication and identification techniques for connected devices. My aim in the following discussion is not to catalog every single wireless protocol that might show up in the IoT space. Nor is it to compare and contrast them and make recommendations about which one might be best for application. That will change over time, and the winner will be determined as much by market forces as by technical merit (remember Betamax?). My goal is to identify some of the major contenders that matter to physical security that will be relevant going forward for at least a few cycles.

Let's start with two of the better-known wireless protocols that have been central to the success of home automation, Zigbee and Z-Wave. They are both mature, low-power mesh radio systems that work for sensors, door locks, and other devices that can run on batteries and require only infrequent (rather than continuous) data exchange. They are both deployed in both home and commercial settings, although Z-Wave tends to be seen as more of a home automation technology and Zigbee a more scalable industrial option.

Zigbee is an open technology that has a strong commercial and industrial base from the start, primarily because it was designed by IEEE standards members who came from highly automated industries. The Zigbee Alliance has 400 members, which provides a lot of choices from competitors going down this path. Although it uses strong encryption, some vary from one manufacturer to another, and some are more readily hacked than others. Zigbee has shipped more chips than Z-Wave, has a good foothold in physical security, and probably will for many years. It supports large device networks like those found in factories and warehouses. It is expected to show strong growth and maintain substantial market share for the foreseeable future.

Z-Wave is an once proprietary (but now public) protocol that has 60%-70% market share in home automation (which is, to be clear, noting other market segments). Nine in ten smart homes have a Z-Wave offering, with many product choices in each category. The chipsets for Z-Wave are available from only a single manufacturer (Sigma Designs), offering the advantage of all the products using being interoperable with the same gateways. The drawback is that this makes it a single-vendor solution at the chip level (although that industry dynamic is not necessarily debilitating, as the case of the Qualcomm-licensed chipsets have shown for years). The limitation of 232 devices per network is an obstacle for larger commercial installations, though.
