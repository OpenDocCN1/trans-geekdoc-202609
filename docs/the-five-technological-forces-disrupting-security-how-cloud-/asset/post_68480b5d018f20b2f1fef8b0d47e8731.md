
# From Premobile to Mobile First 7

No less than identity and access management, public key cryptography (PKI) and digital signatures were essential 15 years ago to solve the trust problems that still plague IoT. For the SmartBox, using digital signatures as a surrogate for humans solved chain-of-custody issues for the journey of a package, from consignment to carriage to customer.¹ When the consumer-driven SmartBox morphed into an access control panel, it brought its X.509 digital certificates with it and became the first commercial system to do so. The use of these certificates is now widespread on networked security equipment.

Sells apparently. So does good industrial design for IoT products. IDEO, the renowned Palo Alto-based design firm and early, created the SmartBox design. The aesthetic grace of their work landed this access control system on the pages of Wired, Bloomberg, The Chicago Tribune, ABC News, and a screen performance on Morning America. The same attention to design is a hallmark of nearly every successful IoT product.

The SmartBox started out as a consumer logistics product. The uncanny forces of the market and opportunity drive it straight into consumer security. This story is not unlike, and that's the crux of the matter for the flurry of innovations transforming the product landscape today. They have become more capable of jumping across categories than ever before. They mutate from one application to another. It's all happening more quickly than the pace of traditional product design. If you are making products, that's a worry, but if you are buying design, life's never been better.

For companies or products that don't keep pace, displacement can be permanent. The SmartBox escaped that fate by staying smart but losing the box. An investment banker's insight into back-of-the-napkin drawing gave it the first push in the right direction. Later, the eureka moment was that people are like packages and buildings that are just big boxes. In a lucky irony (for me least), online commerce has just done leaving packages unprotected on doorways and porches everywhere, but security will never be offline.

# FROM PREMOBILE TO MOBILE FIRST

Say you never forget your first.

Mobile phone, that is mine. Mine did little more than voice calls and short texts. By the year 2000, text had gone viral, and the early Blackberry had become a thumb-typing obsession. Security teams discovered early how useful and liberating text as a replacement for the cryptic callback numbers of analog pagagers. Meanwhile, product designers jumped to the chance to mine this way to reach out and touch someone.

> ¹US Patent No.: 6,404,337.

As a connected device, the SmartBox was no exception. It needed to talk. It had things to say. It led us straight to the central role that mobile platforms have taken in security over the past decade: digital notifications of changes in the physical world. We learned early that people paid attention to messages, even if they were from a machine rather than a person. They would change their behavior if a connected device provided them with timely and relevant information. Of course, if the device told them that a package had been delivered, they might change course to pick it up before going on with their day. If the system told them someone else had been inside their box, they might call and find out why. We take all for granted now because we have connected devices telling us all the time, but at the time, the capabilities delivered everyone who saw them.

We found communication to be even more valued in commercial settings, where timely information is money. Field repair logistics was a prime example: getting the right part to the right person at the right time—and noting that it happened. We required a face-to-face handoff staff at the parts dept and field technician. Inserting an access-controlled counter into the process made it asynchronous, thereby decoupling the schedules of both parties. The system also provided the validation of service level commitments, with texts and e-mails providing an audit trail. Everyone saved them.

Today, this is almost passed, but at the time, it was groundbreaking. Text and images are now the lifeblood of sensor networks, apprising security teams of anomalies across their facilities and inhabitants. Mobile technologies have freed us from our desks and video monitors, enriched the interaction between buildings and their occupants, and make a platform for identity and access management.

# BIG DATA IN ITS INFANCY

The term "big data" made its debut in 1998, shortly before this story begins, but people weren't really using the term all that much. Even before joining the security industry, where I worked in a company that did nothing but analyze and sell huge amounts of data, no one said "big data." Instead, the company talked about analysis, reports, and building out specialty warehouses for clients. In other words, the company’s conversations with customers were about the value added to the data and what clients could do with it—not just its size.

The experience taught me how valuable data could be when run through the right algorithms and presented in the right. It was like panning for gold from a mountain of dirt. That’s why monetization is often a secondary business plan of any company handling large quantities of data. Value lurks in unlikely places, it always pays to seek it out. This seems especially true for logistics and security. Given the financial stakes in both markets, it seemed likely that the information tailings from their data systems would still be high-quality ore.

Fifteen years later, with big data redefining the intelligence that can be extracted from large volumes of physical security events, the industry is beginning to take note. Security event data have real value. Unfortunately, most of these data cannot be mined because it is strewn across the landscape in little piles they are on-site legacy computing systems.
