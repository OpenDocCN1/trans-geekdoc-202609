Who could have known that an electronic mailbox for homeowners would one day compete against the giants in access control? Who could have known that a single smart multisensor would one day blanket an entire home with protection? Or that the battery-powered camera would knock out the heavyweights of analog video? These are all IoT, and they hit the security industry right where we live.

How so?

Electronic security created the business and technology template that has since become the model for IoT. Electronic sensors monitor their environment. Check. Digital transmission of sensor data. Check. Remote control of electromechanical systems. Check. Centralized computing systems that knit whole networks of sensors. Check. Algorithghmic responses to predefined criteria. Check. User interfaces to access humans.

Many of the technical details differ from today's IoT, to be, but the underlying concepts and relationships are remarkably similar. Sensors were larger and used phone lines than the Internet. Digital transmission was slower and had a much smaller vocabulary. Electromechanical systems were less intricate. The hub of the network was at central stations staffed by trained operators. The algorithms were deterministic rather than self-educating. But they got the job done all the.

All of this explains why IoT products have so readily became what classic economic theory refers to substitute goods—things that replace other things—the new Wi-Fi sensors that can see through walls and security cameras for intrusion detection. It also explains why the security is so ripe for consumerization: all the kinds of products we made of the same stuff as convenience and lifestyle innovations. The signature example of this is video management software that replaces security cameras by stitching old networked iPhones onto a Wi-Fi video surveillance. An amateurish kludge, but it makes the point.
