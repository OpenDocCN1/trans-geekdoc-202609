In the context of physical security, many connected devices were built with the tacit assumption that they would live within the relatively safe confines of local area networks. The on-premise client-server paradigm made this assumption reasonable at the time, but not anymore. The walled garden no longer has a wall, and no one will build unless we pay for it.

Once local systems were connected to the Internet, they were vulnerable to the billions of hackers, bots, and automated attack scripts that pound countless URLs every day. Even as our websites, applications, and APIs have become better defended, embedded devices have lagged. That's how we ended up with 140,000 cameras being used for DDoS attacks.

# THE NEED FOR STANDARDS

It would seem that in this day and age, it's hardly necessary to make the case for standards. But as anyone with passing technical familiarity with physical security can tell you, many areas still defy standardization. Instead of boring you with yet tech speak, let's talk about everyone's childhood fascination, choo-choo trains.

> "Gauge" is the width between the two rails of a train track. Today, we take it for granted that this is the same everywhere, but this was not true. In the early days of railroads in the 19th century, companies used different track gauges throughout the world. Trains that operated on one gauge could not travel on a track of a different gauge because the wheels would not fit. This incompatibility meant that human travelers and material shipments had to be off one train and move to another in order to continue their journey. This was great news for freight handlers, restaurateurs, and hoteliers at the junction of those two gauges, but bad news for passengers and shippers.

On the face of it, this was a relatively simple problem to solve: just make all the train tracks the same size, right? The problem was that vested interests had grown up around each type of track and the rail that fit into it, and no one wanted to bear the expense or inconvenience of having to change. The contrary, using a gauge incompatible with the next railroad over was actually an intentional strategy for preserving market share. It forced people to use your track if they wanted to get to areas you controlled. This circumstance was the basis of the famous Gauge War of 1853 in Erie, PA, where the town prevented gauge standardization in order to force layovers and thus local spending.
