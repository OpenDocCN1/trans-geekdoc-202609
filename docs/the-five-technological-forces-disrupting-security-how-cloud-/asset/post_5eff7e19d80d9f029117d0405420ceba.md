
It is also a barrier to trust for any thing of digital ID, because you can't trust what you don't understand. Finally, complexity also introduces vulnerability because of the management challenges. That's one of the reasons most of us still stick along with passwords; anything more is a bridge too far.

## WHAT IS AN IDENTITY STANDARD?

There are standards for nearly everything on the Internet, and identity is no exception. That's a good thing, because without standards the web would not have evolved to what it is today. Everyone who uses a browser has become familiar with least a few standards like HTTP and SSL and possibly HTML. These are surface layer standards that pushed their way into everyday banter in web-speak. But there are many more standards beneath the surface—tens of thousands—that keep the whole Internet glued.

Identity sits in the category of those hidden from sight but doing their job every time you log into anything. Some deal with the technical mechanics of identity validation, but many are more abstract. Like all standards, their purpose is to make things work together that wouldn't otherwise. It all goes back to answering the question "who are you?" in ways others understand, whether they are a person, a computer, or a building.

Two dogs can both wear collars that say "Spike," but that does not mean they are the same dog. That situation is possible because there are no standards for how dogs are named or how collars are monogrammed, and no rules against two of them being the same. That is the absence of standards. It's really inconvenient anyone going in the way of anything, at least not until one of the Spikes gets lost and needs to be reunited with its owner.

Fortunately, for Spike, the RFID chips implanted in most dogs provide standardization for canine identity. By agreement among chip manufacturers, breeders, veterinarians, and owners, canine chips are unique and centrally registered so that anyone can look up if a dog is unfortunate enough to be lost or stolen. That is a standard in action.

RFID chips are a simple example because they do only one thing: emit a number upon request. Most identity standards are far more complex.

## AUTHENTICATION VS AUTHORIZATION

There is no way to talk about identity and its role without getting into the weeds of the two core principles of identity management: authentication and authorization. I've been using both of these through this section and elsewhere in the book without making a fuss exactly what we know. My bet is that you have glossed right over them and, unless you are a specialist in the field, read them as being pretty much the same thing. It's unfortunate that they sound so much alike because it leads to an imprecise usage that confuses many discussions.

Authentication is the process of establishing that you are who you say you are. In the real world, if you get pulled over for a traffic violation, the police officer will ask to see your driver's license, compare the picture on the license with your actual face, and decide if that's really you. That's authentication.

In an online setting, presenting a log-in and password is the oldest and simplest way of authenticating the person claiming to be bob@aol.com. In physical access, presenting an access card to a reader is a form of authentication—not a very good one, because anyone who finds your card can pretend to be you.

Authorization, on the other hand, answers the question of now that we know who you say you are, what are you allowed to do? In the driver's license example, you may be authorized to drive a private vehicle, a commercial vehicle, or a motorcycle. These are three different types of authorization. After logging into a computer system, you may be authorized to see your own files but not those of anyone else. Upon entering a building, you may be authorized to enter the lobby and elevator but not any private offices.

Identity management, log-ins, and identity standards address authentication and authorization. Some cover both topics, and others are aimed at solving just one part of the puzzle.

## AUTHENTICATION STANDARDS

On the face of it, authentication sounds like an online or cyber problem, not a physical security problem. But it's our problem too because it's not the way we identify people in more and more transactions.

Digital authentication standards are intended to enable simpler and more log-ins to online accounts. Simplicity brings with it greater clarity; the need for security without saying it. Most authentication standards are intentionally a narrow set of use cases so they are easy to implement and easy for users to understand. Complexity is a big barrier to user adoption. Unlike some other types of standards we will look at, they do not attempt to ensure you are who you say you are.

## Authentication Standards

There are many methods for authenticating people to computer systems, starting with the oldest and simplest, log-in and password. This model has been strained to the breaking point because of the sheer number of log-ins and passwords that most people must now manage. Recent surveys put this number somewhere in the 20s to 30s (I wish), and for many it is far more. Newer authentication schemes from organizations like FIDO Alliance and the Kant Initiative aim to replace this model with methods that are both scalable and trustworthy.

What models do not address the problem of device authentication for things that need to be on the network but have no user interface of their own. This is generally known as device authentication. When something is plugged into a local network or attached to a Wi-Fi hotspot, how do other devices on the network know whether to trust it or not? Nearly all of the IoT standards we covered earlier attempt to deal with this issue.

Unfortunately, most control systems purchased today still use proximity cards as the primary form of authenticating people. This is known as "one-factor authentication" based on an object in your possession. The truth is it's not really authentication at all. Not only are they insecure, but also they can be lost or stolen by an unauthorized person as if they were a physical key. System administrators must be aware of the loss and delete that card from the system in order to maintain security.
