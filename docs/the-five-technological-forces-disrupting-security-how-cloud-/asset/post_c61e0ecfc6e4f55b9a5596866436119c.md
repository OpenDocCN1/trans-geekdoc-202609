
In the case of IoT systems, there is usually a local wireless network just for IoT devices (although some use Wi-Fi directly). Such networks have their own protocols designed to maximize communication efficiency for the devices themselves. However, these networks still need to be connected to the Internet. Otherwise, there’s no “Internet” in IoT. This is where the gateway comes in. It translates between local wireless protocols and the Internet.

Let's take a deeper look at why not all wireless networks can be connected directly to the Internet.

- First, Wi-Fi is a familiar local area network that uses the same protocols as the Internet. Many wireless networks designed for IoT, however, do not use the Internet. They have their own protocols and need to be translated at multiple levels.
- Second, gateways are responsible if the Internet is unavailable. Sensors communicate with control panels; for example, they may not send alarms outside the building, but they can alert people inside.
- Finally, gateways take care of various tasks such as device registration, power management, and peer-to-peer communication between devices on the network. The trick is being able to put together a system where all the IoT devices use a single standard and gateway functionality.

## WIRELESS PROTOCOLS FOR IoT

One of the most head-spinning areas of IoT technology is wireless communication. The innovation has been prodigious in recent years, and there are now more choices than ever. In the security industry, we rely on several wireless protocols to connect things like cameras, intrusion sensors, and card readers. New families of protocols provide more features and more security than their predecessors.

## CHAPTER 10: IoT Technology and Standards

To realize the full promise of secure IoT deployments, we need better authentication and identification techniques for connected devices. My aim in the following discussion is not to catalog every single protocol that might show up in the IoT space, nor is it to compare them and make recommendations about which one might be best for application. That will change over time, and the winner will be determined as much by market forces as by technical merit.

Let's start with two of the better-known wireless protocols that have been central to the success of home automation: Zigbee and Z-Wave. They are both mature, low-power mesh systems that work for sensors, door locks, and other devices that can run on batteries and require only infrequent (rather than continuous) data exchange. They are both deployed in both home and commercial settings, although Z-Wave tends to be seen as more of a home automation technology and Zigbee a more scalable industrial option.

Zigbee is an open technology that has a strong commercial and industrial base from the start, primarily because it was designed by IEEE standards members who came from highly automated industries. The Zigbee Alliance has 400 members, which provides a lot of choices for competitors. Although it uses strong encryption, some vary from one manufacturer to another, and some are more readily hacked than others. Zigbee has shipped more chips than Z-Wave, has a good foothold in physical security, and probably will for many years. It supports large device networks like those found in factories and warehouses. It is expected to show strong growth and maintain substantial market share for the foreseeable future.

Z-Wave is an once proprietary (but now public) protocol that has 60%-70% market share in home automation (which to be clear, noting other market segments). Nine in ten smart homes have a Z-Wave offering, with many product choices in each category. The chipsets for Z-Wave are available from only a single manufacturer (Sigma Designs), offering the advantage of all the products being interoperable with the same gateways. The drawback is that this makes it a single-vendor solution at the chip level (although that industry dynamic is not necessarily debilitating, as the case of Qualcomm-licensed chipsets have shown for years). The limitation of 232 devices per network is an obstacle for larger commercial installations, though.

LoWPAN, one of the newer low-power wireless networks, is important to physical security for several reasons. First, it uses the same underlying radio technology (IEEE 802.15.4) as Zigbee, which makes it familiar in terms of radio transmission characteristics like distance, interference, and penetration through walls. Unlike the other protocols covered so far, it is an IP standard and provides every device on the network an IPv6 address to be addressable on the Open Internet. Protocols that require gateways, on the other hand, can assign IP addresses to the devices inside their networks.

Bluetooth is familiar to most of us because of its prevalence in consumer products from cars to headphones to electric toothbrushes. Since the Bluetooth Smart (low energy) version was introduced in 2010, the standard has become attractive for IoT devices that require long-battery lifetimes. Bluetooth is also emerging as the dominant technology for mobile credential exchanges that use smartphones instead of a traditional access control card or fob. Bluetooth use is growing in other commercial security applications, such as sensor networks, door locks, and readers. Its 90% penetration rate in smartphones guarantees it long life shelf for both personal and commercial systems.

Wi-Fi is so common and well-known that I hesitate to explain it all. However, it is an important and enduring standard for physical security systems and will be for a long time to come. Wi-Fi is fast and secure, but it is not a low-power network like many of the others we have addressed in this context. Wi-Fi provides system integrators an IP connectivity option that does not require pulling cables. Commercially available products such as door controllers, readers, wireless locks, and network cameras all leverage this advantage. Security organizations and facility managers benefit from less disruptive installations, with the potential for cost savings due to reduced labor.
