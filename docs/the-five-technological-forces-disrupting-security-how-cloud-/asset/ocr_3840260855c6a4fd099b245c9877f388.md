The disruptive opportunity for social would be the widespread use of social identities in core security applications. Recall the discussion of Apple Pay for spaces. # STRATEGY

Security organizations can begin leveraging the power of social networks on their own, even before they show up in commercial security software platforms.

The communication efficiencies of social networks can be applied to many commercial and residential security needs. They are a low-cost, readily available complement to other ways that security organizations already communicate with their constituents. The larger and more diverse, the more useful they will be compared with other digital techniques e-mail lists and mass notifications via text.

What you will likely find a better fit to the task than any of the wide-open public networks, however, is one of the many internal collaboration platforms that started as software development tools (e.g., Slack) but are quickly turning into enterprise-wide messaging platforms. Security communications and topics can easily be adapted to these kinds of products as a new channel. The leverage in this approach is to use the same platform already adopted the rest of your organization.

Leveraging social identities and social sign-on is more challenging. Your strategy will depend a great deal on what type of constituency you are protecting. If you are working with a predominantly group such as employees, membership, or some other group that is conscript under a more or less formal business process, then almost any consistent form of identity will work. Usually this means an e-mail address and employee number, membership number, or some other internal identifier that is only valid within your organization’s IT services. If, on the other hand, your constituency is more open and members are able to self-register, social sign-on will become more useful. Think of applications like admission privileges to movie theaters or sporting events, for example.
