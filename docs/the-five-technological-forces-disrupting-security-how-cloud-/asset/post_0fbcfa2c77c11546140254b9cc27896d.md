I used to joke that everyone in the industry must be from Missouri, because it's like living in one giant show-me state. Early adopters are hard to come by, no wants to go first. This makes the problem of crossing the chasm, to revisit Geoffrey Moore's metaphor, especially acute.

One of Moore's basic tenets is that in the early stages of technology adoption, people don't follow the examples of leaders outside their own vertical industries. People in health care want-health examples, people in retail want retail examples, and people in security want security examples. That's why it didn't particularly matter to most security practitioners that all of Silicon Valley had gone over Web technology by the late 1990s. They needed to hear it from someone else. Someone they already knew.

The stereotype of the early adopter is that they work in a small, maverick community breaking tradition across many dimensions, including the way they acquire and use technology. Startups today don't even consider the old enterprise application approach for their computing needs. They go to the cloud, straight to open source, straight to social, and straight to a subscription revenue models.

Sometimes, though, it's the industry giants that come out of the gates. ADT is a case in point. They adopted my company cloud technology by way in 2003, less than 2 years after we brought it to market. Jay Haunn, VP of product management, was responsible for moving the biggest name in the security into the cloud. He still tells the story of how the former CEO of ADT's parent, Tycho International, tapped him on the shoulder day one and asked him to check out this startup with a cloud subscription model for access control. As a dyed-in-the-wool recurring revenue company, it fit with ADT's worldview to incorporate this new model as another line of business. Early adopters sometimes appear where you expect them.

Anecdototes make for memorable stories, but what we're really interested in is how the industry as a whole is being transformed by each of the five forces trace through this book. How much of an impact has each of them really made? How much more potential do they have to improve or disrupt?

The more I thought about this, the more I found that I needed to develop a scoreboard and treat each one as running its own race. The horses at the Kentucky derby don't all cross the finish line at the same time and neither will these technologies. It also became evident that a single score for both commercial and residential systems would not do justice to the sizeable spread between these two categories. And, while we have not focused on residential systems in this book, it is instructive to view them as a leading indicator of where commercial systems are going.

Table 1 uses the families of the technology adoption life cycle to score the progress of both commercial (C) and residential (R) systems along this continuum. In almost every single case, residential systems are further along. The reasons have nothing to do with the suitability of the technologies in each market; everything to do with who the buyer is, but the channel and end user level.

Cloud. Commercial security systems show up as early adopters of cloud technology because market share for cloud systems is still in the single-digit percent, dwarfed by traditional on-premise deployments. The cloud version of electronic access control, access as a service (ACaaS), has a market share of less than 5% as of this writing, although the research firm IHS predicts a healthy compound growth rate of around 20%. The cloud version of video surveillance, video surveillance as a service (VSaaS), is also currently below 5% and growing at a compound annual growth rate of nearly 20%.

In residential systems, the picture is quite different. Cloud adoption is well into the late majority stage. All the residential market leaders use cloud platforms to provide interactive security and automation features that are nearly indistinguishable in terms of systems architecture. Residential security providers have no choice but to use the cloud (not that they would want to otherwise). With customers numbering in the millions, there is no other way to deliver an interactive experience at this scale. It's what the cloud was designed to do.

Mobile. Mobile is further along the technology adoption curve than any of the other technologies we've examined. That's not surprising, because in general it is the most consumerized, accessible, and globally penetrated. To be at their most effective, mobile applications require a cloud backend. The willingness to fully engage cloud services is once again the underlying factor in the differences between commercial and residential adoption.

In residential systems, mobile applications are nearly universal for the reason that cloud is nearly universal; they are the best way to deliver an interactive experience. Every interactive consumer security platform of scale has a mobile app—without exception. The widespread availability of mobile-enabled products has all but eliminated the scales of systems that do not have them. That's the textbook definition of technology adoption by laggards; they buy it when the older alternatives are simply longer available in the market. Security systems without mobile apps become the rotary phone of the residential security market.

Not so in commercial security, although it is catching up. Customer premise systems have been adding a few morsels of mobile capability. (I have), you'll wish you hadn't. Where there is cloud, there is mobile, and where there is not, it's frankly a bit of a hack. The effect is friction and resistance to rapid adoption.

For these reasons, commercial security systems have not moved as quickly as residential systems and have only reached the early adopter stage so far.

IoT. Depending on how you define IoT and the relevant market segment, identical security systems are somewhere between early adopters and late majority.

Recall that earlier I made the case that the security industry is the original IoT pioneer. In this respect, nearly every device in a modern security system is an IoT device, connected back to the Internet through a gateway (otherwise known as a control or alarm panel). If every interactive residential system sold today is based on the cloud, then every one of them has a deployment of IoT devices inside the home. In this respect, the penetration of IoT among buyers who use any system is nearly 100%. You just can't be a laggard.

The story is somewhat delayed in the commercial sector. A large percentage of commercial systems are still isolated on corporate networks.

Times are changing, however. First, Internet-connected sensor networks are becoming a compelling value proposition. Longer-range radio technology and improvements in battery lifetime are making these more practical than ever. A growing percentage of access control points are now being equipped with wireless locks—that is, IoT devices—connected to cloud-based management systems. Video surveillance is also adopting an IoT framework. Past barriers of bandwidth and storage are falling due to edge computing, video analytics, and massive increase in inexpensive data connectivity available to even the smallest of businesses.

For these reasons, commercial security earns a rating of early majority. Said, it is on the move toward universal adoption.

Big Data. Everyone looking at the future of security technology sees the promise of big data. The question is when. Pattern recognition and predictive analytics that can be built with big data platforms are two of security’s holyils. Investigators and law enforcement look forward to forensic searches across big data stores that provide answers of magnitude more quickly than we have today.

The first thing we need, though, is data. Residential security and home automation are once again in front of them. They are natural aggregators for the data output from millions of homes. A few of the larger residential services have announced big data and machine learning to take advantage of this information and improve the services available to their customers. This is promising and earns a score of early adopter.

Big data has virtually no presence in commercial security systems for the primary reason elaborated earlier; have yet to migrate to the cloud in sufficient numbers to build up data repositories. Said, big data and analytics will become parts of the bigger, smarter, faster systems of the future and the emergence of data-driven security as a new model.

Social. At this moment, social has more promise than track record for electronic security. I believe that this is a usability problem more than a usefulness problem. There is also a trust problem buried in there, but let’s look at each of them separately.

The usability problem for social in physical security is that it is difficult to configure these platforms for the kind of targeted messaging needed for security notification scenarios. This challenge makes it more straightforward to use more direct conduits like e-mail or text or in-app messaging. They are also easier to set up, since everyone knows how they work.
