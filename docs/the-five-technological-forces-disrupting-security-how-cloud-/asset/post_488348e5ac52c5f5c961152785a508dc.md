As an extension of our eyes and hands, IoT devices can perform many tasks we could previously accomplish only by being physically present. In many cases, they can do it faster and better than we can and without the associated risks. A connected thermostat does a much better job of warning about temperatures than we do. A connected garage door that remembers to close itself is much better than relying on human memory. A Bluetooth listener is always known when personal electronics are in use. And a camera with object detection can do a much better job of observing and alerting suspicious situations than a team of human guards.

But what do we want IoT to do for us? How do we decide where we get the most value? What are our biggest problems? In the science of risk management, the more relevant information we have, the more we can reduce risk—assuming it is timely, accurate, and actionable. This is clearly a need IoT devices fill. The cost reductions in sensor technology allows us to afford deploy networks at a fraction of the cost they commanded few years ago. This lower cost structure introduces not only a quantitative change in sensor deployment but also a qualitative one. Sensors can now be deployed at low cost that every square foot of building or public space can be one or more forms of surveillance at all times. Who knew Big Brother would be so affordable?

## IT'S ALL ABOUT THE DATA

Security has steadily progressed from rear-facing research (aka closing the horse after it bolts) to forward-facing prediction (aka an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure). IoT helps but is far from the first tool we leveraged for better data. Ever since we have lived, a single human could detect only what his own eyes and ears could tell him. Needing better tools, people added to the mix as a data multiplier. Much later, modern electronic security produced these capabilities and gave security organization eyes and ears. All of these enhancements, whether biological or electronic, have been moving us in the direction of more through better data. The common denominator across all IoT devices—and the reason they transformational—is that they produce huge streams of data. A recent projection estimates that the size of the digital universe will double every two years.

## CHAPTER 8: Why IoT Matters in Security

The proliferation of sensors will increase the data available for decision-making and improve quality of outcomes. IoT technologies have made these sensors better, cheaper, and easier to use than ever before. The temptation is to put them everywhere. The IoT devices, cameras, detection systems, and net or Bluetooth provide all types of data highly relevant for analysis. We can use API-based software platforms to talk to all these new sources. Every time a Wi-Fi device connects to one of these networks, its unique MAC address² can be recorded. In a centralized monitoring environment, all of these MAC addresses can be collected and analyzed. Analysis might be focused on consumer behavior questions like “has this person ever been in our coffee shop before, and do they visit any others?” These data could also be used to answer risk management questions or as forensic evidence after an incident: “who was there when the murder took place?” That would surely kill off the whole “whodunit” TV genre.

## CHEAPER, SMALLER, AND MORE

Motion detection electronics are a great example of IoT products that can now be manufactured for well under $10. They inexpensive but also easy to retrofit, unlike the complex industrial controls that preceded them. Once installed, they can drive additional data into our risk management equations—if we have systems that can take advantage of them. The best bet for seeing these systems emerge is in the cloud, with incremental analytic capability being very inexpensive to supply.

The rise of IoT is further driving the ability to track motion and presence by making Bluetooth and Wi-Fi an “always on” choice for most smartphone users, flip side to our discussion of mobility and presence earlier. Both of radio protocols require the smartphone to periodically transmit small amounts of data to announce themselves and coordinate with other radios in the area. These radio transmissions from the smartphone (or other connected device) allow the device (although not necessarily the user) to be identified and present within range of the base. In networks with multiple access points, data can be combined to triangulate the smartphone’s position on a floor plan. This data can then be fed into spatial display systems or analyzed to understand movement paths, dwell times, and whether a particular device has been seen in the area before.

It is projected by 2018 90% of smartphones will be equipped with Bluetooth capabilities and 45% are already using it in North America¹. Smart watches, car audio systems, free headphones, Internet tethering, and many Bluetooth-enabled home devices drive more smartphone users to turn on Bluetooth and forget about it. Smartphone makers like Apple are ever surpassing the choice by automatically turning Bluetooth with each iOS update.

Even for users who turn off Bluetooth, Wi-Fi has become an essential tool to avoiding expensive cellular data plans with streaming video and bandwidth-intensive mobile applications. A recent survey, for example, found 76% of consumers connect to Wi-Fi outside the home on a regular basis¹.
