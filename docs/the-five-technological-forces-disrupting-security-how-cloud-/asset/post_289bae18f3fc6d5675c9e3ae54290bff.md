# DEPLOYMENT MODELS: PUBLIC CLOUDS VS PRIVATE CLOUDS

The final dimension of the NIST cloud computing model relevant to physical security solutions concerns deployment models. They describe which individuals or groups can use a particular software or infrastructure under what conditions.

As the name suggests, public clouds are services offered to the public at large. Think of the phone system or wireless mobility services or cable television. Customers create an account, agree to a contract or terms of service, and cloud service begins to flow. Customers don't need to be a member of any special group or get any prior authorization from anyone. The service provider designs the service with the goal of signing up as many people as possible and makes it available to anyone with an Internet connection.

Private clouds, on the other hand, are just what their name imply: used only by people or institutions with prior authorization. Most often, private clouds are created by corporations to host applications or data to be used in a conventional data center. They move to a private cloud to take advantage of the economic benefits and flexibility of the cloud, but without being exposed to the public at large. Often, providers use the same technology to access private clouds as for public clouds: web browsers, mobile apps, etc. However, the services hide behind corporate firewalls, VPN access, or other security techniques that restrict them to authorized individuals.

Using a private cloud service, by contrast, forces a configuration burden on each and every mobile instance.

Last and perhaps most importantly, only public cloud deployments ever scale to the size needed to enjoy the economies of scale that deliver the cost advantages of cloud computing.

# The Tipping Point

The year 2015 marked the first time the security industry saw a conference dedicated to the topic of cluding computing for physical security applications. *Security Systems News* organized and hosted the Cloud+ conference, symbolically convened in the heart of Silicon Valley. With this conference, they came back to repeat history for a new generation of technology. The large and enthusiastic turnout for this first-of-kind conference signaled that the tipping point had arrived for cloud computing in physical security.

Given the industry's many years of reluctance, public doubts, and even open criticism of cloud computing, how did the industry reach this point? What had changed by 2015 that led most polls to indicate that the cloud could be considered a permanent part of the industry's solution set?

# THE DRIVERS OF ADOPTION

Customers have become accustomed to the conveniences and benefits of cloud computing for their personal use, and they wanted it in their professional systems. In that domain, most customers asked if any of the tough questions they brought to the office when evaluating the feasibility of cloud-based computing for their businesses. Instead, they simply accepted that their bank, their broker, their online shopping, their backup file storage, their family photos, and even their social lives could and should be conducted via the cloud.

Customers wanted convenience, ease of use, and lower IT costs than what traditional solutions could deliver. Customers had learned from examples in HR, finance, and customer relationship management (CRM) that applications work on any browser, anytime, anywhere. # A BALANCED VIEW OF CYBER RISKS

The biggest hurdle for cloud computing had always been pervasive doubts about cyber security. This challenge was not unique to the security industry. In fact, cyber security has remained the prevalent reason for corporations' reluctance to move to the cloud. If physical security professionals shared this concern with their IT brethren, they hardly stood out. Few wanted to be early adopters within their own peer group, regardless of what similar groups in other market segments might be doing.

Between 2010 and 2015, however, the software industry as a whole made strides in comforting customers over the actual versus perceived risks of cloud computing. The steady stream of press articles detailing how and more large corporations were shifting parts of their business operations over the cloud helped. Fortune 500 stalwarts announced new cloud initiatives and made them central to their new advertising campaigns. The biggest investment success stories all concerned cloud companies. There was a common thread across all of these stories that cloud computing had become safe enough for a company's most precious data.

The federal government's advocacy also played a huge role in convincing industry. The shot around the world was GSA, under CIO Casey Coleman, announced in December 2010 that it was moving its email system over the cloud. Claiming to reduce inefficiencies and lower costs by half, the shift told government contractors and system integrators that the cloud was now an approved technology. Within the year, spearheaded by the Obama administration’s first CIO, Vivek Kundra, the government put forward in February 2011 a cloud computing strategy that made “cloud first” a policy mandate for all computing.

Given that the government had previously operated under the labyrinth requirements of the Federal Information Security Management Act (FISMA), the emerging attitude appeared to be “If it is good enough to federal enough for us.” With this last holdout embracing a pro-cloud policy, doubters had nowhere left to hide.
