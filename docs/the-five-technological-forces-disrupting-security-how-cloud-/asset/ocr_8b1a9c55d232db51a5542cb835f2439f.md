Studies indicate that only 1% of digital information is analyzed. The rest wastes away as dark data; its value never unlocked. That’s more than a unfortunate irony, especially for security data. Security organizations and service providers have made huge investments in data collection but less in its analysis. It’s a classic case of getting the ball down to the 1-yard line but not into the end zone.

Electronic security systems have always produced a lot of data. Every time a door is opened, a camera detects motion, or a new visitor crosses a threshold, our systems capture its digital record. While our ability to store all these data have been growing for decades, IoT sensors are driving data production to flood levels. Vast numbers of inexpensive sensors produce more data than ever before. Advances in speed and processing power of GPUs have made it possible to track more activity in any given video stream

The state of the art is based on user interface conventions that have been around for a long time in physical security, network administration, SCADA, and similar applications that to visually represent large quantities of real-time information. Users can watch graphs and other dashboard widgets that reveal trends, warn of crossing over predefined limits, and generally summarize low-level data points into coherent. For those who really want to dig in, it’s always possible to watch filtered events scroll by one at a time as they occur (not recommended). All of these systems produce reports, some predefined and allowing a greater degree of customization. In all in, we have a lot of output from these systems, but are they telling what we want to know?

In many cases, no.

Many organizations buy security systems and then never look at the data. Watching graphs, charts, and streams of events on a display is tedious, and noteworthy events are rare. With high ted and low reward, the mind wanders. The crux of the problem with the state of the art: we’ve done a great job of deploying systems and collecting data but less so at applying data science in a way that reveals what we want to know. Our work is only half done. The good news is that there’s an opportunity for improvement and the tools to do the job.

Algorithms that identify important events and warning signs can now sift the data we’ve been expecting our security personnel to interpret. One of the most important things algorithms can do is to improve the signal-to-noise ratio in our data. Algorithms that incorporate artificial intelligence or machine learning may make judgments about what is happening in a given scenario and provide us with a predigested conclusion we can act on.
