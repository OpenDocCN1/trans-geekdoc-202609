## THE EMERGENCE OF DATA ANALYTICS IN SECURITY

Physical security will follow the path of these leading industries as the cost of analytics to decline and the need for better information continues to grow. Providers in this new market will supply a variety of data products and services to security organizations, suppliers, system integrators, and external interests such as law enforcement and insurance.

The primary means of data aggregation for these new data services will be via cloud-based security applications. On-premise systems are unlikely to play a role because they cannot capture sufficient volumes or varieties of data from a single-user installation. The distribution of analytic products will be automated via the web or via professional service engagements for specialized needs.

These new data products will focus on a variety of analytics:

- Norms and benchmarks for individual families
- Industry and customer trend analysis
- Advisory services for customers
- Neighborhood, regional, and national statistics

## WHERE ARE THE SOLUTIONS?

The question then becomes: where can I find complete big data solutions for physical security? Who are the software vendors that will be creating solutions truly ready to use?

Unfortunately, as of this writing, my research turns up no big data products for the commercial security market. There are, however, point solutions that attack specific problems with data analytics. Point-of-sale transactions coupled with surveillance video generate data sets for detecting fraud and theft for cash register transactions. Some video analytics techniques look like big data problems when applied at scale. They are divorced from the other security data streams in the enterprise, and they act only on a single organization's security data, limiting their ability to learn from larger data sets.

It is hard to imagine big data solutions for commercial security will emerge as traditional on-premise licensed software. Instead, they are far more likely to be launched cloud services. To put this in context, this is not a prediction for the security industry. There are virtually no new categories of software emerging under the enterprise licensing model in any industry. In finance, not in CRM, not in ERP, not in retail, not in health care, and not even in so-called desktop software. They are all being created or converted to cloud-based SaaS products.

The same is true for the analytics that will power big data solutions for physical security. IBM, a leader in this space, offers its Watson analytics engine as a SaaS service. AWS provides dozens of big data components and solutions, all available on a monthly subscription basis. Even companies that have been traditional leaders in licensed analytics software are transitioning to SaaS delivery models. This shift shows the way forward for big data solutions in physical security.

These cloud platforms show where products emerge, but they are just tools. They need to be shaped into useful instruments for the security market. New applications will arrive from a number of different providers, such as

- existing security solutions that add analytics as an option,
- start-ups with general analytics applied to specific verticals,
- custom development for very large end users or industries,
- segment-specific solutions for, for example, critical infrastructure,
- residential crossovers that already aggregate big data.

## BIG DATA, NOW ARRIVING IN THE CLOUD

Until recently, most security systems were isolated computing platforms that served a single home or a single business. The same was true for video surveillance. These systems all produced isolated puddles of data. This meant that any analysis performed on these data sets could not benefit from patterns or norms discovered in much larger data sets spanning thousands or millions of locations.

In the first section of this book, we explained how the pooling of resources allow cloud service providers to increase efficiency and reduce customer costs by spreading them over large sets of users. This approach not only only to the fundamental service but also to add-ons—that might be layered on top of the core security subscription. In this model, a customer might see a charge of $100 a month for analytic services, rather than an upfront expense of $100,000 plus annual software maintenance of 15% or more of the purchase price.

Lowering the costs of analytics is relatively easy. Centralizing data will be a bigger challenge. This issue is fundamental to the development of a big data capability in security products. To get, today's data puddles will have to coalesce into a lake or an ocean where there are enough data to drive the analytics market we see in other industries.

SaaS systems for physical security are therefore far more likely than their on-premise forebears to be the first place that advanced analytics will appear in the industry. This means that we can have large data sets for training machine intelligence algorithms and that cost can spread out over a larger set of users. What's more, the industry can begin to move to large-scale pattern recognition and machine learning.

## DATA PRIORITIES

In security, we have many sources of data, but it may not be the right data for a big data project. The types of data you might need and the types of data you can afford to collect vary greatly based on the overall policy view of the security mission, which in turn will establish priorities for physical security.

- What data sources will be used, and how are they created?
- What is the quality and statistical validity of the data?
- How will the data be classified?
- Is this a machine-learning problem? An AI problem?
- How will the resulting model be validated?
- What are the consequences if my model is wrong?
- Will the model operate on real-time data?

## THE LACK OF STANDARDS FOR SECURITY EVENTS

One of the biggest challenges facing any big data implementation in the physical security domain is the lack of data standards. Today, nearly every security manufacturer and software developer store data in their own proprietary formats. For your security team, this means you're effectively on your own unless your security mission is cooperating with you or provides documentation and access to either a file system, SDK, or API. Some do, and some don't. Many consider data models to be proprietary information, not for public release, or even sharing with customers.
