
# THE ROAD TO VOLUME

Electronic sensors have been around for a long time in many industries. Initially, they could accomplish a great deal with simple technology. Think of the humble switch that detects whether a door is open or a $5 motion sensor that detects movement in a room. Each of these introduced a night-and-day difference in remote surveillance. Video surveillance systems are benefiting from the same type of analytic capability that is enabling computer vision for self-driving cars. These are now capable of tracking dozens of objects simultaneously in a single scene. But all of these, individually and collectively, are still only a fraction of the deep intelligence we could have at our disposal within a few short years.

Don't get me wrong. I am not saying that today's technologies are ineffective. They are foundational data collection systems. We would be blind without them. What is missing is the additional layer of data analysis already transforming other industries. As a simple example, I could cite my own anticipation of the comparative energy report I receive each e-mail from my local power and utility. It tells not only how I compare with my energy usage before but also how my energy consumption stacks against several efficient and inefficient neighbors. It also (painstakingly) reminds me of what I could be doing better.

If data can tell us how energy-efficient we are, couldn't they tell how secure we are?

One reason is the structural difference between the electricity and security services are typically delivered to their respective customers. Electric distribution is centralized; most commercial security is not, with the exception of alarm services, which are only one leg of the security stool. Centralization of energy consumption data provides not only for large, regional data sets but also for an economical way to generate and distribute the higher intelligence about usage patterns. In commercial security, the economics of advanced analytics has placed these capabilities beyond security budgets. Even a single security organization could obtain reference data sets (a big problem in itself), the traditional on-premise computing model would be quite prohibitive to run the analytics software. Expensive enterprise software licenses keep ahead intelligence out of reach for most.

In the case of access control, video surveillance, and related security information management systems, the prevailing deployment scenario has been to one or more or mid-class servers inside a protected property. The cost of these servers plus software licenses for operating systems, databases, and applications totals up to a pretty penny, considering that they are only used by a single organization and, even then, usually only by a small number of users.

Asking most companies to bear the full expense of adding more enterprise software-licenses is a tough sell, particularly for constrained security budgets. Mid midsize organizations will look at a price tag over $100 a month per seat and pale at the expense.

If only there were a way to spread out the cost of these analytic software services over a larger pool of buyers, these features could be available to everyone.

Oh, wait; we have that already. It's called the cloud.

Shifting more of the computing workload for security systems to the cloud, physical security data sets are growing in size and usefulness. Previously isolated data sets for individuals are now being aggregated in multitenant SaaS systems that allow them to be analyzed collectively rather than individually. And because these data sets have enough latent information in them to worth extracting, software developers will begin to include such features in their security management platforms. Such intelligence is becoming commoditized in the cloud.

Call it "artificial intelligence as a service."

# HOW NOT TO THINK ABOUT BIG DATA

When I speak at conferences on big data in security, audiences invariably ask which big data products are for security. No offense, but this is the wrong question. It's not about products. Buyers cant evaluate software tools apart from analytic, business, and security goals. It's like asking a car salesman which is the best without describing what you intend to do with it (answer—the one's already on the lot).

In that sense, looking directly to big data sets is a case of putting the horse before the carburetor, so to speak. You don't walk into a new car showroom and start by asking to compare the transmissions in every model. You start by looking at the total package: SUV, sports car, luxury sedan, or pickup truck. Which one of these finished goods delivers the experience you want? Which one has been designed to perform that task you have in mind? You don't buy all the parts to a car and put them together. That would never work. You trust a carmaker to build a finished product that does what you want it to do.

The same is true for software solutions. Security buyers who want to leverage big data should start by searching for complete solutions, not focusing on the underlying tools. There has been an explosion in the number, variety, and sophistication of software tools related to big data, but tools alone do not solve problems. They are just components of a solution, not the solution itself. Security organizations need a finished product that does something useful on the day it is commissioned. Raw tools don’t do that out of the box. How useful are a stack of boards and a bucket of nails when what you really want is a house?

# Smarter

If big data were the ingredients to bake a cake, then data science would be the recipe. A sack of flour and a dozen eggs are no good if you don't know what to do with them.

Lastly, the term data science enjoys nearly as much attention as big data itself, and with good reason. Data is the methodology—the math and the algorithms—for extracting the information hidden in raw data. Data insights don't grow on trees more than other scientific breakthroughs. They arise through specific processes applied under the scientific method. In the immortal words of Matt Damon in The Martian, you have to “science the shit out of it."

# WHAT DO WE WANT TO KNOW?

There is a popular misconception that big data systems provide automatic insights; they can ingest a bunch of data and simply tell us what is going on. There is a little bit of truth to that, but only a. In the world of data science, any automatic insights are broadly known as unsupervised learning. It works for some types of problems, but not for everything, and even then, it requires judgment, fine-tuning, and a sense of the overall goals of the investigation. It's probably a better wager that big data systems for physical security will only provide answers to questions we've asked. We will need to teach them what is important and what we want to know. It's not mind reading, after all.

This puts the onus on us to decide what matters and to express it as an algorithmically solvable problem. With cyber security, there are parts of this task that fall to all of the stakeholders in the security ecosystem. Software or SaaS providers will conduct market research to find out what security organizations would like to know. Or they might feel that they have great ideas and forge ahead, hoping they'll be useful. Security organizations will need to be vocal about the great unsolved problems they think big data could help, may need to change their thinking about cloud platforms and where they will store their data.
