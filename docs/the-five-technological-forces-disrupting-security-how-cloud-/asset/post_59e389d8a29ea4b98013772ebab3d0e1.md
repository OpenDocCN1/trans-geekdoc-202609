
# CHAPTER 20: The Secrets of the Universe

Outside of security, social log-in has a healthy degree of adoption as a single identity that can be used across multiple contexts. Some 80% of us have used social log-in to create new accounts on the Internet and subsequently log in through the same mechanism. However, it is unclear whether this mechanism will retain power in the face of mobile log-ins that use biometrics (e.g., fingerprints) as credentials for an increasing number of apps.

Summarizing all of these factors, social is rated as innovator or early adopter (aka visions) for both residential and commercial uses. The bottom line is that social log-in has not crossed the chasm to mass adoption—least not for security applications. My guess is that it will end up being stranded a while longer.

But it's not all bad news for social in security. Recall that social does have a growing presence in public safety and mass notifications, as well as personal safety check-ins not related to electronic security. In these cases, the very properties of social networks that make them a little messy for directed communications actually make them useful for fuzzy notifications to perhaps reach more than intended. The information bleeds from “Friends” to “friends of friends” that is what you want when you are screaming “fire!” in a crowded theater. You want the people in the lobby to hear and get out.

# MY GENERATION

There's no way to say this without the risk of ageism, but some aspects of adoption are simply a generational. Young people get it; old people don't. Today, means millennials are the primary vehicles that will drive the five forces for the rest of the way into the market. This truth has a major factor in my experience of how and where they have shaped the industry.

One of the clearest examples comes from my own sales team who spend most of their time with the integrator community. They can pitch to a mouthful of commercial sales reps that cloud and IoT are the way forward for security services and devices. At one desk sits an integrator veteran who proclaims with confidence and bluster, “My customers will never use that technology. There's no way they will store their data off-site, and no way they will pay a penny fee.” At the next desk sits a fresh-faced college grad who, upon hearing the patch, says “Yeah, I get it. That is everything now.”

What you can depend on what you believe, which is why the younger reps with no negative bias toward new technology are usually more successful representing these new technologies. What's true at the individual level is also true at the company level, with newer system integrators staking their business on being 100% cloud-based.

# A NEW MISSION FOR SECURITY

I have found that people in the security industry are deeply committed to making life safer and better for their employees, their customers, and the public at large. Every one of the professionals I work with knows that if they don't do their job right, a company could lose valuable property, an entrepreneur could lose her small business, or someone could lose life and limb. They know that the difference between getting it right and getting it wrong is a classroom of children. This makes our careers a very personal and meaningful contribution to society as a whole.

The industry is all about products and professions that literally make a life-or-death difference in the daily lives of real people going about the basics of work and family. In an age of increasing terrorism and violence, they are no longer optional. They are essential to living without undue fear. They are essential to ensuring that violence touches as few as possible.

Even so, an odd thing happens when people start evaluating the risks versus the cost of protection. They start to wonder if it's really worth spending the money on systems designed to prevent low-likelihood events. We see this all the time. People hear about burglaries in their neighborhood, and they call for an estimate on a security system. A few weeks go by with no more bad news, and then they decide they don't need it all. An ex-employee who still has the keys to the office and steals a computer or two, and the business owner decides it's time for electronic security. A month goes by, the worry dies down, and with no security, the entire staff is still vulnerable to the next disgruntled ex-employee—who may do far worse than walk off with a laptop.

We forget about the squeaky wheel as it stops squeaking.
To get beyond, security product and service providers would do well to keep in a broader mind that enables the benefits to be obvious even when nothing is going wrong. As the five technologies transforming security deliver on their promise, all of us will be better able to accomplish that goal.

# WHAT GOES AROUND COMES AROUND

A funny coincidence just happened, literally as I was writing the closing chapter of this book. I received an unsolicited e-mail from a young entrepreneur who had launched a company that provides a secure, cloud-based, connected mailbox. They don't call it a SmartBox, but they might as well. Its purpose is to keep package deliveries safe until the owner uses access credentials to open the box and remove them. It is clearly an IoT device, and naturally, it's mobile and social. They probably have big plans for big data.

I don't know if they will succeed, but I hope they do. Maybe it's time has come.
But if they don't—who knows, they just might invent something that lives to disrupt another day.
