
# The Toothbrush Test

Until very recently, our industry’s apps have been targeted at the relatively small cadre of security managers in our ecosystems, not for all the other stakeholders. They have inadvertently excluded all the employees, tenants, guests, visitors, contractors, customers, residents, members, and dozens of classifications for people who pass through our security perimeters. In other words, much of our technology has not included the people we protect for their partnership. But all we have to do is to put applications in their hands; once we do, commercial security applications will be in a position to benefit from the same interactive revolution that produced Web 2.0. We can think of it as Security 2.0, although I hesitate to use that term because it has already been coopted for dozens of other products and concepts.

Instead, let's call it “interactive security."

# THE TOOTHBRUSH TEST

Interactive security revolves around mobile apps. Since the rise of user-generated content, mobile apps have gained the lion's share of the time we spend connected. That's why interactive security will debut on mobile rather than on the desktop. It will make security dynamic and interactive like the rest of the web. To see this, you need only look at any one of the startups that have taken on mobile security and authentication in the past 5 years. Their advertising always shows a mobile app, front and center. If the medium is the message, mobile is the messenger.

True to however, the commercial security products still lag behind. What has been missing is the vehicle to make it happen: a security application so people want to use it every day.

Cue mobile credentials.

People love them. They pass what Larry Page calls the “toothbrush test”: People must use an app at least twice a day, or it won’t be sticky enough to become a habit. Those that fail the toothbrush test join the 84% of downloaded apps abandoned within a few weeks. To recurring revenue companies, that spells attrition. To their customers, it foretells a future of apps that receive no further development funding and get any better.

Mobile credentials pass the toothbrush test in being indispensable for people to get into a building they frequent. They also succeed because users like them better than access keys and fobs, which always seem to get lost at the worst possible time—like when you parked your car in the underground garage of your office building and can't get back in after happy hour. You can think of mobile credentials as benign Trojan horses; let’s call them

“Trojan puppies.” Once people pick them up and start playing with them, they never let them go. Only later do they discover that the apps can do much more.

# RETENTION MATTERS

There is a lively debate on user retention rates for desktop versus mobile applications. As we already noted, desktops have been declining to the tune of double digits per year. Surveys of all mobile applications reveal that mobile apps lose over 80% of their daily average users (DAUs) within the first week after download. Keep in mind that these numbers include the free and frivolous apps that clog and clutter mobile milieu. Even so, retention rates are nearly dismal for mobile business apps that take all of 90 days to shed the same share of subscribers. If any of our businesses relied on retention rates like these, we would be in line at the soup kitchen.

However, well-designed mobile apps that deliver results embed themselves in our lives ways desktop applications could never achieve. You can't check your desktop 2617 times a day. Smartphones are our constant companions, our second brains, and the digital dossiers for more elements of our identities than any other artifact of modern life. They have become part of our lifestyle, which matters for us because security is, in part, a matter of lifestyle.

In the perennial tug-of-war between convenience and precaution, reinforcing desired security behaviors works best if they are similar to how people would normally act otherwise. Want your people to always carry ID? Put it in a phone. Want your people to say something when they see something? Put it in a phone. Want your people to participate in your security program? Put it in a phone.

In this context, creating security applications that pass the toothbrush test is far more crucial than simply solving the adoption/abandonment hurdle. It also plays into the one business metric of every subscription service in the industry: customer attrition, or its inverse, customer retention. For those who are on the buying side of this equation, the temptation to think that these metrics only matter to suppliers. Instead, view retention statistics as a measure of how well a product serves your needs. They are a proxy for your satisfaction as a customer. When your suppliers have high retention rates, they're doing a good job for you at a good price.

Retention leads back to one of our major themes: the consumerization of commercial security and its role in the choices we make for security investments. Currently, over 80% of enterprises have invested in mobile technology for their own staff. Near half invest in mobile as a way to increase customer satisfaction and retention. What does this tell us? The preference for mobile will drive commercial security toward consumerized products, albeit on the coattails of the residential space. More than any of the other technologies we examine in this book, mobile will become the face of the security organization to its constituents.

Moreover, application licenses—once a hideout per-seat affair in the desktop-days—now skew toward free (or at least "freemium") on mobile platforms. Version update cycles have ratched down from annual to quarterly to monthly and even weekly. Cross-platform compatibility on multiple operating systems has subverted the hegemony of the PC world order. While good for security organizations on count, they also pave the way for broader participation in the security agenda.

# Security Ecosystems

In biology, an ecosystem includes organisms interacting with each other and their environment. Decades of great nature programming on TV (and great biology teachers) have taught us to think of frogs and plants and waterways in terms of a whole. A technology ecosystem functions similarly: a collection of physical devices with software, the communications pathways that connect them, and the humans who, until now, provide the bulk of the interactions between them.

Our mobile ecosystem for security applications contains all of these elements. The physical "species" in this microcosm include smartphones, tablets, and autonomous IoT devices that can interact with our mobile handsets. The software "organisms" are the host operating systems plus all of the apps that inhabit them. Originally, the "nervous system" just ran on voice channels to cell towers and the telephone network. Now, it includes cellular channels and Wi-Fi for their immediate vicinity. Soon, we will add to the diversity with low-power wide area networks (LPWANs) that provide low bandwidth over long distances with little power consumption.

Any discussion of mobile security apps is a discussion about this ecosystem. Our security applications cohabit smartphones alongside undocumented apps bagged by their owners in the world. The radio environment is rife with opportunities for any device to hook up with any other—or dozens at a time—to unknown effect. In any complex system, the permutations are almost endless.

On the plus side, apps like Google Authenticator can give us secure two-factor authentication at virtually no cost to us or our developers. Social networking apps provide communication and notification channels that would take a security organization to duplicate (if ever), even for a small community of stakeholders. And the ability of Bluetooth to connect directly to physical security provides immediacy and resiliency that can't be duplicated by Internet-mediated connections.
