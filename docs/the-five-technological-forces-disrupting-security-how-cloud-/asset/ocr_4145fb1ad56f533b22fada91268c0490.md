Civil libertarians have worried about the same eventuality, characterizing it more as a “mark of the beast” than a coolly efficient management technique. Against this backdrop, the history of a single universal identifier is a tug-of-war between the will to organize and the we to maintain privacy.

In the United States, social security cards famously brandish the words “not for identification.” First issued in the wake of the Social Security Act of 1935, the cards were originally intended as account numbers for tracking contributions and benefits due under the new legislation. Some seem to be for many other purposes and over the years they have become a de facto universal ID for establishing banks accounts, credit cards, health insurance, and many others unrelated to social security. This widespread use has come at a price, giving identity thieves a single key to unlock access to your financial persona and health records, among other things.

Oracle CEO Larry Ellison was widely derided in the early 2000s for his role in advocating a national ID card program that would have required all Americans to carry a new form of universal identification that included a photograph and biometric identifier. Regarded as a transparent attempt to insert Oracle products into the center of virtually every financial, commercial, and legal transaction, the initiative failed because advocates of stripes that would erode fundamental freedoms and paved the path for widespread surveillance of innocent citizens. Mind you, this was before Edward Snowden revealed the full ramifications of the Patriot Act and the extensive use of the NSA’s PRISM system, which demonstrated that our identities are easily reconstructed from many digital breadcrumbs.

In 2011, the National Institute of Science and Technology (NIST) launched a program known as NSTIC, the National Strategy for Trusted Identities in Cyberspace, to improve privacy, security, and convenience of online transactions. The creators of the program envisioned an identity ecosystem that would provide better authentication for participating individuals and businesses interacting with each other. Such an ecosystem would use powerful, verifiable digital credentials commonly used under the prevailing log-in plus password word model that (unfortunately) governs e-commerce and online exchanges. The NIST program ultimately awarded $9 million to fund pilot programs that demonstrated these principles. As with most initiatives, the NIST program faced criticism from privacy watchdog.
