# THE CONSUMERIZATION OF SECURITY

The evolution of the SmartBox into an access control system provides both a hopeful and cautionary tale to the entire security industry. Helpful because innovation is always welcome, cautionary because you never know where or when disruption will appear. Commercial security is a relatively small industry, serving highly specialized markets. Until recently, the thought of consumer products infiltrating these markets was not especially worrisome. New security products don't usually come out of left field and disrupt the market way that smartphones do to cameras or the cloud to enter enterprise computing. I expect we will be seeing more of it. I say as someone who was part of doing this, if only by pure serendipity.

Emerging IoT products in residential automation and security have shown that they can do a better job at a lower cost than many legacy systems designed years ago. No surprise, the steady march of new technology replacing old. Even so, virtually every industry has a tribal complacency that says "it can't happen to us." And yet, for every one of them, we can find abundant examples to show that anything and everything can be disrupted: health care, automobiles, energy, insurance, investment, and logging. Even such incredibly physical enterprises as taxis and transportation are not immune, as amply demonstrated by Uber, Lyft, and others.

The consumerization of IT describes the effect of new technology emerging in the consumer market and moving into the workplace. For employees who would rather their more highly evolved personal electronics than aging, company-issued relics are the carriers of this trend. All of these products such as smartphones, tablets, and mobile apps set the bar for the tools used in a professional setting, and the cycles begin again. In the short span of time since the web has become a way of life, we have seen this time and again. Consumer technology experiences raise expectations for workplace technology, and the innovators who are able to clear the new bar are winners. User interfaces on products, for example, have tracked the design insights and interventions that first appeared in consumer applications. Tablets for work and play are one and the same. Consumer popularity drives professional adoption.

The debt to consumer technology goes far beyond what is possible with our eyes and clickable by our thumbs: it goes right to the core of what's possible. We would

# EARLY CLOUDS, SUNNY SKIES

The SmartBox went from a front-porch convenience to an electronic security fixture almost overnight—if you count 6 months of nonstop overnight. The web app spawned a new user interface designed for physical access control workflows; under the hood, it retained its original cloud architecture. The cloud back end was designed to serve tens of consumers making online purchases and checking for deliveries while behind the scenes processing transactions with smart lockboxes. This mission demanded a robust platform that could serve every shipper who valued delivery and every shipper who valued customer satisfaction.

That was a challenge.

At the time, there was nothing of this scale that operated as both a web service for customers and a distributed transaction platform for millions of smart devices across a geography the size of the United States. There were large e-commerce sites like Amazon, which was already doing a billion dollars a year at the time. And there were precursors like the well-established credit card networks that interacted with millions of point-of-sale terminals on their own private networks. But there was no knowing of combined interactivity of a website with a machine-to-machine (M2M) distributed access network. The SmartBox service is needed to be available to anyone with a browser, process millions of transactions per hour, and most importantly—be secure. After all, the promise of security was the core value of the business.

I worked on consumer-scale systems in previous lives, and I was confident that web technology could support this vision. I was equally confident that an enterprise architecture approach wouldn't stand a chance. My days as a consultant had seen enough Fortune 500 companies stuck in that morass, spending years to disentangle complex enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems before they could support a customer-facing web business. The critical

difference was software multitenancy, a system architecture that allows all your customers to interact with a single instance of software. Salesforce.com was an early and articulate evangelist of this fundamental design principle, and we were right there with them.

In terms of a business model, it was clear that a secure delivery service would have to be sold like most cloud services are today—as a monthly subscription that people could pay for when they were receiving value. This was not a radical concept, even then, but it was nowhere near as prevalent as it is now. To understand the leap of faith investors made with this model, remember that there was less broadband than dial-up Internet; Amazon only sold books by paper, Netflix still delivered DVDs by mail, and it would be 6 years before Spotify sold music as a service. Most of the world still stood rooted in the one-time sales model, and recurring services were testing many waters.

The SmartBox brought the cloud into physical security by way of consumer logistics. Once the reengineering was done, we thought half the battle was over. The cloud eventually, including this new world of access control we had just discovered. And within the next 10 years, most things did: banking, technology, entertainment, mail, chat, photography, books, transportation, pet care, hospitality, even pizza, and hundreds that would fill this page. In short, almost everything except security had stubbornly chosen to follow a longer route. The clouds are breaking (ahem), and the more forward-looking companies in the industry have begun to pay catch-up.

People, if you didn't get the memo, we're all going to the cloud.

# AN IoT STORY

At the turn of the millennium, less than 3% of all Americans had broadband at home. In an environment of such patchwork connectivity, it was far from certain that homeowners understood the power of a smart appliance—or what would be called an Internet of things (IoT) device. It needed an always-on Internet connection and an always-up cloud service, both of which were the experience of the vast majority of consumers. It is difficult to imagine how a novel connected device was at the time, but they were so rare that there wasn't an agreed upon category to describe them. The term IoT had just recently been coined in 1999, but no one had heard it, and it enjoyed none of the buzzword status that it has acquired. People were delighted by the early IoT technologies that seemed to inch us toward a future like The Jetsons. They were magical and dazzling.

- Electronic sensors monitor their environment.
- Digital transmission of sensor data.
- Remote control of electromechanical systems.
- Centralized computing systems that knit whole networks of sensors.
- Algorithmic responses to predefined criteria.
- User interfaces to access humans

Many of the technical details differ from today's IoT, to be, but the underlying concepts and relationships are remarkably similar. Sensors were larger and used phone lines than the Internet. Digital transmission was slower and had a much smaller vocabulary. Electromechanical systems were less intricate. The hub of the network was at central stations staffed by trained operators. The algorithms were deterministic rather than self-educating. But they got the job done all the.
