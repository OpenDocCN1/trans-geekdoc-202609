# BIG SECURITY DATA AND ROI

There are two fundamental criteria that face every big data project, and they will look familiar to anyone who has tried to justify an IT project or any corporate spending initiative. The first is whether your data sources will support the objectives of your project. You will need to have the right kind of data, enough of it, and be able to trust it. If the data are not, you're wasting your time because the oldest adage in computer science still holds—garbage in out. Given the right data and the right analytics, what is the ROI you expect? What outcome would you need in order to make the investment worthwhile? In the world of security, this question can be answered in two very different ways.

In the first case—a purely financial ROI analysis—we can use a retail example. The scenario might be something like this: if Bob and Alice's Key Store invests $1 million in a big data project, they will realize that in reduced shrinkage (and how soon will they realize these savings?) Let's say, for the sake of argument, that their big data project can reduce shrinkage by 2% per year they lose $5 million worth of product every year. This means that they would save $100,000 per year, and it would take 10 years to recover their investment. It's not clear that this would be a great investment for them or they might find more economic means of accomplishing their objective. However, if their data science project could save them 10% per year then it would only take 2 years to recover their investment, and they would come out every year after that. Clearly worth it.

In the second case—a life safety ROI analysis—we are confronted immediately how to place a value on human life. If a million big data project could save one life per year, is it worth the investment? What if it's only one life every 3 years? Every 10? What about the other spending priorities? Security organizations make these difficult choices about resources and outcomes every day. Big data won't eliminate these difficult choices, but it may provide us with better insights on probabilities and risk factors so that we are optimally allocating our resources.

Risk management is the methodology to determine whether any of these approaches is worthwhile. Risk analysis is a part of many kinds of business intelligence: insurance, corporate investment, financial portfolio management, and of physical science. The fundamental principle is simple: if the probability of something bad happening times the cost it will inflict is greater than the cost to prevent it, then it is worth doing. This means that extremely low-probability events, even if they have dire consequences, are not likely to receive funding. At the other end of the scale, high-likelihood events will receive more investment, even if their individual occurrence is less costly.

From this view, data science can be seen as an alternative decision-making framework, as a supporting science that rolls up into the practice of risk management. Risk management depends heavily on data, and the cost of the adverse events we are trying to prevent. Without casting asions, a fact that risk assessment is an exercise in gut instinct and guesswork when there are no big data available. The extent to which big data can improve confidence intervals over our necessarily probabilistic assessments, it has a future in improving physical security.

Proving this case requires making a significant corporate commitment to the effort. If you are a software company providing products to the industry, this means a major product development commitment (i.e., millions of dollars) over many years. If you are a systems integrator, this means serious study of the available options that you must present to your customers. Big data can seem monolithic when you first approach it from the outside. At security conferences and in the security press, it is usually treated as if it were just one big thing—a sledgehammer to be wielded against all our unmet needs for insight and data intelligence. There is little differentiation among the many types of big data and the myriad ways it can be analyzed and used. In this respect, the conversation parallels the way that IoT is treated as one category, when the reality is it comprises many subcategories that are quite different from one another. This means we need to crack open the monolith and find out what's behind the facade. This will reveal a variety of the major kinds of things you can do with analytics once you have a big data strategy.
