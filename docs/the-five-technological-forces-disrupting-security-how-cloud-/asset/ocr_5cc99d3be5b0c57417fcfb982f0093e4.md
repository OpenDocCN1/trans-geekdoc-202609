# One Key to Rule Them All

But there were several important differences between the work of credit cards and the world of electronic access. First, the credit card industry long ago agreed on technical standards so that every credit card reader works with every credit card. This interoperability implies many layers of agreement between bankers, merchants, manufacturers, and payment processors: identically, standardized data encoding, interconnected networks, and—perhaps most importantly—agreement on who you are.¹

The world of electronic access control, for residential and commercial, lacks this degree of standardization. Users need different apps, have different user experiences, and need to master different programming procedures for every brand of equipment they encounter. Imagine if your Ford needed to go to a different gas station than your Chevrolet—to say nothing of Tesla—and you had to memorize which cars fueled up at which stations. Maddening. In fact, we’re seeing this friction with electric vehicles, played out as an excruciating chicken-or-the-egg dance between manufacturers, consumers, and infrastructure providers. We are at the same point with technical standards for smart locks, except there is barely a chicken or egg in sight.

The forging of technical standards among competing companies has always been something of a mud wrestling contest. Everyone enters the ring hoping that technology will win, and they all leave dirty and dissatisfied. But technical standards are actually the easiest part of this problem. The bigger issue is identity.

Identity has emerged as industry analysts like to call “the new perimeter.” The metaphor is apt because your identity now forms a boundary around your assets, both physical and digital. Your identity lets you in—to your bank account, for example—and keeps everyone out, unless someone steals your identity. In case, they’ve breached your perimeter and have access to your world.

In the case of buildings with access control or smart locks, your identity lets you in and tells the building owner you’ve been there. Imagine the damage and havoc someone could wreak if they stole your electronic identity and could come and go in your apartment as they pleased. That’s why each institution issues a token to each person who uses their facilities, making our key chains heavier with fobs, our wallets thicker with plastic, and—soon—our smart-phones more cluttered with apps.
