
# START-UPS SHOW UP TO THE GAME

Cloud has provided a path between buildings and their owners as Uber between riders and drivers and as Facebook among friends. This is most evident in the residential space, where numerous smart lock, smart doorbell, and whatever start-ups have recently launched. Naturally, all of these consumer-facing security services are cloud companies through-and-human. Ironically, though, they don't have to explain that to anyone. It's not even part of their advertising copy. Nobody cares. It is assumed that every new company we see is a cloud company, and we're okay with that.

Within commercial security, there has also been an increase in security start-ups making an entrance with cloud solutions, in video surveillance and increasingly, in access control. While the technology and quality differ considerably from one to the next, there were at this writing over 30-40 companies offering video surveillance as a service (VSaaS) or managed video as a service (MVaaS) offerings in the commercial space.

There are several reasons that the video sector has seen more than growth, as access control. First, almost none of the companies develop their own camera products, which means they can leverage existing products already in the market. Second, this segment of the industry has benefited from the adoption of common standards, most notably ONVIF, which permits interoperability across many cameras and storage services. Finally, video surveillance is subject to fewer jurisdictional regulations than alarm or access control services. This means that products can be used in many places without creating specific versions for each, or by the product through lengthy and expensive regulatory approval processes.

# THE INNOVATOR'S DILEMMA

Many have wondered why few, if any, of established security software providers have jumped into the cloud space. In other words, if you have a good reputation in the industry, a deep understanding of customer requirements, and a proved go-to-market strategy, why not leverage your position to offer cloud-based services?

While I was still in the consulting business, I worked with companies in other industries facing this same question. The conundrum for company management usually comes down to a combination of intertwined challenges. The first is cannibalization of existing product lines. The old guard within the company actively opposes disruptive product developments if they threaten cash cows. For example, passenger railroad companies stayed clear of the airline business, figuring such a move would take business from their established business model.

Is this ever a good strategy? Well, when you're tempted to protect your cash cows, ask yourself: how do you travel cross-country?

While this new-versus-old dynamic can happen with any two products competing for the same customers, it has been played out many times by advocates of a cloud product going up against an earlier generation of technology. Of course, with other companies busy developing new (cloud) products giving the customers what they want, then eternal cannibalization is inevitable. You should always cannibalize yourself before someone else does for you.

The second dynamic at play around established products is Harvard professor Clayton Christensen's well-known Innovator's Dilemma. He explains that established companies focus too much on customers' needs within their existing product lines and fail to adopt new and potentially disruptive technologies that will eventually serve their customers' needs better. Coupled with the large, decade-plus development and investment time between established products, it's difficult to dislodge the incremental improvement mind-set. To succeed, you need to convince a board of directors that the company should abandon what has been a moneymaker for many years and instead try something new and risky.

Good luck pitching that to your boss.

# DISRUPTION

Cloud computing is the most disruptive force to the industry since digital dialer was introduced in the 1970s. Ten years from now, we will look back and see that the cloud technology bifurcated the industry into have and have-nots. The have will be those who adopted the cloud, from their inception as modern startups, or as industry players who had the sense to see what was coming and reinvent themselves and their products. The have-nots will be those, burdened by inertia of older solutions, fall victim to the innovator's dilemma, never loosening their grip on legacy technologies or opening their wallets quite enough to invest in the future. This will be a classic case of disruption, carving along the lines of those who made the transition and those who don't.

The biggest effect will be on the manufacturers and software suppliers themselves. After all, everyone who is downstream can switch to other solutions. Software providers who don't make the transition, however, will find themselves in the death spiral eroding market share, loss of key employees across sales and engineering, and customer defection.

System integrators worry about the pinch from cloud computing as customers have become piecewise movement of selected software components into the cloud. These transitions can start innocently with an organization using, let's say, a cloud-based visitor management system that runs on an iPad in the lobby. Next, an expanding organization may want to use cloud-based access control and video, but only satellite offices and new acquisitions. As this process continues, what starts looking like a rash of excitations hits a tipping point and eventually becomes the normal. In my conversations with many integrators over the years, I've learned that many consider it existential question: what's left to integrate everything is in the cloud?

Fortunately, it's not quite so bleak it sounds, but the ground has definitely shifted. Physical security is irreducible, which means there will always be equipment that needs to be installed on premise: electric strikes, wireless door lockers, cameras, elevator controls, gates, turnstiles, and trucks of sensors. Also, there is still a residue of software integration in many cases because every corporate security environment is unique, at least around the edges where it needs to join with other IT systems. But the work on the remaining IT components does not command the level of expenditure as what the old kings of the hill in security could command. In this world, system integrators in cloud-only world will find themselves on common ground with those in many verticals. The new expertise will be in configuring and managing increasingly cloud-based solutions. In fact, customers ranging from small business owners to large enterprises will be the beneficiaries of this change.

# WHAT IS MY CLOUD STRATEGY?

People always ask me when is a good time to move their security systems to the cloud. My answer can be summed up by an old proverb: The best time to have planted a tree is 20 years ago; the second best time is right now.

In a time where the benefits of on-premise systems have flattened out and cloud-based systems are adding value rapidly, every day of delay is a day of throwing good after. One illustration of this principle is planning the use of big data tools to improve your security posture. I discuss in more detail later, big data tools will emerge within cloud-based systems, not on-premise systems. If you begin to accumulate data with a well-chosen cloud provider today, then as security-specific big data applications emerge over the next several years, you will be in a position to apply them to your historical data and begin learning from it immediately. If, on the other hand, you wait to become a late adopter, you will have deprived yourself of the benefits of the early versions of those tools for the entire time you've waited.

This scenario is actually a specific example of a general technology adoption conundrum. In the fast-paced ascendancy of PCs and laptops, buyers always wondered if they should buy now or wait for next year's more powerful model with even more memory and processor speed. The reasoning remorse behind waiting had a certain amount of appeal if you thought of your possible remorse a year now from having what would be last year's model. However, that year would have been a year when you did every task more slowly than you could have with an earlier decision to upgrade. That would have been a smart decision? Think of all the wasted productivity.

To close out with two more pro to frame this dilemma: are you in a place where you should look before you leap or in one where he who hesitates is lost? Your call.
