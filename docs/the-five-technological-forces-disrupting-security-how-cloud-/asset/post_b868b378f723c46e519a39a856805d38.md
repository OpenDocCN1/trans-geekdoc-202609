Anomaly detection is also now becoming a possibility for physical security systems that can harness the power of big data and cloud computing together.

Anomaly detection is a classification problem. For example, in the case of three shoes and a banana, it's the fruit that is different. In that case, it seems obvious that you can tell by inspection how and why one element in the data set is different from the others. We know this because we have preexisting classifications for everything in the data set: footwear and fruits. In real-world data sets, there may be no obvious classification scheme in place. Normal and abnormal are relative terms, and what's abnormal one day can be the next. This is why fixed classification may work for some problems, but not for others. Big data sets are more challenging because a classification scheme that is even off will end up producing an unacceptable number of false positives or negatives. Fine-tuning is of the essence.

Analytics are catching up with anomaly in big data with the same kinds of learning techniques we've discussed elsewhere in this section. Fine-tuning is beyond the reach of human observers with data sets this large, so we need to rely on machine learning to do it. If we go back to our IoT example of capturing smartphone signatures from Bluetooth and Wi-Fi signals and imagine using this technique in a large public place, it's clear that the data volume and patterns will exceed anyone's guess about what to look for. Algorithms could quickly spot anomalous behavior, like lingering when everyone is moving or moving when everyone is standing. Neither of these may be worrisome in its own right, but they can help focus our attention for further investigation.

The relevance of this high-volume, high-velocity analytics for physical security is increasing as inexpensive IoT sensors, ubiquitous video, and mobile tagging are multiplying at our disposal.

## Statistics

Most of us have at least a passing familiarity with the basic concepts. They are part of the everyday information we hear about the stock market, health, politics, and the price of a home mortgage, to manage a few. They provide many of the major clauses about anomalies in numerical data sets, large and small.

Mean and median—as the most fundamental statistical concepts, the two properties of a data set are the most intuitive. We use them in security on a daily basis without even thinking about it. What's the mean number of visitors in our building? What's the median number of alarms we see in a given day? These values are very easy to calculate and have been a fundamental part of every spreadsheet, reporting tool, and business intelligence system in our arsenal. They help us understand whether our present circumstances are deviating from the norm, and if so, by how much. They lend themselves to graphic representation, which makes them visually understandable in the blink of an eye. They are equally at home on any display medium and translate well to small-screen mobile apps where security interactions are moving every day. For many classes of problems, basic calculations provide an excellent starting point: a way of orienting oneself. However, they don't tell us much about causality. They are a blunt instrument, like an alarm bell. Sometimes, though, an alarm is all you need.

Security applications of these basic concepts include the following:

- Is the number of alarms today above or below the mean?
- How much traffic do we have compared with the average?
- What is the mean number of invalid credentials presented at our building?
- Do my properties compare with each other?

Regression and trending—a single number like a mean or median is not enough to tell the whole story. Sometimes, a set of data needs to be evaluated to see how it changes over time. Linear regression is a statistical technique for modeling the relationship between a dependent variable (Y) and one or more independent variables (X). In this mathematical model, the goal is to find a straight line (or curve) that fits a scattered set of data points on an X-Y plot. The equation for the line tells you the value of interest changes over time. With this information, you can now predict what its value might be, 3 months from now. While this method may require simplifying assumptions about the data, it can be a good approximation of a trend, and sometimes, that's all we need. The proverbial frog in the heating pot of water could use this method to see that if he doesn't jump out now, there's trouble ahead.

Security applications of regression include the following:

- Are losses going up or down over time? At what rate?
- Are alarms rising or falling?
- False alarms growing or declining as a percentage?
- Did error rates decline after we invested in new equipment?
- Did our new policy reduce the incidence of tailgating?

## Segmentation, Clustering, and Association

What captures the popular imagination about big data and machine learning is the idea that machines can find patterns in data all by themselves. This is a nearly magical and literally superhuman feat, made only slightly less magical how casually it happens in movies and television. But art often points the way to what coming in real life, and this is an example where it's right on the mark.

Three of the specific techniques behind these machine-driven insights are segmentation, clustering, and association. They are all examples of what data scientists call unsupervised learning, which is a way for machines to find patterns without direct directives on what relationships there be in the data.¹ We will look at how each of these is relevant using big data in security applications.

Segmentation is the process of dividing a large group of data into subgroups with common characteristics. A familiar example is consumer marketing, where the goal is to segment customers into subgroups that share buying patterns or preferences. Millennials, for example, are more likely than all other age groups using streaming media, so that's a great place to reach them with advertising. If you are targeting Baby Boomers, on the other hand, Facebook and other social media are a better place. Segmentation is not just for marketing. It can apply to many kinds of data sets and behaviors. Profiling is a familiar (and possibly illegal) type of segmentation that groups people by age, race, gender, or other characteristics. While the practice is rife with civil issues, the statistical basis for it is no different than what markets and counterterrorism experts use every day.

Discovering the groups for segmentation is the job of clustering algorithms. They examine across all the attributes of the members of a data set and look for groups that produce unsupervised learning plays a role. If it is already known which groupings will produce predictions about how the members of a group would behave, then on the other hand, there is just subdividing the data based on existing knowledge. If, on the other hand, there is no knowledge or even a hypothesis, clustering can help find the relationships for us. The result may be one or more ways to cluster the data that can be used for additional modeling and research.

One of the characteristics of classification algorithms is that they can be applied to problems where the data attributes are not continuous, which is to say they are not numerical. For example, the attributes of interest may be binary, such as whether or not a person is a citizen. They might also be multivalued discrete variables, such as the highest level of education achieved by a job candidate.
