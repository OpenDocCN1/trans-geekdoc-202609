# WHAT IS IoT, ANYWAY?

Ever confused by the term “IoT”?

You have good reason. In some contexts, the term is used very narrowly to call a cluster of consumer products, like personal fitness trackers, smart thermostats, or smart watches. In other contexts, it is broadly to describe almost any small computing device. The Industrial Internet of Things is again a different set of things that will enable smart factories, cities, and energy systems. In security, it usually refers to sensors and actuators tied to other building operations.

No people feel confused. It reminds me to explain the cloud in the early days. At least in IoT, you have something you can touch. If you're reading this book, you're probably at the head of the class; more than half of the United States has not even heard the term. To understand its impact, we must first understand what it is—and, just as importantly, what it is not.

IoT is not one thing or one type of thing; more than cloud computing is one application or one class of applications. The term IoT is a convenient label—most of the time—for grouping products emerging across a plethora of consumer and commercial markets. To be sure, they have many fundamental characteristics in common—small, connected, data-rich, controllable, and integrated into things—that's not where the similarities end.

By definition, IoT refers to the network of physical objects embedded with electronics, software, sensors, and connected to the Internet. The IoT ecosystem includes all of the other systems that enable us to make use of these devices, including gateways, cloud computing, analytics, dashboards, and mobile application.
