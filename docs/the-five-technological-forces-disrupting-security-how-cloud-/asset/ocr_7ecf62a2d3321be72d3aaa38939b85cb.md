Analysts point out, all of this has been going for some time. What is different is the cost and scale at which these devices can be manufactured and deployed. The cost is now low enough that computational capabilities be embedded in inexpensive products, including throwaway and single-use goods. Low costs have a direct impact on the massive scale expected for IoT devices. For security systems, this means being able to put more sensors in places than ever before.

Not to be overly academic, but it may be more accurate to say that electronic security has long been an “intelligent system” business, even though I’ll continue to use the IoT throughout the rest of this book. In contrast to smaller, less powerful devices of IoT, intelligent systems are “enabled with high-performance microprocessors, connectivity, and high-level operating systems.” This is a much closer fit to many of the components deployed in commercial security systems. Access control panels and network cameras, for example, use high-performance microprocessors and high-level operating systems such as Linux. They also generally Internet connectivity, unlike their forebears typically limited to local serial connections to a PC-based controller.

Gartner projects that by 2022, a typical home could contain more than 500 smart devices at a cost low as a dollar per. That’s a remarkable change from the world we live in today, where only a handful of our appliances and home controls are called “smart” and still command a premium price. Putting aside concerns about having every move digitized and recorded, the presence of this many new data sources in residential and commercial properties has profound displacement effects on other technologies—including traditional security systems.
