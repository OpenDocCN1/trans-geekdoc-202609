# THE INTERACTIVE PARADIGM

In the first generation of the web, we had one kind of content: static Web pages—little more than digital billboards displaying the same message every time you did a virtual drive-by. Usually, these websites described their products, solutions, and the company's profile, even when e-commerce existed as an essentially one-way exchange. The company offered it on their site, and the customer could buy it or not.(Like Henry Ford said,"any customer can have a car painted any color he wants so long as it is black.”) Despite we now view as the Stone Age, this new and exciting, one-way information flow provided more than enough momentum to get the new economy of first gear.

The Internet became much more interesting, though, when the second stage of evolution took off. The year 2004 saw the term Web 2.0 coined to describe the evolution toward user-generated content and interactive social networks. Think of YouTube, Soundcloud, and millions of blog sites that all packed with created content. Think of review sites like Yelp and TripAdvisor or person-to-person transaction sites like Match.com and Instagram that exist only because people are willing to upload every detail of their lives. Think of all the forums on health care and products and every topic of interest to mankind. All user-generated. All social.

The web was no longer a billboard with an occasional cash register. It became an interactive medium for everyone. Suddenly, we could talk with each other instead of just passively consuming ready-made content. This rise of Web 2.0 permanently altered the way we spent on the Internet, what we could accomplish, and with whom we interacted.

Some of these trends have found their way into security applications. We can share security videos over text, email, or social media with just a few finger flicks. We can participate in online forums to share our experiences, opinions, and best practices. We can review products, companies, and events. But what we haven't done yet is to bring all of these capabilities into the day-to-day practice of security.
