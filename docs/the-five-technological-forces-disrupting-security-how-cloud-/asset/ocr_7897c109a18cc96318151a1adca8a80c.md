As a connected device, the SmartBox was no exception. It needed to talk. It had things to say. It led us straight to the central role that mobile platforms have taken in security over the past decade: digital notifications of changes in the physical world. We learned early that people paid attention to messages, even if they were from a machine rather than a person. They would change their behavior if a connected device provided them with timely and relevant information. Of course, if the device told them that a package had been delivered, they might change course to pick it up before going on with their day. If the system told them someone else had been inside their box, they might call and find out why. We take all for granted now because we have connected devices telling us all the time, but at the time, the capabilities delivered everyone who saw them.

We found communication to be even more valued in commercial settings, where timely information is money. Field repair logistics was a prime example: getting the right part to the right person at the right time—and noting that it happened. We required a face-to-face handoff staff at the parts dept and field technician. Inserting an access-controlled counter into the process made it asynchronous, thereby decoupling the schedules of both parties. The system also provided the validation of service level commitments, with texts and e-mails providing an audit trail. Everyone saved them.

Today, this is almost passed, but at the time, it was groundbreaking. Text and images are now the lifeblood of sensor networks, apprising security teams of anomalies across their facilities and inhabitants. Mobile technologies have freed us from our desks and video monitors, enriched the interaction between buildings and their occupants, and make a platform for identity and access management.

# BIG DATA IN ITS INFANCY

The term "big data" made its debut in 1998, shortly before this story begins, but people weren't really using the term all that much. Even before joining the security industry, where I worked in a company that did nothing but analyze and sell huge amounts of data, no one said "big data." Instead, the company talked about analysis, reports, and building out specialty warehouses for clients. In other words, the company’s conversations with customers were about the value added to the data and what clients could do with it—not just its size.
