Another way to think about this is to make a list of decisions you can't make today due to a lack of data or analysis. The whole point of decision support systems is to enable action. Think of how big data is used in other business contexts. In retail and e-commerce, data are gathered not because they are interesting (although it is) but that ads can be targeted more effectively. In every one of these examples, the collection and analysis of big data are done with a very specific outcome, or decision, or in mind. It is purpose-driven.

Answering the question of what we want to know is not a technical exercise. It's mostly common sense, so we can begin with the simple observables and objectives that have been at the heart of security practice for millennia.

We would like to know the following:

- If something bad is to happen (or happen again)
- If something bad happened nearby and might affect us
- How to spot a potential bad person
- If something unusual is being observed for the first time
- If there is more or far less activity than usual
- If our perimeters—physical or virtual—have been compromised
- If our people are where we expect them to be
- If our people are in danger

For distributed enterprises, this list of questions grows even larger:

- Does the activity at this location resemble our other locations?
- Is something happening elsewhere that could happen here?
- What is the average of X across all of our locations?
- How does our security profile compare to similar organizations?
- What are our best and worst locations? Why?
- What factors characterize our good and bad performers?

All of this boils down to the question of what is normal and how do we know when we see it.

## NOT ONE SIZE FITS ALL

One of the challenges in talking about big data for physical security is that not everyone is interested in the same questions. A small business owner, a mall operator, and a multinational security director will all have very different data sets and objectives for their data and analytic resources. This makes it difficult for suppliers to come up with product offerings that satisfy a wide enough swath of their customer base to make them worthwhile. A useful model for thinking about big analytics is the model we currently use for video analytics, where there are specific analytic algorithms in use for each types of physical security problem: license plate recognition, facial recognition, flow analysis, and abandoned object recognition, name a few, are all designed to answer very specific questions. Big data analytics develop the same.

A great illustration of divergent data goals is the requests I see from customers about how and when they would like to use information. Some of them are glued to screens every minute of the day and place a high value on real-time data feeds. Others only log in once week or less to create reports about activity, which they file away for compliance or HR purposes. Others are more interested in using security data as an employee management tool. My favorite example of weird information science is the guy who monitors the smokers' door to their defined smoking area in a courtyard. True story, others have highly unique needs for downstream uses of data, and they choose to consume their data via an API.

The lesson is that information needs of each customer can be quite different. The needs of a small-medium-size business could be entirely different from the needs of an enterprise security director at a multisite corporation. A small business owner might be concerned with knowing that her stores have all opened on time and want only exception reporting in the event of an unopened location. An enterprise security director at a public company will be interested in compliance and a real-time view of any potential risks to his organization.

Software designers and engineers have been dealing with divergent needs like this for generations. It's one of the major challenges in creating a software system or service with broad appeal to multiple types of users. It should come as no surprise that these differences in data requirements would be a part of any big data solutions that emerge in the market. They will be one-size-fits

There are, however, some commonalities of categories that everyone shares. Rare or anomalous events are of interest to nearly everyone. They stand out, but only if you can find them in the sea of data you are confronted with every day. Confronted after the fact with these anomalies, the usual question is “how did you miss that?” Indeed, how did you miss the fact that on three successive Tuesdays, John Doe came into the office past midnight and left only after 15 minutes? How did you miss that an an employee who was dismissed the year ago suddenly woken up at the office on the day of the holiday party? How did you miss that we have nearly twice as many false alarms this week as we did at the same time last year?

These are, of course, examples of extremely rare situations that no one has on their mental watch list, much less programmed into any type of security systems except the most sophisticated high-security installations or government locations wrapped in top-secret clearance requirements. Yet, these are often the very kind of situations that result in security breaches.

Another way to think about this is that people don't want to hear from their security systems unless there is something wrong. They want to be smart enough to leave them alone when everything is OK but tell them when there is something that requires their attention. It sounds paradoxical but thinks of information systems as "information waters," The best waiters are inconspicuous and invisible and yet somehow to know just what you want and when you want it. Information overload is driving the need for all.

## FROM VARIETY TO BACK

While the security industry was following its own road maps, the rest of technology was pursuing a different path. Massive quantities of video that have been uploaded to the likes of YouTube, Facebook, Vine, and others were crying out for analytics that would make them searchable and commercially useful. With the explosion in the number of cameras in smartphones and now cars, drones, home automation systems, and other IoT devices, the human stockpile of video is growing at an unimaginable rate. Because of all these videos are stored in the cloud, a new generation of analytics is growing in accuracy and utility while dropping in cost.

New analytics solutions are now available from companies like Google via their Video Intelligence API, and they are completely independent of any security system or camera vendor. If you have some video data, you can submit via the API, and it will return analytics for a list of characteristics, depending on what you've asked it to do. Want to count how many people passed through a scene? Ask it to do for that. You can do that too. It also provides many other analytics like loitering, falling, movements, and tracking, growing among a list of capabilities that are constantly improving.

There are two significant aspects to developments like this. The first is that you can pick and choose which public API you would like to use for any given analytics task. You don't need to restrict yourself to the solutions provided by a single vendor, and you can change APIs or service providers you use for different types of analytics. The effect is to create an on-demand analytics marketplace where the best algorithm wins. The second significant aspect of kinds of analytics is that they have volumes of data that a single surveillance system—or even a traditional surveillance vendor—could capture on its own. Think of it this way; when you have millions of hour of cat, you become very good at identifying cats. What's also happening in this cloud-based consumption model is that the algorithm behind the API gets better every day because it is continually trained against more and more data.
