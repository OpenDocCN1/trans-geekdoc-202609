My bet is that you have glossed right over them and, unless you are a specialist in the field, read them as being pretty much the same thing. It's unfortunate that they sound so much alike because it leads to an imprecise usage that confuses many discussions.

Authentication is the process of establishing that you are who you say you are. In the real world, if you get pulled over for a traffic violation, the police officer will ask to see your driver's license, compare the picture on the license with your actual face, and decide if that's really you. That's authentication.

In an online setting, presenting a log-in and password is the oldest and simplest way of authenticating the person claiming to be bob@aol.com. In physical access, presenting an access card to a reader is a form of authentication—not a very good one, because anyone finds your card can pretend to be you.

Authorization, on the other hand, answers the question of what we know who are you say you are, what are you allowed to do? In the driver's license example, you may be authorized to drive a private vehicle, a commercial vehicle, or a motorcycle. These are three different types of authorization. After logging into a computer system, you may be authorized to see your own files but not those of anyone else. Upon entering a building, you may be authorized to enter the lobby and elevator but not any private offices.

Identity management, log-ins, and identity standards address authentication and authorization. Some cover both topics, and others are aimed at solving just one part of the puzzle.

# AUTHENTICATION STANDARDS

On the face of it, authentication sounds like an online or cyber problem, not a physical security problem. But it's our problem too because it's not the way we identify people in more and more transactions.

Digital authentication standards are intended to enable simpler and more log-ins to online accounts. Simplicity brings with it greater clarity; the need for security without saying. Most authentication standards are intentionally narrow set of use cases so they are easy to implement and easy for users to understand. Complexity is a big barrier to user adoption. Unlike some other types of standards we will look at, they do not attempt to ensure you are who you say you are in real life. They are just trying to make sure that no one but you—or to whoever you gave your credentials—can log into one of your online accounts. This alone is an important objective and harder than it looks.
