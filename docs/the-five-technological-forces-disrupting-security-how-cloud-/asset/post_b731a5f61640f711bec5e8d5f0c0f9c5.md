## THE DESKTOP REINCARNATED

Do you remember the dark ages before mobile? To look at our stock portfolio, to shop online, or just to check our e-mail, we had to sit at our desk staring at a full-size monitor using Internet Explorer. I can't even imagine going back to that time. Many of the administrative applications for security systems still work that way. Happily, they are migrating by one to mobile. The transition pays security teams, business owners, and system integrators—the entire paybill of actors in the security ecosystem—greater latitude to perform their work from anywhere, anytime. As that happens, we mostly see the desktop reincarnated on our mobile devices.

While administrative applications have become increasingly integrated, they usually align with the three main disciplines of electronic security: access control, video surveillance, and intrusion detection. In general, these applications provide a window into security data and the means to control different parts of the security ecosystem. An access control application, for example, give you the power to unlock a door remotely. A video surveillance application would show you live video, or perhaps let pan, tilt, and zoom over cameras to redirect their focus.

The features first to be recreated on mobile platforms say a lot about types of data and control people consider most important. Personally, I find this a refreshing turn of events because it cuts through the bloat and brings us back to the fundamentals security.

Take the classic activity log—a list of every event that has taken place within the purview of the user—still front and center in many security mobile apps. It has long served as an anchoring point for anyone logging into a security management system and continues, even in its mobile iteration. Activity logs resemble nothing so much as an undigested dump of raw data. They were never made for a good user experience, even with all the screen real estate of a desktop. On mobile platforms, they strain the abilities of small screens to the breaking point. It begs for a higher level of summarization of the kind that could perhaps be provided by a cloud back-end running big data tools that find and only meaningful activity.

The ability to manage user populations—that is, to grant and revoke access permissions—also remains a core capability of most mobile apps. The data my company collects to improve application performance reveal that user management is one of the most frequent uses of security technology, comprising nearly 40% of all log-ins.

Then, of course, we have viewing video surveillance via mobile. The severity of almost any alert—whether originating from the system or a call from a colleague or employee—can be investigated with a look at what's happening on video. Do you really have a break-in? Or did someone work late and forget to disarm the system before leaving? This use of remote video has improved dramatically over the past 5 years as cellular networks added more bandwidth and as compression standards become more widely implemented across mobile platforms.

Minor administrative tasks such as configuring notifications, setting up schedules, and managing holidays; duration of video storage; event retention policies; hundreds of infrequent tasks have yet to cross the chasm from desktop to smartphone.

But they will.

## APPS HAVE A FIELD DAY

Physical security doesn't work without trained field technicians. They do the "truck rolls" to install cameras, door controllers, switches, locks, sensors, wiring, and myriad components. The installation procedures follow the usability and effectiveness of systems in every day it does not. It is challenging, as the slow acceptance of DIY security systems proves.

The economics of the installation business depend heavily on minimizing the amount of labor required per job. Technicians are a part of the labor required to put in place and get it working. This fact makes their efficiency important to both the companies they work for and for their customers alike. Inefficiency shows as lower profit margins for system integrators or as higher costs for security organizations. You can't do the math any other way.

The first generation of mobile apps addressed some of these economic challenges through business process improvement. Workflow apps streamlined many tedious daily operations and reduced errors across the entire process, from customer order and job completion to scheduling, timekeeping, and parts management. These apps interacted with remote business systems such as databases and accounting ledgers but not the equipment that technicians actually installed and configured. These apps welcomed cost savings, but they left a big part of the technician's workload unchanged. On-premise configuration tasks were still performed on laptops or, on client-server systems at the jobsite.

That's where the new mobile ecosystem comes into play. The second generation of apps for field technicians leverages the ability of smartphones to interact with security equipment connected to the same IP network. They can configure equipment directly from their mobile devices, ending the days of laptops perched precariously on top of ladders or toolboxes. Installation apps save many trips up and down ladders for technicians and the accidental one-way trips too often take from those same ladders.

The economics of these apps benefit everyone in the security value chain. Better tools for integrators mean fewer technical support calls and related expenses for suppliers. Faster and more accurate installation translates to cost savings for integrators and less business disruption for their customers. They also cut down on the dreaded callbacks—installation snuffs that require a remote visit to fix what could have been prevented in the first place. Clarity, mobile apps move this needle in the right direction.

## MOBILE CREDENTIALS

Mobile credentials are the phone-based equivalent of the familiar access cards and key fobs that building occupants, tenants, employees, residents, contractors, and customers have used since the dawn of electronic security. Increasingly, they're also used for membership-based services as health clubs, shared workspaces, and common areas multi-unit dwellings.

Mobile credentials are very early in their lifecycle, but it's already clear that everyone loves them. Salespeople tell stories of closing deals as soon as they show a prospect that they can unlock their doors with their smartphone. Integrators love them because they deliver new value to their customers. Property managers treat them as a high-tenacity amenity for modern buildings. Security teams appreciate the simplified management compared to fobs and keys. Users write going reviews about convenience and never having to worry about losing their cards. Mobile credentials come in three technical varieties, each with pros and cons for users and administrators. In the absence of widely accepted standards, they are mostly proprietary; each mobile app works only with the locks, readers, or software systems of a single supplier.

The first type to appear on the market was built on near-field communication capabilities (NFC) added to smartphone platforms around 2010. Because NFC uses the same protocols as smart card readers, they were a natural fit for access control applications. Users also readily understood how to use them because the short activation range (about an inch) mimicked the familiar gestures used to present a contactless smart card. Alas, this was a win only for Android users, as Apple still not provided API access to NFC capabilities. As such, this solution does not cover a significant part of the market.

Bluetooth, the second flavor of mobile credit on the market today, is displacing NFC due to its universality across smartphone manufacturers, many other wearables (such as smart watches). Similar to NFC, a Bluetooth mobile device transmits a digital credential to a wall-mounted reader, and the access control system behind the scenes makes authentication and authorization decisions about letting the user through the door. Bluetooth differs in how you measure the range of communication in hundreds of feet rather than few inches. This larger range presents pros and cons for how the technology can be used. On the plus side, the greater range means users don’t necessarily need to hold the phone to a reader. They can just keep it in their pocket, as you would with the newer keyless systems on cars. On the other hand, this greater range presents challenges when doors are close to each other, as it is difficult for the system to understand which one you want to open. Either way, Bluetooth has taken mobile security credentials a big step forward and it’s now probably be the predominant type of solution.

Finally, there are API-based mobile credential solutions. In this model, the smartphone talks directly to a cloud-based access control system and the cloud system in turn unlocks the door. This method exploits the existing capabilities of cloud-based systems to remotely unlock doors via persistent connections to the control panels that manage them, a capability for at least a decade for browser-based systems, and it’s now being connected to mobile platforms. One of the key benefits of this architecture is that it eliminates any need for local wall-mounted readers. It is also an ideal candidate for retrofitting existing installations, as it requires no new hardware and can be performed remotely via app and credential distribution to existing users.

## INTERACTIVE SECURITY

Interactive security will be the outgrowth of putting mobile security applications in the hands of everyone who uses commercial buildings. If this seems hard to imagine, it should be, because it’s happening already.
