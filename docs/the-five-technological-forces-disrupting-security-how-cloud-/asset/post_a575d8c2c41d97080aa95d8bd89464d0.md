By precalculating values for all the relevant dimensions of a data set, they could respond quickly to queries about numbers they had already crunched. They were built on mostly traditional database technologies and are still useful for many business intelligence problems, like budget analysis, purchasing trends, forecasting, and reporting. They were good at what they were designed for.

Those and similar systems, however, were not designed for high-velocity data sets and the kinds of real-time situational awareness required for big data systems security. They are not fast enough, for one thing, but the bigger problem is that they are designed to provide answers about roughly defined data sets. The security industry does not do well with open-ended or continuously evolving data streams. In the case of high-velocity data, the goal is to provide actionable information for real-time decisions. This is the essence of the streaming model for analytics.

Many physical data sources provide an intrinsically high-velocity information stream. Physical security data streams are the result of sensors measuring millions of activities per second in the real world. Lot of these are the result of routine activities, but some of these are not. Buried in these streams of sensor data are clues about threats that could be mitigated if they can be detected and identified quickly enough. Time is of the essence, literally making the difference between life and death.

Video surveillance produces one of the highest-velocity and highest-volume streams in the business. The amount of data produced by each camera dwarfs any other source in the security domain. A high-resolution camera, for example, might deliver a megapixel image at 15 or more frames per second. At a facility or metropolitan area with hundreds or thousands of cameras, this amount of data can easily lead a LAN to the breaking point, to say nothing of nothing. This explains why video analytics has been such a hot topic. Analytics can be at the edge (in the camera or other local computing device) to detect video segments have relevant information and then throw away that do not. This winning process relieves pressure (and cost) on local networks, Internet connections, and storage.

Today's electronic systems also take advantage of discrete sensors to provide data to security administrators. Most professionally engineered systems include sensors for motion detection, presence monitoring, glass breakage, passive infrared detection, smoke, heat, sound, and position sensors to detect the state of doors, windows, and other openings. None of these sensors produce particularly large volumes of data on their own. The data for each event they track are usually only a handful of bytes; just enough to deliver one. In the aggregate, however, sensor data are a high-velocity data source. It is a nonstop, real-time information flow that must be quickly indexed in order to have any preventive device. The current generation of sensors IP protocols that connect each sensor to the Internet, either directly or via a gateway. This connection provides a pathway to cloud-based security analytics platforms that can extract meaning from this sea of data.

These tandem developments in sensor connectivity and cloud computing have enabled the security of data at a larger scale than ever before possible. The concentrated data streams from all the remote locations in a large chain, for example, can provide the basis for deeper analysis of events at each individual location. In a broader municipal security setting, event data from private businesses and public spaces could be aggregated to form a larger data set with deeper connections and parameters. The resulting analytics could yield far more valuable intelligence than the sum of its individual parts. Cloud-based security systems now consume these rich, multisourced data streams, putting them in a position to deliver higher value intelligence back to their customers.

Additional high-velocity sources are becoming available for physical security due to the ubiquity of smartphones and wearable IoT technologies. One of the most interesting is the capture of Bluetooth and Wi-Fi signals that smartphones use to discover other devices in their neighborhood. These can be aggregated for a variety of security purposes. The monitoring of “presence” tops the list because smartphones are a decoy for the number of people in a room or public space. Indoor positioning systems can use these signals for trilateration of smartphone locations and populate map showing traffic patterns and occupancy throughout a building.

As IoT devices continue to grow in number, more of these high-velocity sources will be available for security analytics.

# LESSONS FROM CYBER SECURITY

Cyber security is lighting the path for the development of big data applications in physical security. There are tens of millions of website attacks per day and billions of malware exploits, to say nothing of personalized phishing attacks and just plain old user error. Sorting through all of this is daunting enough, until you consider it's mixed with trillions of legitimate messages at the same time. This turns into a problem of gargantuan data collection and real-time analysis. Cybersecurity systems are an interesting case study because the event rates they handle are literally the highest velocity in the computing world. They have to be, by definition, because they are the conduits for everyone else's data. This deluge has required network security to move forward aggressively in pattern analysis and threat detection of high-velocity data.

The first lesson from big data analytics in cybersecurity is how to speed up the process of consolidating and correlating threat information. To do that, we had to ditch traditional databases, learn how to digest unstructured data, and move to clustered computing environments that provide much higher throughput when needed. Not coincidentally, many of the underlying tools used for threat intelligence are the same as that power the largest search engines. The engines of this new generation of tools mark a departure from use of tradition security information and event management (SIEM) tools, which begs the question of who will supply the next generation of tools for big data in physical security.

The second takeaway is that more data do not always equate to better information or informed action. Other things being equal, 100-fold increase in raw data will result in 100-fold increase in false alarms. No security organization can function with that additional load unless there is a corresponding improvement in the algorithms under the hood. This is unlikely to happen without the use of machine learning and an approach that includes enterprise-specific training data that learn the unique signatures of its own complement of data collection techniques. What this means that organizations will have to adapt a new generation of solutions that are better informed by data science, because the old filtering and alarm management frameworks cannot keep pace.

The third lesson is that all security equipment needs to be treated like the network it is. Malware is discriminate about what it attacks, and many security devices run the same operating systems that have been successfully attacked in an IT context. This observation forces the issue of either strictly isolating security networks (which is a head-in-the-sand approach) or engaging in deeper cooperation with IT (which is probably not a bad idea anyway). Among other precautions, this implies continuous monitoring of security equipment for infections, outdated firmware, and aberrant behavior.

The final lesson may be that common cyber enemy will forge awareness that there is no longer a choice about convergence. That discussion has been going for a long time, but most organizations have not achieved much of anything near a complete integration between physical security and logical security.
