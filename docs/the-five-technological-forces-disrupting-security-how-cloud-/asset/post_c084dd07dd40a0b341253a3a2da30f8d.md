The fluidity of both real and virtual borders poses challenges for everyone in the security value chain. Software and device makers cannot assume that theirwares will be deployed in a safe and contained area and protected from constant threats of the open Internet. Integrators cannot assume that their work is done where their client's property ends, cordoned off from neighboring networks and vulnerabilities. CISOs cannot assume that their concerns end when actors exit their facilities, locked out by physical doors but still able to walk cyber pathways back to the core of their operations.

These facts underscore the need for change. Thinking of identity as the new perimeter turns the whole question of inside versus outside, well, inside out. Instead of looking for a definitive border, we look instead to what limits movement or access of individuals. For each of us, the question then becomes “what are the places, real or virtual, that my identity allows me to enter?” Which objects can I interact with? What are my personal boundaries? That’s the personal perimeter that each of us experiences, sometimes no more invisible than a pantomime’s wall sketched in the air—but very real nonetheless.

Identity as a perimeter would be simple if we all had just one identity. But we don’t, and there are many who fear that any centralized keeper of that identity would wield undue power. Are we already in the best of all possible worlds?

## Social Saves Lives

“Could Facebook save your life?”

That on the cover of the December 2016 issue of Wired.¹ The related story details the operation of safety check, one of the social media giant's newest features, which provides a means for users in the vicinity of a “major crisis” to let friends know they are okay. The feature works by detecting the emergence of a crisis and setting up dedicated resources—pages, hashtags, and notifications—relevant to situational awareness and management.

This capability is a monumental improvement in our ability to communicate across disaster scenarios in real time. Anyone who wants to plug into the stream of information can do so from an app they have on their phone and already know how to use—two of the primary ingredients for getting people to use any web service. It also frees phone circuits for the first responders and others who need most.

At a sociological level, it changes the dynamic of mass notification and crisis response from a top-down governmental announcement to a crowd-sourced information sharing exercise. This is not to say there is no longer a governmental role. Some arise from afar and cannot be easily known by people who are on the ground somewhere. Thinking of weather events and acts of many, which can be forgotten to some degree.

Most of us deal with crises at a much smaller scale than that of hurricanes or mass uprisings. Muster is a classic example of the need to determine the safety status of an entire group of people. How much easier would this be if everyone just used Facebook? Using of familiar application that almost everyone uses would reduce the training burden, reduce the IT infrastructure and maintenance expense to zero, and do so on a much more effective platform that also allows real-time video feeds, the posting of photographs, and many other capabilities not found in clunky legacy solutions.

## VIRTUAL SOCIAL MEDIA WORKING GROUP

Facebook was not the first to uncover the life safety potential of social networks. Perhaps it was obvious right from the start for those who work with the public for emergency response, disaster relief, and the unfortunately growing incidence of security issues involving intentional mass violence against an unarmed public.

In 2010, the US Department of Homeland Security’s (DHS) Science and Technology Directorate took a giant leap forward into the digital age and enhanced the virtual social media working group (VSMWG). According to their web page, their mission is to provide recommendations in the use of social media technologies before, during, and after emergencies, of which the DHS has certainly managed its share.

One major accomplishment of this working group was the June 2014 DHS publication titled "Using Social Media for Enhanced Situational Awareness and Decision Support." The white paper identifies three major capabilities social media can provide to enhance during an emergency:

- 1. Communications
- 2. Situational awareness
- 3. Analysis

If this list looks like the fundamentals of risk management, that’s no coincidence. The authors—first responder organizations from across the state, local, and federal landscape—are all trained in that discipline. What's interesting is that social media tools created for entirely different purposes neatly match the needs of these organizations during crisis.

## THE UNITED NATIONS

In the same year as the DHS report, the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) published a white paper titled "Hashtag Standards for Emergencies." At first, this sounds like an old just justification of the ephemeral world of tweets and posts after the world of major catastrophes. The paper convincingly demonstrates, that a medium that is able to communicate so quickly with so many would be a natural vehicle for security and emergency coordination.

The line between personal and professional has become thin to nonexistent—and this is a good thing for security professionals and public safety.

## SOCIAL MEDIA FOR EMERGENCY MANAGEMENT (#SMEM)

Official public agencies like DHS and the UN are not the only groups catching the social wave. Unrelated groups involved in one way or another with security and spontaneously adopted social media as a way to communicate among them. #SMEM, or social media for emergency management, is a hashtag shared unoially by security and first-responder organizations as both a real-time communications tool and a sort of professional network for exchanging practices. Education used to be available only in a formal educational setting has now become available to anyone with a Twitter account. Given that training and turnover are two of the perennial concerns of the security profession, this is a welcome learning forum for all of our colleagues.

They have an uncanny way of shape shifting as they are created for one thing but find utility for many others. The Internet, for example, was originally created by and for researchers to exchange scientific information.
