# KNOWN KNOWNS, KNOWN UNKNOWNNS, AND UNKNOWN UNKNOWNNS

As Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld said, we have known knowns, known unknowns, and unknown unknowns. It turns out these categories perfectly match with different degrees of knowledge mobile technology can provide for passive surveillance.

Known knowns are the low-hanging fruit of wireless surveillance—cooperating subjects, either willing or by mandate, who carry a security or identity application that identifies itself to the rest of the mobile ecosystem. They are friends of

Think of groups like employees, tenants, contractors, and visitors, each using an app to access a building, a computer network, or another corporate asset. As they move through our physical spaces with our identity apps, we can follow them by name. The same apps use to open doors, elevators, and conference also transmit identity beacons. We have ambient sensors like Wi-Fi or Bluetooth access points listening for these signals and capturing their airborne name tags, reporting back to a central database. These are our known unknowns.

The known unknowns are like the new neighbors down the street. We can see them and hear them, but we don't know their names yet. There are many reasons for known unknowns to forego identity apps that talk to us directly. Think of shoppers, sport fans, and people queued at a movie theater or waiting for a slice of pizza. They are not a “captive” population like employees or tenants.

They roam our physical spaces anonymously. We can detect their smartphones, but their identities aren't mapped onto our mobile security ecosystem. Yet, in this comparatively data-restricted scenario, their mobiles transmit radio signals by which we can tell them, not by name, perhaps, but by the identity of their mobile platform. Even without identity links, these signals still provide valuable security and occupancy information for use during emergencies or for understanding building usage patterns. Often, these signals are processed into heat maps showing where traffic flows through hallways and courtyards, and which entry points are the busiest throughout the day.

The unknown unknowns are the dark matter of our surveillance universe. Invisible in many respects, we know them by their effect on the rest of the ecosystem. They can be dark for a variety reasons. They might be one of the rare birds that have no mobile device at all—an increasingly rare phenomenon but still a possibility. Or they may have disabled the Wi-Fi and/or Bluetooth on their smartphones, or put phones in airplane mode. Few people go to these lengths in the normal course of a day, but tales of hacking and eavesdropping have pushed at least a percentage of the population into stealth mode for fear of identity theft (or worse).

Passive collection of presence information throws light on all of these groups, with the exception of the unknown unknowns. For them, we will still need to rely on video images, facial recognition, human witnesses, and old-fashioned. But the data that our physical security systems can collect with Bluetooth and Wi-Fi move our discipline one step closer to the kind of analytics our IT brethren have enjoyed for years. If you've ever looked over their shoulders to see what their analytics delivers, you'll know that's a good thing—a big step up in many cases.

# ANTHOMETRICS—WEB METRICS IRL

Anthropometrics—derived from the Greek word anthro (“man” or “mankind”) and the Latin word metricus (“measure”)—is the science of measuring the behavior of large groups of people in a specific context, literally “the measure of man.”

Anthropometrics have become part of the fabric of modern society. It drives our consumer economy with Nielsen ratings, focus groups, surveys, ad tracking, and in sum, by measure of gross domestic product (GDP). It is at the center of understanding modern democracies by vote of polls, opinion surveys, political contributions, and the very act of voting. It’s the foundation of the Internet economy, with detailed analytics on the minutia of online behavior, from unique daily visitors to click-through rates and dwell time on Web pages.

Such analytics provide an adaptable model that points to the useful insights in the physical security domain. This type of analytical approach can also turn security data into operational data that across the entire enterprise. Visitor traffic patterns are directly relevant to building services and energy consumption, for example. Retailers can get enough data to illuminate behavior, and security can again gain value with passive surveillance data filtered and enriched through analytics.

The parallels between traffic analysis and physical site analysis are remarkable. Take traffic statistics. Unique visitors, first-time visitors, and repeat visitors—all core elements of online analytics—map from the virtual world to the physical world. Presence data make this mapping possible. The number of unique visitors tells us many things, like the number of people we need to plan for. That useful information to a security organization because greater numbers of people often signal greater risk. Being able to see new patterns sooner time respond by reallocating resources, redirecting staff, or provides the opportunity to recognize and anticipate periodic or seasonal patterns. Knowing about these patterns means security organizations can plan for handling them differently in the future. At the very least, knowledge of these traffic patterns will have operational value for every other part of the business.

The second major analytic we can carry over to the physical world is the number of first-time visitors in the overall population. This metric tells us what percentage of the population has never been observed. For almost any type of institution open to the public, this is useful information on how your customers behave, another example of turning security data into operational data.
