Three of the specific techniques behind these machine-driven insights are segmentation, clustering, and association. They are all examples of what data scientists call unsupervised learning, which is a way for machines to find patterns without direct directives on what relationships there be in the data.¹ We will look at how each of these is relevant using big data in security applications.

Segmentation is the process of dividing a large group of data into subgroups with common characteristics. A familiar example is consumer marketing, where the goal is to segment customers into subgroups that share buying patterns or preferences. Millennials, for example, are more likely than all other age groups using streaming media, so that's a great place to reach them with advertising. If you are targeting Baby Boomers, on the other hand, Facebook and other social media are a better place. Segmentation is not just for marketing. It can apply to many kinds of data sets and behaviors. Profiling is a familiar (and possibly illegal) type of segmentation that groups people by age, race, gender, or other characteristics. While the practice is rife with civil issues, the statistical basis for it is no different than what markets and counterterrorism experts use every day.

Discovering the groups for segmentation is the job of clustering algorithms. They examine across all the attributes of the members of a data set and look for groups that produce unsupervised learning plays a role. If it is already known which groupings will produce predictions about how the members of a group would behave, then on the other hand, there is just subdividing the data based on existing knowledge. If, on the other hand, there is no knowledge or even a hypothesis, clustering can help find the relationships for us. The result may be one or more ways to cluster the data that can be used for additional modeling and research.

One of the characteristics of classification algorithms is that they can be applied to problems where the data attributes are not continuous, which is to say they are not numerical. For example, the attributes of interest may be binary, such as whether or not a person is a citizen. They might also be multivalued discrete variables, such as the highest level of education achieved by a job candidate.
