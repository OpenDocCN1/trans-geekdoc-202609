# CHAPTER 8: Why IoT Matters in Security

Many observers describe the arrival of IoT in the same breathless terms used to announce waves of new technology: desktop computing in the 1980s, the rise of the Internet in the 1990s, and the shift to cloud and mobile computing in the new millennium. They were each heralded as the dawn of a new era. In many ways, they each of that made tremendous impacts on our lives, both individually and collectively as a society. Personally, I find it hard to imagine the pre-PC, pre-Internet, and premobile that defined the first 20 years of my life. I find it hard to imagine that having billions more connected devices wouldn't produce dramatic changes over the next 20.

There is, however, a point of view that says IoT is nothing new: just a fresh label for existing technology, driven largely by marketers jumping on the latest bandwagon. They point to examples of similar connected devices as proof that phenomenon started a long time ago and is simply coming into its own. In fact, I made a similar point earlier in this chapter: namely, that electronic security has been a connected device business for many years and, in fact, pioneered such things long before they showed up in consumer products. When networked connectivity did appear, it was hailed as huge differentiator: Don’t buy the printer with bulky cable—get the wirelessly connected one instead; don’t buy a mouse with an unsightly wire to your laptop, the one that uses invisible radio waves.

Like most new technology trends, IoT is coming into the market surrounded by many exaggerated claims. A lot of the hype is about the technology itself and not what it can actually do for consumers or businesses. This is unfortunate for those who are building new solutions that solve real problems or enhance people' lives in meaningful ways. Their innovations are trying to gain footing in a very noisy space, making it difficult for buyers to evaluate that technologies are mature enough to be of use them.
