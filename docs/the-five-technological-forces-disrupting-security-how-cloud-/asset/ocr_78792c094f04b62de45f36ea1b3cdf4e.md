# The Toothbrush Test

Until very recently, our industry’s apps have been targeted at the relatively small cadre of security managers in our ecosystems, not for all the other stakeholders. They have inadvertently excluded all the employees, tenants, guests, visitors, contractors, customers, residents, members, and dozens of classifications for people who pass through our security perimeters. In other words, much of our technology has not included the people we protect for their partnership. But all we have to do put applications in their hands; once we do, commercial security applications will be in a position to benefit from the same interactive revolution that produced Web 2.0. We can think of it as Security 2.0, although I hesitate to use that term because it has already been coopted for dozens of other products and concepts.

Instead, let's call it “interactive security."

# THE TOOTHBRUSH TEST

Interactive security revolves around mobile apps. Since the rise of user-generated content, mobile apps have gained the lion's share of the time we spend connected. That's why interactive security will debut on mobile rather than on the desktop. It will make security dynamic and interactive like the rest of the web. To see this, you need only look at any one of the startups that have taken on mobile security and authentication in the past 5 years. Their advertising always shows a mobile app, front and center. If the medium is the message, mobile is the messenger.

True to however, the commercial security products still lag behind. What has been missing is the vehicle to make it happen: a security application so people want to use it every day.

Cue mobile credentials.

People love them. They pass what Larry Page calls the “toothbrush test”: People must use an app at least twice a day, or it won’t be sticky enough to become a habit. Those that fail the toothbrush test join the 84% of downloaded apps abandoned within a few weeks. To recurring revenue companies, that spells attrition. To their customers, it foretells a future of apps that receive no further development funding and get any better.

Mobile credentials pass the toothbrush test in being indispensable for people to get into a building they frequent. They also succeed because users like them better than access keys and fob, which always seem to get lost at the worst possible time—like when you parked your car in the underground garage of your office building and can't get back in after happy hour.
