# The Update Treadmill

Next come security integrators. Unfortunately, many aren't in a good position to manage the cyber updates of connected devices. First, it's not usually written into maintenance agreements, and it certainly can't be done for. Second, even if it were, many integrators do not have enough cyber expertise on their staff. Their technicians are trained to perform installations, not keep track of the latest threats to equipment from dozens or hundreds of manufacturers. Third, it would not be cost-effective for them to apply software and configuration patches across hundreds of individual customer sites. There'd have to be some centralized to do it.

What about manufacturers? Many vaguely believe that manufacturers really ought to be proving these updates. The ideal is appealing in theory but falls apart logically because manufacturers don't typically know where their devices are installed and they cannot reach into a network to make changes to them—unless they are already cloud-connected devices in the first place. Manufacturers can provide automated updates, but many customers won't want them because they have seen updates fail. The best we can do is to provide tools and choices and hope customers and integrators use them.

# THE UPDATE TREADMILL

So, let's imagine that in some perfect future, we've overcome the knowledge and governance gaps that get in the way of keeping our connected devices up to date. You may also feel free to imagine that you've won the lottery. It's about likely.

IoT devices in physical security applications likely share the same network with numerous other IoT devices deployed for different purposes. This is always the case in home and small businesses, where it is prohibitive to set up a secondary network for physical security devices. This means that the new normal for security equipment will be network coexistence with an unknown number of random devices from mystery manufacturers.

Writing in the a16z blog, Benedict Evans makes an insightful comparison between this aspect of IoT proliferation and the earlier rise of electric motors. Our grandparents knew how many electric motors owned because they were newfangled curiosities and few in number. We used to be in this position with respect to connected devices in our homes, but it's quickly escaping our grasp and will beyond the reach of our children. At that point, we will have forgotten them and any need they may have for cyber attention.
