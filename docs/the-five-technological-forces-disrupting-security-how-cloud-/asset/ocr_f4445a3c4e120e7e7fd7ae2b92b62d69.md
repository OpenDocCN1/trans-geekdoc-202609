# WHAT ABOUT RESIDENTIAL?

The residential market has always led the industry in creating innovative apps for security and automation systems. The leaders in this market have strong mobile offerings since at least the late 2000s, right on the heels of the iPhone introduction and all the other smartphone platforms that followed. These residential systems pioneered the era and are still the model for many commercial products. At that time, they displaced the old clunky, wall-mounted alarm panel. They boasted new capabilities like remote video and kids alerts told parents when their children arrived home school. Some of this innovation was by necessity. As a consumer product, residential security faces higher expectations for convenience and engagement than what commercial users demand.

That said, residential applications are necessarily different from commercial applications. Commercial applications face a different set of requirements and design challenges than residential, and many of these differences are beneath the surface. Each residential account, for example, has a much smaller number of users who can log in. This reduced scale has broad impacts on the design of the user interface and complex subjects like role-based permissions and authentication. Residential accounts also naturally limit the number of locations, cameras, doors, and sensors compared with enterprise-class systems. Designing for security assets that live under hundreds of roofs is different than designing for dozens or even hundreds that live under a single root.

Commercial systems must also support a much greater diversity of third-party products than residential systems, in some respects we have the luxury of a "walled garden" approach. Nowhere is this more evident than in support for literally thousands of models of high-quality video cameras, in the case of commercial systems, versus the small set of web cams that suffice for most homeowners. Commercial users also demand video retention periods of as much as 1 year, for compliance reasons, whereas homeowners are generally content with a few weeks. A similar story plays out with video quality, where commercial uses require high resolution, high frame rates, and demanding low-light performance, while residential systems relax most of the requirements to fit market pricing and homeowner budgets.
