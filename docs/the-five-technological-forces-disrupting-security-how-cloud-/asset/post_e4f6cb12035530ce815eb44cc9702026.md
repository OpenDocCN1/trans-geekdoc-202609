In the context of physical security, many connected devices were built with the tacit assumption that they would live within the relatively safe confines of local area networks. The on-premise client-server paradigm made this assumption reasonable at the time, but not anymore. The walled garden no longer has a wall, and no one will build unless we pay for it.

Once local systems were connected to the Internet, they were vulnerable to the billions of hackers, bots, and automated attack scripts that pound countless URLs every day. Even as our websites, applications, and APIs have become better defended, embedded devices have lagged. That's how we ended up with 140,000 cameras being used for DDoS attacks.

## THE NEED FOR STANDARDS

It would seem that in this day and age, it's hardly necessary to make the case for standards. But as anyone with passing technical familiarity with physical security can tell you, many areas still defy standardization. Instead of boring you with yet tech speak, let's talk about everyone's childhood fascination, choo-choo trains.

> "Gauge" is the width between the two rails of a train track. Today, we take it for granted that this is the same everywhere, but this was not true. In the early days of railroads in the 19th century, companies used different track gauges throughout the world. Trains that operated on one gauge could not travel on a track of a different gauge because the wheels would not fit. This incompatibility meant that human travelers and material shipments had to be off one train and move to another in order to continue their journey. This was great news for freight handlers, restaurateurs, and hoteliers at the junction of those two gauges, but bad news for passengers and shippers.

On the face of it, this was a relatively simple problem to solve: just make all the train tracks the same size, right? The problem was that vested interests had grown up around each type of track and the rail that fit into it, and no one wanted to bear the expense or inconvenience of having to change. The contrary, using a gauge incompatible with the next railroad over was actually an intentional strategy for preserving market share. It forced people to use your track if they wanted to get to areas you controlled. This circumstance was the basis of the famous Gauge War of 1853 in Erie, PA, where the town prevented gauge standardization in order to force layovers and thus local spending.

## CHAPTER 10: IoT Technology and Standards

Track gauges in many countries and regions were standardized based on the common efficiencies of doing so. Ultimately, the Pacific Railway Act of 1863 sets standards for the transcontinental railroad, and others followed suit. Now, trains roll from coast to coast without problem.

Today, the world of IoT devices is roughly where the railroads were before the arrival of standard gauge. The protocols they use to communicate are not the same gauge. This prevents them from interoperating with one another directly. It is also directly responsible for the proliferation of gateways, hubs, and base stations, plus all the authentication procedures needed to make them work together. In short, the jungle of wireless devices in a modern IoT implementation can easily rival the mid-PC era fumble of specialized cables for serial, parallel, SCSI, USB, Ethernet, and other peripherals that would only communicate with a specific type of wire.

The real issue is here one of interoperability. Ideally we would like for all IoT devices to talk to each other in order to accomplish some greater task, like alert occupants to danger. But there is no guarantee that any two IoT devices can work together without a lot of custom integration. That approach doesn't scale.

For the security industry, this lack of standardization poses a huge problem for IoT adoption. Forgetting the mess for a moment, the more serious problem is what it means to be on a single protocol family, only to find a few years later that it lost in the standards war and is no longer manufactured. If you are a buyer, you are now stuck with a system difficult to expand. If you are a system integrator, you will have to be the bearer of this bad news to your customers. And if you are a manufacturer, you have to place a bet that's as long as your product life cycle (or several bets).

This also has major implications for your workforce. Training is a constant treadmill exercise for most companies; technology obsolescence only makes it much worse. No sooner do your technicians know how to install one type of IoT network than something else comes along to replace it—with no guarantee of backward compatibility. As quick as your operations staff understand the quirks and foibles of one deployment, another must be placed alongside to build out your next layer of infrastructure.

This rolling stew of proposed standards from an evolving ensemble of too many chefs in the kitchen forces many companies to change their strategy over and over. In the earlier days of home and industrial automation, handful of radio protocols like ZigBee and Z-Wave seemed enough to put together a network of sensors and control devices. Unfortunately, while these technologies gave the appearance of standardization—and they were, on the overall applications still spoke their own languages with little interoperability across vendors. The new crop of IoT communication standards has multiplied these complexities 10-fold.

Not that IoT incompatibility is the first standards gap our industry has encountered. Outside of alarm signaling, video encoding, and some aspects of card access, electronic security systems have been resounding nonstandardized for much of their history. For anyone watching even a little, it's not hard to point out the many cases of vested interests surrounding proprietary equipment and software. It's like you're living in antebellum Erie with your vendors ripping up the tracks that allow you to move freely to other solutions. Buyers have rightly complained about vendor lock-in and have begun to give greater consideration to standards. They need to carry this behavior over to any plans for leveraging the benefits of IoT in a physical security setting.

Do your homework before you spend your money.

## ARCHITECTURE MATTERS

In the physical world of buildings and bridges, good architecture ensures that things look good, serve their intended purpose, and don't fall down while doing both. It makes structures safe to live, on walk, beneath, and inhabit.

System architecture in networked electronic systems plays the same role. It is the art and science of making systems perform well, operate according to plan, and don't fall over while doing something.

Falling over doesn't mean the same for connected devices and networks as it does for bricks and mortar, but it's just as real. It used to be that, for computer systems, falling over mean software crashes that were usually the fault of some intrinsic coding error— unresponsive keyboards, core dumps, and infamous blue screen of death. Now, falling over is more likely to suggest hacking or external compromise—data loss, data threat, denial of service, or denial of safety, in the case of physical security networks.

Architecture also provides a model of what things are and how they behave, especially their interactions with each. It literally provides a blueprint for the construction of something useful and safe. It translates complex design and engineering principles to drawings accessible and executable by anyone who wants to build something. If the builders, installers, or integrators follow these plans, we can expect the resulting system to perform to expectations and be resilient to natural and man-made threats.
