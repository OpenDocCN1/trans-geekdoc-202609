# CAN BIG DATA SAVE YOUR LIFE?

That was the question posed by Dr. David Agus in the January 2016 issue of Fortune. If your doctors practice data-driven medicine, the answer, happily, is yes. That because their medical decisions are informed by large sets of data about many patients rather than just yourself. The data-driven enterprise, analytics provide the doctor with diagnoses and prognates that can't be derived from individual cases. Machine learning adds even more to this equation, with trained algorithms now better able to identify disease conditions than a specialist with a lifetime of education and experience. Not knocking docs, the issue is that there are just too much data and not enough for any one to make decisions as quickly as they need to be. Made soft to the rescue—it's a lifesaver.

Contrast this model of clinical practice with that of the security guard staring bleary-eyed into a video monitor. This overtaken sentry is the unfortunate poster child for traditional video surveillance in our buildings and public spaces. The hope is that this vigilant soul will spot anomalies and take action to prevent harm to people or property. But this scenario is actually a poor way to catch anything in real time. It is worst in the same sense that Churchill said, "democracy is the worst form of government, except for all the others." So it is with event and video monitoring by humans: they are the worst form of vigilance, except for all the others. Sure, its better than having cameras at all, but that's not saying much.

Limits on human attention span make these models ineffective. Numerous studies show that attention capacity diminishes from 100% to near zero within approximately 20 minutes. This means that well over 90% of the time, we are expecting to be vigilant; they are actually doing a terrible job at it. Im not knocking guards; it's not human possible.

The traditional row-by-row display of events and alarms has been rendered ineffective by the volume of data in modern security systems. Reams of data scroll the human eye on a computer display, but the brain can't begin to process it or recognize signs of potential trouble. Only after the fact do the voluminous logs in our security systems serve any real purpose, and by then, it's usually forensic, not preventive. Dashboards of dials and meters and digits have become de rigueur for taming all this data and adapting them to our cognitive strengths. As the amount of underlying data has grown, however, the number of dashboard widgets has grown so large that not even NORAD of the classic War Games has the screen space to show them all.

It's 10 pounds of data in a 5 pound sack.
