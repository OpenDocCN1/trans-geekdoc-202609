Many aspects of IoT system architecture are driven by their need to be small and battery powered. All of these devices run on specialized wireless networks for low-power communications. ## BUILDING BLOCKS

Connected devices have been entering our lives subtly, in much the same way that solid-state electronics (i.e., transistors) did over the previous couple of generations. There was a time in automotive history, for example, when cars were manufactured with no transistors whatsoever; now you find hundreds of microprocessors and hundreds of millions of transistors in every ordinary car, to say nothing of the emerging autonomous vehicles called "super computers on wheels."

Or consider a humble example: the household washer or dryer. There was a time when all the controls and sensors were electromechanical, no transistors and microprocessors. Around the late 1960s, it became more economical to manage temperatures, times, humidity, and water levels with transistors. Washers and dryers got added to the list of "transistorized" appliances along with the hearing aid, the transistor radio, and the first portable television.

No one bought these products just because they contained transistors (well, maybe a couple of geeks). Most buyers were probably unaware. Who knew the new Kenmore washing machine used transistors to clean their clothes? They were just products. They sold themselves by virtue of superior functionality and improved user experience.

The same has been happening with connected devices for the last decade or so years. They have crept into our lives without even thinking about it. We have purchased IoT products without buying them for that particular reason. When automakers began embedding Internet and cellular technology in cars so they could tell their dealer they needed service, no one thought “hey, my car is an IoT device!” The technology came as an invisible part of the service you wanted: advance knowledge of whether something was to go wrong with your car.

What has changed? We now look at IoT as a capability set that can improve any product or service. In part, that's because it has become small and cheap enough to be fitted into practically any product you can imagine. Miniature and Moore's law have seen to that. It's also because the ability to connect to the Internet has been extended into every nook and cranny of our world, mostly to the variety of wireless communication options that a wide range of distance, data rate, and energy consumption profiles.

Yet, there is something about the new embodiments of these technologies that calls to mind Arthur C. Clark's famous third law: any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic.

Magic starts with the familiar. Microprocessors are an inescapable part of everyday life and part of every IoT device. At some point, the twin triumphs of miniaturization and low-power consumption crossed a line and morphed a quantitative difference—smaller size and power—into a qualitative difference: now these things can go anywhere!

The magic continues with the invisible radio waves in connected devices. Wireless communication networks are all part of everyday life and part of every IoT device. Again, multiple engineering triumphs like signal processing and lower transmission energy drive connected devices across a metamorphic phase transition, as different as vapor and ice.

What is different now how these technologies are interconnected and how they are used. First and foremost, it's impossible to plan for IoT products without including a cloud service component. The Internet is right there in the acronym, that shouldn't be a surprise. However, with so much of the security industry still struggling to connect to cloud services, it's a real consideration. There is no way to think about IoT with cloud as part of the equation. Moving your security program to the cloud is a real consideration.

Second, you are entering a new era of communication protocols. Many of these are designed for local area networks, as measured by size. Many of these are designed for local area networks Others for wide area networks that are similar to cellular technology, but optimized for machine-to-machine (M2M) applications. These 5G networks provide a big boost to IoT by building additional communication infrastructure of our own.

## GATEWAYS TO THE FUTURE

A “gateway” is simply a device that connects one network to another. The name derives from their similarity to physical gateways, because both act as a connector between two different domains. What makes them interesting and useful is that they can connect networks that use different rules or speak different languages.

Imagine connecting two road networks. If the rules of the road are the same on both sides, it is trivial to connect them. Just lay down some pavement, and let people drive from one to another. But what if the rules are different on the left and another where they drive on the right? If we just pave the space between them, cars moving from one to the other will have head-collisions. So, we need a transition zone (or gateway) where the left and right lanes are switched over and to provide drivers with new rules (or protocols) about how to navigate the other side.

But that's not all they do.

If the data expressed on the two networks are different, the gateway will modify it while passing it from one to the other. In this context, gateways can be thought of as translators that connect groups of people who speak different languages. Looking at the other way around, translators are gateways who switch between groups who would not otherwise be able to communicate with each other. Of course, a translator doesn't just listen to the words used and repeat them to the other. That would be pointless. A good translator substitutes new words, syntax, and rules of grammar to accurately express the underlying message.

Technically, this capability provides a method for connecting devices across similar networks, that is, networks without the same underlying protocols. For example, something that does not use IP protocols can be connected to something that does, like cloud services.
