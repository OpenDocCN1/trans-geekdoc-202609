# Time and Frequency Analysis

Time and frequency are inverse twins of one another, and each have a story to tell. One who tells the better story depends on the type of data and what we need to know. They are both relevant to finding anomalies in big data sets.

In the time domain, events are viewed as a linear sequence or series. This point of view highlights certain kinds of relationships, especially where causal order is of interest and the order of events is key to understanding them. It may also highlight trends in the data and look for mathematical relationships that point to an underlying case. Trends may be caused by either a deterministic or statistical process that causes changes to the observable variables. Time-based analytics may also look for specific sequences of events and search for a signature or correlation that is worrisome or alarming based on prior experience. Finding these event sequences becomes more challenging the larger and faster the data stream, as the cyber security sector has learned all too well.

Now, let's think about frequency or events that repeat at particular intervals of time. Frequency patterns are difficult to view along a time line. In any large data set, they are mixed in with many other events, and it is challenging to discern periodic patterns. But if time sequences are inverted and viewed as frequencies, recurring events stand out. It's like X-ray vision for time.

When you follow this approach of looking at frequency rather than time, an entirely different set of patterns emerges. For example, it may be completely normal for something to happen four times an hour but not 20. Now, imagine having to comb through sequential data to understand whether this frequency limit was being observed or not. This is the equivalent of being a card counter in Las Vegas. If you instead imagine analytics provided a display of event frequency, all you would have to look at that number and see if it was over the limit. That's looking at things in the frequency domain.

Another type of analysis that occurs in the frequency domain is for cyclic events or cyclic norms. Daily cycles of events would be an example. In many domains from telecommunications to Internet usage to road traffic, there are cyclic patterns that repeat themselves day in and day out. Once these patterns are known, we can compare current real-time values with historical cycles and know whether things are normal or not.

# Faster

Faster data processing is the difference between an autonomous car and one that requires a driver. Faster data processing is the difference between beating a grand master at chess and being a toy for beginners. Faster data processing is the difference between humans and machines—not at everything, perhaps, but once machines learn how to do something, they always win. Just ask folk John Henry.

The ability to process data quickly and smartly makes all the difference between technology that is merely an amusing curiosity. The combination of raw processor speed and parallel processing has taken specific types of artificial intelligence from theoretical novelties to practical real-world disruptions. The changes will come to security as the knowledge and tools become more widely available and applied to new domains. The effects will impact how well we provide security. They will also impact who or what fills the security routes of the future.

Earlier, we cited human brains—housed security guards—as an example of outdated and overwhelmed technology for many of the things we ask them to do. People are not great at watching because we are so easily succumb to boredom, fatigue, and distraction, none of which are ideal qualities for analytics. Video and other environmental sensors are already eliminating the surveillance aspect of this job for many locations that require a live person. Progress in object, facial, and sentiment recognition will continue to push this boundary further.

If you don't believe we're going there, think about what's going to happen in the trucking industry over the next 20 years. The forecast is that autonomous tractor trailers will take over the majority of the open roads from human truckers. Now ask yourself, is walking a guard tour more complex than driving a truck? What made the difference for trucking (and all driving, for that matter) is that we can now process data enough to make decisions in real time. All these years of waiting for artificial intelligence and robotics to pay off we were waiting just one thing: more speed.

Now, what if we look at the second half of the guard equation? Physical presence. Guards in many cases provide physical enforcement, interdiction, restraint, and physical custody during emergencies and crimes, among many other life and safety services. They also carry guns, Tasers, and batons that do a lot of the work of convincing someone to do what they want to. Robots are now capable of carrying and using all of these deterrents and provide the mobile eyes and ears we need for ongoing situational intelligence—I mean, data. No, it's not legal to send a gun-tuning robot out into the night, at least not in any civilian setting. Such tasks are transitioning from people to machines for jobs that are far more complex than the majority of the hours logged by guards. There is no reason to think the superior economics of automation won't push into this job description in the same way that it has many others.

What will make this transition possible? More speed.

Speed and data are intertwined. They need each other. A fast processor without a lot of data is the computing equivalent of all dressed up with no place to go. Data without fast processing is just excess baggage. The two are now coming together in physical security systems, at both the edge and the center of the computing model. By “edge,” we mean the processing that happens locally, inside a sensor, a camera, a smart lock, or any other computing device. The term derives from viewing networks as having a central hub of some sort (computer, cloud, and switch) plus many connected things at the edge. The center was classically a large computing device like a mainframe or big server that has now become virtualized along with everything in the cloud. The edge has become faster and smarter and to do more on its own, it can also produce more data than ever before and send back to the center. increase in the data velocity at the cloud-computing center on which big data is converging.
