The fluidity of both real and virtual borders poses challenges for everyone in the
security value chain. Software and device makers cannot assume that their\wares will be deployed in a safe and contained area and protected from

when actors exit their facilities, locked out by physical doors but still able
to walk cyber pathways back to the core of their operations.

These facts underscore the need for change. Thinking of identity as the new
perimeter turns the whole question of inside versus outside well, inside out.
Instead of looking for a definitive border, we look instead to what limits move-
er or access of individuals. For each of us, the question then becomes “what
are the places, real or virtual, that my identity allows me to enter?” Which
objects can I interact with? What are my personal boundaries? That’s the-
sersonal perimeter that each of us experiences, sometimes no more invisible than a
pantomime’s wall sketched in the air—but very real nonetheless.

Identity as a perimeter would be simple if we all had just one identity. But we
d’t, and there are many who fear that any centralized keeper of that identity
would wield undue power. Are we already in the best of all possible worlds?
