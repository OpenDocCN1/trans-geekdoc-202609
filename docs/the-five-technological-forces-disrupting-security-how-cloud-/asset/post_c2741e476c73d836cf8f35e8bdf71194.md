
## CHAPTER 2: The Run-Up to Cloud

## BEFORE WE CALLED IT "CLOUD"

During the first era of the Internet economy, we didn't use the term cloud. Software and network engineers knew the cloud symbol by looking at network diagrams for years, but the same symbol could refer to just about networking technology (e.g., nearly forgotten relics like X.25 and SNA). Unlike popular cloud depictions today, it lacked the implication that invisible clouds hide, performing magical services. It simply served as a way of abstracting and simplifying the representation of a spaghetti ball of connections. It said anything touching it exchange messages with everything else.

The first cloud companies were remote data centers running your applications for you. The earliest name for them was application service provider (ASP). Companies operating under the ASP model ran their applications on their servers and took care of maintenance, licensing, storage, and backups, thereby relieving your IT staff of developing specialized knowledge for every application you used. This breakthrough model shared some of the characteristics of today's SaaS companies but felt short of creating new, multitenant applications optimized for emerging cloud usage.

Some used the term host, as in "hosted access control" or "hosted video surveillance." At best, the term is vague, imprecise, and misleading. It says nothing about the underlying computing model. Multitenant? Client-server? Web? It says nothing about where or how the computing resources are deployed. A data center? On the Internet? In a closet? Finally, it sweeps many deployment models into a single bucket, as if to say all off-site computing is created.

Nothing could be further from the truth.

## FIVE ESSENTIAL CHARACTERISTICS AND WHY THEY MATTER

In nearly every public speaking engagement I've had over the past 10 years, someone in the audience invariably asks for a definition of the cloud. Either they've never come across a clear explanation or they've heard too many conflicting answers. Deceptive marketing claims only add to the confusion. Fortunately, we have remarkably good agreement on the precise definition, thanks to The National Institutes of Standards and Technology (NIST).

NIST has become the worldwide leader in defining cloud technology and driving consensus on a core framework to describe it. One of the most useful elements of the NIST framework is the list of five essential characteristics of cloud computing, published in 2011. These separate the wheat from the chaff and illustrate the relevance of cloud computing to electronic security.

- 1. **On-demand self-service**: the ability to provision computing capability without requiring human intervention. In other words, you can create an account, set up a new application, and complete the financial transaction without calling a support center or wait an IT person to do something.
    - Why it matters for integrators: the value of on-demand self-service is that you can turn on application services immediately and get on with installing controllers, cameras, sensors, and the like without waiting for a server to be set or software licenses to be installed. On-demand self-service also means that technicians require less training and can become more productive quickly after being hired. Couple that with few opportunities to make mistakes on in-field deployments, and you can see a big boost.
    - Why it matters for security organizations: your new security system can be installed more quickly than the traditional model. With no-premises servers, your integrator will require less time on-site and fewer resources from your IT staff. You should also see a lower up-front cost of installation (assuming that the integrator passes these savings).

- 2. **Broad network access**: cloud services must be available over the network and accessible through standard mechanisms such as browsers, cell phones, tablets, and workstations. The security profession had necessarily become mobile and 24/7 well before the rise of the Internet, Contrast this with the old-school model of system access only at a guard's desk or the back office, and you can deny the benefit.
    - Why it matters for integrators: broad network access allows integrators to deploy the same solutions, regardless of where their customers happen to be located.
    - Why it matters for security organizations: broad network access to security solutions means that you can plan on deploying a single solution across all your facilities. Uniformity and centralization of security applications and personnel provide tremendous savings and operational benefits to every organization.

- 3. **Resource pooling**: truly the special sauce of cloud computing, underlies the ability to provide all of the other essential characteristics, along with the economies of scale that make cloud computing a bargain for the buyer. Resource pooling—very related to the concept of multitenancy—is simply sharing a common set of computers, storage, network, and other resources with multiple consumers. The physical and virtual resources may be dynamically assigned and reassigned according to demand with some control over or apparent.

- 4. **Rapid elasticity**: resources can be elastically provisioned and released to scale out rapidly with demand. To the consumer, the available resources may appear nearly infinite and can be acquired in any quantity, giving the illusion of unlimited capacity at the provider.
    - Why it matters for integrators: rapid elasticity allows integrators to scale security solutions up or down based on the customer's needs without waiting for new hardware to be installed.
    - Why it matters for security organizations: rapid elasticity allows security organizations to respond quickly to changing threats or expanding needs without over-provisioning local hardware.

- 5. **Measured service**: cloud resources are necessarily made available and monitored and controlling usage at some level (e.g., storage, processing, bandwidth, performance, and number of users). Resource usage can be controlled and reported with transparency for each consumer.
    - Why it matters for integrators: measured service allows integrators to bill customers only for what they use, which can be a compelling selling point.
    - Why it matters for security organizations: measured service allows security organizations to pay only for what they use, converting capital expenses to operating expenses.

## SERVICE MODELS: APPLICATIONS VS INFRASTRUCTURE

The concept of service models distinguishes the two most important categories for security buyers, whether as innovators incorporating a solution for their customers or as security organizations evaluating options for their next generation architecture. The two major service models for these purposes are software as a service (SaaS) and infrastructure as a service (IaaS).

Unless you develop your own applications and make a business of operating, when most people think of SaaS, they think "cloud". In SaaS, the consumer shares applications running on cloud infrastructure. The user never deals directly with the cloud infrastructure, which is the main appeal of this model.

In the case of a system integrator, being a SaaS consumer means using a third-party cloud-based video or access control solution, for example, and delivering SaaS service to your customers along with on-premise equipment installation (e.g., locks and cameras), scheduled and every maintenance, consulting, help support, and all the other services offered by security integrators and developers. In neither does the integrator represent their customer as having written the software, created the hardware, or operating the SaaS service. They have specified what they believe is a best-of-breed solution for their customer and embedded it into an overall service package.

For a security organization, SaaS services are presented as complete applications that are ready to use, with no IT assistance, configuration, or setup required. They work, same as an e-commerce site, online banking. Internet dating, or any other online service: SaaS, by any other name.

IaaS is an entirely different offering. It's raw computing and storage capability that developers and IT administrators turn into a useful application service. Think of it as a data center that's dressed up with nowhere to go. It can't do anything unless applications are added to it. In that respect, IaaS is a foundational layer underneath SaaS.
