These are, of course, examples of extremely rare situations that no one has on their mental watch list, much less programmed into any type of security systems except the most sophisticated high-security installations or government locations wrapped in top-secret clearance requirements. Yet, these are often the very kind of situations that result in security breaches.

Another way to think about this is that people don't want to hear from their security systems unless there is something wrong. They want to be smart enough to leave them alone when everything is OK but tell them when there is something that requires their attention. It sounds paradoxical but thinks of information systems as "information waters," The best waiters are inconspicuous and invisible and yet somehow to know just what you want and when you want it. Information overload is driving the need for all of our information systems to behave this way. We have so many sources of alerts, notifications, reminders, and other distractions that unless we develop effective ways to throttle them, we will continue to be overwhelmed. Our security systems should not be so chatty that we become inured to their warnings and cease giving the attention they deserve.

# FROM VARIETY TO VIDEO AND BACK

Being able to process a large variety of data types has become one of the characteristics of big data tool sets. Earlier generations of database and business intelligence software did a fairly good job of working with well-defined and structured data sets that all conformed to a known schema. Big data systems can also process unstructured data, which, as its name suggests, is data that have no predefined data model or organization. Ideally, a system should deal with both types of data to make the most accurate security assessments, warnings, and forensic records.

Most of the data types in traditional security software systems have been structured. Sensor data, log files, event records, and administrative activities all produced (albeit proprietary) data records that conform to an overall schema imposed by the developers. Analyzing these types of records fits nearly within the capabilities of traditional business intelligence tools and the relational databases that are typically used to store this information.
