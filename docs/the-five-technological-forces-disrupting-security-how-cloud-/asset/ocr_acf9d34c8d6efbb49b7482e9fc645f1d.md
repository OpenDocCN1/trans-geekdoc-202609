# CURRENT STATE OF PLAY

Dozens of organizations and consortia work in the field of IoT standards. They each have their own distinct mission and focus on different aspects of standardization to varying degrees. Some are in competition, and some are complementary. Some focus on local networking interoperability, for example, while others refine communication standards for wide area networks. Some focus on device identity and authentication, while others work toward the adoption of standardized metadata to create “platform of platforms.” Some focus on radio protocols, and others work toward the adoption of common semantics so that everything gets the same way every time.

In short, the world of IoT has grown large—quickly. It is difficult to navigate and difficult to even know what's relevant to your own interests. Comp matters whatever you know might be outdated by years from now. However, there have been some gains in areas that will have lasting impacts.

Security organizations need not pay attention to all of the standards. Many lower-level standards that you never interact with directly. Many are bundled into products that don't always manifest at the level of user experience. Some of them, however, will matter a great deal as your organization begins to leverage IoT.

A number of the major IoT standards organizations and consortia have joined forces and begun toward common specifications, collectively recognizing the short-term pain of unification and harmonization is better than the ongoing chaos of fragmentation. The members of these groups are some of the most powerful holdnames in electronics, chip manufacturing, networking, and telecommunications, so they have the weight to make these standards successful.

In a similar spirit of unification, some large technology companies have published or open-sourced their own proprietary frameworks. This is essentially the old IBM or Microsoft playbook: we’re big enough that we can create an ecosystem of our own with enough benefits that people will gladly play our game.

Such propetary frameworks are not all bad when it comes to building consensus. Compared with the yearlong process it takes for groups of competing companies to develop technical standards from scratch, it can be a blessing for a single organization to hand down a fully realized vision to implement.
