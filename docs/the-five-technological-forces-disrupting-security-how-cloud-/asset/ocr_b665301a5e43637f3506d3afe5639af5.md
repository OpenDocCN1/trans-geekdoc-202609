In the public sphere, for example, NIST collects and publishes such data in a National Vulnerability Database (NVD) that provides a centralized resource for security researchers and practitioners. Mitre’s closely related and federally funded Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) is “a dictionary of publicly known information security vulnerabilities and exposures.” The federal government also maintains a Computer Emergency Readiness Team (US-CERT) that respond to major cyber incidents around the world with goal of making the Internet safe for everyone.

And there are numerous private resources, such as IBM’s X-Force Exchange, which is a library of shared security intelligence data, including 700 terabytes of raw aggregated security information for researchers. The service includes real-time data that can be useful in preventing or limiting damage from current or ongoing attacks. At an ingestion rate of 15 billion security events per day, X-Force provides a cloud-based platform that allows organizations to collaborate on security incidents and responses.

# ADOPTION, TRANSFORMATION, AND STRATEGY

Adoption of big data within commercially available security systems is virtually zero out of a few pioneering features in smart home products. I wish it were otherwise, but big data will not transform much of anything in the commercial security market for least the next 3–5 years. It won’t be in the market for least that long.

Over time, big data will eventually follow the path of many other technologies. What once differentiators become commoditized features that every vendor is expected to include. At point, there will be those who have kept pace an incorporate a big data strategy into their product design and those who haven’t. The change will be similar to the much earlier falling away of vendors who do not make the transition to the cloud.
