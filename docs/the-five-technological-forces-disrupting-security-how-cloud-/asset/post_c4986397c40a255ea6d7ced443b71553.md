
Being a late adopter isn't all bad. We have the benefit of the new technologies matured and worked out the kinks. Cloud technologies presented numerous software development and deployment challenges during their infancy in the late 1990s.

- Expense. Each company essentially had to write its own applications from top to bottom; they had to “roll our own” and find what worked the hard way.
- Lack of tools. Software tools and frameworks had not yet caught up with the unique demands of cloud systems. Developers often created their own, added to overall effort to launch new products.
- Developer scarcity. Software developers who understand how to build these systems were rare, expensive, and in high demand. (I guess some don't change.) Consultants were daycharging high fees to all the companies who wanted to “do something about this Internet thing.”
- Infrastructure. Managed infrastructure services like AWS and Azure didn't exist at anywhere near their current level of mastery. This forced application service providers to bear the burden of their data centers. Everyone needed their own computer and networking experts and lots of capital for equipment.
- Broadband availability. Not least, the broadband network connectivity now taken for granted was spotty; every sales called a discovery process to determine whether a potential customer had the bandwidth to sign on.
- Cyber security fears. Fear of cyber attacks on cloud providers cannot be underestimated as an inhibitor of early sales in this space. People instinctively feel safer with a computer inside their own building than one outside of it.

By the time physical security caught on to the relevance of cloud computing a dozen years later, many of these problems had been solved. On one hand, this was good for physical security companies that had been slowed down to a toe in the water; they could move forward with lower implementation costs and risks than those who'd taken the plunge a decade before. However, it also meant that they'd invested another decade's worth of systems development (and capital) into single-tenant, client-server bases.

# SKATING TO WHERE THE PUCK HAS BEEN

The timeword hockey analogy by Wayne Gretzky has become a familiar business affair. It is usually meant to signal that a company or product is forward-looking and anticipates that customer demand will lead. Many people would use it to describe their own company's posture toward cloud computing. However, it could be the truth to say that the industry is not skating to where the puck is going rather to where the puck arrived 5 or more years ago—at least as far as the rest of the enterprise software world is concerned.

As a newcomer to the security industry in 2001, I wanted to catch up. I voraciously read many of the issues of trade magazines as I could get my hands on. The company names were all new to me, as were the product categories, the channel model, and almost everything about physical security. I did bring with me, nearly 15 years of experience with IP technologies, first as a programmer and then as a systems engineer and consultant. I knew it was fast, reliable, secure, and increasingly the backbone of American commerce.

Imagine my surprise when I encountered a prominent magazine featuring a point-counter-style article that pitted a pro-IP position against an anti-IP. The pro: use of IP technology in physical space was both inevitable and positive. The opposition was reactionary and bizarre: IP technology will never be good enough for the family jewels of the security industry, and we use it at peril.

That was 15 years ago. No one would make the argument against IP in general or the Internet as unsuitable for any kind of digital communications. It's not even a debatable subject. As if to prove the point, telecoms have been replacing copper wire with fiber optics for years, rendering moot the opposing position that argued for sticking with plain old telephone service.

Debate closed. Finally.

# SaaS BY ANY OTHER NAME

I believe the security industry always believed in SaaS, but we just didn't know it. Consider the evidence:

- For decades, we've used pooled computing resources to provide centralized monitoring.
- For decades, we've provided on-demand self-service to create new accounts on the fly.
- For decades, we've provided broad (telephone) network access to shared computing services.
- For decades, we've required customers to store private data at off-site locations.
- For decades, we've provided pay-by-drink measured services, with monthly costs proportional to consumption.

# CHAPTER 2: The Run-Up to Cloud

Perhaps more salient than any of these, the entire residential side of the industry is structured around a financial model that reduces upfront costs in favor of flat monthly payments, replacing capital expenditures with operating expense in the process.

Clearly, the core tenets of cloud computing and its financial benefits aren't new to the security industry. Far from it. Recurring monthly revenue (RMR)—more generally known as subscription revenue on the IT side of the tracks—has the financial engine of its origins are often linked with the invention of the printing press in the 15th century. Pivotal invention lowered the cost of production—and, as importantly, the time to produce—to a level where higher publication frequencies could support predictable revenue streams. While historians just cite the printing press as one of the most influential inventions in human history, enabling subscription revenue stands as an equal accomplishment in the history of business models.

In the 20th century, the invention of alarm monitoring services did the same for security that the printing press had done for publishing five centuries earlier. They made it centralized and automated instead of dispersed and manual. The technology shifted a corresponding economic shift that supports a pay-as-you-go revenue model. In this light, the security industry could claim to have invented the subscription computing model long before the current incarnation.

# MARKETERS RUN WITH IT

When marketing departments rise up in unison and start singing from the same choir book, take the access control and simply appended the word "cloud" to its company name. In an era when Microsoft dominated the industry, nearly all security applications required users to install client software. These systems were usually accessible only on the corporate network or by dial-up VPN.

The IP-based products of the early 2000s improved the wiring of security systems, but they did not necessarily change the topology or structure. You still had a local PC running client-server software to control local panels, cameras, and other peripherals.

Who could blame them, with all the misrepresentation? This sort of hype and confusion is typical during the early stages of new technologies. Fast-forwarding to the present, a quick look at conference topics or product marketing indicates that nearly everyone in the industry has become a cloud evangelist. In fact, cloud has gone from being a differentiator to a must-have. How did the stalwarts and doubters finally come around?

# INTERNET PROTOCOL BLAZES THE TRAIL

Internet protocols (IP) define the Internet, which course enables the cloud. These protocols emerged while creating ARPANET, a Department of Defense precursor to the public Internet that linked the computers at research facilities across the country into a single network to share data and computing tasks. Since the first widely distributed protocols in 1974, the collection of technical specifications for the Internet sits at roughly 10,000 documents (and counting).

As in most industries, IP protocols first gained the attention of physical security (not cloud computing itself). Used mostly for point-to-point connections on local area networks (LANs), the IP protocol suite enabled security software to communicate with peripheral devices on networking cables rather than older, clumsier types of wiring. While this was a big step forward in terms of cost and convenience, it did nothing to the fundamental architecture of security systems. You still had a local PC running client-server software to control local peripherals.

Today, IP devices pervade the industry. Thick clients on PCs are all dead. IP cameras outsell analog cameras. The remaining on-premise server systems provide browser-based interfaces for access control and video management accessible on the local network, albeit not from a cloud service. On the whole, the industry has made a complete transition to IP communications, with only a few holdouts.
