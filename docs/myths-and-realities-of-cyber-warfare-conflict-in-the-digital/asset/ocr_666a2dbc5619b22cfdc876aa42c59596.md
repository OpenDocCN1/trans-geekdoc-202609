Although this is correct from an analytical standpoint, it does not address the question of psychological impact providing a multiplier effect; historian Alex Roland aptly noted that psychological effects are surprisingly useful in combat. Furthermore, psychological impact lies at the heart of terrorists' objectives, even compared to a nation-state. A reasonable conclusion might be that although nation-states are more likely to provide the resources needed for a spectacular cyberattack a non entity as a terrorist group would be more likely to desire one. A decade ago, analysts explained that "terrorist groups in general do not have the expertise to conduct advanced or complex cyber attacks" and therefore for the time have been "limited to exploiting the same basic vulnerabilities that are constantly targeted by sands of hackers around the world."67

Perhaps this explains statements such as the U.S. deputy for information warfare stating that although

today we face the threats from both individual lone wolf of actors out there [and] nation-states ... it's the nation-state actors that keep me awake at night and that we have to be more concerned about . . In spite of the fact that crimeware and commodity malware is out there and potentially hitting our networks every day ... the big issues that we have to worry about are coming from the nation-state actors: Russia, China, Iran.68

That said, while the consequences of a nightmare kinetic-effect nation-state cyberattack beggar the imagination and represent a legitimate topic of concern, they currently remain hypothetical. This can invite imaginations to run wild, it invites others to discount the chances of such an event.
