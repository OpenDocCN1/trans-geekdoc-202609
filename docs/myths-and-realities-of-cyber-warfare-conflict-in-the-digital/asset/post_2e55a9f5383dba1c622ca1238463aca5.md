This realization seems to explain the intent in conceptualizing the role of resilience in cyber defense. Resilience—the ability to continue despite intrusions—has been suggested as a way to raise the bar for attackers,35, and it is sometimes even implied to serve a different function. Conti and Raymond point out that, far from being “just resident software,” vulnerabilities also appear throughout “firmware, network protocols, security procedures, human users, physical security,” and elsewhere. Beyond software coding, complexity breeds markets, as does a rush to market or to field a technology and anaundiced attitude regarding inconvenient processes to limit errors.38 These factors apply across the spectrum of cybersecurity issues and are applicable points regarding security even broadly.

On the technical side of the cybersecurity equation lie weaknesses that pose the prerequisite gaps in the armor through which an exploit can be used. The fact that software imperfections can be leveraged through technical attack explains the philosophy of blaming the victims for technical cyberattacks, as described in Chapter 3. But “everything electronically typically has a vulnerability in it. There is no such thing as a perfectly secure system.” High-end commercial software is estimated to contain an average of one error in 20,000 lines of code.39 Internet history has seen massive growth in the software that is widely used. The Windows 95 operating system fit within ten million lines of code; XP appeared six years later and was forty million lines long, and its successors from Vista onward have extended about fifty million lines. Average rates of errors would suggest two and a half thousand software errors, on machines used on billions of devices worldwide. This contributes to global standardization on Microsoft products has drawn from some in the security community.40.

As a result, “if you put enough resources against it, you will find a vulnerability somewhere.. Furthermore, software problems have been compared to roaches: “when you find one can bet that there are a free dozen you didn’t see.” While marketplace preferences of “fast and cheap over good” likely accelerate the rates at which mistakes are made, the task of producing millions of flawless code boggles the imagination.41 It is useful, if only reassuring, to recognize that any coding error in the kind of vulnerability can be used to form an exploit, and malware does not necessarily need only a single exploit to be successfully utilized.

Other factors sharpen the security challenges posed by lengthening software. Some trends such as BYOD (bring your own device) and the push to connect systems that had not been designed for connectivity with the broader world. Both trends aim to reduce costs of operation, respectively by shifting device acquisition and maintenance onto users and through more efficient monitoring of industrial or infrastructure systems.42 However, these moves also mean that more will be expected (or assumed) of users who are inexperienced in security and systems that people rely on for critical services will be more accessible than they had historically been to third parties.

Growth in the attack surface poses another area for security consideration. Schneider reminds us that “as the system gets more complex, the attack surface gets larger” and more complicated to defend, and “we are designing systems that are getting faster than our ability to secure them” is improving. ## THE IMPACT OF THE INTERNET OF THINGS

Science and technology journalist and author James Burke, in his land-work Connections, introduced the topic of technological dependency by describing systems that are misunderstood and eventually malfunctioning as being “technology traps.” The eagerness with online connectivity has been thrust into an ever-expanding range of products that makes unclear how “noncomputerized alternatives” could remain available to interested buyers.⁴5

The shift from internet protocol version 4 to version 6 (called IPv6) reflects planning responding to the deluge of internet connectivity, connected items that already flows onto the world's markets and networks. Google executives suggest that "the internet is becoming and more pervasive... a little water or electricity; you cannot live without it, or you will be without it."⁴⁶ The comparison of internet access and services to basic utilities is interesting. In modern history, the world's most prosperous countries have defined basic needs in increasingly expansive ways, and the identification of needs has been soon associated with ostensible guarantees to access, and frequently to changes in scrutiny and regulation. A consensus about the “utility-zation” of the internet presuppes answers to how access and regulation might change as a result.

The comparison to utilities matters in another way that touches directly to conflict. The first manipulation of SCADA occurred in the spring of 2000, with the Maroochy Shire that resulted in raw sewage being released to endanger the local public and damage the local environment. Inexorable internet-connection of infrastructure and other systems raises the prospect of similar events becoming commonplace. Libicki, notable for his work to be defied, worries that the cyber concerns he considers to be, hijacking IoT devices expands opportunities to wreak kinetic effects through cyberattacks. Libicki and others have noticed that most IoT technologies “will be owned by people unable or unwilling to pay requisite attention to “security” and who may not recognize how weakly secured IoT devices are by default or how their tasks (such as in refrigerators or other such appliances) involve their being operational and connected 24/7.⁴⁷
