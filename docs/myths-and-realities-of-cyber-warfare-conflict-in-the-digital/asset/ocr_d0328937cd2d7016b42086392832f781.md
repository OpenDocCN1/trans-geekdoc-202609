# War's Character and Nature, and the Stuxnet Trap

Stuxnet stands as both the sole example of a kinetic-effect cyberweapon and as an exception to many of the ruling patterns regarding weaponry, the urge to extrapolate from its example carries uncertainty. This has prevented comment about the future of cyberwar, based on the experiences and interpretations of Stuxnet.

Important aspects remain unclear a decade after the malware's discovery in the spring of 2010. Different sources disagree about the spread of the malware's final version. Some analysts have described Stuxnet as “a well-designed cyber weapon,” and others used it as evidence that “a cyberweapon could be on a short leash.”72 In contrast, one New York Times article in the months after the malware's discovery declared that “Stuxnet was splattered on thousands of computer systems around the world, and much of its impact has been on those systems.” Symantec reported that the initial infections to reach a total of 100,000 devices located in more than 100 different countries. An executive at Microsoft reflected in 2018 that “we essentially had to end defending over a billion customers in a response to what was ultimately meant to be a highly targeted, highly precise action.”73 Of course, different vantage points can shape diverging views of what constitutes the impact of an attack. Whether it is an insert piece of code half the size of a single camera phone image, on one hand, or the shadow of a cyberweapon, on the other, depends in part on perspective.

Libicki seems to conclude that uncertainty may prove to form a pattern with cyberattacks; in contrast to the tools for battle damage assessment leaving a kinetic strike, cyberweapons “may leave even the victim scratching his head about what the damage was.” Rid noted that the slow and erratic trend of eroding system efficiency helped preserve the malware’s secret, as the malware was believed to have run for two years before its discovery. Other researchers have suggested that its belated discovery was retarded by the same secretive and hermetic approach to security-related issues that had made the enrichment technologies such an otherwise inaccessible target in the first place.”74

Despite important and lingering ambiguities, Stuxnet has inspired some notable predictions about the future of struggle in the cyber domain. Keto identified “doctrinal quandaries” about making the malware, including issues of “tactical viability,” the potential of the technology to be adopted and repurposed by other entities, and “anxieties over the dangerous precedent that the operation would work.”75 Other experts predicted that a mere six months would elapse before copycat attacks were conducted by criminal groups eager to leverage sophisticated code.”76 Credible accounts of such an occurrence with respect to this code had not appeared in ten years, although analogous allegations have been made regarding repurposed weaponization of other leaked software.
