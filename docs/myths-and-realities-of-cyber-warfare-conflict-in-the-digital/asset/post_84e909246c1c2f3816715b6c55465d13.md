The term “hacktivist” for a hacker-activist is not new, and examples of hacktists date at least to the last days of the Cold War with computer-savvy members of the nuclear movement. The mindset of hackers feeds into traits seen in hacktivism. One cybersecurity expert has explained that for hackers, “there is a shared ethical and moral set of values; there is a shared culture of things that they find funny, and other people would not find funny.” Making the computer for all pursuits, spanning work and socialization, connects with transcending of boundaries and applying the discipline of hacking to everyday life.”29 If this is the case for hackers, one might suggest that hacktivists these characteristics are combined with some common sense that hacking should be used to pursue a shared vision of the world that aligns with idealistic ideas and boundarylessness. Holding these views (which may vary too much to smoothly apply) is the long pole in the tent for hacktivism; considerable variations in actual hacking skills exist among members.

Easily the most commonly identified hacktivist “group” is Anonymous, and it illustrates the dynamics of hacktivism. To a large extent, the political campaigns conducted by Anonymous grew from a zeitgeist focused on crude “lulz” humor directed at individuals, societies, and institutions that Anonymous members deemed anathe to the world. Hacktivists are by definition self-motivated, and this represents a double-edged sword. Provided that hacktivists are sustained by some source of motivation (and provided that their material needs are at least in the short term met somehow), they can operate without provision by the kinds of external material support that might be required for attackers a “nebulous focus” seemingly with “a life of its own,” it could be directed only in a short-term, limited, and narrow sense. Persuading clusters of individuals to imbue any specific, shared, and enduring sense of purpose can pose a challenge. This explains the reasons for observations such as the “fundamental divide between those who believed in Anonymous’s roots in fun and lulz, the new, activist direction” promoted by participants who wanted the schadenfreude of attacks to serve some larger purpose. Because membership in such an amorphous organization cannot be formalized, the entity’s population included “cyber punks” interested in leveraging an existing brand to build or sustain their own diverse agendas.34

Anonymous participants have been quoted saying things as, “sometimes I care about something, but the next minute I don’t,” or “something will happen and you suddenly care about it… matters for thirty seconds.” This phenomenon parallels the impact of modern news cycles,35 which present a succession of rumored or semi-relevant topics whose significance disappears as successor topics are continually presented to replace them. By the coining of the term “fake news,” this pattern was identified and dubbed “junk news,” alluding to the way it simulated news while constituting the information equivalent of junk food empty.36 Strategies are hard to build or sustain in this environment.

For those aiming to mute damaging effects against targets, even on a short-term basis, harnessing the latent power of participants could seem valuable. Likewise, being a part of something larger and victorious carries cache, particularly for people who feel an absence of purpose. Participation in Anonymous could lead to eventual disillusionment, but it also offered a “gateway to political activism” that some participants embraced. Participants in Anonymous sometimes marveled at the inaccurate ways in which media outlets and authors have tried to describe the “group” as “hackers,” when their community resists such precise terms: it does not possess such precise terms. A threat to the so-called center of gravity, the lynchpin of society or government forces, is a long-standing concern in the Western conception of warfare; but since hackivist lack clear parallels to these centers of gravity, finding key people or assets to target is more complicated. Nation-states possess vital and frequently identifiable assets, and these must be defended from attack.
