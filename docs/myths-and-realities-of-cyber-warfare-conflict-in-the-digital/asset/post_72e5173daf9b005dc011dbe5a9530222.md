## USER POLLUTANT OR USER PAY DIRT?

Countering the concerns about inaccuracy are yet more arguments that arguably imply threats to privacy. With enough access to enough data, this outlook asserts, a relatively clear picture emerges despite whatever incidental noise occurs. Singer and Brooking, while not necessarily celebrating the conclusion, note that "the endless churn of content produced on the internet day provides a limitless pipeline of data with which to train … increasingly intelligent machines," which will "predict, accurately, what one person might want to buy or even whom they might vote for."

Joel Brenner, a former senior counsel at the National Security Agency, has noted "most of what is worth knowing in the world is not classified, and technological convergence will make axiom that true ever. In an era of data breaches of increasingly monumental scale, Paul Roszweweig argues that leaks will reveal less than "the patient piecing together of open-source materials" in a range of contexts. To some extent, this dynamic is not new; as a relatively open society during the Cold War, the United States was regularly more vulnerable to open-research society by its adversaries than was for the Soviet Union. This, in fact, one of the reasons that U.S. policy makers opted to improve surveillance technologies via reconnaissance satellites, going so far as to impact the country's posture on the making of space policy.

In another sense, however, the advent and popularity of social media magnify the impact of a tendency that had been in evidence before well the creation of cyberspace. Social media explicitly invites acquaintholders to answer prompts, such as, "What's on your mind?" Mountains of data are solicited from users by platforms that are presented as being free to use, but the monetary free services are made possible by the monetization of the resulting data. Some pundits have gone on to assert that users' provision of this data should be considered a form of digital library. Researchers in 2015 reported that, armed with four data points regarding three months' credit card metadata, they could identify 90% of individuals. Social media facilitates the haphazard sharing of data and it also enables the swift and widespread dissemination of what has been shared, and this can pose security risks as well as economic vulnerabilities.

Some analysis indicates that the problems are likely to mount as time progresses. Singer and Brooking have claimed that foreign intelligence operatives saw President Donald Trump's Twitter activity as a reservoir of data from which to compile intelligence studies; however, since Trump's Twitter activity began in his sixties, "nearly every future politician or general or soldier or voter will have a much bigger dataset, from much earlier in life available for analysis.16 Continuous engagement with social media makes much data visible to friends, followers, potentially any and every onlooker, and this is the data that Singer and Brooking refer. Social media platforms offer a vantage point from which survey, store, and potentially monetize an even greater array of user data.

Additionally, Schneider’s point that “when a computer stores your data, there’s always a risk of exposure” makes inescapable.17 Users may feel squeezed between a need or counsel to utilize the technologies of the information age, on one hand, and a need or desire to shield themselves from the implications of their data being used to exploit them. Throughout of the Middle East, privacy is such a requirement as a desire, that corresponds to the popularity of more discrete networking sites such as WhatsApp and Snapchat relative to openly alternatives like Facebook and Twitter.18

Alternatively, the kind of “noise” that have been described earlier as reducing the reliability of data can be actively embraced. One example is search term “chaffing.” Parallel to its World War II precedent when Allied bombers sought to defeat German air defense radar by dropping strips of aluminum foil, chaffing saturates the sensors rather than seeking to starve them. When successful, chaffing confuses sensors until they are unable to distinguish the decoy and the genuine. Although a clearer notion, it is far from foolproof: unlike the false closely resemble the genuine ones, the effort will be ineffective; addition-even if they are unable to determine which searches are genuine and which are chaff, they can be expected to consider the deliberate consumption of resources and saturation of extraneous data to be an imposition. Conti, while noting that he considers free-to-tools and services by Google and others as beneficial, that data disclosure is most effectively prevented by avoiding its being generated, because “a bulletproof, anonymous web-browsing experience doesn’t exist.”19

Digital trails are the by-product of activity in the digital age. Schneider asserted that “data is the pollution problem of the information age, and protecting privacy is the environmental challenge.”20 There is no sense in which digital exhaust can be understood–not in competition with Schneider’s interpretation but perhaps alongside it. Activity online leaves digital exhaust constantly as a person’s breathing exhausts carbon dioxide. The materials that we breathe out are a vital resource to entities within the ecosystem–and this is true biologically also well. It might be too much to imagine the entities (corporate and strategic) that utilize data as being the cyberspace counterparts to plants that convert our carbon dioxide exhaust into fresh breathable oxygen. Even a mutually beneficial outcome in the ecosystem stems from the fact that a symbiosis is mutually advantageous, and not all utilizations of data exhaust have a beneficial or even neutral impact on individuals producing the data. Nonetheless, the ecosystem has grown so that people's data exhaust is far more valuable to some entities than being so much empty air.

## DATA—RAW MATERIALS FOR ECONOMICS

Conti observes that, rather than being either evil or altruistic, companies aim to secure a profit, and their policies are structured to support this. To support a dynamic ability to maintain profitability when an environment change, policies (including privacy policies) are continually subject to change.¹ A long-standing feature across many online services has unchanged, but there is no such thing as a free lunch, and free-to-use services require alternative methods to deliver revenue stream; sites like Wikipedia may rely on donations, but search engines, email providers, others monetize data to third parties. These, in turn, use the data for advertising or other purposes. The built-in logic of this system is collecting more data about a customer facilitates understanding the customer deeply, which enables continuing and even increased services and the consequent flow of economically valuable data. As early as the mid-1990s, some analysts noticed that “this private intelligence-gathering gives people the creeps, but the model developed, and it progressed."²

Schneider amies that “these are not good or fair bargains,” but they are readily accepted as online users free-to-use functionality, and as a result “the overwhelming bulk of surveillance is corporate, or it occurs because we hastily agree to it.” This pattern of collective decisions leaves individuals with unennially narrowed practical options, as illustrated by the fact that significantly publicly available discourse occurs on social media platforms or in the comments sections of reports on media outlets. However, this publicly readable information resides in legally private spaces, since the companies that provide the services set the policies that govern usage. This implies a connection between private spaces' policies about accessible speech and those ability to translate the resulting data into profitability. The interests of hosts and clients intersect, but they are distinct not synonymous.³
