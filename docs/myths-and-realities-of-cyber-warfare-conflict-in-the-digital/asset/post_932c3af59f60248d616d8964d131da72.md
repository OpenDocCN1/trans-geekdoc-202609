## Myths and Realities of Cyber Warfare

Whereas Facebook’s epic growth derives from bandwagon effect and the perception of community among users connecting with friends, Twitter replies on brevity and rapidity for the dissemination of compact announcements. A landmark study of weaponization of social media notes Twitter “focuses on bringing people together and more on bringing them together.”25 Tweets are “just little blurs of information that you can put out there, instead of having to have exhaustive discussions on Facebook.”26 Twitter’s active user population stood at 330 million in 2018, and Japan is the only country in which Twitter actually exceeds all other social media rivals in popularity. In terms of size and earnings, Twitter lags behind Facebook. But it draws considerable attention, which magnifies its impact in global discourse. One researcher explained that “Twitter may not always be the best medium for receiving a clear and impartial picture of events during a conflict, it’s the perfect way to get your version of events out immediately.” The result is “without cause,”27 which is tantamount to an ability to reshape the contours of various forms of discourse (including debates) continuously.

That power is extremely useful in wartime propaganda as in other information operations. With respect to physical conflicts and less overt strategies, analysts note similarities in how information operations utilize social media such as Twitter. Steering online discourse takes various forms, known as trend distribution, trend hijacking, and trend creation. Trend distribution refers to inserting messages into existing lines of discourse in the hope that they will attract attention; an example would be tweeting a flurry of plentiful statements or images, each tweet carrying a hashtag selected for its popularity rather than its pertinence. Trend distribution could be thought of as the Twitter counterpart of earlier social media phenomenon of photobombing people posting selfies. The price of trend distribution is low but the likely payoff is low too.

Trend hijacking and trend creation deliver higher payoffs but demand more effort. Hijacking involves selecting a popular trend and posting so many tweets about a particular topic (unrelated to the trend) that the original (topical) traffic is washed out. Hijacking requires much more effort than distribution, but successfully hijacking can provoke wider attention. Creation involves launching a new and pertinent hashtag and elevating it to the prominence of a trend through a massive volume of traffic rivaling the size of existing popular trending topics of discussion. Given the volume of traffic required for hijacking and creation, these two approaches are expected to rely heavily on the use of autonomous programs called bots that simulate human stakeholders.28 Distribution, hijacking, and creation are not exclusively undertaken for strategic purposes, but they encompass the prominent approaches taken by information operations planners to leverage social media. They and other actors have embraced the opportunities for dialog manipulation, dubbed "commanding the trend." Evidence has been seen in the fact that Twitter has bots for 5% of accounts in 2014 and 15% by 2017.29 It means that the proportion of bots in the Twitter landscape tripled at a time when the overall growth during the same period registered a mere 15% and 20% growth.30
