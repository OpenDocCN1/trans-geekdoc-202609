Anonymous participants have been quoted saying things as, “sometimes I care about something, but the next minute I don’t,” or “something something will happen and you suddenly care about it… matters for thirty seconds.” This phenomenon parallels the impact of modern news cycles,35 which present a succession of rumored or semi-relevant topics whose significance disappears as successor topics are continually presented to replace them. By the coining of the term “fake news,” this pattern was identified and dubbed “junk news,” alluding to the way it simulated news while constituting the information equivalent of junk food empty.36 Strategies are hard to build or sustain in this environment.

For those aiming to mute damaging effects against targets, even on a short-term basis, harnessing the latent power of participants could seem valuable. Likewise, being a part of something larger and victorious carries cache, particularly for people who feel an absence of purpose. Participation in Anonymous could lead to eventual disillusionment, but it also offered a “gateway to political activism” that some participants embraced. Participants in Anonymous sometimes marveled at the inaccurate ways in which media outlets and authors have tried to describe the “group” as “hackers,” when their community resists such precise terms: it does not possess the organizational traits implicit in “group,” and while some participants have been skilled hackers, many of its “hacker” members appear to be little more than undertrained script script-kiddies.37
