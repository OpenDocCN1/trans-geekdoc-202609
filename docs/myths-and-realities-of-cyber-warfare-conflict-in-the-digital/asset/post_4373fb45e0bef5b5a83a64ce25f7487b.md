Apparently, employees of the troll farms are not uniformly gung-ho zealots. Many of the employees are desperate for work and have stories like Vitaly Bespalov, who had attempted to find work as a journalist and accepted a position at a troll farm after finding no relevant openings elsewhere and being assured that the work “will be neutral” rather than propagandistic. Early phases of the work involve massaging terminology in existing articles so that would align with the state’s agenda and also remain high-ranking matches when Google and other search engines sought stories on topics such as the fighting in eastern Ukraine. Redubbing Ukrainian troops into battalions of “volunteers” or “guards” and retitling separatist rebels into “militia” were minor adjustments that threw a shadow on the legitimacy of a defending state while legitimizing rebel groups. Terminological tweaks turned Ukraine (a country into a region lacking sovereignty). For many, the work seemed like easy money. But the monotonous propagation of lies and inventive rumors—all doing the electronic version of telling one’s voice—can become exhausting. Being a troll is emotionally dissecting work, especially for someone contemplating its impact, but even generally it means placing one’s self inside the house of mirrors half the day and bending reality to the creation of political surrealism. One former troll, who had been a Russian Internet Research Agency employee, was describing the difficulty of pretending “to be red from Kentucky, then... some white guy from Minnesota who worked all his life, paid lives in poverty,” and then fifteen minutes later having to “write in the slang of [African] Americans from New York.”^{41} Those who have left employment as trolls encountered violent threats and recruitment for disengaging or speaking out.
