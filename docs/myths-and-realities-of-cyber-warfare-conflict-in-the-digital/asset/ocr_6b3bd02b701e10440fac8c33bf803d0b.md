War does not occur in a vacuum, and changes in technology or tactics will trigger reactions among both friends and adversaries. Perceptions of cyber activity mean for war almost certainly impacted by revelations about what cyberattacks accomplish. Figures Hayden have already stated as much when referring to the first kinetic-effect cyberattack attributed to public objectives as being a crossing of the Rubicon. Conti and Raymond unambiguously that “cyberspaces can be destructive and lethal” either as first-order effects or as second-order effects triggered by the malfunction of key systems, by the fact that “even without malicious instructions, the lack of a computer to guide a process can cause physical destruction.”76

As other forms of limited war, the concept of conflict contained to cyberpace is predicated on the concept that the stakes are not sufficiently high on either side for either side (or a third party with interest in the conflict outcome or stirring of interests) that actions will escalate to include kinetic actions or cyberattacks intended to exert kinetic effects. A related argument appears from pundits like Richard Clarke, posting that inadequate defensive tools could accelerate escalation beyond the cyber domain.\77

# VISIONS OF CYBER AT THE FOREFRONT

The more optimistic appraisals nonetheless advance ideas that the cyber domain can reduce the destruction that has traditionally been an inherent part of warfare. These perspectives rest on a precondition that the parties to a conflict each seek to pursue their aims while wanting to avoid bloodshed, and these arguments implicitly go on to assume that the avoidance of injury and destruction will trump other policy goals in the midst of a conflict.

Such advocates point to the opportunities to limit conflict.
