Social networking sites entail a range of potential dangers. They include the open-source harvesting of posted data for ulterior purposes; a 2010 survey found that over half of U.S. social media users posted "sensitive information that makes them vulnerable to cybercrime."^27 This statistic is alarming, because it is so avoidable at the level of the accountholder. It points to the need for increased awareness of cyber hygiene, and more, an understanding of how carelessly shared information can be leveraged for illicit purposes.

Users themselves are by no means the source of vulnerability, and the others for illicit data access lie beyond the accountholder's realistic opportunity to prevent abuse. These include technical methods to lure users into inadvertently allowing malware onto their machines and hacking of the companies themselves, in order to access the troves of data they collect in exchange for providing free-to-use services. This data represents mountains of revenue, which are tempting targets of cybercriminals to access them illicitly. The apparent and reported vulnerability of such data is a cause of outrage.
