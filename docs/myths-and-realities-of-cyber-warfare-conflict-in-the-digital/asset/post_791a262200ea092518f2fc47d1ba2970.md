By February 2018, forensic experts found that the attacks had been made possible by the fact that a “clunky” accounting-software remained in widespread use across many Ukrainian businesses despite not being supported by its master for the past four years.48 March 2018 saw ransomware attacks targeting government institutions in Atlanta, Georgia; these attacks, one example of a panoply of ransomware events targeting municipal agencies around the United States and beyond, cost more than $2.5 million in recovery and was traced in November 2018 to Iran.49

Third-party repurposing of malware forms yet another source of concerning ransomware. After the discovery of the Stuxnet malware in 2010 and research leading forensics to conclude that it was developed to target Iranian nuclear enrichment technologies, authors on cybersecurity topics voiced concern that the extremely sophisticated Stuxnet malware would be reverse-engineered and repurposed for use by criminals, particularly for launching ransomware. One author wisely noted that “subsequent attacks might not be as carefully crafted or controlled” as the case when the original designers worked to ensure that only the intended target was impacted by the presence of the code.50 Conclusively derivative ransomware harkening to Stuxnet has yet to be reported in the past decade. In contrast, the WannaCry ransomware attacks that struck impacted a reported quarter of a million computers, including many used by the National Health Service’s facilities in England and Scotland (although not apparently in Wales or Northern Ireland). Nearly four dozen hospitals were impacted. Other entities worldwide, including at least two medical facilities in Indonesia, were also impacted. The WannaCry attacks were linked to North Korea, which already had a history of using online activities to shim economic support for their expensive and ambitious military projects undertaken by one of the poorest states on the planet. Numerous reports asserted that WannaCry was created using illicitly disclosed information as a technical foundation.51

# CONCEPTUALIZING AN INFORMATION BATTLESPACE

Mark Twain is credited with the idea that there are no new ideas but simply “combinations indefinitely” of existing pieces, like the glass shards in a kaleidoscope.”53 The novelty of social media can be seen as resembling a fresh opportunity for the old pattern of influencing others’ ideas through the strategic propagation of suggestive messages. The social media agents appear to have attained a permanent state of dominance over internet usage and discourse around the world. Social media dominance and impact is likely less permanent than it appears in the first quarter of the 21st century, which implies that the current array of challenges will adjust and recede in the face of others in the future—and that traits should be identified in the weaponization of social media so that they can be recognized later as part of a pattern when they might recruit.

The emergence and importance of the digital realm do not mean that physical domains stop mattering. Also, examples of struggle within the digital realm do not mean that conflict in the tangible domains stop or that it will cease to matter. The investment of time and human energy into the digital domain does mean, however, that the implications of conflict

here cannot be ignored, because they are already occurring. Many people are engaged online to the extent that they necessarily remain in the tangible domains, they have also partly migrated to digital areas. Disengagement from the interconnected environment does not solve the problems manifested online, partly because divesting of the internet would not insulate individuals or societies or states from the turmoil it can host.

Yannakeorgos aptly noted in 2014 that “psyber warfare” has been seriously overlooked and that “in the near-term, this is the more likely use of cyber to create physical effects.”54 Advers mobilisation of cyberspace for psychological warfare or information operations has sparked periodic concern. Authors pointed out gaps overlooking the dangers of information operations via cyberspace at least as early as 2007, and analyses appearing a decade later highlight the problems stemming from public discourse being seeded with information operations messaging through social media.55 The use of novel technologies puts a new lease on the life of well-known methods for sowing discord and uncertainty in a targeted state’s homeland. Approaches that have been quickly and widely identified as manipulative can proceed for a long time unquestioned when they carry a new guise.

Some of the figures have noticed that “the battle space for the center is social media”57 also argue that the United States needs a rebooted manifestation of the U.S. Information Agency (USIA), an entity established in August 1953 under President Dwight Eisenhower at a time when the Cold War was relatively new as superpowers were racing simultaneously for arms advantages through thermonuclear weapons and for messaging superiority, especially in the uncommitted “Third World” that was emerging from the status of imperial possession by European states that had been damaged by World War II. The USIA was closed in October 1999, as U.S. policy makers celebrated the first decade of the post-Cold War world and immediately after Russia’s president tapped a former intelligence officer to become the future leader of that country.

The history of representative states’ establishing information offices to contest the information environment overseas includes some controversy, in connection with concerns that some of the materials distributed by such an office might find domestic audiences and inadvertently constitute propagating in a technical sense. Such controversy, coupled with the perception by the late 1990s that such an office was unnecessary, proved crucial in the closure of the original USIA. Advocates of a reboot organization capable of contesting online information would necessarily have to address issues of domestic exposure to the satisfaction of policy makers.
