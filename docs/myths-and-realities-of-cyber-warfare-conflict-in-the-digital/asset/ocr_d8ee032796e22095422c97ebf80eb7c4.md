Each example shows a different expression of merging tactics and technology to address challenges to conflicts of their respective eras. Despite the important impact each would have on the character of an individual war, none of the three alters the nature of war. The pike-carrying infantryman the tercio, the 18th-century musketeer, the tank commander, and any of their comrades, participate in the use of force to support a policy goal of their respective society or state. The nature of war endures, and the introduction of new tactics or technologies does not change the nature of war.

With new technologies, new domains of combat alter the character of wars, but they do not change the fundamental nature of war. However, whereas new domains frequently appear on battlefields, new domains for fighting emerge only rarely. This can lure people into imagining that the emergence of new fighting domains—could somehow be of revolutionary warfare. Moreover, most of the new domains for exploitation have emerged recently, lending a false sense of validity to the idea that new domains change the core of warfare. Whereas land combat dates to at 10,000 BCE, the first fighting at sea is believed to have taken place around 1200 BCE, after a lag of ninety centuries. In contrast, introduction of the other domains has occurred with breathtaking succession from a historical standpoint: heavier-than-air flight inaugurated the air domain in 1903, successful strategic intelligence exploitation of the space domain began in 1960, and research into the networking of computers starting in the 1960s led to the development of the World Wide Web and the internet. Thus, it is possible that a person born shortly before the Wright Brothers flight could have personally lived to see the air, space, and cyber domains radically change the character of security affairs and conflict.
