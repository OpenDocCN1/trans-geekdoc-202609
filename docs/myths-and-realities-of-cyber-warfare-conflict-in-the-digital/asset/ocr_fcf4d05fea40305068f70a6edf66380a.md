Nor was the mechanized utilization of Facebook entirely a bolt from the blue. An early and evocative misuse of social media involved the cyberbullying of thirteen-year-old Megan Meier on MySpace that ended in 2006 suicide and an enormous blow to the reputation of what been a first-generation social media icon.^17 Various regimes have exhibited that dialogue on Facebook might constitute, from their view, dangerous speech; as result, Facebook access has been blocked at various times in countries such as China and Turkey, and it (alongside YouTube and Twitter) by discontented Tunisians in January 2011 helped galvanize what would become known as the Arab Spring. Tunisian authorities, unwise to allow network traffic and block Facebook, on January 27, 2011, to eliminate all internet activity. This has been described as “the first time in Internet history that a state purposely went offline to halt information flows.^18

Historical perspective provides important reminders that social media may accelerate or magnify rather than ip facto create seemingly revolutionary dynamics. Michael Neiberg, a noted scholar specializing in World War I, observed a century-old case that matched social media mobilization in its rapid frequency. The Shandong Connection at the post-World War I Treaty of Versailles ceded Chinese territory that had earlier been controlled by Germany to Japan, who had captured it in the early phases of the war. The brazen squashing of Chinese self-determination preferences at a conference that ostensibly veiled veiled “incredibly unpopular everywhere except Japan.” Thanks to rapid communication by wireless technology, people in China learned within hours of a decision reached just west of Paris, and violent protests as the “May 4th movement ensued, while Chinese ex-patriots in Paris conducted simultaneous nonviolent demonstrations. As Neiberg observes, this visceral and mass-organized reaction by Chinese in Paris and in China happened “without WiFi, without Facebook, without #May4th. And it worked.^19 Social media can profoundly impact social mobilization, but such mobilization has happened without online social media platforms, and it has occurred before their appearance.

Silicon Valley perhaps has little sympathy for the totalitarian autocratic regimes with youthful networked protesters, but a more earnest examination of the Arab Spring might have highlighted that social media could not always be so to support political unrest and that future targets might not be so anathema in the eyes of social media’s moguls.
