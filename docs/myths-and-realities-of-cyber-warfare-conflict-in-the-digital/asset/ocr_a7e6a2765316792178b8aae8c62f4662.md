Kello, as an international relations scholar, calls cyberspace “a breeding ground for political disorder and strategic instability,^28 and the calculated spreading of misinformation clearly illustrates that dynamic. Tweeted rumors of Ebola cases in the continental United States or chemical explosions on the Gulf Coast^29 can spread quickly and illustrate how a lie can circle the Earth before a correction can be announced. The first years of the 21st century saw U.S political and military efforts focused largely, and understandably, on confronting jihadi terrorism. One government study from this “growing impact of persuasive technologies to manipulate the abstraction of reality and consequently the truth,” adding that the tendency to seek news from already trusted views has been presented “of necessary context,” perhaps heightening the changes of “nonstate actors to directly impact” perceptions and attitudes.^30 Subsequent events brought popular dissatisfaction with aspects of the campaigns against terrorist organizations and signs of renewed alertness to the approaches and capabilities of other nation-states, but the conclusions in 2005 remain relevant.

Analysts note that some cultures and organizations, such as in China, interpret the U.S military concept of OODA (observe, orient, decide, act) loop differently from U.S. strategists. Developed to illustrate aerial combat, a competitive speed advantage in OODA process provides a substantial advantage for one combatant over another. However, whereas many thinkers see gaining a speed advantage in OODA by accelerating the pace of each step, researchers suggest that Chinese strategists see opportunity in using warfare to slow the adversary’s progress through each step, gaining a comparative advantage without requiring a wholesale revision of one’s OODA processes.^31

Inducing chaos would be an effective method of slowing their OODA steps, and the cyber domain offers different avenues through which to accomplish this.
