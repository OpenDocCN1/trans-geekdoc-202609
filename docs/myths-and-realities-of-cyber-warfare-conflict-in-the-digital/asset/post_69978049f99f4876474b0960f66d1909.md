On one hand, devices that are set up and left to unattended make virtually ideal recruits for DDoS botnets; lacking protection, they can easily be hacked, and once under a bot herder's control, they will operate for extended periods. On the other hand, manipulating IoT that impacts health and safety (Libicki offers cars and drones as examples) can cause “serious harm to their owners and worse, third parties, if their controls are usurped.” These are dangers in a world where, Conti and Raymond respond, “an enemy combatant by Grandma’s toaster”; Schneider confirms that computers “hands and feet” means “the ability to affect the world in a direct physical manner. IoT devices also provide an and already leveraged opportunity for surveillance of a target.”

Experts have recognized that weapons must be sufficiently controllable to be useful. Lacking control over a weapon means firing a loose cannon. This is a valid factor to consider with respect to the ways IoT devices could conceivably be transformed into cyberweapons for harassment, ransoming, or kinetic effects. Another important caveat, however, is that not all states or nonstate actors set the same threshold with respect to what a “sufficiently controllable” weapon looks like. In the physical domains it is rife with weapons that some combatants spurned while others deemed reliable, or accurate, or precise. Having demonstrated across the land, sea, air domains, and in the exploitation of space, something similar can be expected in the cyber domain as well.

Even sober advocates of IoT, and the related concept of the Cloud, concede that IoT “offers great promise” while also meaning that “where everything is connected, then anything can be disrupted.” If thinking about the need for resilience has been justified by realizations that hackers have long permeated networks and slipped around or the security walls that characterized perimeter-security, the implications of IoT and the Cloud underscore the risk for robust security. One software executive admitted, “if you’re using Cloud, you’ve extended your attack surface.” Another, seeking to refute suggestions that the “Cloud’s not secure,” pointed to the “assume breach,” abandon the perimeter-style approach to security, simplify to customers, and “embrace ... machine learning” for defense.”

Other analysis similarly carries an undercurrent that the answer to technologically precipitated challenges involves more technology. At the Center for a New America have argued that “cloud computing may also allow smaller businesses with limited resources to better protect their information against sophisticated attacks.” At face value, this argument seems to accept the purported advantages of distributing computing and storage without weighing other considerations about how such placement might impact an adversary’s ability to access the same data. While using more technology to address a technology-enabled problem may seem counterintuitive, it is not necessarily as convoluted as it might seem—provided the additional technology is used for specific reasons and in meaningful ways. Conti and Raymond predict that the “not too distant future” will involve “counter robot tactics” in cyberwar autonomy purposes. However, “becoming a Luddite isn’t a solution” because citizenship in the 21st century means “learn[ing] to cope with these new realities.” Coping with those realities requires comprehending dynamics at play in the present and anticipating what lies ahead.

# DIGITAL FEUDALISM?

While some industry executives have suggested that the path to better cybersecurity is “a long journey” and that “taking a user perspective is the first step,” some researchers raise alarms suggesting that the user perspective is being altered in ways that redefine dynamics between human users and institutions. Both of these possibilities can be expected to impact conflict in the digital realm. Arguably, trends that are identified from one vantage point responding to the user’s perspective, when examined from another standpoint, exert a shaping influence upon the options available to users.

The interconnections that characterize the modern internet rest on interoperability. If computer history is divided into epochs, and a prehistoric era computing is distinguished from the development and then the expansion of the internet, the techniques, and technologies allowing communication across different systems by definition play a central role. Protocols written to permit connection between different kinds of systems revolutionized the utility of individual machines by constructing a network through which they could be used to link to other, different, computers. A cornerstone of the popularity of consumers computers and android devices has the connection and functionality established among them. Connection and interoperability have allowed impressive efficiencies, although some security-conscious researchers have also noted that these dynamics also provide opportunities for more sinister actors to reach and manage more devices and users.

Apple, famously or famously, spurs the type of interoperability that characterizes so much of the world of computers. The functionalities that users expect from applications nonetheless require designers to create platforms that can operate in diverse ways. For example, a smartphone is a telephone for voice calls, but it must allow “videoconferencing, downloading video clips, listening to music, using a GPS to navigate to a location, and connecting to any number of networks including Global System Mobile or Wi-Fi.” Since some of these standards entitle royalty payments for intellectual property, this can impact device and service costs.

Increased functionality brings the insulation of larger numbers of potentially different standards. This contributes to the growing complexity of development and can shelter software flaws described earlier. Businesses have displayed a noticeable awareness with respect to shouldering responsibilities and burdens associated with cybersecurity. Research asserts that “Silicon Valley must accept more of the political and social responsibility that the success of this technology has thrust upon it.” A potentially compelling case to this effect can be made; an important consideration, however, involves questions about what responsibility obligates or empowers companies to do. Responsibilities fall into areas that remain governance. Companies are predictably loathe to enter the simultaneously thankless, expensive, and controversial realm of governance. This contributes to the Wild West of confined policy makers hoping to protect intellectual property, guard national security, and preserve freedom of speech without facilitating propagation of criminal activity or violence.

However, even if Silicon Valley companies were to heed the advice given by Singer and Brookings and “accept more... political and social responsibility.” it is not clear how widely their embrace of governance roles would be greeted with exuberant enthusiasm. Those steps would empower private sector entities in determining what speech or commerce is appropriate or acceptable. Most situations will fall into greyer regions than black-or-white edges of the spectrum, and Silicon Valley (and its global counterparts) hold positions as nondemocratic governments. Entities that have been widely decried for doing little in the face of various information manipulations could be entrusted with decisions that impact an unprecedented public. Users of services and platforms are not citizens, and are not voters. While customers may seek to vote with their dollars and take business elsewhere, this can prove challenging.
