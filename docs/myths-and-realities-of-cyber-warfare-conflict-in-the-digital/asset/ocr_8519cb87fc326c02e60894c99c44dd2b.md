Cybersecurity author Demchak observed that the ease with which cyberspace enables long-distance access also means organizing multiparty alliances and coalitions for attacks more likely.^{40}

Scholars have noted, particularly in countries where political dissent or nonconformist behavior is interpreted as a serious threat to the state, hackers can direct their activities in unauthorized patriotic directions. Patriotic hacking (e.g., in Iran) provides evidence of loyalty to a government the state hosts anyway. A state may facilitate and encourage participation. Russia refused to cooperate in investigations into the cyberattacks against Estonia in 2007 and an official belonging to the United Russian political party declared, “about the cyber attack against Estonia don’t worry, that attack was carried out by my assistant.” Websites with Russian domain names appeared in the mid of cyberattacks against Georgia in 2008, as a Belarusian journalist writing for Slate describing “how easy it was for an Russian-speaking internet user to acquire the tools necessary to throw an ‘e-Molotov Cocktail.’^{41}

In turn, a state may present patriotic hacking as an alternative to punishment for nonconformists facing the legal ramifications of other domestic crimes. “Russian hackers of convicted crimes are sometimes given a choice to work for the intelligence services instead of going to prison.^{42} and it is a short leap, from this sort of effort to turn criminals into individuals and organizations for unofficial purposes. Efforts at website defence, DDoS attacks, and similar harassment and subversion are believed to be much more likely to be undertaken by patriotic hackers than trained cyber warriors.^{43} The reason is entirely practical: systematically trained cyberworkers are skilled professionals who, in the event of a conflict with a cyber component, would be needed for intricate–and arguably more impactful–activities.

In recent years, scholars have pondered the status and legitimacy of groups as patriotic hackers. Tallinn Manual 2.0 academics have concluded that “inhabitants of unoccupied territory who engage in cyber operations as part of a levee masse enjoy combat immunity and imprisonment of war status” in the event of capture, but consensus seemed to erode as to whether such levee masse be deemed possible when cyberattack skills remain fine “the cyber-capable members of [a] population”^{44} rather than being held more broadly.
