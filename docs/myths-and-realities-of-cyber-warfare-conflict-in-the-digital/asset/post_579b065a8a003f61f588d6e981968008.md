## ENCRYPTION

Predictably, encryption is as complex an issue as is espionage. Generally, the purpose of encryption is to enable confidential communications to remain private. Since technologies are not "good" or "bad," and cannot be reserved for use only by "good guys" or "bad guys," encryption technologies might be better of as used for different purposes and by different actors as is the case regarding espionage.

Nonstate actors' involvement in war illustrates a demand and use of encryption. The creation of the internet in the 1990s occurred at the same time jihadist groups consolidated their political hold on Afghanistan and as one of their former financiers, Osama Laden, formed a network of militants known as Al-Qaeda. Intent on launching periodic attacks against Western targets, planners in different locations used email to coordinate efforts and prepare a series of bombings, most of which were directed at diplomatic or military targets in Africa or the Middle East. The expansion of the internet coincided with the Global War on Terrorism, in response to Al-Qaeda's attacks against key political, strategic, and economic landmarks on the U.S. eastern seaboard. A muscular military response by the United States and other nations included a multipurpose and eventually controversial military operation in Iraq. Evident concern among jihadist forces about the possibility that commercially available encryption tools might not be secure for their communications reportedly spurred unaffiliated groups towards the development of indigenous encryption tools. The first of these, Asrar al-Mujahideen, was developed by an al-Qaeda affinity called the Global Islamic Media Fund (GIMF) in 2007 and its existence was subsequently advertised in the al-Qaeda platform Inspire.63

The advantages of privacy notwithstanding, for a variety of reasons terrorists do not always adopt practices to ensure security. For example, the extremists affiliated with the Islamic State of Iraq and Syria (ISIS) launched attacks in Paris in November 2015, but they reportedly “didn’t bother to use encryption while communicating[ing].” This neglect, which ran afoul of operational security (OPSEC) guidance distributed on contemporary ISIS forums, enabled police to raid borders before they could undertake a fo-attack on the business district of western Paris.64

A secrecy can pave a path to paranoia. Al-Qaeda reportedly created and released additional encryption tools under the GIMF banner as a false-flag so that jihadist groups would not recognize its exact origin. These tools were devised “to keep track of rival rivals,” as well as to guard against that one or more militant organization might be a decoy established by U.S. or allied entities to communicate with, identify, and entrap extremists. GIMF issued a recall in November 2013 on an encryption tool to have been created by the ISIS. Many ISIS members are reported to have favored the so-called Snowden Phone because of the Android device's prominent encryption capabilities.65

Nonstate militants and violent extremists are not the only groups desiring private communications or adopting encryption, however corporate giants in the world of electronic search, networking, and telecommunications have responded to this interest with projects and policies designed to preserve client privacy from external surveillance. Google began its investment in undersea cables as a means of carrying traffic as early as 2008. In the following years, it would initiate work on its own fiber-optic lines from the United States to Asia and Europe, spanning both the Pacific and Atlantic Oceans. Information assurance may have played a role in these developments and clearly was involved in the company’s decision to encrypt the data transmitted between its data centers. Apple, another giant of the 21st-century economy, took steps to provide encryption not only from individuals and groups but even from Apple itself. This model was promoted through the argumentation that if Apple possessed an ability to monitor communications on its devices, then any and ever every government around the world might then make claims on Apple to do so, for a variety of reasons and with a range of privacy and security implications.66

Inadequate or flawed encryption invites danger through a false sense of security, and the extreme German confidence in the World War II-era Enigma machine stands as a paramount example of this. An expediency of functionality can combine with technological confidence to precipitate inadequacies even or the absence of security tools such as encryption. Dramatic advances in the practicality of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and their increasing and diverse utilities encouraged rapid functionality that some have suggested was delivered at the cost of security. One result was that a Russian-developed commercially available $26 software application SkyGrabber reportedly allowed insurgent forces in Iraq "to view, record, and share video relayed by U.S. UAVs. Although the software did not extend to other hacking capabilities, the case was used to illustrate and question the rush to functionality as being short-sighted.67

Some writers on the dynamics of encryption have commented on the paradoxical balance between strong and weak encryption alternatives; controls over exportation and dissemination of encryption tools wanted at the same time that the internet began to grow, and as a result, maintaining encryption implied an adversary's ability to use the same tools to resist surveillance, whereas weaker encryption systems might allow surveillance but would also leave one vulnerable to the adversary's own efforts.68 Experts have also noted that even the ability of strong encryption does not preclude an entity's ability to derive conclusions from data. "Anything that is worth nothing now is being encrypted," Hayden conceded in 2017, but the absence of content itself means that organizations that had previously analyzed messages and broken encryption must adapt, using other information from the flow of traffic to provide usable information.69 This matters because it illustrates an enduring trait in the nature of conflict. Antagonists respond to their environments and to the actions of one another. Evolution is endemic to conflict, and can make accurate extrapolation a challenging task.
