Whatever changes activity in the cyber domain are exerting on conflicts, the presumption that cyberwar or online information operations have extracted combat from warfare would be extremely (even dangerously) mistaken. As with the invention of weapons such as the tank or the airplane, social media could be understood as becoming a part of the fighting environment rather than a replacement of it. Generals and soldiers in the first decades of the 20th century learned that armor and aircraft were useful parts of combined arms fighting, and even if they provided indispensably necessary they were unlikely to be sufficient for victory. The fact of ISIS as a quasi-state reinforces a set of conclusions.

The first actions to curtail ISIS's Twitter activity exerted an apparent desultory effect, and clues to the ineffectiveness appeared before ISIS began its assault on the cyber landscape. Nine months after Mosul fell, al-Shabaab terrorists killed sixty-seven people in an attack on Nairobi's Westgate in September 2013. The terrorists used Twitter as a platform to proclaim their attack and to project misinformation that was apparently accepted and disseminated by media outlets unaware that their reporting had been coerced by terrorists. Twitter eventually responded by suspending a terrorist Twitter account, although al-Shabaab simply set up other accounts and the effect was negligible.⁴²

Twitter's early response to ISIS bore many of the same hallmarks. Accounts such as @IslamicState and @jihadISIS were closed, but some users created new accounts, providing only minimal modification to the name, such as adding a digit to the end. ISIS observed the hundredth iteration of the @IslamicState account by posting a picture of a birthday cake. Some analysts have optimistically pointed out the inconvenience of these suspensions “changed the once-free terrain for ISIS,” even when one ISIS account in mid-2015 complaining that “Twitter has become a battlefield!”⁴³ Since Twitter became a battlefield through ISIS's own introduction of the platform, the conflict in isolation looks as much like a state-ment.

The ISIS Twitter footprint recovered significantly over the next two years. Twitter, perhaps seeking to emphasize the contrast with its earlier reluctance to become involved in content moderation related to violent extremism, announced in August 2016 that it had suspended 360,000 accounts between mid-2015 and mid-2016 because of their posts courting terrorism.⁴ By the end of 2017, a total of 600,000 account suspensions had been tallied.⁴⁵ As much as this formidable number of account suspensions reflects Twitter’s efforts to curb violent speech, it indicates the ease with which ISIS sympathizers were able to relaunch new accounts.
