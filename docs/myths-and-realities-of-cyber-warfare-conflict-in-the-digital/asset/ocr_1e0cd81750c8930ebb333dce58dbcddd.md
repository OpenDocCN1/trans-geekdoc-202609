Ordinary inhabitants of the infosphere, exhausted after tangling with innate counts that turn out to be or sometimes bots that cannot be persuaded or mollified, are brought to confusion in which actual “dissentient voices appear to be another mirage; thus stifling the impact if not the enunciation of political opinion. Social platforms that claim to focus on facilitating human connection nonetheless maintain profitability; their designers pay scant attention to the respect these spaces would use for political (let alone weaponized-information) campaigning. It is both expedient and advantageous to persist in treating political messaging like commercial advertising. The internet grew popularity because of the tremendous access it provided and the anonymity implied to users who believed “on the internet, no one knows you’re a dog.” Microtargeting, founded on the conclusions derived from data collection and opaque algorithms, paves the path toward a house of mirrors effect in which users encounter occasional and unsettling clues that anonymity is largely mythical, but the information landscape remains tailelessored (or narrowed) to meet their preferences—according to the ways it is interpreted through data analysis.¹⁶

Singer and Brooke argue “companies must proactively consider the political, social, and moral ramifications of their services.”¹⁷ Such a case can certainly be made, but it immediately raises other questions about what kinds of ramifications should be encouraged or discouraged. Beyond even that lies about bad effects or ideas (once they are defined) should be curtailed. Such questions touch heavily on the topics that belong to official institutions of government. But the governing of speech means that issues like net neutrality carry high stakes for a range of issues beyond the technical considerations that had earlier framed them, and arguably “neutrality . . . itself [is] a value judgment.”¹⁸

Google’s executive overseeing Europe and the Middle East, “I believe that the individual should have the right to choose their moral values and that the state should not replace citizens with this incorrect and immoral system.”¹⁹ To borrow from Jean-Paul Sartre’s most famous concept, to what degree is it possible for an internet giant to become a global information power and yet seek not to choose? Intelligence veteran Ron Marks observed that the perpetrator of disinformation campaigns during the 2016 U.S. election season “wanted. . . to mess up that [democratic] system” and succeeded. However, Marks continued, the captains of social media giants have ignored how the popularity of their platforms transformed them into public communication utilities, and “to sit there and pretend that they are neutral in this process is laughable.”
