Fourteen months later, another small country on Russia’s borders suffered DDoS attacks but with more successful results for the attacker. The status of separatist regions in Georgia presented one item in a list of tensions between Georgia and Russia, as the former country sought ties with Europe and the United States and the latter held suspicions as with other countries in “near abroad” building relationships with other powerful states. DDoS attacks interrupted access to Georgia’s government websites in July 2008¹⁹ and continued into August, coinciding with kinetic fighting between Georgian and Russian soldiers. Georgia’s government was effectively gagged on the world stage, while its forces were pressed back into the interior. In addition to DDoS attacks to eliminate accessibility of government websites, relatively common attack types like SQL injections were used to deface Georgian websites, such as by conflicting images of the country’s then-president Mikhail Saashvili with those of Nazi dictator Adolf Hitler.⁰ The pairing of internet interruption attacks like DDoS with simple website defacements reinforced the point that in terms of messaging and diplomacy, Georgia was cut off from the larger world as it already was geographically.

Political leveraging through DDoS attacks is not limited to Russian political objectives. Contemporary to the Georgia war in 2008, targets for geographically disparate Burmese political dissidents and Ukrainian newspaper websites were temporarily muzzled by DDoS attacks²¹ As will be discussed later with respect to coordinating or directing a cyberattack is a complex process. In the wake of the DDoS attacks against Estonia noted that “sources of the attack were wider than concentrated in a few locations,” and as a result some suggested that they were “the product of spontaneous anger from a looser freedom of separate attackers,”² where others have the fingerprint of a more centralized coordination and the use of bot devices that are centrally controlled, although the devices’ owners are unaware. Where DDoS attacks are launched by individual users, networks connected for strategic purposes, or botnets routed on the Dark Web, DDoS attacks represent an easy and quick-acting approach in which,”you don’t need a lot of skills to do this. But you can do a lot of damage with this.”²³ Attackers’ capabilities can advance steadily, as Mirai malware was advanced by a botnet encompassing an estimated 400,000 devices, and the heaviest DDoS attacks had already reached by the fall of 2016 exceeded a peak of 1.1 terabytes per second.²⁴

This does not mean that DDoS attacks pose insurmountable problems, Libicki noted not only that “DDoS attacks are . . . not all that powerful” but also that “their military uses are modest,” since they interrupt access to data that neither destroy nor manipulate data. What DDoS can accomplish is to keep sites temporarily from being accessible and thereby restrict ‘freedom of maneuver’ in cyberspace’ that a target might otherwise possess.25 Others have pointed out that appropriate equipment, knowledge, and information bandwidths can overcome a would-be DoS, and as a result ‘DDoS attacks are not the breathless military assaults that the press makes them out to be.’26

# ADVANCED PERSISTENT THREATS ESPIONAGE

The ramifications of long-standing espionage activities cause enduring problems after a DDoS has ceased interrupting access to one or another website. Pernicious espionage efforts by advanced persistent threats, or APTs, so-called because of their technical sophistication and the longevity of their efforts, cast a long strategic shadow—and yet the fact that such espionage has emerged in favor of necessarily treating such activities as “attacks,” per se.

Spying is understood as something generally by countries rather than something unique to particular states or political philosophies. Indeed, cybersecurity writer Brian Mazan observed that ‘the preventent cyber actors [have] more to gain in engaging in cyber warfare than significantly restricting it’ and if such is the case with cyberfare, it is all the truer until regarding espionage.

Rather than following a haphazard approach to confirm whether common vulnerabilities leave a target undefended before continuing on forward screening of another potential target, APTs carefully select targets that are suspected to possess some data of value, economic or otherwise. A reconnaissance team may prepare for months by watching for patterns in a target’s communication habits, in order to eventually launch a phishing attack against a target and frame a message plausibly as possible to appear legitimate and trustworthy. Successful attempts deploy malware against a target by means of bogus links that a targeted user follow or malware-laden attachments that a targeted user trusts.28 From this point, the espionage team reinforces and expands its ability to access and navigate a system. This can include ensuring new backdoor routes through which the APT can regain entry if the target were to recognize and see the intrusion. The risk of the target’s realization is minimized, however, by the APT obtrusively elevating the access privileges of users it has compromised, or else by compromising further users within a system.

One eerie and infamous espionage tool is to activate the microphones and webcams that have been almost ubiquitous on computers and phones for more than a decade.29 Another reported tool is a keystroke logger that records and compromises what is typed onto a device. APTs can operate, navigate, and collect for months or even years without detection. Collection can proceed quietly for an extended timeframe, compiling staggering troves of data. In fact, the enormous scale of the data that may be exiled is reputed to be one of the more serious potential challenges confronting an APT. The abrupt exfiltration of a torrent of data could cause a telltale sign of illicit presence within a network, prompting APTs to take steps that obscure the flow of data and potentially the paths data takes as it flows back to the APT. This reportedly offers a side benefit, complicating the process of following the data flows as a means of achieving attribution.

The term "advanced persistent threat" (APT) reportedly dates to 2006, when the U.S. Air Force was trying to describe at the unclassified level the patient and persistent espionage originating in China. A few years later, engineers at Google noticed signs of the kind of sophisticated espionage that would come to define APTs. In order to gain authority to operate in China, Google had already agreed by 2005 to structures that would not offend the regime, whose interest in "social harmony" encompassed elimination of inquiry and discussion deemed controversial ranging from pornography to the regime's handling of the Tiananmen Squares in June 1989. Google also learned that its mapping services were running all of the regime's preferences.

Operation Aurora was a turning point, however. By January 2010, forensics showed that hackers had been aggressively covering a range of information from Google. One notable target was the source code for Google's search engine, something of clear economic value to any cyber thief. Other targets, such as the Google accounts of human rights activists, supporters of the Dalai Lama, and Google's records about what court orders had been delivered to the company regarding potential foreign agents, pointed to the People's Republic of China as the operation' instigator. Google chairman Erik Schmidt reflected to a nationalist later that "the Aurora attacks had pretty much ended the debate inside the company about what our future was on the mainland" of Asia. After uncovering the operation, Google threatened to stop cooperating with its earlier agreements to restrict Chinese searches, and within months Google was leaving China.

Simultaneously, other intricate espionage projects were occurring against a range of other targets. Espionage traced to IP addresses in China is reported to have operated from 2007 to 2009 and to have targeted defense contractors developing the F-35, the stealthy fifth-generation jet multiple fighter undertaken by the United States. Some have alluded to a connection between these activities and the 2011 first flight of China's J-20, its own introduction to the fifth-generation class of fighters.
