Collection can proceed quietly for an extended timeframe, compiling staggering troves of data. In fact, the enormous scale of the data that may be exiled is reputed to be one of the more serious potential challenges confronting an APT. The abrupt exfiltration of a torrent of data could cause a telltale sign of illicit presence within a network, prompting APTs to take steps that obscure the flow of data and potentially the paths data takes as it flows back to the APT. This reportedly offers a side benefit, complicating the process of following the data flows as a means of achieving attribution.

The term "advanced persistent threat" (APT) reportedly dates to 2006, when the U.S. Air Force was trying to describe at the unclassified level the patient and persistent espionage originating in China. A few years later, engineers at Google noticed signs of the kind of sophisticated, well-sourced espionage that would come to define APTs. In order to gain authority to operate in China, Google had already agreed by 2005 to structures that would not offend the regime, whose interest in "social harmony" encompassed elimination of inquiry and discussion deemed controversial ranging from pornography to the regime's handling of the Tiananmen Squares in June 1989. Google also learned that its mapping services were running all of the regime's preferences.

Operation Aora was a turning point, however. By January 2010, forensics showed that hackers had been aggressively covering a range of information from Google. One notable target was the source code for Google's search engine, something of clear economic value to any cyber thief. Other targets, such as the Google accounts of human rights activists, supporters of the Dalai Lama, and Google's records about what court orders had been delivered to the company regarding potential foreign agents, pointed to the People's Republic of China as the operation's instigator. Google chairman Erik Schmidt reflected to a nationalist later that "the Aurora attacks had pretty much ended the debate inside the company about what our future was on the mainland" of Asia. After uncovering the operation, Google threatened to stop cooperating with its earlier agreements to restrict Chinese online searches, and within months Google was leaving China.

Simultaneously, other intricate espionage projects were occurring against a range of other targets. Espionage traced to IP addresses in China is reported to have operated from 2007 to 2009 and to have targeted defense contractors developing the F-35, the stealthy fifth-generation jet multiple fighter undertaken by the United States. Some have alluded to a connection between these activities and the 2011 first flight of China's J-20, its own introduction to the fifth-generation class of fighters.
