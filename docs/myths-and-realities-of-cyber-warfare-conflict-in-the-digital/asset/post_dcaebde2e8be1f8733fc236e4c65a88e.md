
## Myths and Realities of Cyber Warfare

Nor the struggle between jihadist terrorist entities and the planet’s moderate and democratic blocs been at all the only space in which Facebook has been adapted for war. In 2014, Russian-backed separatist campaigns followed up on Russia’s recent absorption of Ukraine’s Crimean region by detecting Ukraine’s eastern borders. This forced a difficult set of challenges on Kiev, whose principal sources of security were an underequipped military and a twenty-year-old agreement known as the Budapest Memorandum on Security. This pact had arranged for Ukraine and two former Soviet republics to divest of their Soviet-era nuclear arsenals in exchange for security guarantees from Russia, the United States, and the United Kingdom. With the pact undercut by the eastern security breaches and the country’s military reeling, citizens’ groups within the country began to organize fighters who would battle the separatists.

As a platform tailored to communications between friends and built-to-one and especially one-to-many communications, Facebook proved easily adapted for the purpose. The app’s global reach allows the dissemination of the group’s message to reach beyond the country’s frontiers, and reportedly the group receives some donations from abroad. In a country where many people suspect that donations not find their way to intended recipients, the app’s functionalities such as posting photos allows that donated clothes or equipment might not reach the front: user-donors actually see the boots or coats that they have been worn and by the nationalist troops. Facebook is not used to launch attacks but rather to sustain kinetic fighting, and soldiers in the fighting have described the organizers of the Facebook supply as effective as combat medics serving on the battlefield in noncombat roles.¹²

According to journalist David Pararakaras, online mobilization “endows citizens with the ability and means to act in dynamic ways their government cannot” in the case of miserably less powerful states.¹³ Pararakaras examined social media and has argued that it is changing the nature of warfare, and while it may be a stretch to assert that the nature of war is itself changing, the character of conflict is clearly being impacted by the ability to mobilize quickly and across considerable geographic distances. These opportunities for mobilization also raise questions about the relationship between soldier and civilian, and Singer and Brooke argued that social media campaigns constitute a new form of “LikeWar,” and that “every LikeWar is a battle for attention with a specific objective in mind,” in which a command of “conveying narrative, emotion, and authenticity, welded with community-building” ensures virality and relevance, and ultimately helps deliver victory.¹⁴

The relationship between evoking an emotional reaction and attaining the qualities that create (or, likely, simulate) relevance have been identified in studies and are actively pursued by groups seeking to find geopolitical change.

## RESPONSE TO REPURPOSING

Eric Schmidt, serving as executive chairman of Google’s retroactively constituted parent company Alphabet, explained that “it is inconceivable to any of us... that you would use Twitter, Facebook or YouTube for these videos are designed to terrify... We did not understand that you could sufficiently terrify people using the online media to recruit people” to terrorist movements.¹16

Facebook built an admirably robust and user-friendly platform that to date has a base that rivals the total population of the People’s Republic of China and of India combined. The expectation that no one would allow a serious effort to repurpose the platform for a strategic purpose certainly in retrospect appears utopian, even naive. Unfortunately as it may be, the launch and growth of social media giants like Facebook new dimensions of opportunity for communication, and when people communicate, they do not necessarily say only the things that their masters want to hear.

History indicates that new technologies will be used for strategic aims, barring extraordinary and conscious efforts to preclude them. In fact, earlier technologies that had been heralded as bringing connection, advantage, and understanding to humanity have been repeatedly used for voice and political strategy. Radio technology that connect societies was soon to disseminate propaganda. Chemistry that alleviated hunger was quickly repurposed to facilitate explosives in wartime. Even maps and cartography have a long history that is intertwined with warfighting. No one wants to lose a war because the price of defeat can be devastating. This is, as a result, a powerful impulse to use what is available to assist in times of struggle. Arms limitation efforts and law of armed conflict face the uphill climb of combating this impulse with respect to particular kinds of weapons or actions. But expecting social media to not become embroiled in conflict is to ignore how the impulse to avoid defeat has engulfed myriad technologies across history.

Nor was the mechanized utilization of Facebook entirely a bolt from the blue. An early and evocative misuse of social media involved the cyberbullying of thirteen-year-old Megan Meier on MySpace that ended in 2006 suicide and an enormous blow to the reputation of what been been a first-generation social media icon.^17 Various regimes have exhibited that dialogue on Facebook might constitute, from their view, dangerous speech; as result, Facebook access has been blocked at various times in countries such as China and Turkey, and it (alongside YouTube and Twitter) by discontented Tunisians in January 2011 helped galvanize what would become known as the Arab Spring. Tunisian authorities, unwise to allow network traffic and block Facebook, on January 27, 2011, to eliminate all internet activity. This has been described as “the first time in Internet history that a state purposely went offline to halt information flows.^18

Historical perspective provides important reminders that social media may accelerate or magnify rather than ip facto create seemingly revolutionary dynamics. Michael Neiberg, a noted scholar specializing in World War I, observed a century-old case that matched social media mobilization in its rapid frequency. The Shandong Connection at the post-World War I Treaty of Versailles ceded Chinese territory that had earlier been controlled by Germany to Japan, who had captured it in the early phases of the war. The brazen squashing of Chinese self-determination preferences at a conference that ostensibly veiled veiled “incredibly unpopular everywhere except Japan.” Thanks to rapid communication by wireless technology, people in China learned within hours of a decision reached just west of Paris, and violent protests as the “May 4th movement ensued, while Chinese ex-patriots in Paris conducted simultaneous nonviolent demonstrations. As Neiberg observes, this visceral and mass-organized reaction by Chinese in Paris and in China happened “without WiFi, without Facebook, without #May4th. And it worked.^19 Social media can profoundly impact social mobilization, but such mobilization has happened without online social media platforms, and it has occurred before their appearance.

Silicon Valley perhaps has little sympathy for the totalitarian autocratic regimes with youthful networked protesters, but a more earnest examination of the Arab Spring might have highlighted that social media could not always be so to support political unrest and that future targets might not be so anathema in the eyes of social media’s moguls.
