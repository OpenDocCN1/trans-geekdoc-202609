## A “CYBER PEARL HARBOR

U.S. Defense Secretary Leon Panetta’s comment in 2012 that cyberattacks against infrastructure could trigger “physical destruction and the loss of life” and “be a cyber Pearl Harbor”45 leveraged provocative imagery about the direct prospects of an act of cyberwar. The 20th-century forebearer attack on Pearl Harbor was a stunning blow that would foreclose U.S. opportunities to undertake strategically or operationally significant actions opposing Japan’s seizure of petroleum resources in the Dutch-owned islands of modern Indonesia. Although not a war-winning act, it devastated U.S. naval power in the short term even more contemporary than reports to the U.S. public. A forgotten point about Pearl Harbor was its prominent but not singular place within a larger context of surprise attacks against various targets in more than half a dozen locations stretching 6,800 miles from Singapore to Hawaii. The Pearl Harbor attacks are the most widely remembered in the United States; but from a strategic standpoint in 1941, the destruction of U.S. battleships nested within a context of surprise attacks. Those surprise attacks supported Japan’s objective of seizing and securing oil fields and other key resources in the southwest Pacific Ocean. Understanding that Pearl Harbor was a massive portion of a larger campaign is important if the allusion to Pearl Harbor is to be utilized in contexts such as cyberspace.

Another crucial point is that Panetta’s statement was not the first Pearl Harbor allusion regarding a vulnerability in cyberspace. However, the concept of an “electronic Pearl Harbor” dates to 1991, as demonstrated by computer security analyst Winn Schwartz’s testimony to Congress summer: “computer systems are so poorly protected today they can essentially be considered defenseless; essentially, an electronic Pearl Harbor waiting to occur.”46 While individual hackers appeared to represent annoying challenges,

I don’t think that we have to worry about hackers as we do organized groups who are more well organized, well funded, and well motivated, who may have real reason to do penetrations of systems for economic or industrial advantage.47

## Myths and Realities of Cyber Warfare

The prospect of organized and politically motivated cyberattack is fundamental to any coherent concept of a cyber Pearl Harbor. An awareness of the fact that the original Pearl Harbor fit within a larger strategic context—that the attack supported a strategic purpose and was the most prominent among what was actually virtually simultaneous strikes—should also be demanded of sources producing digital equivalents.

The term has been adopted with a degree of gusto. While Richard Siemnon notes that "only a crippling military defeat outweighs controlling of the cyber domain deserves to be labeled a Cyber Pearl Harbor," he has also predicted that "in the future there will be a devastating use of cyber attack against military systems; a true cyber Pearl Harbor.”48 Whether or not the prediction is realized, the phrasing does at least indicate an awareness that cyberattack “Pearl Harbor” carries meaningful connotations. Not all authors seem to fully appreciate this: "imagine if Pearl Harbor had been attacked and there had no response from Washington. This is the actual case today" through the "secret war against the United States conducted through cyberattacks.49

Other authors have skithered Pearl Harbor imagery or used it to highlight potential distinctions between the provocative visions and the potential dangers they envision. Ronald Deibert, the founder of the Canadian cybersecurity analysis group Citizen Lab, pointed to fragility in internet infrastructure from threats as a ship's anchor securing undersea communications cables to cascading satellite debris known as the Kessler Syndrome "ending" global cyberspace as we know it."50 Perhaps partly because this fragility is not vulnerable to intentional actions, Deibert avoided using "Pearl Harbor" allegories. A work describing cyberwar's "potential to make planes fall from the sky or nuclear power plants to melt down" nonetheless need to some length to inserting Pearl Harbor illusions.51 Pearl Harbor can even to extent provide a foil to the prospect of cyber warfare: whereas Japanese planes strafing Ford Island or bombing USS Arizona were marked with Japan's red-sun rumble, attribution "won't be simple" in the wake of a crushing cyberattack, and after we answer the question of who did it, the next is "Is it war?"52

For Conti and Raymond, although a shattering cyberattack "possible, and will likely occur, the predominant context is marked by states "quietly sparring in the shadows to gain access, positional advantage" and intelligence information. They describe an extensive range of targets across the military, commercial sector, defense industrial base, emergency services, food production and distribution, manufacturing, transportation, and other sectors. ## WHERE ESPIONAGE FITS

Where scholar Paul Rosenweig observes that "in cyberspace, the line between intelligence and war is ill-defined, at best," Hayden is even more blunt "the distinctions break down entirely in the cyber domain."55 This complicates the implications of other concepts about espionage, and paint the cyber domain as a dangerous landscape. The Tallinn Manual 2.0, published in 2017 and built on the shoulders of its predecessor, declares that "the International Group of Experts agreed that customary international law does not prohibit espionage per se." Thus, although the spy may be virtually indistinguishable from the cyber warrior, the former's activities cannot be universally condemned in an abstract sense. The year Tallinn Manual 2.0 appeared, Hayden himself added, "the theft of data is acceptable international practice. It is honorable national practice."56
