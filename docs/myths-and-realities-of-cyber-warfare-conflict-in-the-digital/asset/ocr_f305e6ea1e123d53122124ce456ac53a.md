Social networking sites entail a range of potential dangers. They
include the open-source harvesting of posted data for ulterior pur-
poses; a 2010 survey found that over half of U.S. social media users posted
"sensitive information that makes them vulnerable to cybercrime."^27
This statistic is alarming, because it is so avoidable at the level of
the accountholder. It points to the need for increased awareness of cyber
hygene, and more, an understanding of how carelessly shared
information can be leveraged for illicit purposes.

Users themselves are by no means the source of vulnerability, and
the others for illicit data access lie beyond the accountholder's
realistic opportunity to prevent abuse. These include technical methods to
lure users into inadvertently allowing malware onto their machines and
t hacking of the companies themselves, in order to access the troves
of data they collect in exchange for providing free-to-use services.
This data represents mountains of revenue, which are tempting targets
of cybercriminals to access them illicitly. The apparent and reported
vulnerability of such data is a cause of outrage. Conti's warning that "the
most effective way to eliminate the problem of web-based information
disclosure is to generate no data in the first place"^28 bears repeating
 emphasis. When entities decide to collect such data about individu-
als, the obligation to work actively to secure it is obvious.
