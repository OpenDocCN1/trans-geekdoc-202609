Evident concern among jihadist forces about the possibility that commercially available encryption tools might not be secure for their communications reportedly spurred unaffiliated groups towards the development of indigenous encryption tools. The first of these, Asrar al-Mujahideen, was developed by an al-Qaeda affinity called the Global Islamic Media Fund (GIMF) in 2007 and its existence was subsequently advertised in the al-Qaeda platform Inspire.63

The advantages of privacy notwithstanding, for a variety of reasons terrorists do not always adopt practices to ensure security. For example, the extremists affiliated with the Islamic State of Iraq and Syria (ISIS) launched attacks in Paris in November 2015, but they reportedly “didn’t bother to use encryption while communicating[ing].” This neglect, which ran afoul of operational security (OPSEC) guidance distributed on contemporary ISIS forums, enabled police to raid borders before they could undertake a fo-attack on the business district of western Paris.64

A secrecy can pave a path to paranoia. Al-Qaeda reportedly created and released additional encryption tools under the GIMF banner as a false-flag so that jihadist groups would not recognize its exact origin. These tools were devised “to keep track of rival rivals,” as well as to guard against that one or more militant organization might be a decoy established by U.S. or allied entities to communicate with, identify, and entrap extremists. GIMF issued a recall in November 2013 on an encryption tool to have been created by the ISIS. Many ISIS members are reported to have favored the so-called Snowden Phone because of the Android device's prominent encryption capabilities.65

Nonstate militants and violent extremists are not the only groups desiring private communications or adopting encryption, however corporate giants in the world of electronic search, networking, and telecommunications have responded to this interest with projects and policies designed to preserve client privacy from external surveillance. Google began its investment in undersea cables as a means of carrying traffic as early as 2008. In the following years, it would initiate work on its own fiber-optic lines from the United States to Asia and Europe, spanning both the Pacific and Atlantic Oceans. Information assurance may have played a role in these developments and clearly was involved in the company’s decision to encrypt the data transmitted between its data centers. Apple, another giant of the 21st-century economy, took steps to provide encryption not only from individuals and groups but even from Apple itself.
