Few works as robustly comprehend and convey cyberwar as Greg Conti and David Raymond’s On Cyber:

Cyber brings new capabilities to bear and will fundamentally alter warfare. However, at its heart, war will remain war. Warfare is ugly, despicable, and something that remains with us... Just as airpower cannot solve all problems, nor can cyber. Cyberspace operations however, will play an increasingly important role in all future conflict.¹¹

Despite this clear point, many works, including several excellent and quotable studies, even suggest that classic philosophy about warfare has been overtaken by cyber conflict “expanding the range of possible harm and outweighs the concepts of war and peace.”—¹² New forms of danger posing menacing threats, but they do not invalidate a fundamental understanding of warfare, any more than the introduction of artillery, airpower, or battlefield weapons changed the nature of warfare despite their undeniable impact on the character of conflicts.

Furthermore, examples of policy-based tension that resist categorization under terms of war and pose vexing policy challenges; this is not an entirely novel dynamic in the history of military affairs, however. Important stakes, in terms of mortal danger for personnel and policy objectives for states, mean that “war encourages—even forces—effort to innovate. A part of the nature of warfare is that the character of individual wars will change.¹³ Not only can vex challenges be new than they may appear, but they are likely to be attempted in hopes of upsetting an antagonist’s preparations and helping to secure victory.

Adversaries’ quests for competitive advantage bring the kinds of challenges that recount the character of different conflicts. Where researchers speak of “the mix of technical, policy, and social dimensions ... combining] to create and complete coevolving, complex adaptive system”¹⁴ in the cyber domain, historians identify parallels in the physical realms of combat. Thus, the character but not the nature of war reshapes itself. The application of hostility for purposes remains central to the meaning of warfare, even if criminal acts and war acts can be difficult to distinguish as they take place. The “implications in calling [a] cybersecurity crisis as a cyberwar” are considerable because the responses that may be considered in defending against an aggressor diverge from the work involved in apprehending a criminal.
