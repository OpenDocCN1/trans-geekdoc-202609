On one hand, devices that are set up and left to unattended make virtually ideal recruits for DDoS botnets; lacking protection, they can easily be hacked, and once under a bot herder's control, they will operate for extended periods. On the other hand, manipulating IoT that impacts health and safety (Libicki offers cars and drones as examples) can cause “serious harm to their owners and worse, third parties, if their controls are usurped.”48 These are dangers in a world where, Conti and Raymond respond, “an enemy combatant by Grandma’s toaster”; Schneider confirms that computers “hands and feet” means “the ability to affect the world in a direct physical manner. IoT devices also provide an and already leveraged opportunity for surveillance of a target.”49

Experts have recognized that weapons must be sufficiently controllable to be useful. Lacking control over a weapon means firing a loose cannon.50 This is a valid factor to consider with respect to the ways IoT devices could conceivably be transformed into cyberweapons for harassment, ransoming, or kinetic effects. Another important caveat, however, is that not all states or nonstate actors set the same threshold with respect to what a “sufficiently controllable” weapon looks like. In the physical domains it is rife with weapons that some combatants spurned while others deemed reliable, or accurate, or precise. Having demonstrated across the land, sea, air domains, and in the exploitation of space, something similar can be expected in the cyber domain as well.

Even sober advocates of IoT, and the related concept of the Cloud, concede that IoT “offers great promise” while also meaning that “where everything is connected, then anything can be disrupted.” If thinking about the need for resilience has been justified by realizations that hackers have long permeated networks and slipped around or the security walls that characterized perimeter-security, the implications of IoT and the Cloud underscore the risk for robust security. One software executive admitted, “if you’re using Cloud, you’ve extended your attack surface.” Another, seeking to refute suggestions that the “Cloud’s not secure,” pointed to the “assume breach,” abandon the perimeter-style approach to security, simplify to customers, and “embrace ... machine learning” for defense.”51

Other analysis similarly carries an undercurrent that the answer to technologically precipitated challenges involves more technology. At the Center for a New America have argued that “cloud computing may also allow smaller businesses with limited resources to better protect their information against sophisticated attacks.”52 At face value, this argument seems to accept the purported advantages of distributing computing and storage without weighing other considerations about how such placement might impact an adversary’s ability to access the same data.
