If they are acts of war, why do we not respond with all our forces? Why do we seem to have ineffectual responses to so many of these incidents? They are semantic issues with underlying policy questions that many people have tried to address in the last few years. In particular, the term “cyberwar” was used more frequently in some circles, but not everyone is comfortable with that: if it is genuinely war, why is there no full-scale declaration and response (other than the horror and devastation that war would bring)? If it is not, then what is it?

Professor Sambaluk on these questions, and, more, in this book. He brings the perspective of a meticulous military historian with technical expertise to a problem set often addressed only by those with narrower domain expertise. He explains the questions posed by our increasing reliance on cyber technologies, along with the answers put forward by those working in the realm, backed by extensive references.

In Sambaluk’s book, you will find extensive coverage of the questions of how cyberwar might be defined, of how influence operations in social media are aware the difficulties associated with attack attribution, the role of data as a “battles,” and several other difficult topics. This coverage both illustrates misconceptions about these items and exposes some of the thorny disagreements about nomenclature and importance that occur over these topics.

As already noted, I have been working in the field for forty years and reading this book both reminded me of some critical incidents that had occurred over that span and also exposed me to some significant items I had not seen before—which I attribute to his careful research and exposition. What is more, I appreciate that the book does not promote a particular interpretatretion of the prior work and instead exposes many of the essential facets of the issues. As a reader, I am presented with a wealth of information that may be considered, and to enable formulation of my own considered set of conclusions.

Students and colleagues often ask me about “cyberwar” and what that mean. I am well aware of the complexities and differences of opinion on the topic, and thus I usually respond with the unsatisfactory (but concise) response “It’s complicated.” Now I’ll be able to respond, “It’s complicated, read Nick Sambaluk’s book to understand why.” That is still concise but gives me the satisfaction of providing a useful answer.

This book is going on my shelf of primary references; I suspect other scholars of the field will similarly value it, as will anyone trying to better understand the complexities of the topic.
