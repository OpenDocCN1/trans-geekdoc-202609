Frustrated Indian users, realizing that the attackers were making use of the social media updates, hurried to warn fellow users against posting further information and even claimed that the government had ordered an end to such posts.60

Tools can be used in disparate ways, even in the context of violence, as the November 2015 attacks on Paris demonstrate. Despite the precedent of the Mumbai attacks, credible reports indicate that the attackers leveraged panicked victims' tweets as a tool to identify the locations of survivors had not surfaced. However, because the attacks left large numbers of people disoriented and vulnerable, many Parisians responded with the hashtag #PortOuverte (open door) to identify locations where survivors could safely shelter. One million users using this hashtag appeared within a space of ten hours.61 The response to the Mumbai attack of November 2008 and the Paris attack of November 2015 each saw important information posted on Twitter, and the difference in results correlate coincidentally the difference between who was watching and reacting in the two cases.

ISIS was not alone in using social media in connection with fighting in the Middle East. In the battle against ISIS, many of the militias that fought on behalf of Iraq were reported to have received extensive support from neighboring Iran. Hezbollah, Iran's power in battles with Israel, had already fought ISIS in Syria. When an Iraqi captured a suspected ISIS fighter in 2016 and launched an Instagram vote about whether to kill or release the prisoner, 75,000 votes were posted globally. A follow-up post uploaded two hours later consisted of a selfie photo of a militiam with the prisoner lying in a pool of blood. The caption, 'Thanks for vote,' accompanied the image.62

Other actors, outside ISIS and often beyond Islamist terrorism, have also employed social media in ways that potentially affect the state's dictional monopoly on violence. Significant cases across Europe and Asia point to how contested uses of social media are complicating ideas about the relationship between states and the violence associated with national security policy.

Some analysts point to the use of official statements on social media as an inflection point for tacit accepted violence. Filipino president Rodrigo Duterte's rhetoric in opposing drug cartels' power in his country has been identified as an example. An estimated 12,000 people have died in violence met out individuals including drug dealers but also extending ordinary odds and reportedly other enemies and targets of the regime's police.63 Confirming the existence and extent of such dynamics can be extremely challenging, although history suggests that the elimination of identified threats to a society can lead to a snowballing effect in which an array of other grievances and grudges become enmeshed in the selection of victims.
