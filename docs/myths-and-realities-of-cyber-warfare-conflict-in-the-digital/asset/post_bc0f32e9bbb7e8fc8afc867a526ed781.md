Extrapolating patterns from these examples have proven problematic. The personality factors in the Australian case mean that it was indisputably not "cyberwar," it suggested that cyberattack could trigger hazardous malfunction for a computer-monitored infrastructure system. The Aurora test conducted by Idaho National Laboratory in 2007 demonstrated the same point, and as an experiment, the event was specifically not "cyberwar." Because researchers linked Stuxnet to physical damage impacts and a specified political motivation, it has attained prominence as a key example of cyberwar. Ambiguities regarding the German steel mill include the identity of the targeted mill, the exact portions of the complex that were damaged, attribution to the origin of the cyberattack, and motivation. The paltry information precludes the notoriety or examination to which Stuxnet has been subjected.

Forensics experts and cybersecurity analysts describe Stuxnet as a large and sophisticated piece of malware. Half a megabyte in size, it was between twelve and fifty times larger than most examples of malware examined by cybersecurity professionals. To ensure its success into targeted systems, it repeatedly leveraged zero-day exploits. This term denotes a method taking advantage of a vulnerability that has not been identified to the public or even to the creators and the topic will be addressed further in Chapter 2. The presence of one or more zero-day exploits indicates sophisticated code that requires time and money and analysts estimate Stuxnet's development in the low double-digit millions of dollars.

Whereas many examples of malware, as the mysterious Conficker worm that infected six million computers in 2008, are designed to facilitate their own propagation, analysts noted that Stuxnet was configured by Siemens to run a series of centrifuges in the exact setup at Natanz. As a result, analysts concluded that the malware had been written with the objective of slowing Iran's nuclear enrichment, which many experts believed was associated with that country's ambition to develop nuclear weapons. Initially limiting its own propagation and written so that its sharp focus on Iranian centrifuges rendered it harmless to other networks.

## War's Character and Nature, and the Stuxnet Trap

Stuxnet stands as both the sole example of kinetic cyberweapon and an exception to many patterns regarding weaponry, the urge to extrapolate from its example carries uncertainty. This has prevented comment about the future of cyberwar, based on the experiences and interpretations of Stuxnet.

Important aspects remain unclear a decade after the malware's discovery in the spring of 2010. Different sources disagree about the spread of the malware. Some analysts have described Stuxnet as a well-designed cyber weapon, and others used it as evidence that a cyberweapon could be on a short leash. In contrast, one New York Times article in the months after the discovery declared that Stuxnet spread to thousands of computer systems around the world and much of its impact has been on those systems. Symantec reported that the initial infections reached a total of 100,000 devices located in different countries. An executive at Microsoft reflected in 2018 that “we essentially had to defend over a billion customers in a response to what was ultimately meant to be a highly targeted, highly precise action.” Of course, different vantage points can shape diverging views of what constitutes the impact of an attack. Whether it is a piece of code the size of a single camera phone on one hand, or the shadow of a cyberweapon on the other, depends in part on perspective.

Libicki seems to conclude that uncertainty may form a pattern with cyberattacks; in contrast to the tools for damage assessment leaving a kinetic strike, cyberweapons may leave even the victim scratching their head about what the damage was. Rid noted that the slow and erratic trend of eroding system efficiency helped preserve the malware’s secret, as it was believed to have run for two years before its discovery. Other researchers have suggested that its belated discovery was retarded by the same secretive and hermetic approach to security-related issues that had made enrichment technologies such an inaccessible target in the first place.

Despite important and lingering ambiguities, Stuxnet has inspired some notable predictions about the future of struggle in the cyber domain. Keto identified “doctrinal quandaries” about making the malware, including issues of “tactical viability,” the potential of the technology to be adopted and repurposed by other entities, and “anxieties over the dangerous precedent that the operation would work.” Other experts predicted that a mere months would elapse before copycat attacks were conducted by criminal groups eager to leverage sophisticated code. Credible accounts of such an occurrence with respect to this code had not appeared in ten years, although analogous allegations have been made regarding weaponization of other leaked software.

Contrasting statements have noted that the complexity of computerized supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems reduces the likelihood of anti-SCADA malware being something eligible for rapid development or early leverage against a large number of targets. This does not alter others’ concern about long-term exploration and potentially seeding complex systems with malware, to be triggered at a moment of the adversary’s choice.

Still, other predictions argue that “clearly, future cyber attacks will target systems in a more sophisticated manner than Stuxnet.” Pronouncements such as this carry a palpable air of certainty—and an element of a subconscious presumption that sophisticated weapons are more modern and more effective. While the development and use of cyberweapons may possibly reveal a trend toward increasingly complex and sophisticated forms of malware, there is no deterministic set of forces forcing them to do so. Cyberweapons are technologies—and technologies are made, adopted, and adapted, to address perceived needs in acceptable ways. Trends in the development and use of cyberweapons will be contoured by ideas about policy goals and also by decisions about strategic, operational, and tactical approaches that will support the pursuit of those objectives. Understanding that fact facilitates a meaningful understanding of warfare. Studying conflict in cyberspace means questioning commonplace wisdom about cyberweapons and examining operations through cyberspace more deeply.

# Temporal Mythologies of Cyberwar

# INSTANTANEOUS SPEED

Perhaps the most important of the temporal mythologies regarding cyberwar deals with time itself. The actions targeting Georgia in 2008, coinciding with the kinetic combat between that country and military units, was for example dubbed by some as “the internet of blitzkrieg.” This in itself is a significant characterization. “Blitzkrieg,” the infamous use of “lighting war” by Nazi forces against Allied powers, evokes such visceral imagery that it has become almost a cliché. In a sense, this illustrates the nature of mythologies. What journalists from neutral nations saw in 1939 and 1940 and called “blitzkrieg” was, for the actual practitioners, something different. Certainly, it was a “quick and lively” approach to campaigning that sought to leverage speed in order to sow confusion within the enemy and facilitate an attacker’s exploitation of opportunities. The instantaneous speed of German advances in World War II was itself an impression,—a methodology.
