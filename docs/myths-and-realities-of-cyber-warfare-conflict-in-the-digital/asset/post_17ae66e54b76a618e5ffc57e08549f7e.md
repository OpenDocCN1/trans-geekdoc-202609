
# Myths and Realities of Cyber Warfare

What makes the People’s Republic of China garner the attention of cybersecurity experts is not that it is alone in spying but that forensics points to both widespread efforts by concentrated teams, coupled with self-reinvent interpretations of international norms. Specifically, targets include both strategically relevant entities (which it apparently defines broadly) and a range of economic players and research institutions as well. Richard Clarke’s vivid description of “very interesting lab, company, and research facility in the US being systematically vacuumed by some foreign entities,”who “never leave marks that they were, except when they want to know,”35 might hyperbolize but it does make the point while APT dig deep, they also mine for a range of information across many economic sectors. The first identified APT was to have been involved in espionage against organizations lately to twenty distinct industries and sectors.36

Other cybersecurity figures have suggested that “economically motivated cyber espionage” exert only limited impact on countries like the United States whose intellectual property is being sucked (to a degree that is impossible to know, the stealthy character of cyber espionage).”37 This argument overlooks the fact that not only the scale but also to a considerable degree the character of the information being exfiltrated cannot be known. Also, unknown are the motivations behind the seizures. A result, presumably that intellectual property is being violated “only” could potentially be an overly simplistic conclusion that misses part of the strategic picture. Chinese diplomats refuted accusations about cyber espionage by insisting that their own country is a principal victim of hacking.”38 It is worth noting that Chinese officials claim some unrestricted web searches and blog postings to constitute “hacking,” and foreign corporations are regularly infiltrated, Chinese companies are linked to the regime in ways that allow the regime to consider them inviolate.39

Analysts can point to a wealth of information leading them to conclude Chinese culpability. It is that espionage was conducted “almost always” during “office hours (China time) and not [during] national holidays (in China).”40 Although others could certainly run false flag attacks so to implicate, this illustration is accompanied by the targeting of a range of industries and sectors that have been identified by the Chinese government as priority areas for development. Other incidents, such as the temporary redirection of 10% of the world’s routers to send a disproportionate segment of the world’s internet traffic through Chinese telecommunication networks in April 2010,41 raised some concern among those following cybersecurity news.

Mandiant’s APT1 Report in 2013, which officially identified People’s Liberation Army Unit 61398 in Shanghai, formed a landmark event in the process of understanding and confronting the issue of sophisticated espionage groups. Citing activity that began in 2006 when the Air Force was struggling to describe the then-new operations, APT1 in the following seven years targeted at least 141 different organizations, the overwhelming majority of which (98.6%) were English-speaking and the vast majority of which (81.5%) were located in the United States.³32 The case made by Mandiant reputedly created a diplomatic space in which nation-states (specifically U.S. policy makers in the Barack administration) could more comfortably follow on and raise the topic of APTs; this thinking provides that a nation-state would be expected to proffer a more exhaustive amount of forensic proof of Chinese culpability would be demanded of a private company specializing in cybersecurity issues.³43

By no stretch of the imagination has the APT been a creature solely of Chinese making, although its activities do confirm a plurality of the ones identified by cybersecurity researchers. A European Union study identified APTs by name, just over half of were attributed to the People's Republic of China. Various APTs specialize in particular sectors. APT18 (attributed to China) pursues aerospace, defense, and engineering targets, all has also been associated with communications and media. APT15, also attributed to China, targets European government ministries; APT16 focuses on high tech, government, and financial areas in Japan and Taiwan. APT28's targets include NATO and various European militaries, while APT29 targets Western European governments and was connected to the hacks of the Democratic National Committee during the 2016 elections; analysts believe the "Snake" is a Russian entity dedicated to targeting the German government. North Korea's identified APT focus on South Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, as well as various industries such as manufacturing and aerospace that are seen as valuable by the regime. Iran's APTs have been associated with espionage against the United States as well as countries in the Middle East region and beyond, various industrial sectors including energy, telecommunications, and finance, and also a range of individual targets working in.

Whether interpreted as an opportunity or as a danger, the point that cyberattacks can be launched by far-off adversaries have given rise to an assumption that distance becomes irrelevant in warfare—or least in the forms of conflict related to cyberattack. While there is ample reason to conclude that the cyber domain is reordering how distance matters and how distance might be defined, geography has not been made so irrelevant as any assertions would imply.

The changed relevance of distance and geography due to communications technology is less than new. One of Britain’s first actions in World War I was the cutting of German transatlantic cables. While these internet traffic, the effect was fundamentally to interrupt Berlin’s communications with the disparate holdings of its empire, and its ability to communicate with neutrals outside Europe. The United States responded by allowing German diplomatic traffic to flow on neutral U.S. cables so that at least German embassies could keep abreast of instructions with their capital.
