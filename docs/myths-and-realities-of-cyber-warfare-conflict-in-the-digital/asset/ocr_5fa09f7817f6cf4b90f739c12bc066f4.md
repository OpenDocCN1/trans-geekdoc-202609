# Temporal Mythologies of Cyberwar

In recent years, researchers in the European Union have pointed to signs of APTs gaining long-standing access to a range of networks across various key industries and in several countries, and cybersecurity advocates have reportedly pondered whether such access might allow long-scale reconnaissance but also preparation of latent threats to infrastructure.95

The vulnerability studies at Idaho National Laboratory known as the Aurora vulnerability constituted an ever more explicit wake-up call. While some writers declared that the test 'lends credence to the suggestion that the manipulation of computer code can be just as effective in destroying critical infrastructure as a missile would,' and others argued that the vulnerability 'extends' to several sectors in SCADA systems operate.96 The validity of the last point may stand or fall to some degree based on the extent to which the standardization trend identified by Rid is adopted across different sectors. Thus, although experts know that tailored attacks against complicated and obscure systems require time reconnaissance of the target and preparation and testing of attack tools, an awareness that SCADA as a class of potential target has existed for the quarter century since Eligible Receiver 97, and it has heightened by Aurora more than a decade ago, in addition to the actual examples of attacks that have taken place.

Forensics studies show dramatically different degrees of sophistication and technical surprise with respect to cyberattacks, including those that have been deployed against SCADA systems. At the high end, the Stuxnet malware with its four discrete zero-day exploits, a decade after its discovery, continues to represent the apparent high-water mark of sophistication in a known anti-SCADA malware. The repeated attacks against Ukrainian electrical power infrastructure embody the other extreme in terms of technical surprise. The 2015 attack leveraged a vulnerability that had actually identified a dozen years earlier. But the inherent criticality of critical infrastructure precludes practices such as patching or updating devices over SCADA systems. As a result, the factors that Singer and Friedman have long noted the patching of known vulnerabilities on many personal computers worldwide are still more extreme for systems that must be 'on' continually.

In short, interrogating the temporal mythologies of cyberwar invites the conclusion that cyberattacks (broadly defined) are diverse enough to resist generalizations about the speed of cyberwar, about the supposed irrelevance of distance or geography, about their cost-effectiveness, and about their potential shelf-life as single-use weapons.
