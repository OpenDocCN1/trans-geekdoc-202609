The implicit message of such graphic imagery was that ISIS was unstoppable and inescapable. Despite being traditionally used as an escape from other controversies and concerns, sports was being invaded by violence and extremism.

Concurrent with hashtag hijackings were other forms of propaganda, such as the intermittently published but visually slick English-language ISIS journal Dabi. Named for the site of a Muslim prophesied battle equivalent to Armageddon, ISIS released a dozen issues that centered on a jihadist theme roughly every month or two.39 Overlapping messages in different formats or seemingly from different sources is a time-honored tool in propaganda, due to the reinforcing effect it can exert. A narrative spreads across social media because it matches of existing understanding, it is promoted by a cadre dedicated to popularization, and cadre uses human and automated assets to produce a magnifying effect.40 Tweets foster graphic images of violence; the journal elaborates on extremist philosophy; or tweets announce the release of the journal; still more add further graphic images or information, or to normalize the extremist viewpoint or to show outsiders the need of both simultaneously.

Recruitment efforts insiously played on the yearning of potential recruits for human connection and for a sense of purpose. ISIS operatives saw benefit in inserting less graphic, and sometimes even seemingly whimsical, images of their personnel. This explains the thinking behind the "Cats of ISIS" images of apparently playful housecats amongst ISIS fighters and their military kit. Such juxtaposition can be baffling in the absence of a deliberate purpose, particularly within a group that was notoriously draconian about telecommunications access. Frightening outsiders and inside insiders permitted the same organization to post images of human-headed soccer and of cats lounging Kalashnikovs. Then-defense secretary Ashton Carter claimed that ISIS was "the first social media-fueled terrorist group."41
