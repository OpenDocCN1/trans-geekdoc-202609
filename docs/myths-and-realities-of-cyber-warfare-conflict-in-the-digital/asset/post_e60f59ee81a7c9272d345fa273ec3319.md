When this statement was made over three decades ago, computers were infinitely less ubiquitous and less interconnected than they are today. There is no real way to follow the earlier precept as advice if one wanted to do. Interconnection stands at the center of what modern devices are intended to do. Air-gapping is a standard compromise for protecting devices whose operation is of critical importance whose functionality is not crucially impacted by electronic isolation.

The literature reflects about the degrees of effectiveness of partial air-gapping. It has been suggested that time-sensitive attacks against air defense system might be foiled or at least frustrated by “intermittently air-gapped system.” Cybersecurity writers described how the introduction of an air gap complicates the journey of malware to a target but also obscures the effect of the malware by observation by attackers, who are left to extrapolate or guess target system, the effects, and the defender’s response.60 Personnel at the U.S. Naval College identified cyberattacks in 2006, and as these activities continued unabated, officials extended the air-gap of the Naval War College by disconnecting the facility offline. Similar steps were taken the following year at the Pentagon when cyberattacks triggered the decision to “temporarily disconnect the unclassified network [NIPRNET] from the Internet.”/61

Although temporary or intermittent air-gapping is not an uncommon practice in the face of an identified and episodic cyberattack threat systems that can survive a period of disconnection, it is an impractical option for protecting systems that must always be operating and connected—and periodic air-gapping is of little use in the face of attacks that aim for quasi-random results. The introduction of espionage malware to U NIPRNET, discovered in 2008 and addressed by actions dubbed Hobbes Yankee, required the reimaging of a reported several million Lion Department of Defense (DoD) electronic devices.62

Cybersecurity experts have noted both that “air-gapping has to be complete to be totally effective” and also that even and diligent air-gapping may not be sufficient. If an infected but air-gapped machine sits close enough to an infected connected machine, the two may be able to exchange signals over low-power radio or low-frequency sound waves.⁶ This point also highlights the fact that machines are designed to connect can be difficult to keep separated. The fact that air gaps can be foiled by malicious use of their embedded and ubiquitous microphones shows that when the logical distances have been consciously designed to establish isolation for critical systems, the default configuration of devices can be leveraged to subvert that effort. Furthermore, it is worth noticing that physical distance can continue to play a role in such attacks. Rather blithely consider that the cyber domain has no bound to the significance of geography and physical distances, it is more useful to consider how the cyber domain impacts the way distance, geography, and maneuver can be conceptualized.

# Temporal Mythologies of Cyberwar

## CHEAP AND WIDELY AVAILABLE

The price and availability of cyberattack tools range as wildly as their potential effects. As a result, the widespread cliché that cyberweapons are rampant and virtually free is simultaneously true and misleading, depending on what kind of target and what sort of cyberattack is being imagined.

Forensics analysts conclude that the high-end of cyberweapons are tools that demand extensive development and investment. In a similar way to how building is made constantly destroying, destroying in a specific way can also form a high bar. To reliably weaken specific outcomes, testing is required, and this translates into costs that are manifested in time and money. Additionally, the potentially global reach of a weapon means that attack sponsors must determine how they balance precision with cost-effectiveness. Minimizing the possible impact on bystander machines—or on one’s own devices—begs the question about further investment till. The Slammer worm that spread in 2003 was just 376 bytes long, which accurately facilitated its propagation across networks⁴ Cybersecurity experts state that Stuxnet was a 500-kilobyte piece of malware that leveraged four-day exploits, the Flame malware that is reported to have helped support and prepare for Stuxnet's use was a 20-megabyte piece of malware constituting 650,000 lines of code. Experts “it would have a team of half a dozen programmers at least three years to code it all.”⁶⁵

These concerns point to a conclusion highlighted in the early 2010s, indicating that kinetic-cyberattacks would be poor choices from a cost-effectiveness standpoint. Calculating the materials costs of the 1995 Oklahoma City terrorist bombing, the preparation for the September 11 terrorist attacks, and estimates about the costs and effects of cyberattacks attacking a regional hydroelectric dam air traffic control system, analysts have pointed to steadily diminishing returns:

$30 lethality in Oklahoma City, $160 per lethality on September 11, and between $8,000 and $24,000 per lethality for the hypothetical cyberattack.66

Although this is correct from an analytical standpoint, it does not address the question of psychological impact providing a multiplier effect; historian Alex Roland aptly noted that psychological effects are surprisingly useful in combat. Furthermore, psychological impact lies at the heart of terrorists' objectives, even compared to a nation-state. A reasonable conclusion might be that although nation-states are more likely to provide the resources needed for a spectacular cyberattack non entity as a terrorist group would be more likely to desire one. A decade ago, analysts explained that "terrorist groups in general do not have the expertise to conduct advanced or complex cyber attacks" and therefore for the time have been limited to the same basic vulnerabilities that are constantly targeted by hackers around the world."67

Perhaps this explains statements such as the U.S. deputy for information warfare stating that although today we face the threats from both individual lone wolf of actors out there [and] nation-states ... it's the nation-state actors that keep me awake at night and that we have to be more concerned about . In spite of the fact that crimeware and commodity malware is out there and potentially hitting our networks every day the big issues that we have to worry about are coming from the nation-state actors: Russia, China, Iran.68

That said, while the consequences of a nightmare kinetic-effect nation-state cyberattack beggar the imagination and represent a legitimate topic of concern, they currently remain hypothetical. This can invite imaginations to run wild, it invites others to discount the chances of such an event. The vast majority of of cyberattacks to date have been comparatively simple to launch, and many of them have been irritants whose effects can frequently be described as "reversible." While we definitely see the volume [of threats] increase, one cybersecurity executive has explained, the more troubling piece is thatwe see the sophistication of those threats grow exponentially. So, this is no longer your teenager in a hoodie in the garage. This is sophisticated cybercrime with talented and very refined individuals," resulting in companies like Cisco Systems employing more than 250 personnel to deal with cyberattacks, when broadly defined, number more than 2,500,000 per second.69
