At present, this inciting concept carries too much in the hypothetical realm to explore comprehensively, although it carries a few similarities to nations Italy in the early 20th century, whose geopolitical prudence and martial posture outpaced its actual industrial potential, would proportionately enlarge the strategic benefits of military developments until the introduction of the air domain to warfare.

Hayden stated that “I’m not worried about nation-state conducting a cyberattack as a bolt from the blue. I’m beginning to worry about the mid-range, isolated rogue nation-state—the North Korea or in some circumstances the Iran—who, out of desperation, might actually try to do something like that.”⁴⁷ Author and journalist Gordon Correa concurs because cyberweapons are easier to produce or to use nuclear, they are “an attractive option for weaker states.”⁴⁸ Robert Mandel argues that a powerful country can use cyberattacks to inflict “parity at every turn,” “despite the high military power disparity” that might exist, such as the case for Ukraine as it copes with Pro-Russian separatist military activity and domain power attributed to Russia.⁴⁹ Conti and Raymond do not go so far as to suggest that smaller countries exceed the power of larger ones, but to observe that reliance on sophisticated weapons can accentuate vulnerabilities.⁵⁰

The dogma of the superior impact of the offensive has gained traction with some reason, and with other generalizations, it can persevere as a dogma as long as it is continually fed by sufficient and seemingly plausible evidence that validates it. One additional factor that helps sustain the offensive dogma unfolds from the fact that a successful attack (particularly an overt one with abruptly visible effects) to some extent tips the hand of an attacker and draws attention to the event.
