Honeypots can also be “poisonous,” containing code that can harm an intruder. Poisonous honeypots are more controversial than other types intended for tracking intruders, and scholars disagree in their views about the implications and reactions to their use. Often-soriented digital countermeasures “may unnecessarily antagonize other states to use such an extent that kinetic hostilities erupt,” but other scholars note that “the State utilizing a weaponized honeypot may be able to defend the legality of its actions on several grounds,”particularly particularly since the damage wrought by the honeypot was caused by the intruder’s actions rather than the defender’s. A U.S. Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) honeypot from the 1980s was declassified in 2004, referring to US intelligence seeding pipeline control software (which Soviet agents were known to steal) with malware that reportedly precipitated a malfunction and explosion causing millions of dollars in damage to Siberia.25

In line with the character offensively oriented countermeasures could bring escalation, renowned cyber analyst Martin Libicki argues that “a cross-domain response may be viewed by others as an act of aggression, despite the antecedent cyberattack.”26 This,\,\ with his advocacy of what he calls “The Vegas Rules,” in which what happens in cyberspace should stay in cyberspace and exchanges should not be allowed to expand to other domains and escalate. Other researchers agree, suggesting that an eye-for-eye approach in responding to cyberattack “is too permissive” or asserting that classifying aggressive cyber activities, such as the 2008 Georgian whites attributed to Russia, as attacks would probably have led to more intense and prolonged hostilities which could have resulted in more destructive physical effects on the ground.”27 These considerations clearly clearly aggressive cyber attacks act as from—and less-than—“real” acts of war and these arguments point for narrow definitions of cyberwar in order to avert the casualties expected to accrue from escalation of tension.
