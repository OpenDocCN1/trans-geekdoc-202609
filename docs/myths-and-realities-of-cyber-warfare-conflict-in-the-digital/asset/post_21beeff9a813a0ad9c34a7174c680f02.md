# THE DEFENDERS' POSITION

Some factors might mitigate what appear to be an overwhelming advantage of offensive cyber, and different researchers have pioneered alternatively to limitations of a cyberattack or opportunities available to defenders.

Two notable limitations in cyberattack have been explained by Rid and Libicki, whose work frequently aims to dispel what they view as blown bears or exaggerations about the domain. Relying on the point that the use of a cyberweapon betrays the existence of the exploit and vulnerability, Rid has indicated that “when it comes to cyber weapons, the offensive has a shorter half-life than the defense.”⁵² This may be true, although the record shows many cyberattacks have been successfully employed ever (sometimes many years after) their first use would theoretically initiated the countdown on their relevance in cyber conflict. It does not address the point (outlined by Libicki) that given adequate testing facilities an attacker can examine effects as part of modifying and developing a cyberattack tool that becomes visible only upon deployment.³³ This means that although an attack may indeed have a shorter half-life than is the case with a defense, the clock begins running for the defense when it is released, and the clock on the attack is started only with the launching of an attack.

Seeking to dispel excessive notions of the power of the offensive in cyber, Libicki writes that “a cyberattack cannot disarm a target state's cyberwarriors.”⁵⁴ This also may be true, but it too should not be carried too far. Rather it might be compared to when a bomb destroys a tank or a parked aircraft without injuring a disounted crew; the bomb makes a weapon system inoperable but does not disarm those combatants. However, the combat power of various specialized forces is intrinsically related the equipment they are skilled in using. Put simply, tankers lacking tanks and redundancy planes vastly less combat power when they become foot-bound infantry; although they are not rendered helpless, their effectiveness is definitely impacted. Libicki is right about encouraging readers not lead to conclusions about cyberwar that are reminiscent of interwar strategic bombing advocates who insisted that airpower could induce fatal industrial paralysis by dropping high explosives on factories. This pitfall can be avoided while acknowledging that attacks will exert a potentially significant impact without being totally devastating.

Greater perspective on the limitations of a cyberattack can help mitigate an excess faith or fear in the power of attack in cyberspace—ideas about defenders' own options and opportunities are valuable tools, developing response plans help put into place to take in response to an event that will occur at the time chosen by the attacker, and infrastructure redundancy will provide a cushion against some attacks and can buy time for a defender to put its contingency plans into action.¹⁵⁵ International cooperation could be another useful, but difficult to orchestrate, tool. Cybersecurity advocates have observed that the NATO partners prioritized cybersecurity threats before witnessing the evidently politically motivated and orchestrated DDoS attacks against Estonia in 2007. Not only did cybersecurity experts provide assistance to Estonia during this high-profile watershed event, but NATO by the following year reevaluation of the importance of cybersecurity; although not considering the cyberattacks to trigger the NATO charter's Article 5 clause was established and the Estonian capital city of Tallinn as new NATO Cyber Security Centre of Excellence was selected as headquarters in a point meant to signal solidarity with the new NATO member that had been subjected to the wave of cyberattacks.¹⁵⁶

For the future, Conti and Raymond predict an ongoing "race for advanced capabilities" which defenders will continue "lag significantly behind" While this seems to predict a future that looks much like the present, they note that future research will lead to an explanation of opportunity areas for defenders who have so far been overlooked. For example, "deception isn't only for the offense,"⁵⁷ and a more conscious use of deception by defenders could help them withstand attacks and gain intelligence about attacking adversaries. This would turn on its head notions that an attacker would have better in-the-moment intelligence about an attack than would the defender. Nor is the concept without precedent, since the development of a honeypot on the Berkeley computer system played a key role in enabling researchers to trace the 1986 Cuckoo's Egg hackers (the first famous case of strategic online espionage) to their origins in East Germany.

Mythologies of Cyberwar Effects

Unpackaging the dynamic between defenders and attackers indicates that the offensive in the cyber domain indeed include a number of advantages for the attacker. But analysis shows that the defenders are not necessarily left entirely hapless. This conclusion, whether holistically as more reassuring or foreboding, dovetails with another recurring assertion over cyberattacks: that the victim is at fault.

# BLAMING THE VICTIM

Some cybersecurity authors ask whether, if defenders' actions can substantially foil an attack or can mitigate its impact, the defender should not, therefore, shoulder some actual degree of responsibility for a breach. Indeed, since many of the most lucrative targets in cyberspace are institutions that possess and utilize the data entrusted to them by vast numbers of people, the breach of an institution can seriously threaten the integrity of individuals who have no input about the security measures undertaken their data—or any acknowledged right to refuse the breached institution from collecting the data the first place. Questions of responsibility grow still more complex when cooperative or contracting arrangements trigger connection of systems with different cybersecurity standards.

The 2013 and 2014 saw large-scale data breaches at Target and Home Depot, resulting in data compromises for millions of individuals. Following the breaches, forensics teams in both instances discovered that the in-the-moment detection was impeded by attackers using valid vendor credentials, but that in the case of Target, the networks were arranged without controls to limit a user's access within the system. The hack of the Office of Personnel Management (OPM) was disclosed a year after the 2014 Home Depot hack, and hit an organization with storing data of current and past employees as well as of contractors associated with federal projects. It was yet reported that after the attack it was realized that OPM lacked even one of its own servers and no regular scanning was done to search for possible vulnerabilities. When the breach was first disclosed, OPM underestimated the number of victims by tenfold, only later conceding that information of twenty-million people had apparently exploited; then, the response resembled the reaction to a criminal attack despite outrage that the action was actually conducted for intelligence purposes.

The understandable outrage of individuals whose information has been compromised by security lapses raises the question of whether the victim deserves blame for an attack's success.
