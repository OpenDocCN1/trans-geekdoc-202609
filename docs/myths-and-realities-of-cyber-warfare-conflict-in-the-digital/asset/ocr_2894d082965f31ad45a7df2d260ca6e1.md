In early April 2019, just days ahead of the Scandinavian country’s upcoming elections, Finnish police were “probing a cyber attack on a web service that vote tallies. The cyberattacks could not wreak physical damage, nor could they tamper with the vote counts since Finland does not employ electronic voting, but it was feared that an untimely takeover of the vote reporting “could severely hamper the media’s access to election results and undermine public trust in the elections.” Those intending to sow discord or undermining domestic feeds do not necessarily need to change an election outcome. Undermining trust can bring tangible results in the physical realm–results that carry geopolitical consequences.

Democratic processes can be upset indirectly through disinformation operations (hardly “reversible”) operations already demonstrated via social media. While Russia’s artificial and false-flag social media activities in 2016 have been the subject of extensive controversy in the United States, they are neither the only nor the most recent example. As is discussed later in this book, social media platforms have begun to respond to the public and political outcry by (belately) taking a more vigilant stance on accounts meant for information operations. But, as expected in conflict, adversaries respond to each other, and the defender’s reaction to aggression is apt to be met by the aggressor’s counteraction. Facebook now watches for politically active accounts emanating from St. Petersburg (reported to be the nexus of Russian interference activity).
