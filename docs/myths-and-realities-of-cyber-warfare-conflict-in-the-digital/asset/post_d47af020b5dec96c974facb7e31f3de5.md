## Unpacking the Mythologies of Social Media

### ISIS VIOLENCE IN THE SOCIAL MEDIA LENS

During its brief but bloody life as a quasi-state, ISIS prioritized the establishment of “its own brand” online and especially via social media.53 Its social media activities defied the tendencies identified among most other terrorist organizations in their online messaging campaigns; however, ISIS used social media as if to establish its own claim on violence, to define itself as a state, and thus to later the definition of statehood on the international state.

In broad historical terms, the emergence of nations-states coincided with the violence that might otherwise result from chaos in a state of nature among humans. Strands of contemporary philosophy gave rise to ideas about the responsibility of the state to answer the interests of the governed to the extent that when governance mutated into oppression, theressor could be overthrown so that a new governor could be established to respond to the grievances and interests of the citizenry. While the political philosophies of figures like Robert Filmer, Thomas Hobbes, and those who disagree sharply on one point for another represent some of the more iconic philosophers on the cusp of the Enlightenment. Such ideas provide a bedrock for precepts that the state possesses a monopoly on the legitimate use of force and when other actors conduct violence their actions are inappropriate and merit censure or punishment. These ideas, coupled with ideas about the natural alignment of political state boundaries with cultural populations, helped define the international frontiers and many of the political issues of the modern era. Although ostensible legitimacy in the use of force is far from the significant factor with respect to ISIS and with respect to its use of social media in conflict.

ISIS rejected a range of predominant concepts and models about human and interstate relations. In its claim to constitute a Caliphate, ISIS aspired to a global reach beyond the narrow definitions that had until decades later became the conceptualization of nations-states. Although three-quarters of its propaganda appeared in Arabic, notable proportions in English (18%) and French (6%)54 pointed to an interest in alluring following the Middle East region by employing two of the leading global languages.

Crucially, ISIS's online activities reflect its intent to alter the geopolitical landscape. Condemations of historic colonialism or red-herring accusations about the Sykes-Picot agreement's implications for the Middle East might be interpreted in this vein. More significantly, the movement's online presence reflected this attempt to change the meaning of statehood and to conjure a state from extremist holdings across Syria and Iraq.

## Myths and Realities of Cyber Warfare

Interestingly, its direction and activities to al-Qaeda can be recognized by comparing its online strategies and activities to al-Qaeda's apparent reaction to ISIS's messaging campaign. Al-Qaeda efforts to create its own outlet fizzled and the group continued to rely on existing semi-periodic prime announcements. Reportedly, al-Qaeda's reclusive head Osama bin Laden scorned the messaging used by proto-ISIS figures; notebooks seized in bin Laden's Abbottabad hideout included criticism that he had scrawled in frustration over fanciful killing machines touted in the extremist propaganda materials prepared by the future leaders of ISIS.¹⁵⁵⁵

Despite being aligned ideologically in terms of radical interpretations of Islam, secrecy cloaked al-Qaeda in a way that its leaders struggled to prove the effectiveness of blatant and graphic violence depicted by then-ISIS groups. This may reflect the fact that al-Qaeda understood itself to be a nonstate actor. Its attacks on diplomatic facilities, military installations, and, of course, its attacks on September 11 show that al-Qaeda was fully bent on conducting violence and using it as a tool through which to manipulate world events. That use of violence stands at the center of what defines terrorism. ISIS certainly utilized violence for similar terrorist tactics. ISIS also wanted to create a physical caliphate, and this required the making of a quasi-state, which would hold territory and at least resemble a traditional geographical entity. Al-Qaeda versus ISIS needs to be understood as quibbles representing the practicable goals rather than constituting measurable daylight between the two worlds. Although disagreements among radicals can rapidly spiral into extreme manifestations, there is little difference between their objectives outside of the younger group adhering to a certain warped optimism about how quickly it could translate extremist violence into tangible gains.

This matters because it can help illuminate the reasoning behind ISIS's bloodcurdling celebration of violence, in ways so graphic that al-Qaeda operatives criticized them for their potential to backfire as propaganda. If one imagines ISIS's overt celebration of its brutality as a political expression of itself as a state—that it implied agreed to claim statehood by demonstrating its monopoly over violence in the territories it controlled—then rejection becomes understandable in an abstract sense. ISIS interest in jihad games gamification of violence on popular console games like Grand Theft Auto,⁵⁶ does not only encourage early engagement by potential recruits but also normalize violence and set it within a context of legitimacy that a war-oriented state would idealize.
