SOCIAL MEDIA, ACCESS, AND THE DEMOCRATIZATION OF SPEECH

The impact of electronic connectivity is profound. Its sheer pace has been equally staggering.
The unveiling of the internet in the closing years of the 20th century unlocked an unprecedented expansion in access to information—provided that a person resided with one of less than 15% of Americans had a computer and an ability to connect online, or that a person could access such a machine. Looking back from a perspective decades into the 21st century, it is important to recognize that these personal devices were typically full-blown desktop computer stations, that they almost always carried a cost in the thousands of dollars without cheaper alternatives, and that internet connection speeds were orders of magnitude slower than connection speeds a quarter century later. Memories hard, and processors have experienced experienced revolutions in capacity, alongside other improvements in areas such as visual and sound experiences.¹ Connection in the past century has not just become nearly ubiquitous across most of the United States, but globally. The rates of connection in underserved parts of the world have been little short of stupefying: between 2000 and 2007, internet usage more than doubled across the world and increased by 870%–920% in Africa and the Middle East.
