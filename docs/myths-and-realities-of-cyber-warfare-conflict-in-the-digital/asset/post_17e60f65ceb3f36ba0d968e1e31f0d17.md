Thus, although experts know that tailored attacks against complicated and obscure systems require time reconnaissance of the target and preparation and testing of attack tools, an awareness that SCADA as a class of potential target has existed for the quarter century since Eligible Receiver 97, and it has been heightened by Aurora more than a decade ago, in addition to the actual examples of attacks that have taken place.

Forensics studies show dramatically different degrees of sophistication and technical surprise with respect to cyberattacks, including those that have been deployed against SCADA systems. At the high end, the Stuxnet malware with its four discrete zero-day exploits, a decade after its discovery, continues to represent the apparent high-water mark of sophistication in a known anti-SCADA malware. The repeated attacks against Ukrainian electrical power infrastructure embody the other extreme in terms of technical surprise. The 2015 attack leveraged a vulnerability that had actually identified a dozen years earlier. But the inherent criticality of critical infrastructure precludes practices such as patching or updating devices over SCADA systems. As a result, the factors that Singer and Friedman have long noted the patching of known vulnerabilities on many personal computers worldwide are still more extreme for systems that must be 'on' continually.

In short, interrogating the temporal mythologies of cyberwar invites the conclusion that cyberattacks (broadly defined) are diverse enough to resist generalizations about the speed of cyberwar, about the supposed irrelevance of distance or geography, about their cost-effectiveness, and about their potential shelf-life as single-use weapons.

## Mythologies of Cyberwar Effects

### REVERSIBILITY OF EFFECTS

A number of widespread ascriptions about cyberattacks concern their effects on a target. These include the common but not universal idea that attacks produce only temporary and reversible effects, arguments that the victim of a cyberattack essentially deserves the blame for its occurrence, the predominant interpretation that attack is superior to defense in the cyber domain, and assertions that indicate expectations that cyber hostilities may come to eclipse or displace fighting in the physical domains. The idea of reversible attacks may be the most useful starting point in an effort to unpack and consider these conceptions.

Many of the voices that spurn “cybergedgedon” imagery choose to underscore their view that cyberattacks’ effects are limited, temporary, or reversible. This is why Thomas Rid has suggested that cyber “war” has more metaphorical than descriptive value”, and specifically: “no cyber offense has caused the loss of human life. No cyber offense has ever seriously damaged a building.”² Strategy writer Colin Gray has embraced three of the mythologies in a single sentence, arguing that cyber offense is swift, but it is not likely to be deadly, and it should not work twice.³ The swiftness of cyberattack has already been shown to be dependent on the kind of attack launched, and although they, in fact, do succeed after first appearing in the wild. Gray’s second assertion may prove to true, but it is at least flanked by characterizations that require substantial contextualization.

In a real sense, the picture drawn by Gray lacks internal cohesion. Many of the cyberattacks that are swift are also tools like DDoS, which are the most prone to repetition. To date, these also see them as the attacks least likely to inflict damage or draw blood directly—although they may be used in concert with other actions, as the 2008 DDoS attacks against Georgia coincided with combat between Georgian and Russian personnel. Syria’s civil war, which rallied essentially from 2011 to 2017, triggered the creation of a “Syrian Electronic Army” (SEA) sympathetic to the governing regime; SEA counted on simple (and reversible) cyberattacks such as website defacements.⁴ It can be suspected, but not confirmed, that these attacks were reversible because they were easier for an unevenly skilled band of cyber military to prosecute targets than a more damaging and less reversible series of attacks might have been.

One of the principal reasons that cyberattacks are frequently considered to be reversible stems from an enduring generalization enunciated by Singer and Friedman in 2014 that “the good news is that there are three things you can do to a computer: steal its data, misuse credentials, and hijack resources.”⁵ The implications that from tampering in these areas are more fully explored in the final chapter, but suffice to say that the insinuation of attacks with a range of either convenient or essential processes that mean theft, misuse, and hijacking can exert less finite kinds of deletes than may be at first apparent. Analysts specialising in cyberwar have observed that although attacks (including via cyber as kinetic) can conceivably destroy physical hardware, “data and code can be restored to the power of the latest unsupported backup,” and “infinite copies of information may be made at near zero cost,” which is a core factor in an information age economy and also “means that producing identical copies of cyber weapons at scale is effectively free.”⁶

Some kinds of impact resist blithe belief of “reversibility” even despite their not directly triggering physical destruction. The countries on Russia’s “near abroad” have been subjected to several kinds of cyberspace. The generally low-sophistication attacks against Estonia (in 2007) and Georgia (in 2008) are the most widely known. In early April 2019, just days ahead of the Scandinavian country’s upcoming elections, Finnish police were “probing a cyber attack on a web service that vote tallies. The cyberattacks could not wreak physical damage, nor could they tamper with the vote counts since Finland does not employ electronic voting, but it was feared that an untimely takeover could severely hamper the media’s access to undermine election results. Those intending to sow discord or undermining domestic feeds do not necessarily need to change an election outcome. Undermining trust can bring tangible results in the physical realm–results that carry geopolitical consequences.

Democratic processes can be upset indirectly through disinformation operations (hardly “reversible”) already demonstrated via social media. While Russia’s artificial and false social media activities in 2016 have been the subject of extensive controversy in the United States, they are neither the only nor the most recent example. As is discussed later in this book, social media platforms have begun to respond to the public and political outcry by lately taking a more vigilant stance on accounts meant for information operations. But, as expected in conflict, adversaries respond to each other, and the defender’s reaction to aggression is apt to be met by the aggressor’s counteraction. Facebook now watches for politically active accounts emanating from St. Petersburg (reported to be the nexus of Russian interference activity). Therefore, in Ukraine March 31, 2019 elections, Russian intelligence agents have adopted a different approach in pursuit of an older goal. Analysts identified Russian actors who wanted to sell their accounts or temporarily remove them out in order to publish political ads or to plant articles. As a Brookings Institution blog observed in connection with this play, “Russia has used Ukraine as a test-lab for its arsenal of political warfare and Ukraine’s experience is a beteller for assessing the Russian tactics that may be deployed against the West.
