Other kinds of actions may seem nonviolent in and of themselves, they are harder to undo or do they fit within a context of escalation that comes to include irreversible actions. The reported confrontation between the nebulous hacker collective Anonymous and the Mexican drug cartel Zetas in 2011 illustrates the point. When a potential member of Anonymous was reported kidnapped by the gang, fellow hackers declared that they gained access to relevant databases and threatened to disclose the identities of officials who had been bribed or were cooperating with the Zetas gang. This threat to publicly identify or dox a vulnerable figure has become a part of hostility in cyberspace, as the actual release of dosing or damaging personal information. Although dosing does not directly cause violence, it would be difficult to credibly suggest that a victim of dosing had been dealt a “reversible” attack. The gang’s escalatory response of threatening to murder civilians for ever dosing reportedly brought an end to Anonymous’s threat.20 Even actions that are not directly destructive involve irreversible effects.

## NOT NECESSARILY REVERSIBLE

If ostensibly nonviolent activities in cyber are of questionable reversibility, the physical damage wrought by cyberattacks specifically tailored to particular kinds of systems such as SCADA is inherently irreversible.

The insider threat attack on Maroochy Shire’s sewage treatment plant ushered in the beginning of infrastructure attacks just prior to the start of the 21st century. Vitek Boden, a contractor who had worked on the county’s sewage treatment control systems, was angered when he was not later given a position overseeing the facilities he had helped build as a contractor. Using a radio transmitter, a laptop, and his own knowledge of the control system, Boden arranged the improper release of hundreds of gallons of untreated sewage into public parks and waterways, resulting in both “environmental disaster” and “a health hazard to the local population.”21

While being the most famous example to date of an attack on SCADA, and the one commonly ascribed to political motivations, Stuxnet was neither the first nor the most recent example of SCADA being targeted in order to yield a kinetic effect. ## CHAOS

Elizabeth Dubois of the University of Ottawa succinctly notes that “chaos changes the balance of power internationally, and so if you don’t like the amount of power you currently have in the global system, creating a chaotic system is going to help you, potentially, maneuver to a position where you have more power.^26 In short, actors pursuing a revisionist agenda will likely seek to introduce discord or uncertainty into a

status quo in hopes to then achieving a fact accompli. An infamous modern example involves Russia, especially regarding the controversy about the nature and extent of its activity in the lead-up to the 2016 elections in the United States (although which allegations of cyber-induced chaos have appeared). According to one former international professional, Russian president Vladimir Putin “wanted . . . to muck up that system and he did a beautiful job of it,” although “we have also recognized that” and potentially awakened to the threat of psychological tampering with the internet. Journalist David Sanger wrote blunt, “if the Russian goal was simply to trigger chaos, it worked.”^27

Kello, as an international relations scholar, calls cyberspace “a breeding ground for political disorder and strategic instability,^28 and the calculated spreading of misinformation clearly illustrates that dynamic. Tweeted rumors of Ebola cases in the continental United States or chemical explosions on the Gulf Coast^29 can spread quickly and illustrate how a lie can circle the Earth before a correction can be announced. The first years of the 21st century saw U.S political and military efforts focused largely, and understandably, confronting jihadi terrorism. One government study from this “growing impact of persuasive technologies to manipulate the abstraction of reality and consequently the truth,” adding that the tendency to seek news from already trusted views has been presented “of necessary context,” perhaps heightening the changes of nonstate actors to directly impact” perceptions and attitudes.^30 Subsequent events brought popular dissatisfaction with aspects of the campaigns against terrorist organizations and signs of renewed alertness to the approaches and capabilities of other nation-states, but the conclusions in 2005 remain relevant. Analysts note that some cultures and organizations, such as in China, interpret the U.S military concept of OODA (observe, orient, decide, act) loop differently from U.S. strategists. However, whereas many thinkers see gaining a speed advantage in OODA by accelerating the pace of each step, researchers suggest that Chinese strategists see opportunity in using warfare to slow the adversary’s progress through each step, gaining a comparative advantage without requiring a wholesale revision of one’s OODA processes.^31

Inducing chaos would be an effective method of slowing their OODA steps, and the cyber domain offers different avenues through which to accomplish this.
