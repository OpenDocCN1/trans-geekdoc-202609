# A “CYBER PEARL HARBOR

U.S. Defense Secretary Leon Panetta’s comment in 2012 that cyberattacks against infrastructure could trigger “physical destruction and the loss of life” and “be a cyber Pearl Harbor”45 leveraged provocative imagery about the direct prospects of an act of cyberwar. The 20th-century forebearer attack on Pearl Harbor was a stunning blow that would foreclose U.S. opportunities to undertake strategically or operationally significant actions opposing Japan’s seizure of petroleum resources in the Dutch-owned islands of modern Indonesia. Although not a war-winning act, it devastated U.S. naval power in the short term even more contemporary than reports to the U.S. public. A forgotten point about Pearl Harbor was its prominent but not singular place within a larger context of surprise attacks against various targets in more than half a dozen locations stretching 6,800 miles from Singapore to Hawaii. The Pearl Harbor attacks are the most widely remembered in the United States; but from a strategic standpoint in 1941, the destruction of U.S. battleships nested within a context of surprise attacks. Those surprise attacks supported Japan’s objective of seizing and securing oil fields and other key resources in the southwest Pacific Ocean. Understanding that Pearl Harbor was a massive portion of a larger campaign is important if the allusion to Pearl Harbor is to be utilized in contexts such as cyberspace.

Another crucial point is that Panetta’s statement was not the first Pearl Harbor allusion regarding a vulnerability in cyberspace.
