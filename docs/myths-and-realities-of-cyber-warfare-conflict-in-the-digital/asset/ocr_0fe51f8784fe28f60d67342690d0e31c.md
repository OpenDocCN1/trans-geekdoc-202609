The functionalities that users expect from applications nonetheless require designers to create specific platforms that can operate in diverse ways. For example, a smartphone is a telephone for voice calls, but it must also allow “videoconferencing, downloading video clips, listening to music, using a GPS to navigate to a location, and connecting to any number of networks including G[lobal] S[System] M[obile cellular or Wi-Fi.” Adding functionality to a device inevitably needs to embed various standards that make different functionalities possible. Since some of these entitle royalty payments for intellectual property represented in the standards, this can impact device and service costs. 55

Increased functionality brings the insulation of larger numbers of potentially different standards. This contributes to the growing complexity of development and dovetails that can inadvertently shelter software flaws described earlier. Businesses’ have displayed a noticeable awareness with respect to shouldering responsibilities and burdens associated with cybersecurity. Research asserts that “Silicon Valley must accept more of the political and social responsibility that the success of this technology has thrust upon it”56 A potentially compelling case to this effect can be made; an important consideration, however involves questions about what responsibility obligates or empowers companies to do. Responsibilities fall into areas that remain governance. Companies are predictably loathe to enter the simultaneously thankless, expensive, and controversial realm of governance. This contributes to the Wild West of confined policy makers hoping to protect intellectual property, guard national security, and preserve freedom of speech without facilitating propagation of criminal activity or violence.

However, even if Silicon Valley companies were to heed the advice given by Singer and Brookings and “accept more . . political and social responsibility.” it is not clear how widely their embrace of governance roles would be greeted with exuberant enthusiasm. Those steps would empower private sector entities in determining what speech or commerce is appropriate or acceptable. Most situations will fall into greyer regions than black-or-white edges of the spectrum, and Silicon Valley (and its global counterparts) hold positions as nondemocratic governments. Entities that have been widely decried for doing little in the face of various information manipulations could be entrusted with decisions that impact an unprecedented public. Users of services and platforms are not citizens, and are not voters. While customers may seek to vote with their dollars and take business elsewhere, this can prove challenging.
