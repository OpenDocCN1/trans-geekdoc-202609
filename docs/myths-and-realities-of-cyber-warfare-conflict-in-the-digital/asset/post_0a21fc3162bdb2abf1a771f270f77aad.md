In a gay nightclub in Orlando, Omar Mateen called emergency responders in order to pronounce his allegiance to ISIS and then used his smartphone to track whether his attack had gone viral. Mateen's actions reflect an acute interest in social media as an avenue for disseminating terrorist messages. Another vividly example was ISIS's use of social media to invite sympathizers to “suggest a way to kill the Jordanian pilot pig,” or the uploading of countless videos of fiendish murder methods to websites, displaying the deaths of various people caught in ISIS territory and anathema to his beliefs. 58

But a further, and also disturbing, conclusion can be drawn as well: The shocking images of wanton murders, carefully edited would be gruesome but kept short of a graphic threshold that would lead to media outlets' censure and the inciting of dissemination, yet another sinister purpose. Unabashed portrayals of murderous executions might have reflected the group's attempts to establish itself as a state and bestow itself to possess a monopoly on violence. ISIS appears to have attempted to demonstrate its statehood through the seizure of territory, confirm it through the establishment of a theocratic governing elite, and broadcast it through the advertising of official masses. The fact that the eradication of ISIS's territorial changes to its social media suggests that its propaganda was indeed bent not only on celebrating ideological propaganda but on the establishment of a jihadist ideological state. Its endurance could have impacted—warped—the image of statehood, along the lines that other historical massacres and oppressions have tarnished the image of traditional geopolitical forms in the eyes of many during the modern age.

## SOCIAL MEDIA'S IMPACT ON THE STATE MONOPOLY ON VIOLENCE

Social media can be leveraged in different ways in connection with violence. Some nonstate actors directly use social media to sharpen the effect of their attacks. The jihadist attacks in Mumbai in November 2008 were launched with a considerable degree of coordination that made both electronic communications and the leveraging of social media. The gunmen were linked by cell phone to control in Karachi, Pakistan (500 miles away). Bystanders near the attacks would witness responders' actions and tweet what was happening, and see ones caught in the attack were not the only people watching these posts; personnel in control gleaned an extraordinary amount of information about the authorities’ response which could then reach the gunmen by cell phone, updating them on police activity and ensuring that the attacks could continue longer.

Frustrated users, realizing that the attackers were making use of social media updates, hurried to warn fellow users by posting further information and claimed the government had ordered an end to such posts. 60

Tools can be used in disparate ways even in the context of violence, as the November 2015 attacks on Paris demonstrate. Despite the precedent of the Mumbai attacks, credible reports had not surfaced. However, because the attacks left large numbers of people disoriented and vulnerable, many Parisians responded with the hashtag #OpenDoor to identify locations where survivors could safely shelter. One million users using this hashtag appeared within ten hours. The response to the Mumbai attack of November 2008 and the Paris attack of November 2015 saw important information posted on Twitter correlate the difference between who was watching and reacting in the two cases.

ISIS was not alone in using social media in connection with fighting in the Middle East. In the battle against ISIS, many of militias that fought on behalf of Iraq were reported to have received support from neighboring Hezbollah, whose power in battles with Israel had already fought in Syria. When an Iraqi captured a suspected ISIS fighter in 2016 and launched an Instagram post about whether to kill or release the prisoner, 75,000 votes were posted globally. A follow-up post uploaded two hours later consisted of a selfie photo with the prisoner lying in a pool of blood. The caption, 'Thanks for vote,' accompanied the image. 62

Other actors outside ISIS and often beyond Islamist terrorism, have also employed social media in ways that potentially affect the state's monopoly on violence. Significant cases across Europe and Asia point to how contested uses of social media complicate ideas about the relationship between states and national security. Some analysts point to the use of official statements on social media as an inflection point for accepted violence. Filipino president Duterte's rhetoric in opposing drug cartels in his country has been identified as an example. An estimated 12,000 people have died including drug dealers but also ordinary odds and targets of the regime's police. 63 Confirming the existence and extent of such dynamics can be extremely challenging, although history suggests that the elimination of identified threats to society can lead to a snowballing effect in which an array of other grievances and grudges become enmeshed in the selection of victims.

## Unpacking the Mythologies of Social Media

Harnessing popular sentiments can become a dangerous game. Other scholars have identified the restlessness of patriotic hackers as a potential source of unrest. While some states have directed patriotic hackers toward external targets as deflecting their malice away from potentially vulnerable institutions alike, this appears to be a relatively short-term expedient. Expectantly hackers grow frustrated that their homeland, which they are permitted or encouraged to believe through their online activity, is not more strident in its official policy. One study noted that the Chinese military’s “escalation” at the end of 2012 followed popular nationalist protests against Japan and the Chinese Communist Party party-state—but not proving—that an autonomous public was one proximal cause of the Chinese government’s escalation of the dispute. Analysis indicated that a combination of real-world street demonstrations and social media opinion “played a role in escalating” a state’s handling of a territorial dispute. 64 Given the state’s strong preference for social harmony, the prospect of such an impact would undoubtedly upset officials and policymakers, incentivizing policy shifts meant to restrain the opportunity for activists of any stripe to autonomously shape policy.

The events of the attempted coup in Turkey announced in July 2016 remain controversial and murky. One account described the coup as initially following the contours of a conventional coup attempt being foiled because social media was used to detail the unintentional cooperation of many of the plot leaders’ rank-and-file personnel. Soldiers, mobilized to support the coup, told they were merely undergoing training exercises, were reportedly tipped off by bystanders loyal to the regime; these civilians were able to show ordinary soldiers social media information related to the coup attempt, and upon learning that their “training” might be used to support a revolt, they ceased cooperating with their commanders and the coup collapsed. Notably, in the wake of the coup, Turkish president Recep Tayyip initiated a crackdown on opinion makers. This meant the closure of over 1000 schools, sixteen television stations, fifty-eight newspapers and magazines, and twenty-nine publishers as well as the dismissal of 135,000 servants. Social media also faced restrictions, despite its apparent use by opponents. 65 In Turkey, social media has been used by the state’s supporters in order to prevent the regime’s overthrow, and the regime in its recognition of the value of social media has to tighten its control over it already apparently within its grasp.

Social media has been notably involved in the low-intensity conflict smoldering in eastern Ukraine, where pro-Russian separatists and the Ukrainian government have clashed since 2014.
