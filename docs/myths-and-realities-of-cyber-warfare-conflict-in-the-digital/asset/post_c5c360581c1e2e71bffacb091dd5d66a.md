## Myths and Realities of Cyber Warfare

To say that Rabin and Yoo support an unconventional view among Western Scholars is an understatement. Other scholars have suggested that more careful and curtailed usage of cyberweapons could be accepting. Noting that “an ideal war would be a war where civilian casualties were minimal or nonexistent and where acts of violence perfectly discriminated between combatants and noncombatants,” one modern philosophy scholar has argued “cyberfare has made possible this kind of ideal warfare.”

While some advocates tout using the domain to advance policy aims analogous to the use of war, still others define cyber broadly, while doing so in ways that seek to restrict the accepted range of activities. Legal and ethical issues have been extended to the defender’s work to trace and punish an intruder. Because this active defense, also referred as hack, “normally involves breaking into a number of computers, it is generally illegal under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act.”²³ This line of argumentation shows that a broad definition of cyber should be assumed necessary to represent for hostile activities in the domain.

## DEFINING “WAR” NARROWLY

Many authorities on cybersecurity issues prefer to define war more narrowly. Reluctance to engage in acts deemed cyber accompanies these views, although some of these scholars go so far as virtually declare the concept of cyber exploration of systems on one hand (which are deemed legitimate) and the destruction or manipulation of systems on the other (considered illicit).

As hack involves actually tracing and potentially retaliating against an assailant, more passive alternatives exist as well. One timehonored approach is the use of so-called honeypots. Borrowed from the physical domains, notably during the Cold War, or of interpersonal seduction to gain intelligence against targets, cyber honeypots attract an intruder to explore red herring elements in a defender’s system. They are maintained specifically to watch for intruders, since to an outsider they may resemble a valuable target but to insiders, they are extraneous or false elements in a system. Cyber honeypots come in several forms, including honeyports designed to “detect network scanning and enumeration attempts,” and honeywords that simulate an administrator’s passwords. Honeypots quickly alert defenders to the presence and activity of an intruder, and this helps defenders work to prevent future intrusions and minimize the impact of future incidents.”²⁴

Honeypots can also be “poisonous,” containing code that can harm an intruder. Poisonous honeypots are more controversial than other types intended for tracking intruders, and scholars disagree in their views about the implications and reactions to their use. Oftenoriented digital countermeasures “may unnecessarily antagonize other states to use such an extent that kinetic hostilities erupt,” but other scholars note that “the State utilizing a weaponized honeypot may be able to defend the legality of its actions on several grounds,” particularly since the damage wrought by the honeypot was caused by the intruder’s actions rather than the defender’s. A U.S. Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) honeypot from the 1980s was declassified in 2004, referring to US intelligence seeding pipeline control software (which Soviet agents were known to steal) with malware that reportedly precipitated a malfunction and explosion causing millions of dollars in damage to Siberia.²⁵

In line with the character that offensively oriented countermeasures could bring escalation, renowned cyber analyst Martin Libicki argues that “a cross-domain response may be viewed by others as an act of aggression, despite the antecedent cyberattack.”²⁶ This with his advocacy of what he calls “The Vegas Rules,” in which what happens in cyberspace should stay in cyberspace and exchanges should not be allowed to expand to other domains and escalate. Other researchers agree, suggesting that an eye-for-eye approach in responding to cyberattack “is too permissive” or asserting that classifying aggressive cyber activities, such as the 2008 Georgian whites attributed to Russia, as attacks would probably have led to more intense and prolonged hostilities which could have resulted in more destructive physical effects on the ground.”²⁷ These considerations clearly aggressive cyber attacks act as from—and less-than—“real” acts of war and these arguments point for narrow definitions of cyberwar in order to avert the casualties expected to accrue from escalation of tension.

Thomas Rid, the political scientist famous for declaring that cyber war will not take place,” concedes that “when seems to be a more appropriate conjunction than an “if” with respect to “when the first casualty is caused by a cyber attack.
