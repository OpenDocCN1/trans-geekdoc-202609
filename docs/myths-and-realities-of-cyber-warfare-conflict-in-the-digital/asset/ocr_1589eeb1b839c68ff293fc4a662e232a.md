Traditional media may be playing a game by trying to remain relevant by (and despite) upholding social media examples as sources of news information, the concept that the internet would lead to an inexorable decentralization of authority and power seems to be only partially realizing itself.

Alert state actors seeking to influence opinion either at home or abroad can be expected to study these trends. The television and semio news outlet Russia Today (RT) has attracted considerable attention for activities that bear this hallmark. Launched in 2005 with a $30 million annual budget, RT has expanded prodigiously until it now boasts YouTube subscribers than any other news analysts have pointed to an alignment between RT reports, trend hijacking on Twitter, and statements by state officials such as Russia’s foreign minister; these combine to establish an echo chamber effect in which assertions from one source can be reached and validated by another. RT and its peer in Russian news called Sputnik were also among the traditional media outlets that portrayed “Jenn” prior to being discovered as a product of the Russian Internet Research Agency.³32

This should not be easily dismissed as a temporary anomaly. As Paritarakos notes, “the Internet will inevitably come to benefit the oppressor as well as the oppressed; . . . in the end there will always use the same tools to fight back.”33 Questions about the impact of social media, relative both to institutional advantages in the realm of speech and the state’s monopoly on violence.
