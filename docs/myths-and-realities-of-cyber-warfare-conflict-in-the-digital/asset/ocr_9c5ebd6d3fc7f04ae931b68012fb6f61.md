In a sense, also, the idea that cyberwar 'skips' the battlefield misses an opportunity to explain how attacks in the cyber domain could do something much more like transcending or extending the bataille simply skipping it. The latter imagery more closely matches the historical pattern in which weapons possess a potential for longer reach are leveraged not only at the most extended ranges but are instead used across the spectrum of them. The development of long-range strategic airpower did not preclude the extensive use of combat planes at the operational level or in close air support across battlefields from World War I onwards. Thus, although cyber advocates may argue that "what makes cyber attacks" revolutionary is that they can strike from thousands of miles away, it should be noted that this means that geographic ranges are less pressing than the adversary's distance and physical location may be difficult to discern. Thus, advances in cyberwar capabilities constitute the emergence of a fighting domain where logical geography may trump physical geography.

One upset of this is the implications it brings for strange bedfellows and unusual coalitions, although in the past geographically conflicts have shared the wartime alignment of diverse interests: Western democracies shared little common with the Soviet Union under the threat posed by Nazi regime, and rebellious Sepoys garrisoned in Singapore had no more common cause with Germany in 1914 than Faisal bin Hussein's tribesman had in common with the British Empire two years later. Demarch's observation that "cyberspace enables long-distance access" but also increases the chances of "multiparty alliances and coalitions for attacks" fits in that context.

The capacity for attackers to "set up anywhere where internet cafes are found" or to "initiate attacks from almost anywhere on the globe instantaneously" is a shocking thought when long ranges of attack are considered wholly from a geographic standpoint. The map of logical terrain, which is artificial and continually subject to flux, overlays the geographic map. Furthermore, its implications will grow to seem more natural—will be viewed as "intitive" they become familiar. In a similar way, transportation technologies have long been taken for granted, and for reason few would be surprised to imagine that a woman in the Tokyo airport is ever closer to New York City than a man on foot standing in Buffalo: we have become accustomed to the implications of transportation technologies and their impact on the time factor, even though the former is a distance of 6,740 miles and the latter is 134.5, just 2% away from the destination in physical terms.
