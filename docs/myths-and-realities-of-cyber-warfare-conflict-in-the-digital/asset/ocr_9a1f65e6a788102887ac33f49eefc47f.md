No cyber offense has ever seriously damaged a building.”² Strategy writer Colin Gray has embraced three of the mythologies in a single sentence, arguing that cyber offense is swift, but it is not likely to be deadly, and it should not work twice.³ The swiftness of cyberattack has already been shown to be dependent on the kind of attack launched, and although they, in fact, do succeed after first appearing in the wild. Gray’s second assertion may prove to true, but it is at least flanked by characterizations that require substantial contextualization.

In a real sense, the picture drawn by Gray lacks internal cohesion. Many of the cyberattacks that are swift are also tools like DDoS, which are the most prone to repetition. To date, these also see them as the attacks least likely to inflict damage or draw blood directly—although they may be used in concert with other actions, as the 2008 DDoS attacks against Georgia coincided with combat between Georgian and Russian personnel. Syria’s civil war, which rallied essentially from 2011 to 2017, triggered the creation of a “Syrian Electronic Army” (SEA) sympathetic to the governing regime; SEA counted on simple (and reversible) attacks cyberattacks such as website defacements.⁴ It can be suspected, but not confirmed, that these attacks were reversible because they were easier for an unevenly skilled band of cyber military to prosecute targets than a more damaging and less reversible series of attacks might have been.

One of the principal reasons that cyberattacks are frequently considered to be reversible stems from an enduring generalization enunciated by Singer and Friedman in 2014 that “the good news is that there are three things you can do to a computer: steal its data, misuse credentials, and hijack resources.”⁵ The implications that from tampering in these areas are more fully explored in the final chapter, but suffice to say that the insinuation of attacks with a range of either convenient or essential processes that mean theft, misuse, and hijacking can exert less finite kinds of deletes than may be at first apparent. Analysts specialising in cyberwar have observed that although attacks (including via cyber as kinetic) can conceivably destroy physical hardware, “data and code can be restored to the power of the latest unsupported backup,” and “infinite copies of information may be made at near zero cost,” which is a core factor in an information age economy and also “means that producing identical copies of cyber weapons at scale is effectively free.”⁶
