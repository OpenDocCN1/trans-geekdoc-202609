One of the originating motivations in the development of the first effective 20th-century computers was itself the identified need to break enemy codes and gather intelligence. While computers have not removed the need for human beings in various areas of espionage, computerization has provided increased efficiencies while reducing costs and risks.¹ The advent of cyberspace may not allow instantaneous spying, but it can enable activities at geographic ranges to protect individuals conducting espionage, and it has been used to obscure attribution or to create a leaf of protracted deniability.

# ENCRYPTION

Predictably, encryption is as complex an issue as is espionage. Generally, the purpose of encryption is to enable confidential communications to remain private. Since technologies are not "good" or "bad," and cannot be reserved for use only by "good guys" or "bad guys," encryption technologies might be better of as used for different purposes and by different actors as is the case regarding espionage.

Nonstate actors' involvement in war illustrates a demand and use of encryption. The creation of the internet in the 1990s occurred at the same time jihadist groups consolidated their political hold on Afghanistan and as one of their former financiers, Osama Laden, formed a network of militants known as Al-Qaeda. Intent on launching periodic attacks against Western targets, planners in different locations used email to coordinate efforts and prepare a series of bombings, most of which were directed at diplomatic or military targets in Africa or the Middle East. The expansion of the internet coincided with the Global War on Terrorism, in response to Al-Qaeda's attacks against key political, strategic, and economic landmarks on the U.S. eastern seaboard.
