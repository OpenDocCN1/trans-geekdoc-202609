A CNBC article quoted the CEO of a bitcoin research company saying on Feb. 10, “These marketplace changes will inevitably slow nefarious activity and open channels to more and more institutional investors. In my opinion ‘PBoC [People’s Bank of China] clean-up is the best thing that could have happened to bitcoin this year.” This is obviously speculative but is consistent with the sharp appreciation of bitcoin’s value in late February. As the quote suggests, the potential benefits apply to use of bitcoin as money—or more generally as a financial asset.

Indeed, both regulators and bitcoin’s users in the marketplace invite the question of whether bitcoin is actually money at all. The FAQ section on bitcoin.org is very clear in describing bitcoin as digital money. In the US, the IRS declared in 2014 that virtual currencies as bitcoin would instead be considered property.

However, the status of money is determined not only by lawmakers. Bobby Lee was quoted by Business Insider calling bitcoin “a very ripe opportunity for day trading to make money,” but he also noted that bitcoin “has not taken off in China as a form of payment.” Overall, as shown in the New York Times article, only a small proportion of bitcoin transactions put bitcoin into individual wallets to be exchanged directly for goods and services. The vast majority of activity is on the market exchanges—people buy and sell bitcoin on the exchanges, but they spend traditional currency. Most people do not own bitcoins, and it seems may of those who use bitcoin to store value, but not as a primary medium of exchange or unit of account.
