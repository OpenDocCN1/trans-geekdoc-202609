## Chapter 4

### Deep learning for cuffless blood pressure monitoring

Jamal Esmaelpoor, Zahra Momayez Sanat and Mohammad Hassan Moradi

There is a great motivation to use easy-access bio-signals to alleviate the difficulties and shortcomings of present invasive and cuff-based blood pressure monitoring techniques. Many studies propose methods mainly using photoplethysmogram and electrocardiogram signals. Most of these methods are based on hand-crafted features. Although they take advantage of our previous knowledge, it is highly desirable to propose models that are less dependent on feature engineering.

To this aim, in this chapter, we develop several architectures based on deep neural networks. Through discussing their advantages and drawbacks, we try to develop more appropriate models to meet the demands of the British Hypertension Society and the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation standards. The building blocks of our models are convolutional neural networks and long short-term memories. We evaluate our models on 200 subjects.

### 4.1 Introduction

Cardiovascular problems are responsible for the death of almost 50% of all people who die because of non-communicable diseases. Therefore, since blood pressure (BP) is among the most vital physiological indicators that reflect the cardiovascular situation, its continuous measurement is very crucial. It can be a true indicator of many health issues, such as hypertension, heart attack, asthma, and kidney failure [1].

Several methods are available for BP monitoring. The most prevalent techniques are intra-arterial catheter insertion, auscultation, oscillometry, and volume clamping.

The intra-arterial catheter insertion approach requires the insertion of a catheter in a vessel. The method makes accurate and continuous BP reading possible. It was first practiced by Hales et al by inserting tubes into some animal vessels in 1733. However, Cushing performed the first invasive method based on the catheter insertion in a surgery room in 1901. In spite of the high accuracy of the method, due to its invasive nature and the difficulties it creates for patients, its application is limited to hospital environments, under the close supervision of experts, and merely for patients who are in critical conditions [2].

Auscultation is an indirect method to measure BP. In this method, a cuff is closed around the brachial artery on the upper arm, then using a mercury sphygmomanometer the pressure in the cuff becomes measurable. Then a stethoscope is employed to make some sounds in the squeezed artery audible [3].

Oscillometry is another cuff-based method. The measurement is based on the oscillation in the inflatable cuff during the deflation. The oscillation starts even when the BP is above the systolic BP and resumes under the diastolic pressure [4].

Cuff-based methods are straightforward and involve pretty simple procedures. However, if the patient is obliged to do them frequently, they cause discomfort. Moreover, different interpretations of important events in these methods might cause significant variability and low accuracy.

Volume clamping is a non-invasive and continuous BP monitoring technique. It considers the dynamic vascular unloading of the arterial walls of the finger vessels. The method uses a finger cuff and a photoplethysmographic sensor in a feedback control loop to keep the blood flow constant. In this condition, the applied cuff pressure though the same value as the BP [2].

Even though volume clamping methods provide a non-invasive and continuous reading of BP, they are most of the time too expensive and complex to use. Therefore, there is a lot of motivation to use easy-access bio-signals like photoplethysmogram (PPG) and electrocardiogram (ECG) to measure BP continuously, non-invasively, and conveniently. In this chapter, we aim to explore the potential of deep neural networks (DNN) to achieve this goal.

This chapter is organized as follows. In section 4.1, we reviewed the available BP monitoring methods briefly. Section 4.2 provides a physiological model for BP. Section 4.3 discusses the data used in this chapter, and the preprocessing steps applied to them. Section 4.4 presents four different DNN architectures and their results. Section 4.5 discusses the models’ performances, and finally, section 4.6 concludes the chapter.

### 4.2 Physiological models

Photoplethysmography is an optical approach to indicate blood volume fluctuations caused by the pressure pulse in the peripheral circulation arteries. This measurement is carried out by illuminating the skin using a light-emitting diode and detecting the reflected or transmitted light by a photodetector [5]. Electrocardiography, however, is a method to detect electrical excitations of the heart. It is detected by placing some electrodes on the prespecified places of the body [6]. Consequently, both PPG and ECG signals contain crucial information related to the cardiovascular system and its BP.
