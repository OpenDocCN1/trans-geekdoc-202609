### 7.2.3 Common reasons for diagnostic failures

Skill gaps in bedside examination abilities, poor clinical reasoning skills, and problems in synthesizing information are significant contributors to diagnostic errors [17–19]. Knowledge and skill gaps have been underestimated in this context, while cognitive bias has been overemphasized [17, 20]. In primary healthcare, common reasons for diagnostic errors include failure to order appropriate tests, faulty interpretation, problems in follow-up, failures in referring patients, transfer of care, and insufficient consultation techniques [2, 9].

Education in clinical content knowledge, awareness of bedside history, and correct physical examination skills are examples of factors that can reduce diagnostic errors [7]. In emergency department settings proper diagnostics relates to acuity recognition, information synthesis, and straight communication with patients [21]. More advanced imaging technologies have improved physicians’ ability to detect, diagnose, and treat conditions while allowing patients to avoid invasive procedures [22]. However, diagnostic radiology errors, especially shorter viewing times, are clear contributors to adverse outcomes [23–25]. Pathology is an essential step in cancer diagnostics and better integration of quality and safety principles is seen as necessary in error prevention in this area. Diagnostic error prevention in pathology requires, e.g., collaborative teamwork, enhanced training about the diagnostic process, and a safety culture focusing on health information technology and other strategic changes [26]. As is known, proper investigation intensity is critical in healthcare. Many challenges, however, in this area can be met and poor clinical outcomes could be prevented by enhancing diagnostic processes [27, 28].

### 7.2.4 Preventing diagnostic errors in cooperation with patients

The importance of patient involvement and mutual communication between patients and doctors are emphasized widely in prevention of diagnostic errors. Improved patient access to clinical notes with co-generation and feedback possibilities represent one form of error recovery and shared decision-making [2, 29]. The majority of patients who access their patient records report positive experiences [29]. However, this is only one step toward better patient-centered care. In prevention policies in a Scandinavian study [9], patients emphasized the value of nationwide online medical records, regular comprehensive physical examinations, and clarity in routines for the follow-up. Availability of contact information for doctors and other medical professionals was seen as important in situations of normal test results but remaining symptoms [9]. Healthcare professionals may propose a working diagnosis in cases of uncertainty [2]. When the diagnosis stays unclear, the follow-up care is often inadequate.

## 7.3 Diagnostics and safety challenges in healthcare

### 7.3.1 Patient safety and equity challenges

Patient safety is a multidisciplinary goal and a primary task in healthcare. In addition to technological innovations, a hospital culture should emphasize change-positive leadership in clinical practice [30]. Healthcare organizations should promote safety-conscious culture and learn from diagnostic errors [2]. Encouraging transparency about errors can make healthcare safer and foster a culture in which healthcare professionals are supported in identifying and learning from mistakes [2, 31].

In many countries, patient safety is poor, especially among the older patient population, partly due to an inadequate and disorganized healthcare system [32]. In western society, patients who have taken good care of their health may remain relatively healthy until the age of 80. At this age, specific health problems often occur, in which case, there would be a particular need for high-quality care and higher treatment intensity. In spite of that, medical treatment intensity is lower among the older patient population (aged 80 and over) [33]. Old age is easily perceived in healthcare as a so-called ‘period of fragility.’ However, older adults form highly heterogeneous groups, and their health and functioning may be better than negative stereotypes suggest [34]. This age represents a specific period in life when, in many cases, intensive care may be needed for the first time. The diagnostic process for the older patient population can be challenging due to its multi-problem character [35]. The high prevalence of pain may be considered a normal phenomenon in older age [36, 37]. This complexity can easily result in treating only the symptoms and not the cause of the symptoms.

Also, critically ill elderly patients show favorable clinical outcomes in intensive care. Thus, underlying medical conditions rather than age per se should be considered for determining intensive care [38]. Inaccurate estimates of operative risks however prevent older people from receiving sometimes even standard surgery because they are deemed unfit for demanding treatments [39]. Almost one-half of all critically ill patients with primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) admitted to ICU were alive six months after ICU discharge [40]. Frailty-rating scales or simple, standardized tools alone are not acceptable prognostic assets for predicting unfavorable outcomes, for example, in acute care [41–43]. Quality auditing and transparency are needed, especially in elderly care.

One of the key ideas in translational medicine is to improve a patient’s health in an ethically acceptable way. Shared decision-making has a solid place in translational design in healthcare; hearing the client’s voice is one step toward better equality. Such decision-making means intense collaborative processes between patients and providers [2]. Shared decision-making is about giving weight to the patient’s values and opinions, and also when older patients are in question. Thus, tuned feedback systems, suitable for all age groups mean important and vital development areas. The COVID-19 crisis is an area fostering the use of modern information and communication technologies among older adults [34]. Equality in the decision-making process among different age groups refers to the state of ethical values in organizations.

### 7.3.2 Enhanced patient safety with rational cost control policy

Cost-saving pressures in healthcare may pose a challenge to a doctor’s clinical work. Inadequate savings targets in patient care, for instance in the area of diagnostics can disturb clinical practices and be very costly. A common approach to save money is random reduction of laboratory budgets [28]. Diagnostic management teams represent an advanced model for more intensive and meaningful cost-effective processes. In this model professionals with expertise advise colleagues in clinical practice on test selection and interpretation [28, 44].

AI offers more proficient methods for the optimal use of human resources and the planning of logistical processes [45]. Those methods offer new options to find rigorous cost savings opportunities because human resources form in hospitals the most significant cost factor [46]. Thus, personnel costs do not have to be viewed only as fixed costs. This optimization modeling will also be principally taken care of by those resourced for these planning tasks, which is a step forward.

Advanced AI tools and methods have their role to play in sound cost management policy and, therefore, in safety enhancement when enabling doctors to have better focusing primarily on their clinical work. Also, intelligent diagnostic systems like AI-assisted decision support systems can improve diagnostic accuracy and aid in disease management and thus help doctors decrease routine clinical tasks and focus first and foremost on patient care [47, 48]. These systems also do not increase the duration of the doctor consultation or the number of ordered tests [1]. Digital scribes, like AI-powered digital notes enable doctors to fully engage with patients by reducing the time invested by clinicians in the documentation process [49].

## 7.4 Importance of patient feedback in the diagnostics phase

### 7.4.1 Need for timely feedback

It is essential that doctors get patient feedback on their diagnostic skills and competence. Overconfidence is more prevalent than low confidence, especially at lower competence levels [50]. Also, extensive experience does not necessarily mean greater diagnostic accuracy [17, 51]. Only a few healthcare organizations have genuinely concentrated on identifying diagnostic errors [2, 52]. Healthcare professionals often do not have the opportunity to hear from health consumers about their diagnostic performance [2, 53, 54]. Time limits may prevent real engagement with customers in the diagnostic process [2, 55]. Clinicians need to be ready to communicate with their customers about the occurrence of diagnostic errors but also may find the feedback on their diagnostic performance uncomfortable [2, 56]. However, in feedback training programs, clinicians found the exercises constructive and helpful [56]. Organization-wide and improved customer complaints coordination may also help individual doctors develop in their self-evaluative processes by enhancing their diagnostic calibration properties [57, 58]. Diagnostic calibration is about self-knowledge and understanding of the limitations of one’s knowledge [58].
