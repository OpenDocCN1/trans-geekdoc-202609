Earlier, the medical imaging checks had been taken on radiological film, but nowadays all are processed digitally.

There was an exponential growth in the amount of data generated by the healthcare sector and the use of digital data in the form of images and videos become an integral part of the medical field also. This has led to an increase in research interest for the processing of medical data and to inform public policy. Customized and specially developed software are required to store and process those huge data and these approaches and methods can be useful in other areas including research on the cardiovascular area. Patient genetic and historic health record information can benefit reducing cardiovascular risk, whereas crowed sourced environmental data helps in cardiovascular epidemiology.

European Organization published a report on Nuclear Research (CERN) in 2013 that revealed that a data center stored 100 petabytes of medical data in the last two decades, but in just the last 3–4 years, 75% of total data was recorded and generated by the Large Hadron Collider [1]. Biological sciences and medical data are one of the major sources of such a huge amount of data. Fifty thousand sequenced genomes are available in the online database and less than $1000 are charged for whole-exome sequencing and per patient almost 700–800 GBs of uncompressed data [2, 3]. In today's scenario, a test done by a CT scanner can generate 600 MB to a few GBs of data with higher in-plane resolution. In many hospitals, the medical data and related huge data sets are stored, retrieved, processed, and managed by Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS). Wherever the amount of data becomes huge, data/image compression becomes essential for efficiently managing those data with limited resources. Since the beginning of PACS, various new compression techniques were developed even before the standard compression methodologies were adopted [3]. However, designing of exclusive compression methods significantly increases the expense and the work needed to integrate and shift data between divergent sources and systems, and interoperability and compatibility of these schemes require the usage of standards for compression in a communication system [4]. The outsized genomic datasets were known for a long time, and solutions were also proposed to store these huge data and how to interrogate them for disease association studies [15]. High-resolution equipment such as MRIs produces progressively detailed images that support the diagnosis but needs vast and increasing amounts of storage and computational power to do the analysis.

Image compression decreases the bit budget of a graphics file in bytes without degrading the image quality to an undesirable level. The reduction of file size makes it possible to store more images in a given amount of disk or memory space. The time taken for the so compressed photographs to be transmitted over the Internet or downloaded from websites is also reduced. Several different methods are used in which it is possible to compress image files. The two most popular formats are JPEG and GIF formats for Internet use.
