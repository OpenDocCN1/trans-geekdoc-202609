
## 7.4.2 The role of timely feedback

The purpose of patient feedback systems is to offer high quality health information. Such information can work as an enabler for enhanced organizational and patient outcomes [59]. Patients’ opinions about their visits to the doctor offer beneficial information for health professionals and comprehensive awareness of the causes of diagnostic errors with critical, unique insights [6, 60]. Healthcare providers should ensure that systematic feedback on diagnostic performance extends to doctors and care teams involved in care and organizational management [61, 62]. Periodic feedback is necessary for improved diagnostic performance and quality improvement in healthcare but is often absent [17, 53, 63, 64]. Such feedback, however, could be valuable as a regular part of the assessment policy [2].

Diagnostics-centered feedback systems should provide evidence-based information on critical areas of clinical practice [2]. Such feedback is welcome from both professionals and healthcare consumers [2]. Healthcare clients’ more prominent role in the diagnostics phase means that patients are ‘at the center.’ Such policy reflects ‘rehumanized medicine’ [62]. High-quality feedback from diagnostics can mean enhanced organizational and patient outcomes that benefit both patients and doctors by eliminating preventable adverse events and improving the diagnostic process [2, 53, 59, 60, 65].

Patient involvement should thus have a stable position in professional development within the clinical practice. However, this presupposes the existence of such cultural attitudes and values that foster patient-centered care and continuous learning [57, 58]. Even complete re-engineering of services and novel technologies without correct staff engagement do not change policies accurately [30]. Feedback should be part of learning systems that focus on improving diagnostic performance [56]. This helps target individual counseling assistance and aids in identifying doctors who may need educational support or retraining [66].

Patient engagement is a matter of supporting people in benefiting more from the healthcare system [2]. However, this engagement may be challenging for patients and their families [2, 67]. Disease-related stress with lower physical health can prevent people from engaging genuinely in the diagnostic process [2]. Some patients prefer active involvement in all aspects of the decision-making process, while others would instead rely on a doctor’s assessment [2]. Feedback systems should also allow space for those health consumers who have already experienced a diagnostic error. Such individuals are often ready to share their experiences to enlighten future patients’ diagnostic processes [2, 68, 69].

## 7.5 The challenges of diagnostics-centered clients’ feedback

An effective feedback system for the client on the diagnostic process’s success controls the quality of care and improved patient safety. Consumer targeted feedback systems are often very comprehensive, focusing on the entire healthcare process. There is a particular need for feedback systems that focus specifically on the patient’s view of diagnostics’ success and the patient’s experience of working collaboratively in this context [46]. Feedback systems that focus on the actual area of diagnostics should be linked closely to the treatment or care process in question. In this way, the doctor in charge could get the patient’s view of the diagnosis’s correctness and get additional information for evaluative purposes.

It is possible to assess the investigation intensity in the field of diagnostics by patient basis. The number of tests, examinations, and other research and consultation carried out in connection with the diagnostic process reflects the degree of intensity in the diagnostics phase. If the client believes that the degree of investigative intensity of their own case is too low for proper diagnostics, they must have a pathway to address their concerns. The current problem is that feedback on the validity of the diagnosis does not reach the doctor in charge quickly enough. On the other hand, the patient’s feedback on the medical performance should be specific enough to allow corrective action to be taken [70].

The feedback of diagnostics should be adequately specified, so that feedback delivery would be easy for the patient and beneficial to clinicians. Therefore, carefully constructed but economical (quick to fill) formats may enhance the process. Free-text comments are useful for service improvement if patient feedback is provided in near real-time [63, 71]. Due to AI algorithms, the utilization of free-text format is also more efficient. Thus, narrative feedback from the diagnostics phase also has its place as a basis for broader evaluation.

## 7.6 Enhancing technology acceptance in system development

Healthcare organizations should promote open and transparent environments in which patients can engage in the diagnostic process and share their feedback and concerns with ease [2]. At the same time, feedback systems should be sufficiently detailed, user-friendly, clear, and understandable. Also, there are problems in such areas as survey length, infrequent sampling frequency, and slow feedback. To be useful, patient feedback results should be timely, intelligible, and easy to interpret by all stakeholders. Clarity, ease of use, suitability for frequent use, and multiple features (e.g., feedback with paper, touchscreens, smartphones, tablets, and telephones with automated interactive voice response) are essential characters of a beneficial feedback system [63, 72].

In the technology acceptance model (TAM), the primary determinants for technology acceptance are perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use [73]. The model by Yan et al [74], describes aspects connected to the human–computer trust interaction. ‘Communication trust’ is one incentive for use. It covers areas of ‘perceived privacy’, ‘perceived identity’, and ‘communication context’. The customer must be able to trust that security issues concerning customer privacy are in order, e.g., in feedback system options [74]. Clients do not even use beneficial healthcare services if they are unsure about their information security [75]. Computer system trust relates to aspects of trustworthiness like ‘system and information quality’ and aspects of flexible use [74].

Motivation is an action inspiring humans in objective-guided activities and keeping them in motion [76, 77]. Pintrich [78] distinguishes among motivational beliefs for value-, goal-, ability-, and control-connected beliefs. Patients are motivated to give feedback only if they trust their input's validity and the change it enables in their own situation (goal-, control-connected beliefs). The application should represent values that are acceptable to the patient. Although some patients want to leave the responsibility for diagnostics to the doctor, many patients appreciate the opportunity to participate in evaluating their own care, especially in the case of diagnostics (ability-connected beliefs).

## 7.7 Phases of diagnostics and the requirements for doctors

### 7.7.1 Requirements for competence and compassion

Patients expect that the doctor has sufficient expertise to provide both right diagnosis and adequate care. Medical ‘competence’ for a doctor is assumed to include technical capability and willingness to provide needed information. Consumers also expect ‘compassion’, empathy, and moral support when making healthcare decisions [79]. Effective communication, trust, and an encouraging environment are aspects of mature consultation skills [60]. In surgery and intensive care, physician competence, like technical skills, is often preferred while in the end-of-life vignette, compassion is emphasized. In chronic care, preferences for physician competence and compassion vary. In pediatric cases, a doctor’s competence is preferred, but the importance of empathy is also valued [79].

### 7.7.2 Diagnostic process from the view of doctors

In their self-evaluative actions, many doctors use a variety of often individual-tuned process models. These checklists play a role in improving the diagnostic process [2, 80]. The best process models describe different phases of the diagnostic process. With aid of taxonomic models it is easier to detect what went wrong in diagnostic process. Taxonomic failures can be assessed from several standpoints in the diagnostic process; from access and patient history to the final stages of referrals and follow-up. In each step, embedded sub-categories clarify items for evaluation. In ordering tests, e.g., mistakes may include delays in test ordering or sequencing [61].

Schiff and colleagues [81] divide the diagnostic process into seven stages:
- access and presentation
- history taking/collection
- the physical exam
- testing
- assessment
- referral
- follow-up.

A patient’s clinical history includes documentation of the current health problem, history (medical, family, social), and other needed information like medications [2, 82]. Physical examination skills have a substantial role in diagnostics, but these skills have often been underemphasized in professional education [2, 83]. Diagnostic testing contains areas of laboratory, medicine, anatomic pathology, and medical imaging [2].
