Three-stage training vectors were developed using line spectral frequency and MFCC features for both continuous speech and sustained vocals in the MEEI database. The first stage was trained to distinguish healthy voice from physiological laryngeal abnormalities, while the second stage was accompanied by a comparison of healthy and neuromuscular laryngeal abnormalities. The unsupervised classifier has been trained to recognize neuromuscular and physiological abnormalities at the third level. The research work indicated an overall accuracy of 98.7% [102]. Physical symptoms of brain and spinal cord disorders, such as tumor, neuron degeneration, spinal cord injury, brain lesions, and other injuries, have been shown to influence the way a person speaks. Such abnormalities were also observed by [117] authors using speech digital signal processing algorithms. This also indicates that voice pathologies can also occur due to physical changes in regions other than the vocal fold. Thus, a speech signal is a rich source of information that can be used to present a drawn portrait of the mental condition of the person. Researchers at [117] pointed out the advantages of using 13 MFCCs using a 20 ms Hamming window and a 10 ms overlap generating 149 frames for each 1.5 s long speech recording. The SVM classifier has been trained with these vector features to differentiate between normal and different abnormal conditions.

**C. Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM).** This is another commonly used unsupervised classification method that works on the basis of the probability principle. It is commonly used in both voice pathology and speech classifications [118]. As an unsupervised process, the labels of samples are not known beforehand, but are learned iteratively instead. The GMM is based on the idea of the theoretical GMM concept. Authors of [119] have used a Mel-frequency filter bank to remove features from short regular speeches. The goal was to identify the speaker by training the unique vectors of each person with the GMM model. Implementation of live (undistorted) and telephonic (distorted) speech showed a wide range of accuracy of 96.8% and 80.8%, respectively. This has shown that accuracy is also dependent on sound quality. An improved variant of the GMM algorithm known as the GMM-universal Background Model (UBM) was proposed for voice classification in [120]. UBM is adopted after the extraction of the feature vectors. The maximum a posterior algorithm (MAP) drives the UBM algorithm using two stages. The parameters are initially evaluated in the MAP algorithm, and then new knowledge is combined with the old ones. The drawback, however, is that they are affected by a different language. In another work [121], authors have trained 11 different MFCC features with the GMM model to differentiate a regular voice from a pathological one. More precisely, they received 60 s-long voice recordings from 30 speakers, after which they applied 32 ms-dimensional hamming windows to each sample. Inside each window/frame, a filter bank of size 12 is used to explore 11 specific MFCC features within the 0–8 kHz range. Feature vectors belonging to regular or pathological groups are trained with GMMs of varying orders. It was known that the highest order GMM implementation gave the highest accuracy.
