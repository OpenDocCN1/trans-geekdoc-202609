
The time delay for the pressure pulse to travel through vessels is known as pulse transit time (PTT). PTT equals the time delay between the proximal and distal arterial pressure pulses and is inversely correlated with the BP value [7].

In this section, we consider a simple model for pressure wave propagation to define the relationship between PTT and BP. An arterial vessel is depicted as a flexible and cylindrical tube in figure 4.1. If we neglect viscosity, the resistance against blood current will be negligible. Compliance (C) that is defined as the ratio between cross-section area variations and pressure changes is defined as follows:

$$C = \frac{dA}{dP} = \frac{2\pi r^3}{E \times h} \tag{4.1}$$

where r is the radius, and h is the wall thickness. Elasticity is not unique and depends on P, as described in equation (4.2).

$$E(P) = E_0 \times e^{\alpha P} \tag{4.2}$$

Parameters $E_0$ and $\alpha$ are positive and subject-specific. If we assume that A is constant, the mass and momentum conservation rule results in the following equations.

$$\frac{d(A dx)}{dt} + Q(x) - Q(x + dx) = 0 \rightarrow \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} + C(P) \times \frac{\partial P}{\partial t} = 0 \tag{4.3}$$

$$A \times [P(x) - P(x + dx)] = \rho \times dx \frac{dQ}{dt} \rightarrow \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + L \times \frac{\partial Q}{\partial t} = 0 \tag{4.4}$$

Here, x and t are space and time, Q is the volume flow rate, $\rho$ indicates blood density, and constant $L = \rho/A$ equals arterial inertance per unit of length. Moreover, C is inversely related to P. The relationship between C and P in central arteries is:

$$C(P) = \frac{A_m}{\pi P_1 \left[ 1 + \left( \frac{P - P_0}{P_1} \right)^2 \right]} \tag{4.5}$$

here, $A_m, P_0$, and $P_1$ are subject-specified. Equations (4.3) and (4.4) indicate the following form for the pressure pulse in vessels:

$$P(x, t) = f\left( \frac{x \pm t}{\sqrt{LC(P)}} \right) \tag{4.6}$$

Figure 4.1. Wave propagation in central arteries.

It reveals that the velocity of the pulse in a vessel which is known as *pulse wave velocity* (PWV) equals \([L \times C(P)]^{-1/2}\). Therefore, the time for the wave to pass a length of \(l\) (PTT) is:

$PTT = l\sqrt{LC(P)}$ (4.7)

According to equation (4.7), PTT depends on BP inversely. If we assume that C is constant, the PWV becomes:

$PWV = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L \times C}} = \sqrt{\frac{A dP}{\rho d A}} = \sqrt{\frac{Eh}{2 \rho r}}$ (4.8)

This equation is known as the Bramwell–Hill and Moens–Korteweg equation [7].

Besides PWV and PTT, many other studies try to define other hand-crafted features and find out their relationship with BP [8, 9]. In this study, however, we emphasize automatic feature extraction using deep-learning techniques. We believe that similar to many other applications, machine-learning abilities can be utilized for finding appropriate representations of signals for BP estimation. Afterwards, we develop several methods to achieve this goal¹.

## 4.4 Deep learning models for blood pressure monitoring

In this section, we propose and implement four different DNNs to estimate BP. In each step, we try to analyze the results and find out the main restrictions of the model. Then we try to provide a solution for the restrictions and enhance the accuracy in the next step. Our models are based on two common and versatile neural networks: long short-term memories (LSTMs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs).

LSTM networks are a kind of recurrent neural network (RNN). Unlike feedforward neural networks, these types of networks have feedback connections that allow them to process not only current inputs but also previous ones. In other words, they have feedback routes to store current events in their memory states. In theory, classic RNN networks can learn arbitrary long dependencies in the input data. However, the propagating gradient can vanish or explode during the training procedure. LSTM networks solve this problem of vanishing gradient by allowing it to propagate unchanged [16–18].

CNNs are inspired by the connectivity patterns among visual cortex neurons. Each cortical neuron is stimulated by a restricted region of the visual field recognized as the receptive field. The receptive fields, then, partially overlap, and they cover the whole visual field. Moreover, since these networks utilize convolution operations instead of matrix multiplication, they have many fewer parameters and connections. This reality facilitates the training process considerably. They are also more effective in automatically detecting important features in the input data compared with their predecessors [17–19].

In this chapter, two-hundred records were selected from the MIMIC-II database to evaluate our models. After applying preprocessing procedures discussed in section 4.3, each record was divided into three separate parts: the training, validation, and testing sections each claiming 70%, 10%, and 20% of the whole length of the records, respectively. Consequently, the training, validation, and testing sets include 35 965, 10 574, and 5354 cycles, respectively.
