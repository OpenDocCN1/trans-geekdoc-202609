Timely diagnosis and proper medication of HPT may help in reducing the risk of various cardiovascular diseases [5, 6]. From clinical epidemiological observations, optimal systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) are less than 140 mmHg and 90 mmHg, repectively, in a healthy person [7]. HPT becomes severe when SBP and DBP are more than 180 mmHg and 120 mmHg, respectively [8]. A sphygmomanometer is commonly used to measure blood pressure [8]. Due to various environmental limitations it is being replaced by computerized measurement devices such as aneroid sphygmomanometers and digital electronic pressure transducers [9]. But these meters may provide inaccurate results due to the tendency of error in estimating SBP and DBP in adults [10].

As the pressure of blood can vary, 24-h, monitoring of blood pressure has emerged as a useful tool for diagnosing HPT [11]. Continuous HPT will sharpen heart, nerve, and kidney diseases which will be deliberated in the electrocardiography (ECG) report. Varying electrical activity of the heart is measured by using an ECG report [12]. The isoelectric line is calculated from the baseline voltage of ECG, which is the portion of tracing following the T-wave and preceding the next P-wave [13, 14]. ECG analysis helps the cardiologist to diagnose the symptoms of the various diseases [15].

Segmentation of ECG signal plays a vital role in the analysis of coronary artery disease, blockage in the heart, hypertrophy of heart chambers, etc [16]. ECG signal is the combination of P-wave, QRS complex, and T-wave. P-wave is also known as atria complex, and occurs when the senatorial node produces an action that depolarizes the atria. The P-wave is the first and positive wave of the ECG [17]. The height and width of a normal P-wave is less than 2.5 small squares. Taller P-wave occurs due to the enlargement of the right atria, which is greater than 2.5 mV and causes a pulmonary chronic respiratory disease called pulmonary HPT. A wider P-wave occurs due to the enlargement of the left atria having a duration of 2.5 s. Q-wave is the negative wave due to the depolarization of the basal portion of the interventricular septum. R-wave is the positive tallest wave due to depolarization of the apical portion in the ventricular septum and muscle. S-wave is the smallest negative wave due to depolarization of the basal portion in the ventricular muscle near the atria ventricular ring. The association of Q-, R- and S-wave is called QRS complex or initial ventricular complex. T-wave is the positive wave that occurs due to repolarization of ventricular musculature; with the help of ECG a segment cardiologist can identify the various types of heart activity [18].

# 1.1.1 Various intervals of ECG

P–R interval: is the time difference between starting of P-wave and starting of Q-wave. A normal P–R interval lies in the range 120–200 ms. Greater P–R interval (value more than 200 ms) indicates first-degree heart block, and if the P–R interval is less than 120 ms it indicates Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome.

Q–T interval: is the time difference between starting of Q-wave and ending of T-wave. A normal Q–T interval lies in the range of 360–440 ms. A long QT interval (QT > 440 ms) indicates hypocalcemia. A short Q–T interval (QT < 360 ms) indicates hypercalcemia.
