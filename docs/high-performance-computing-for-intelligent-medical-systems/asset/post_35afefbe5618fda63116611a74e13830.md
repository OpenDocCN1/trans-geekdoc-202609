## Chapter 12

### Recent trends in medical image segmentation with special focus on brain tumours and retinal images

**Mithlesh Prasad Singh, Aheibam Dinamani Singh, Rajesh Kumar, Kishorjit Nongmeikapam and Wahengbam Kanan Kumar**

In a variety of areas from diagnosis and prediction to treatment based on the course of a disease, medical imaging plays a pivotal role. Image segmentation is a process that extracts the area of interest automatically or semi-automatically. Multimodality imaging is a concept used to capture an area of interest in the format used by various types, such as computer tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron tomography (PET), etc. Various literatures demonstrated that their combination generates better classification of normal and abnormal tissues of brain, retina, lungs, kidney, etc. This chapter surveys state-of-the-art researches on biomedical image segmentation for brain tumours and retina segmentation, abnormality detection, advantages, disadvantages, and automated-learning techniques ranging from traditional machine learning to deep learning. The study also examines popular databases and contemporary approaches published so far. The chapter also provides a glimpse of key gaps and potential future scope for biomedical image segmentation of retina and brain tumours.

### 12.1 Introduction

One of the critical aspects of today’s medical diagnosis process is biomedical imaging, as it is used in all fields of modern medicine [1]. Biomedical image examination is virtually always a complicated and prolonged task which specialists still struggle with [2, 3]. Biomedical image evaluation by medicinal specialists is an essential process for detection of pathologies. Analysis of different medical imaging modalities using computers has been in usage since as early as 1982 [4, 5]. Many techniques have been introduced since then, with each having an objective to increase accuracy/efficiency during computerized automated detection [6]. Today’s technological progress could make it feasible to use new methods in the health domain. Throughout the practice with biomedical imaging, a medicinal practitioner relies upon subject reports and consequently makes specific and appropriate diagnosis about the subjects’ status. Depending on the type of task there exist some forms of biomedical image examination techniques; some of them include segmentation based on organ and substructure, detection based on object, registration, prediction based on classification, classification based on objects, detection based on region and landmark, segmentation based on the item and others. A maximum number of researches focuses on segmentation based on organ and structure.

In patients’ diagnosis and medication, imaging helps radiologists or clinicians make the diagnosis and prescription efficient. Currently, there is a wide variety of modalities for imaging soft and hard tissues/regions of the body. It has paved the way for more improved and early detection of abnormalities, and for possible early course of treatment. The foremost widely used modalities can be partitioned into two general categories: anatomical and functional. In this chapter, we only discuss the anatomical modality. These images can be represented in 2D, 3D and 4D systems. The elements of an image in 2D are referred to as pixels, while in 4D, they are referred to as voxels.

#### 12.1.1 Types of biomedical imaging

- A. X-ray. The most prevalent mode of screening that exists for identifying breaks and dislocation in bones is x-ray imaging. The image developed is 2D. One hundred thousand chest x-ray samples were provided by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) with relevant information and diagnostics to enhance imaging analysis techniques [7]. Similarly, a dataset comprising a sample of more than 350 000 body part x-rays for improving machine learning algorithms has been released by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) to automatically diagnose specific disorders such as pneumonia or pinched lung, etc [8].

- B. Computed tomography. It is also commonly referred to as a CT scan, and uses a computerised form or image acquisition in which 360 degrees of x-rays are aimed at the patient to create accurate cross-sectional representations of the body’s inner organs, bones, soft tissue and blood vessels. Traditionally, the images are taken in the transverse plane or axial plane perpendicular to the body’s long axis. However, it is possible to reformat these images, also known as slices, into multiple planes and create a three-dimensional image. By locating the presence of tumours and their scale, it is widely used to identify cancer and is among the most frequently addressed biomedical imaging issues. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) had given easy accessibility to 32 000 CT scan images with related information and diagnoses for enhancing lesion identification accuracy [9].
