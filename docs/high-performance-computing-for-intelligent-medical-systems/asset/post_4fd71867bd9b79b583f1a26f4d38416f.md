
Three-stage training vectors were developed using line spectral frequency and MFCC features for both continuous speech and sustained vocals in the MEEI database. The first stage was trained to distinguish healthy voice from physiological laryngeal abnormalities, while the second stage was accompanied by a comparison of healthy and neuromuscular laryngeal abnormalities. The unsupervised classifier has been trained to recognize neuromuscular and physiological abnormalities at the third level. The research work indicated an overall accuracy of 98.7% [102].

Physical symptoms of brain and spinal cord disorders, such as tumor, neuron degeneration, spinal cord injury, brain lesions, and other injuries, have been shown to influence the way a person speaks. Such abnormalities were also observed by [117] authors using speech digital signal processing algorithms. This also indicates that voice pathologies can also occur due to physical changes in regions other than the vocal fold. Thus, a speech signal is a rich source of information that can be used to present a drawn portrait of the mental condition of the person. Researchers at [117] pointed out the advantages of using 13 MFCCs using a 20 ms Hamming window and a 10 ms overlap generating 149 frames for each 1.5 s long speech recording. The SVM classifier has been trained with these vector features to differentiate between normal and different abnormal conditions.

### C. Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)

This is another commonly used unsupervised classification method that works on the basis of the probability principle. It is commonly used in both voice pathology and speech classifications [118]. As an unsupervised process, the labels of samples are not known beforehand, but are learned iteratively instead. The GMM is based on the idea of the theoretical GMM concept. Authors of [119] have used a Mel-frequency filter bank to extract features from short regular speeches. The goal was to identify the speaker by training the unique vectors of each person with the GMM model. Implementation of live (undistorted) and telephonic (distorted) speech showed a wide range of accuracy of 96.8% and 80.8%, respectively. This has shown that accuracy is also dependent on sound quality. An improved variant of the GMM algorithm known as the GMM-universal Background Model (UBM) was proposed for voice classification in [120]. UBM is adopted after the extraction of the feature vectors. The maximum a posterior algorithm (MAP) drives the UBM algorithm using two stages. The parameters are initially evaluated in the MAP algorithm, and then new knowledge is combined with the old ones. The drawback, however, is that they are affected by a different language. In another work [121], authors have trained 11 different MFCC features with the GMM model to differentiate a regular voice from a pathological one. More precisely, they received 60 s-long voice recordings from 30 speakers, after which they applied 32 ms-dimensional hamming windows to each sample. Inside each window/frame, a filter bank of size 12 is used to explore 11 specific MFCC features within the 0–8 kHz range. Feature vectors belonging to regular or pathological groups are trained with GMMs of varying orders. It was known that the highest order GMM implementation gave the highest accuracy. The research work listed in [117] suggested the use of Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) to disintegrate a voice sample into three wavelet sub-bands to test the MFCCs individually for each band. A total of 142 and 147 healthy and pathological recorded voice samples were used for the extraction of MFCC features, which were later trained in the GMM-SVM and GMM-UBM classification models. Each recording has a duration of 1.5 s with a sampling frequency of 44.1 kHz. The model that uses the MFCC features extracted from each of the GMM-UBM training sub-bands gave 96.96% accuracy. It is significantly higher than the 85.18% achieved while training GMM-UBM with MFCC features derived from a normal voice, that is, without any DWT disintegration. The main feature highlighted by their technique is the need to overcome the reliance on phonemes such as /a/, /u/ and /i/. MFCC features have been commonly used to classify speakers, speech pathologies, amnesia, Alzheimer’s, and emotional and physical trauma in individuals. It is not robust enough as it does not account for the non-linear acoustic structure released from the body during voice development [122]. Due to this aspect, the authors of [122] used an 11-set of non-linear methods with MFCC to extract feature vectors from healthy and abnormal voice samples in the MEEI database. A hybrid training model that utilizes the generative ability of GMM and the discerning capabilities of the SVM classifiers has been introduced. More precisely, the GMM and SVM classifiers are split into two different classification levels. Their methodology showed a significant accuracy of 98.23%. However, it remains to be seen if their strategy is doing well in the light of multiple classification problems.

The SVM classifier has also been trained with a feature descriptor developed by the joint implementation of the Daubechies DWT and the linear predictive cepstral coefficient (LPCC) [93]. Time-frequency analysis was able to effectively identify pathologies in a voice. The use of MFCC and other feature vectors with SVM classifiers and GMM for the design of the Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) model [123] was another recent and landmark research work. More precisely, the authors have developed a Hidden Markov Model Toolkit (HTK) to detect and identify voice abnormalities. A total of 297 speakers were used in their investigation, of which 176 samples were taken from individuals suffering from five forms of vocal fold disorders, of which 121 are typical individuals. Apart from the MFCC features, PLP, RASTA-PLP and LPC features have also been extracted, forming a special vector function for each voice sample class. The normal and abnormal categories investigation uncovered two distinct things. The implementation of the MFCC and LPC features supported 94.44% and 77.5% accuracy, respectively, whereas the use of PLP and RASTA-PLP feature descriptors provided an accuracy of 94.44% and 89.62% respectively. On the other hand, the pathological classification task performed mean accuracy equal to 76.16%, 87.14% and 87.65%, respectively, when the LPC, RASTA-PLP and MFCC feature descriptors are used. The use of PLP feature descriptors provided an accuracy of 88.63%, which is the highest of its feature descriptors. The investigation showed reasonable accuracy for standard samples, although the use of the different features of the descriptors individually resulted in poor accuracy. Manual segmentation of voice samples is a significant downside to the proposed work.

The GMM model was also used in [124] to diagnose spasmodic dysphonia in voice recordings. A total of 39 coefficients of feature descriptors were used in the work, including shimmer, jitter and MFCCs. The researchers also argued that the use of a second MFCC derivative offers an even better diagnostic quality.

## D. Mixed ML classifiers

kNN classifier is a simple and efficient ML technique that can divide a feature vector into different categories by using a distance metric. Application in voice pathology detection has also been reported. Examples of some of the features that have been used with kNN are:
- HNR
- shimmer
- turbulent noise index
- jitter
- normalized first harmonic energy

The use of these features with kNN on the MEEI data set resulted in accuracy of 96.1% [125]. The authors of [126] have used kNN with HNR and energy spectrum features to diagnose pathologies such as adductor, vocal fold nodules, laryngeal paralysis, polyp, and Edema. They extracted HNR features using four different frequency ranges after which they were combined to form a single feature vector. The resulting single vector was extracted for all voice samples and concatenated to represent the HNR features of the specific class. Another feature vector used by the authors is the normalized energy produced by the use of 21 bandpass filters. The second collection of features is an intentional effort to mimic the neural structure of the human auditory system. The two unique features of the kNN classifier showed very similar performance, that is, 94.28% and 92.38% accuracy for HNR and normalized energy characteristics, respectively. Research has shown that these two features can aid in the identification of laryngeal pathologies. It is also computationally less expensive as it needs only short duration signals. While other simple frequency and acoustic features extraction techniques work better with longer period signals, making them more computationally expensive.

Random forest (RF) or decision tree has also been used for speech features for the binary classification of pathological voice signals. The idea of RF is to combine a lot of binary decisions that are made using different bootstrap samples of the original data with random subsets to get an accurate predictor. The advantages of the RF method are that it is robust when additional trees are added and that the error of generalization is of limited value. For example, [127] researchers have developed a combined feature vector using 28 separate speech feature vectors:
- 10 MFCC features
- kurtosis
- fundamental frequency
- jitter
- energy
- shimmer coefficients
- power factor
- amplitude
- timing
- minimum and maximum signal values

The training of these combined and class-specific feature vectors produced 100% accuracy. However, there is a need to analyze a dataset where several pathologies are present.

K-means clustering is an unsupervised technique that assigns hard cluster numbers to data points by iteratively deciding the best value of cluster centers. The Euclidean distance metric is used to evaluate data points with identical properties after which they are grouped into clusters. The [128] researchers have introduced a k-means clustering concept on the features extracted from the voice samples in the MEEI data collection. Features are measured using non-negative matrix factoring and adaptive time–frequency distribution. Pathological voice is non-uniform or non-stationary in nature and thus affects its distribution over time. Consequently, the crucial problem seen with such a short-length signal extraction technique is the loss of information that is more likely to occur when signals are chopped off.
