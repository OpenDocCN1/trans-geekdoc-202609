Although most of the papers were medical papers, it is expected that such a retraction is extended to other multidisciplinary studies such as COVID-19 medical data mining. We provided an example for the explanation. Different deep learning algorithms were compared by Alakus and Turkoglu [62] for COVID-19 diagnosis. 80 COVID and 520 non-COVID patients were examined, and the laboratory data were recorded. Using 10-fold cross-validation, the average AUC, F1-score, accuracy, precision, and sensitivity were reported as 0.625, 91.89%, 86.66%, 86.75%, 99.42% (figure 5.9).

First, CI 95% was not reported for any performance index. It is critical for indices such as AUC (0.625 in this study). If the CI 95% includes 0.5, the user does not benefit from the diagnosis methods more than a random generator in practice. Moreover, it is necessary to report MEAN and SD of the performance indices over test folds to identify each fold’s reliability. The SD values were not provided.

Moreover, the specificity of the proposed method was not provided by the authors. However, using the parameters AUC and sensitivity, the specificity of the method is approximately 26%. It is directly related to the type I error and must be reported in the paper. There is also a contradiction in one of the paper figures (figure 5.10), where the AUC of the LSTM was mentioned as 0.83. However, the ROC tells a different story. Such a number was used in other calculations, resulting in some inconsistencies in the paper. Moreover, it is not clear how the accuracy of the method is high (86.66%), knowing that most of the subjects were healthy and the specificity of the system was very low (26%). Such issues must have been identified and corrected during the peer-review procedure.

## 5.3 Conclusion and future scope

Undoubtedly, the ML methods are valuable tools for medical data mining. However, there are some critical issues to consider to improve their visibility and clinical applications. Tables 5.1–5.4 summarized the critical STARD/TRIPOD issues that were not considered in the published papers. One reason could be that the original STARD or TRIPOD guidelines were not developed for ML and AI methods. This is why their extensions, such as TRIPOD-ML [63] and STARD-AI [64], are underway. Nevertheless, it is possible to use the critical STARD/TRIPOD checklist to improve ML methods’ clinical application.

During the pandemics, many papers were published in the literature focusing on all aspects of COVID-19 diagnosis, prognosis, and long COVID. Many journals provided special issues, although some were not related to the journals’ aim and scope. Inadequate peer-review procedures and the journals’ rush to maintain or improve their impact factor while some clinical journals were the front runners created a situation where publishing was the main issue. Valuable research requires time, and there was no time in the race. This time, the term ‘publish or perish’ was completely clear. It might be a good idea to have the peer review after the publication of COVID-19 papers, where researchers could comment or discuss the published papers. Pubs provided an open community review (https://pubns.com/publon/covid-19/?sort_by=date). However, not all of the published COVID-19 papers were included.

Here, we provide a list of critical issues to consider for ML methods for COVID-19 diagnosis and prognosis to improve their clinical application:

- The flow diagram of the study in which the inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown must be provided.
- The sample size calculation and the sampling method must be mentioned.
- Type III error must be avoided. Using only the hold-out validation method could create this problem. Alternative solutions such as repeated hold-out or re-sampling methods are good alternatives.
- When there are repeated measurements from each patient, subject cross-validation must also be provided.
- The mean and SD of the performance indices must be reported over each test fold. A cross-validated confusion matrix could be provided, enabling the statistical comparison between different methods (i.e., the proposed method versus the state-of-the-art).
- The analyzed sample must resemble the population in terms of disease prevalence. Otherwise, the method’s performance over balanced datasets must be analyzed and elaborated when using in the population.
- Proper performance indices must be provided when the dataset is imbalanced. Accuracy is a biased estimator in this case.
- A variety of performance indices must be reported. An example is providing the agreement rate between the diagnosis system’s output with that of the PCR results, as there are criticisms on using PCR results as the gold standard.
- The CI 95% of the performance indices must be reported, showing the reliability of the estimation.
- Biostatistical knowledge of the biomedical engineers and those working on ML methods in medicine must be improved. An example is the importance of selecting the correct survival models and ML methods when the in-hospital prognosis is performed.
