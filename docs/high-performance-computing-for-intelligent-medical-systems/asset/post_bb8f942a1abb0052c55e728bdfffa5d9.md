## 3.2 Electroglottography

This technology is low-cost, non-invasively measuring changes in the relative vocal fold area during the production of laryngeal voices. EGG was introduced 30 years ago [42, 43] in the late 1980s and early 1990s [44–49] and has passed through a golden age of growing scientific interest. A high frequency, low ampere current is transmitted between two electrodes located on either side of the thyroid cartilage. Vocal fold vibration transmits changes to the vocal fold contact analysis (VFCA), allowing the EGG signal to vary by VFCA. The lack of spatial information during the calculation of EGG is a crucial weakness. Vocal fold vibration is a complex three-dimensional phenomenon that is mapped to a time-variable value, that is, VFCA. Differences in vibrational features, such as inferior-superior or front-posterior [50], are difficult to resolve. The concept of a multi-channel EGG estimate was demonstrated by [51]. Three years later, two channels using different modulation frequencies were used to capture both horizontal and vertical signals [52]. The concept of a multi-channel EGG was also explored in [53] by setting up a system to decode 36 multiplexing current paths with two 2×3 electrode grids. The ability of the device to monitor vertical and horizontal larynx positions during singing and swallowing was demonstrated by limited pilot data obtained using the prototype of this design. In a different method, the developers of [54] replaced multiplexing time division with frequency division multiplexing. By proposing an electrical impedance tomography (EIT) procedure, Liu et al [55] have broadened the concept of collecting spatial information from a non-invasive physiological voice signal outside the EGG. EIT technology appears to be promising in future voice research and diagnostics, although daunting in mathematical and engineering terms.

Two different methods have been used to establish the physiological meaning of the EGG waveform and its landmarks, using experimental evidence from controlled experiments and using analytical techniques. For example, with photoglottography (PGG) [56], videostroboscopy [57], in vivo air circulation in glottis [58], some initial empirical multiple-signal validations have been carried out. A number of outstanding contributions in the ‘golden era’ provided a sufficient understanding of the landmarks of the EGG waveform and its connection with the vocal fold vibration [45, 47, 49, 59–63]. These investigations used some form of time-synchronous laryngeal imagery, using a fast video imaging technique with frame rates of up to 5000 fps. The authors of [64] who used the first EGG signal derivative have further verified their findings.

The most prominent characteristics of the EGG waveform are hypothesized moments of interaction or decontact of the vocal fold. The ‘knee’ in the EGG waveform represents another signature [59]. It is a convex dislocation on the side of the waveform [45]. This EGG feature found during the decontact phase of the glottal cycle corresponds to the opening along the top edge of the glottis. [45, 65] discussed the relationship between the presence or absence of an EGG ‘knee’ and the spectral acoustic waveform characteristics and [66] suggested an intelligent algorithm for detecting ‘knee’ and other EGG waveform inflexions.

**EGG (de) contacting versus glottal opening and closure.** EGG is often not used and interpreted in itself as a VFCA measurements process, but instead as a non-invasive approximation quantification that theoretically emulates glottal airflow of high-speed video (HSV) recordings. The explanation would be that the reliability of relationship between the laryngeal air flow varies from time to time since it is dictated by the dynamics of the vocal fold’s vibration, and the sound quality generated by the larynges. The localized pressure gradient that occurs through a mostly abrupt ceaseflow of the laryngeal flux, at the glottal closure incidence, constitutes a significant proportion of that tone. The timing of the occurrence of glottal closures—commonly known in the speech processing domain as ‘glottal closure instants’ (GCIs)—is a significant component towards voice quality, and a corresponding physiological measure would therefore be beneficial. To a minor extent, the same reasoning applies to glottal openings (GOIs) [67–69]. Findings from early work indicate that positive dEGG peaks are well-related to that of the GCI [60, 70], while the authors have reported the GOI, which would have been thought to be more variable might occur at various points throughout the background of the glottis [70]. Therefore, it may be difficult to identify GOIs by using the EGG signal’s feature alone. But it may also be inaccurate if GCI is in precise synchronization with the positive dEGG peak. [71] records an error rate of 0.24 ms in the GCIs of ten speakers that has been deduced using inverse-filtered airflow and dEGG positive peaks, respectively. The deviation was 0.68 ms, which would equate to approximately 8.2% of the glottal period in males and approximately 13.6% in females.
