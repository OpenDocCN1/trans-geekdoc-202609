## 12.1.2 Biomedical image segmentation

Biomedical image segmentation (BIS) has been a comprehensive application since the 1990s. Segmentation is a process of partitioning an image picture into sub-images having similar grey scale intensities. The other elements can be colour, texture and brightness. The segmentation is used to subdivide the objects into detailed pictures used for depth analysis. Some of its crucial tasks are as follows: firstly, investigating the internal structure of human anatomy in detail; secondly, recognizing the precise location of abnormalities such as tumour or cancerous cell growth; thirdly, it helps in identification of multiple numbers of lesions, exudates, and other anomalies; and finally, it measures tissue volume to measure tumour expansion.

BIS approaches can be divided into three distinct classes depending on the extent of human involvement: manual segmentation, semi-automated segmentation, and fully automatic BIS [11, 12]. The following categories are described:

- A. Manual segmentation of the biomedical image. Here, the region of interest (ROI) is specified by a well-qualified expert after which a contour is drawn around it [11, 13]. Prolonged and challenging work is manual segmentation. This process of manual segmentation is again composed of two different types, which are known as ‘inter-rater’, and intra-rater. In the case of the former, multiple experts segment the image in various ways, whereas in the latter, a single expert segments an image in a variety of ways [14]. Manual segmented images are utilized as ground truths in automated segmentation algorithms for teaching artificial intelligence (AI) on how a disease should look. High-resolution images can lead to images without sharp edges, which leads to small differences in the choice of ROI boundary pixels. The fact that the approach relies on knowledge and experience is another issue of manual segmentation and thus, therefore, experiences considerable heterogeneity between and within the experts [15]. Although manual segmentation is dependent upon the evaluator, it is used in clinical studies with non-significant time limitations [16, 17].

- B. Semi-automatic segmentation. Semi-automatic segmentation strategies demand a restricted number of user encounters with automated algorithms in order to achieve precise segmentation outcomes [18]. The user interaction may include a set of estimated initial ROI, which is ultimately used to segment the whole picture. To reduce segmentation errors, manual testing and area boundary editing may be needed. The following are examples of semiautomatic segmentation strategies:
  - (1) The Seeded Region Growth algorithm, which is based on a user's original seed point iteratively merges identical neighbourhood pixels [19].
  - (2) A level-set active contour model that starts from an original boundary shapes with a specified ROI and updates them iteratively by shrinking or expansion process is not needed [20].
  - (3) Localised region active contour methods that use region boundary conditions to use tiny local sections to define the image's foreground and background and have the benefit of managing heterogeneous textures [21].

- C. Fully automatic segmentation. There is no interference from the customer. However, many of these systems concentrate on supervised methods of learning and training data, such as atlas segmentation methods, support vector machines (SVMs) and deep neural networks (DNNs). In unsupervised learning both training samples and the sample validation require ground truth images. With a completely intelligent segmentation in biomedical images, significant variations in form, size, texture and in some cases ROI colour between patients and low contrast between section are obvious challenges [22].

Noise may also lead to substantial deviations during data capture, as is often the case with modern devices. As a result, most emerging methods based on clustering and machine learning are confronted with the lack of global deployment that restricts their use to a limited range of applications. In addition, the feature extraction techniques used by SVM or neural networks takes a while and does not process natural data in its raw state and may not necessarily adapt to changing data. However, deep learning approaches can examine data without extracting features manually [23]. For semantic segmentation they are most favoured for BIS [24].

## 12.1.3 Segmentation evaluation

### A. Accuracy

Manual accuracy represents the percentage of correct biomedical image pixels as described in equation (12.1). It is also recognised as a complete pixel precision. This is the most fundamental assessment method, but in the case of class imbalance it restricts the error of representation of biomedical image segmentation results. The other class inequality arises when one segmentation category dominates. In such a situation, a higher accuracy will overwhelm the lower accuracy of the other class and thus a skewed result for the dominant class. Per class accuracy analysing and then estimating the proportion of pixels correctly labelled for each group is an alternative to this concept of accuracy [25]. This test can be used in the case of a class imbalance for sample data set. A decline in the confidence measurement of specific classes is the downside of average class precision. Fewer cases of a given class can lead to a substantial variance in the reliability of the results in comparative analyses.

```
Accuracy = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + FN + TN} (12.1)
```

where TP stands for true positives, FN for false negatives, TN is the number of true negatives, and FP is the number of false positives.

### B. Precision

As specified in equation (12.2), precision is reflecting the relation of disease pixels in automatic segmentation results measures that corresponds to the pixels of the ground reality disease. Precision is a valuable measure of performance segmentation as it reacts to over-segmentation. The over-segmentation leads to low accuracy values.

```
Precision = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} (12.2)
```

### C. Sensitivity

This value reflects the true positive rates reported in test data. It indicates the number of times the test provided a positive outcome when the sample did indeed have a condition [26]. Defined in equation (12.3)

```
Sensitivity = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} (12.3)
```

### D. Specificity

This specificity value indicates the true negative rates reported by the test data. It refers to the proportion of negative samples identified by the test that are actually negative [26]. The specificity is described in equation (12.4):

```
Specificity = \frac{TN}{TN + FP} (12.4)
```

### E. F1 measure

The measure F1 specifically states the harmonic mean of precision and recall and is suitable for contour matching the predicted segmentation with ground truth segmentation.

```
F_1 = \frac{2TP}{2TP + FP + FN} (12.5)
```

### F. Hausdorff distance (h)

This value is a measurement of distance among two test samples set. It is a set's maximal distance from the closest point in another set [27]. Hausdorff distance is shown in equation (12.6).

```
h(X, Y) = max_{x \in X} min_{y \in Y} ||x - y||   (12.6)
```

where X = {x1, x2, x3, ..., xn} and Y = {y1, y2, y3, ..., yn} are sample sets.

## 12.2 Retinal images segmentation

### 12.2.1 Datasets

Several publicly accessible professional data sets containing blood vessel descriptions are available. The first step in the retinal database is to train and assess classifiers for segmenting blood vessels. Researchers have access to these databases—STARE and DRIVE etc, along with their ground truth files, which can be obtained freely. The performance and exactness of the classifier can be evaluated with the following data sets.

### A. Structured analysis of the retina (STARE)

It contains 400 retinal images and possesses a 605 x 700 pixel 8 bit Color channel via TOPCON's TRV-50 fundus camera with 35-degree field of view (FOV). The average diameter of the FOV is 650 x 700. STARE consists of twenty samples of vessel ground truths files which can be used for training classifiers to segment blood vessel to demonstrate the presence of healthy and distinct types of ocular diseases. The very first specialist separated 10.4% of the blood vessel's pixels, whereas the second expert manually segmented the thinner retina vessel 14.9%. The ground truth obtained from these manual segmentations of the first professional is commonly used for evaluating a trained classifier's precision and efficiency

### B. Digital retinal images for vessel extraction (DRIVE)

This dataset is a very widely used for studying segmentation of retinal blood vessels. It is made from 20 photographs of the retinas, each of which reveals mild retinopathy, with 33 healthy retinal fundus images and 7 images suffering from moderate diabetic retinopathy (DR). The Canon CR5 camera with 45 degrees FOV and then a color channel of 8 bit in JPEG format was used for taking photographs at 768 x 584 pixels. The circular field of view of each images has radius of 270 pixels. The DRIVE dataset was divided into 20 pictures each in a training and test package. In the training sets, 14 of 20 sample images were segmented by first specialists, while the second specialists segmented the six remaining sample images. The vessel was segmented twice in the test set for evaluation. During the first circumstance 13 and 7 sample pictures were segmented by first and second specialists, while the second scenario consisted of a segmentation procedure by a third specialist. 12.7% and 12.3% pixels were identified in both the circumstances.
