# ## 7.2 Focus on diagnostics

# ### 7.2.1 Diagnostic error as a concept

Diagnostic error is defined as ‘the failure to establish an accurate and timely explanation of the patient’s health problem or communicate that explanation to the patient’ [2]. Errors arise ‘as a result of actions not taken or as a result of the wrong action taken’ [3]. Active errors are made by people, between clinicians, and an object in question [2, 5]. Latent errors result from problems in system or process design and may easily go unnoticed. System-related errors may be associated with communication, care coordination, inefficient processes, or technical failures [2, 5]. In daily practice, diagnostic errors are often unrecognized and under-reported [6, 7]. Health professionals’ indifference to report mistakes may be due to fear of penalty or sanction [5, 8]. Individual and systematic factors are some of the most common reasons for the under-reporting of diagnostic errors [7].

# ### 7.2.2 Diagnostic errors in healthcare

Medical errors are a critical problem in healthcare. According to statistics, medical errors are assumed to be the leading cause of death in the United States [5]. These errors include e.g., surgical, diagnostic, medication, devices, equipment, system failures, and other problems with healthcare technology. Higher error rates usually occur in stressful environments such as emergency departments, intensive care units, and operating rooms but high error rates are seen also in primary healthcare [9, 10]. Medical errors are often associated with extreme age, high acuity, and new procedures [5, 9, 11]. Communication errors are a common source of medical error [5]. Quality of care is lower among female patients and women report more harm than male patients [9, 12]. Errors related to medication occur in 3–10% of all consultations when drugs are prescribed [9, 13]. Common factors contributing to medication errors include communication, transfer of care, and continuity of care [9].

Diagnostic errors contribute as much 70% of medical errors [14]. Vascular disease, infections, and cancer account for about 75% of diagnostic errors [15]. In a Scandinavian retrospective cohort study [16] in a sample of a total of 4830 cases of preventable harm, 46% were due to diagnostic errors.
