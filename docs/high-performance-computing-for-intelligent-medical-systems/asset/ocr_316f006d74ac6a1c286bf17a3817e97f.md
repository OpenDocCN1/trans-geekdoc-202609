Two different methods have been used to establish the physiological meaning of the EEG waveform and its landmarks, using experimental evidence from controlled experiments and using analytical techniques. For example, with photoglottography (PGG) [56], videostroboscopy [57], in vivo air circulation in glottis [58], some initial empirical multiple-signal validations have been carried out. A number of outstanding contributions in the ‘golden era’ provided a sufficient understanding of the landmarks of the EEG waveform and its connection with the vocal fold vibration [45, 47, 49, 59–63]. These investigations used some form of time-synchronous laryngeal imagery, using a fast video imaging technique with frame rates of up to 5000 fps. The authors of [64] who used the first EEG signal derivative have further verified their findings.

The most prominent characteristics of the EGG waveform are hypothesized moments of interaction or decontact of the vocal fold. The ‘knee’ in the EGG waveform represents another signature [59]. It is a convex dislocation on the side of the waveform [45]. This EGG feature found during the decontact phase of the glottal cycle corresponds to the opening along the top edge of the glottis. [45, 65] discussed the relationship between the presence or absence of an EGG ‘knee’ and the spectral acoustic waveform characteristics and [66] suggested an intelligent algorithm for detecting ‘knee’ and other EGG waveform inflexions.

**EGG (de) contacting versus glottal opening and closure.** EGG is often not used and interpreted in itself as a VFCA measurements process, but instead as a non-invasive approximation quantification that theoretically emulates glottal airflow of high-speed video (HSV) recordings. The explanation would be that the reliability of relationship between the laryngeal air flow varies from time to time since it is dictated by the dynamics of the vocal fold’s vibration, and the sound quality generated by the larynges. The localized pressure gradient that occurs through a mostly abrupt ceaseflow of the laryngeal flux, at the glottal closure incidence, constitutes a significant proportion of that tone. The timing of the occurrence of glottal closures—commonly known in the speech processing domain as ‘glottal closure instants’ (GCIs)—is a significant component towards voice quality, and a corresponding physiological measure would therefore be beneficial. To a minor extent, the same reasoning applies to glottal openings (GOIs) [67–69]. Findings from early work indicate that positive dEGG peaks are well-related to that of the GCI [60, 70], while the authors have reported the GOI, which would have been thought to be more variable might occur at various points throughout the background of the glottis [70]. Therefore, it may be difficult to identify GOIs by using the EGG signal’s feature alone. But it may also be inaccurate if GCI is in precise synchronization with the positive dEGG peak. [71] records an error rate of 0.24 ms in the GCIs of ten speakers that has been deduced using inverse-filtered airflow and dEGG positive peaks, respectively. The deviation was 0.68 ms, which would equate to approximately 8.2% of the glottal period in males and approximately 13.6% in females.
