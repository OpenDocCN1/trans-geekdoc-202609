## Chapter 7

## A model for advanced patient feedback procedures in diagnostics

Marjo Rissanen, Antti Rissanen and Kalle Saastamoinen

In healthcare, patients’ experience is monitored in many ways. However, accuracy of diagnostics is a common concern of healthcare clients. This chapter proposes a model for improved patient feedback in diagnostics. The proposed model is beneficial for organizations because it offers more precise information on patient opinions. Moreover, it helps healthcare consumers formulate and express their opinions quickly and easily about perceived diagnostic phase in their care. This approach combines clients’ free-text feedback and structured responses in areas of physician competence and compassion in diagnostics. Breakthroughs in biomedical information processing like advanced data, text mining techniques, and machine learning algorithms are at the forefront of success in controlling information overload and text clustering problems. These aids are beneficial in categorizing free-text feedback and binary answers, as well in analysis and in visualization. The proposed model represents intelligent medical management and such computational intelligence which can benefit patients, doctors, and health organizations. The advantage of the model is its economics for patients but also for quality auditors, and data management sites.

### 7.1 Introduction

Diagnostic errors result in higher healthcare costs, unnecessary deaths, and mental suffering [1–3]. Diagnostic errors lead easily to increased numbers of doctor visits, longer hospital stays, or to inappropriate treatments [1, 4]. Evaluative information gained from patient feedback plays an essential role in responding to patient concerns and making necessary changes to diagnostic practices. To improve patient safety related to diagnostics, feedback systems focused on diagnostic evaluation should be clear and efficient. Therefore, it would be desirable to distinguish diagnostics connected patient feedback from other general patient feedback into its separate feedback systems to make progress quickly. However, providing specific feedback can be challenging for the patient when it comes to diagnostic practices. Some areas in the diagnostic phase are easier to handle in feedback procedure by clients if the stages and content of the process are presented in simple, easy-to-read formats. Thus to a certain degree structured phases in addition to open text formats are recommendable in diagnostics focused consumer targeted feedback systems. Nowadays also the processing of narrative feedback phases is smoother. Improved artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms and intelligent big data analytics offer more efficient tools to refine open format based material for further analysis.

### 7.2 Focus on diagnostics

### 7.2.1 Diagnostic error as a concept

Diagnostic error is defined as ‘the failure to establish an accurate and timely explanation of the patient’s health problem or communicate that explanation to the patient’ [2]. Errors arise ‘as a result of actions not taken or as a result of the wrong action taken’ [3]. Active errors are made by people, between clinicians, and an object in question [2, 5]. Latent errors result from problems in system or process design and may easily go unnoticed. System-related errors may be associated with communication, care coordination, inefficient processes, or technical failures [2, 5]. In daily practice, diagnostic errors are often unrecognized and under-reported [6, 7]. Health professionals’ indifference to report mistakes may be due to fear of penalty or sanction [5, 8]. Individual and systematic factors are some of the most common reasons for the under-reporting of diagnostic errors [7].

### 7.2.2 Diagnostic errors in healthcare

Medical errors are a critical problem in healthcare. According to statistics, medical errors are assumed to be the leading cause of death in the United States [5]. These errors include e.g., surgical, diagnostic, medication, devices, equipment, system failures, and other problems with healthcare technology. Higher error rates usually occur in stressful environments such as emergency departments, intensive care units, and operating rooms but high error rates are seen also in primary healthcare [9, 10]. Medical errors are often associated with extreme age, high acuity, and new procedures [5, 9, 11]. Communication errors are a common source of medical error [5]. Quality of care is lower among female patients and women report more harm than male patients [9, 12]. Errors related to medication occur in 3–10% of all consultations when drugs are prescribed [9, 13]. Common factors contributing to medication errors include communication, transfer of care, and continuity of care [9].

Diagnostic errors contribute as much 70% of medical errors [14]. Vascular disease, infections, and cancer account for about 75% of diagnostic errors [15]. In a Scandinavian retrospective cohort study [16] in a sample of a total of 4830 cases of preventable harm, 46% were due to diagnostic errors.
