## 5.2.5 Machine learning methods

There are usually two COVID-19 data mining research questions answered in the literature, including COVID-19 diagnosis and prognosis. Some examples are provided, and the critical issues are then discussed.

Cai et al [35] investigated the application of CT scans for the prediction of clinical outcomes and assessment of disease severity. 99 COVID-19 patients underwent physical examination, chest CT, routine blood test, and arterial blood gas tests on admission (figure 5.1). The clinical data, such as the treatment, outcomes, and disease severity, were collected. Random forest (RF) models were used to investigate CT quantification’s validity (moderate, severe, and critical). The length of ICU stays, oxygen inhalation, and hospitalization was also assessed. Prediction of ICU partial versus prolonged recovery showed an AUC of 0.960.

The allocation of resources and focusing on the attention on high-risk patients is a struggling task for physicians. Implementing machine learning algorithms could support physicians in predicting high-risk individuals. Subudhi et al [41] developed prognostic ML methods based on electronic health records (figure 5.2). The developed model was validated by the hold-out method (20%). The accuracy of the developed model was 90%. The most important features included lymphocyte, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH).

Zoabi et al [59] used a gradient-boosting machine model built with a decision-tree to predict COVID-19. The training set included 51,831 subjects (9% positive COVID-19), while the test set was 47,401 subjects (9% positive COVID-19) from the subsequent weeks. The dichotomous input features were age ≥60 years, gender, contact with an infected subject, cough, fever, sore throat, shortness of breath, and headache. The feature importance was provided in figure 5.3. The proposed model's performance on the prospective test set was assessed as follows: AUC = 0.90 [CI 95%: 0.892–0.905], sensitivity = 87.30%, and specificity = 71.98%. It is one example in which various performance indices were reported, and external validation was used. Moreover, the dataset was not balanced. Thus, the performance of the developed system could be extended to the population. This critical issue is discussed later in this chapter.

Gao et al [48] used an attention-based dual branch combination network on CT images for COVID-19 segmentation and classification. In total, 1918 CT scans from 1202 participants (210,395 slices: 498 controls versus 704 patients) were obtained in ten hospitals. The data were split into an internal training set (6,130 slices: 75 controls versus 48 patients) from one hospital and an external validation set (204,265 slices: 423 controls versus 656 patients) from nine other hospitals. For the internal dataset, 5-fold cross-validation was used. It achieved an AUC average of 98% and 97% on individual-level and slice-level, respectively. The external dataset was only for individual-level, and the AUC of 97% was reported (figure 5.4). They used only the internal dataset for the segmentation task, with 5-fold cross-validation, and achieved Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 99% and 83% for lung and lesion detection. No CI and standard deviation over the test folds were reported.

An algorithm for forecasting intubation over the test folds were reported. An algorithm for forecasting intubation of patients diagnosed with COVID-19 was developed by Arvind et al [49]. They collected data from 4087 patients from 5 hospitals, wherein 11% of patients were intubated. They used the following features for training a time-series prediction based on Random Forest classifier: Systolic BP, diastolic BP, time from admission, temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, HCO3, pH, arterial CO2, oxygen saturation, WBC count, arterial O2, platelet count, C-reactive protein, creatine, and D-dimer. Using a hold-out (70% train and 30% test) validation, they achieved an AUC of 0.84 for predicting intubation presence in 72 h. They followed the TRIPOD standard and applied statistical analysis to the results. However, the CI was not reported for the performance indices.

Nayak et al [50] used transfer learning for COVID-19 x-ray image classification. Different models such as Inception-V3, MobileNet-V2, AlexNet, GoogleNet, VGG-16, ResNet-50, SqueezeNet, and ResNet-34 were evaluated on the publicly available dataset, and an AUC of 98% was reported for ResNet-34 as the best model using hold-out (70% train and 30% test) validation strategy (figure 5.5). They also did not report the CI or run the proposed method with shuffling data.

Chao et al [51] extracted the first, second, and higher-order features from CT images and combined them with non-imaging features, such as age, gender, temperature, white blood cell counts (WBC), SpO2, lymphocyte count, and lymphocyte count-to-WBC ratio, for predicting COVID-19 (figure 5.6). The evaluation was performed using 5-fold cross-validation, and an AUC of 88% was achieved using random forest. Random forest outperformed the SVM and deep neural networks. The CI was also provided for the performance indices (figure 5.7).
