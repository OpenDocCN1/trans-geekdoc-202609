### 7.8.5 Meaning and management of the free format phase—phase 2

AI provides more efficient possibilities for processing free text. Free text feedback is especially useful in complex cases where, for example, the patient feels that his or her own diagnosis is prolonged. This may be due to poorly coordinated co-operation between different sites. Poor coordination causes situations that may threaten patient safety and increase healthcare costs [85, 86]. As speech recognition and processing systems have also evolved, it makes sense that the patient can also give free-form feedback on their phone interactively with the help of a robot as flexibly as possible.

Unstructured data can be challenging to organize and analyze. This kind of data is typically in text, such as open-ended text responses. In the case of hospitals, the volume of free-text data can be massive. Analyzing handwritten responses or large volumes of text data is time-consuming, expensive, and subject to human error [87, 88]. Big data is defined as ‘a collection of a very large amount of data with a wide range of types, making it hard to process using conventional database management systems’ [88]. This is why AI-based text analysis and automated knowledge representation and reasoning capabilities can be extremely helpful [87].

Customer feedback may be in the form of PDF documents, photos, or other images, scanned documents, or printed or handwritten documents. Optical Character Recognition (OCR) is the best tool for converting text to digital format in all of these cases (figure 7.4). Most OCR tools provide 98% to 99% accuracy [89]. This level of accuracy would be sufficient in most cases; however, accuracy may suffer in handwritten documents, blurry pictures, and languages other than English.

Handwriting Recognition (HWR), also known as Handwritten Text Recognition (HTR), is an active research area that can deliver a 4.0% Word Error Rate (WER) and 1.6% Character Error Rate (CER) when using deep learning techniques, like bidirectional, long short-term memory networks (BLSTMs) [90].

Another option is feedback in the form of audio. Tools are available to transform spoken audio into digital text. In many European hospitals, doctors' assessments are recorded and converted to text. Digital scribes (speech-processing and natural language processing (NLP) modules) can also convert the audio to text, and to extract salient information from the text [49]. There are many speech-to-text platforms available. They are standard tools, similar to OCR or translating tools. Most of the applications have sufficient accuracy to be usable.

Once open-ended responses are gathered (free-text phase) in digitized text, the material is ready for text analysis. Text analyzing techniques use machine learning algorithms to find quantitative or qualitative data in the text. In free-text form, data is ordinarily unstructured. ### 7.8.6 Clients’ opinions of the feedback delivery system—phase 3

It is noticeable that health consumers can give responses and ideas of usability aspects of feedback systems. User acceptance issues deal typically with flexibility of feedback delivery systems. It is important that feedback delivery is fast enough, the system and its guidelines and outlook are understandable enough, and system use is easy enough for all kinds of users. Aspects of patient privacy are such issues that require special attention because patients are not eager to use applications if they have doubts and questions in this area. Feedback gives material in evolving the system better ‘fit for use’.

## 7.9 Client feedback as a translational development challenge

### 7.9.1 Enhancing process synergy in organizations

Using an accurate but reasonably simple application, the patient can quickly and simply provide clear and informative feedback on the diagnostic process they experience. Moreover, the open format (open format-narrative phase) allows for individual feedback. It is essential that the patient accurately identifies those aspects they perceived to be deficient in the diagnostic process for their doctor’s particular area of practice. The model provides the physician with a better understanding of the patient’s experience than is typically possible during a limited consultancy session. The physician also gains an understanding of the patient’s view on how the diagnostic process is progressing.

At the organizational level, it is possible to obtain information on the issues that present the most significant challenges in the patient’s view. This assists human resource training leaders to focus on issues that are relevant to quality assurance. Up-to-date information on a specific physician helps to target individual counseling assistance. It is also essential to get feedback from system users about the usability of the system. This information is useful for the further maturation procedures of diagnostic-focused feedback systems.

Perceiving entities is essential in systems thinking and in translational design approach. Proper functioning depends on how successfully the parts of the process work, integrate, and form a seamless whole [94]. The overall process should be sufficiently elastic, even in exceptional situations and periods of change. The diagnostic process must be seen as a whole, requiring multi-faceted communication and collaboration to face current challenges. Efficient patient feedback in this area can improve the synergy of the process. The feedback delivery process must be accessible to all customer groups. Accordingly, particular attention should be paid to the needs of older patients when designing user interfaces.

### 7.9.2 Maturing and validating patient-targeted feedback systems

Well-functioning patient feedback systems in diagnostics are an essential tool for healthcare organizations to improve their image quality. Easy-to-use feedback systems can assure patients that their voice is heard at the most critical stage of the healthcare process. Healthcare organizations can thus increase their image quality value in a remarkable way through intensive and flexible feedback possibilities.

In the area of product quality, patient-targeted feedback systems should focus particularly on privacy, ease of use, and credibility. Customers are only interested in giving feedback if they believe it will be helpful. To ensure customer quality, feedback on the functionality of the diagnostic process is essential. Because doctors are also users of the feedback system, they should also be consulted on how well a feedback system serves them in assessing the diagnostic process's quality.

Diagnostics-focused feedback systems should provide hospital administrators with information on the current state of patient satisfaction about diagnostics and how feedback systems can be improved. This information will provide additional input and material for physician training to enhance the diagnostic process. Patient targeted diagnostics focused feedback system aims to improve the diagnostic process's safety and reliability and thus improve overall process quality.

Patient-targeted feedback systems need their maturation stages to reach acceptable levels of innovation quality. AI brings new opportunities to the field of product design. Systems must evolve through the feedback they receive about their usefulness to ensure that all patients can benefit from the feedback system. System development should not cease at the first well-functioning state in the field of application design.

## 7.10 Conclusion

The correctness of diagnostics in healthcare is a key area of concern for patients. To ensure patient legal protection, it is important to develop feedback systems that are well-suited to providing feedback on the accuracy of diagnostics. Feedback delivery should be easy for the client and provide care planners and caregivers with sufficiently informative information about the patient's perspective. Narrative feedback defends its place because it gives the patient a free hand to give feedback. The transformation of free-form text into informative and usable data has evolved in part thanks to artificial intelligence applications. However, giving feedback on the diagnostic portion is not easy for the patient in all cases. For this reason, the chapter also presents a format model representing structured feedback that promotes the patient's ability to provide useful and sufficiently informative feedback. Because patients' willingness to use the time required for feedback naturally varies, it is important to consider different levels of time use when developing feedback systems
