## 11.3 Retinal diseases

The various existing diseases related to the retina are:

- 1. Detachment of retina/tear problem;
- 2. Retinopathy caused by diabetes;
- 3. Hole in the macula;
- 4. Degeneration of macula;
- 5. Disorders of optic nerve;
- 6. Glaucoma;
- 7. Diabetic macular edema (DME);
- 8. Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP).

### 11.3.1 Retinal tear

In retinal detachment, there is separation of retina from the pigment epithelium. This may be due to an injury or tumour or inflammation to the eye or inflammation of the underlying choroid. The characteristic feature is the development of a hole as well as a tear. The tear is formed due to the forces applied on attachments between the retina and the vitreous humour that fills the interior of the eye. Vitreous pulling of the retina can be due to injury or changes due to age factor or sudden movement of the eye which further causes fluid to enter the small hole and causing striping. Retinal detachment is common to myopic (near-sighted) eyes. The pull of the vitreous on part of the retina creates a retinal tear. There is observation of brief light flashes observed by the affected person during eye ball rotation. The perception of coming shadow in the front of the eye indicates the beginning of a tear in the retina.

### 11.3.2 Diabetic retinopathy

Diabetic retinopathy is an eye illness that results due to the metabolic disorder of diabetes. In this alarming disorder a high level of glucose damages the retina of the eye. There is cessation of blood through the blood capillaries, hence abnormal dilation is possible. There is additional development of blood capillaries on the retina. This all leads to the development of blurring then blindness. Swelling of the capillaries can result in the creation of edema of the macula. The disease progression signs include two-fold vision, eye ache, macular hole, floaters.

### 11.3.3 Macula hole

One important part, the macula, plays a vital role in the various activities related to vision such as book reading, vehicle driving and perception of things, thus causing promotion of fine proper vision. The drawing of the eye fluid vitreous humour is responsible for the creation of a gap in the macula. The gap is responsible for the softening of the macula. Extreme short sightedness, advancement of epiretinal membranes, separation of the retina and diabetic retinopathy all contribute to the creation of macular gap.

### 11.3.4 Degeneration of the macula

The degeneration of the macula can be inherited or can be from age related factors. It is an uncommon class of retinal degenerative diseases collectively called retinitis, pigmentosa of retina and similar disorders. The preliminary sign is night blindness, observed in childhood because of a change in the function of the rods. The thinning of vessels and the dispersion of pigment clumps progresses to 'tunnel vision'. Retinitis pigmentosa is due to malfunctioning in genes coding of amino acids sequences engaged in light sensitivity. Age-related macular degeneration (ARMD) is possible in patients over fifty and affects central vision and spares peripheral vision. This is a major contributor of vision loss. Age-related macular degeneration disease is categorised as

- (a) Dry type macular degeneration

  There is development of colour filler, that is, drusen slightly yellow; the other incorporation is epithelium percentage, all collectively leading to the dry type of degeneration.

- (b) Wet macular degeneration (containing abnormal blood vessels)

  In this, there is bleeding, and detachment of vessels filled with blood in the gap that exists between two anatomical features of sensory retina part and pigment epithelium part. This is noticed in someone suffering from a high blood glucose problem and high blood pressure. Degeneration of the macula affects central visual acuity, however, it does not cause total blindness but makes reading or fine detail work difficult or impossible.

### 11.3.5 Disorders of the optic nerve

The optic nerve is responsible for the signal transmission from the eye to brain and from brain to eye, therefore, proper nerve transmission is important for the good health of the eye. Due to the development of the various pressures inside the skull the eye may face problems which in due course of time tend to the development of various eye diseases. When this pressure remains persistent for continuous time it inflames the nerve and hence swelling can occur. This starts to damage the fibres of the optic nerve resulting in various disorders of the optic nerve like low vision. Young adults are the main sufferers. Optic neuritis may be a manifestation of multiple sclerosis where the sheaths of the optic nerves have changed and interfere with the transmission of nervous impulses.

### 11.3.6 Glaucoma

This is caused when drainage of the aqueous humour is blocked due to blockage of specialized drainage channels at the base of the iris. This intraocular pressure may cause atrophy of retinal nerve fibres and causes blurred vision, so enhanced strain on human eye pressure is a risk contributor for the development of glaucoma, which is combination of problems that result in the development of nerve damage. Glaucoma is a strain sensitive disease depending mainly on the optimum strain of the eye. In normal conditions the human eye can withstand strain in the range of ten to twenty mm of Hg. So, the calculation of the strain is considered an appropriate marker for the detection of this disease, however, there are many clinical cases reported where there is glaucoma in spite of normal strain values. Two main categories of this disease are open angle type, common in older patients and angle closure glaucoma, caused due to blockage of the outflow of aqueous humour through the trabecular meshwork.

### 11.3.7 Diabetic macular edema

Fluid accumulation in the macula, or central portion of the eye, caused by the complication of diabetes results in swelling of the macula. Filling of the macula with cells causes blurred vision that plays a vital role for sharp, straight ahead vision that helps in reading and driving.

### 11.3.8 Retinopathy of prematurity

Retinopathy of prematurity is a disease which widely affects children or infants and is the main cause of vision loss. Basically, there is a problem occurring in the retina of babies having non-appropriate weight.

## 11.4 History of retinal imaging

In order to get the visual photo of inner retina, various special techniques are needed. For various analyses there was publication of a method that applied fluorescein angiography for image analysis based on mathematical morphology, primarily focused on vessel segmentation [9], done by Matsui et al. The French physician Jean Mery successfully depicted that on immersing a live cat in water, the cat's retinal vessels would become visible from the outside and this idea inspired Jan Evangelista Purkyně, who was a researcher, to invent the principles of the ophthalmoscope in 1823, then Charles Babbage reinvented it in 1845 [10, 11]. Lastly, the same attempt was made in 1851 by researcher von Helmholtz [12]. In the year 1853 the Dutch ophthalmologist van Trigt [13] published the first image of the retina. In 1891 Gerloff, a doctor from Germany, showed very important photographs of the eye retina depicting the blood vessels [14].

The fundus camera was developed by Gullstrand in 1910 and used in modern times to image the retina and for this he received the Nobel prize for his contribution [15]. The invention of fluorescein angiographic imaging was the next major development where the functional state of the retina was known by the help of a fundus camera with narrow filters [16]. Due to safety concerns this was replaced by tomographic imaging methods. In 1964 Allen described stereo fundus photography to represent 3D shape of the retina, further there occurred the development of confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscopy where at different confocal depths multiple images of the retina are obtained.
