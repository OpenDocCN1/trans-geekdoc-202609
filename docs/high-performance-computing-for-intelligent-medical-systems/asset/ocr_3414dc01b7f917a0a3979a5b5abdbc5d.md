Testing of the three trained classifiers was carried out using the MEEI dataset. The DNN classifier provided a very strong classification rate for both the male and female groups, that is, 94.26% and 90.52%, respectively. It is realized from their research work that the use of several neuronal layers and the optimization of weights will allow the DNN to outperform other ML classifiers.

Another recently published research literature [9] dealt with the problem of defining the exact pathology associated with voice dysfunction rather than a simple binary classification. The innovation of the research work is the use of the computer vision (CV) based deep spatial exploitation capability to carry out the proposed multiclass classification. All previous approaches used a range of acoustic/speech-based features for the training of ML algorithms. The use of these handcrafted features to diagnose pathologies has become rather monotonous and overstretched. The work of [137, 138] has also shown that there is a huge amount of untapped potential for deep learning in the field of pathological voice recognition. To this end, the research work of [9] explores the use of multiple techniques such as signal denoiser, multiple time–frequency scalograms, image-based analysis using a dilated fully convolutional neural network, swarm intelligence [139] for iteratively boosting prediction score. Samples were initially extracted from the VOICED dataset, which was followed by a deep learning signal filtering process. The filtered signal corpus was then used to construct morse, bump, and analytical-morlet wavelet time–frequency scalogram image corpuses. Three separate 3D-CNN networks were trained three times each using image corpuses, after which an iterative optimization technique called ‘group decision analogy’ was applied to improve accuracy. The classification task, which includes four groups, that is, normal, hyperkinetic dysphonia, hypokinetic dysphonia, and reflux laryngitis, showed an overall accuracy of 97.7%, while reflux laryngitis and hypokinetic dysphonia were 100% accurate. The approach has been very effective, but some of the factors that have not been applied are five-fold cross-validation and a methodology that uses a single classifier to minimize training and testing times. It can also be further expanded to handle other voice disorders.

[138] uses multi-layer neural networks to automatically extract useful features from raw input data, which can also be related to as an end-to-end approach. In the end-to-end mode, a raw voice signal or spectrogram is directly used to train a deep learning model to target output labels [140, 141]. Multi-layer neural network disadvantages, however, include over-fitting and a conceivably extended training time due to many model parameters. To fix the downside, CNN-based models were proposed using transfer learning and input voice signal representations such as Mel-spectrogram and octave-spectrogram, which achieved a state-of-the-art output of 97.5% accuracy [142]. Researchers also studied the combination of CNN with long short-term memory (LSTM) networks [143] and, similarly, the combination of LSTM and MLP [144] for pathological voice detection.
