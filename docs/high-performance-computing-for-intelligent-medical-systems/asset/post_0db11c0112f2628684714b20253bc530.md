## 8.2 Understanding the concept: video compression

Video compression represents the technique for reducing the bit requirement to encode digital video content, which leads to faster processing, less storage requirement, and lower transmission bandwidth requirement. The key idea behind video compression involves the removal of any kind of noise, redundant and irrelevant information which are not critical to watching the video contents. A video compression codec (format) is regarded as an efficient one if it can achieve the above benefits without causing much hardware overhead and without compromising the visual experience of the contents of the video. Higher and efficient data compression is possible for digital video data than that of analog video data. Lossless and lossy are the two techniques for data compression, where with the first type there is no loss of video information and the same information can be decoded as in the original, while in the second type the loss of video information happens due to the removal of less important or irrelevant information. Balancing the quality, speed, resource utilization and power consumption are the challenges in video compression techniques. To efficiently process the video data, videos must be encoded through a compression process.

As the videos are a collection of images or frames, the encoding of videos will include the encoding of each of the frames. But there is a difference in encoding still images and video sequences. The images or frames only have spatial redundancy, whereas the signals in video vary in space as well as in time and they have both spatial and temporal redundant information. The spatial redundancy is due to the correlation that exists between the nearby pixel values and the temporal redundancy exists between the signals that vary in the time since the frames are captured very fast. This can be well understood by the fact that the videos are captured by high-quality cameras and at very high speed represented by the frame rate of videos like 30 fps (i.e., 30 frames per second) meaning that within a second, 30 different frames are being captured, that is, to capture 1 frame, 33 milliseconds are required. It is a well-known fact that the variation in video information is owing to the physical displacement of the object from one frame to another, so, it can be argued that there will be a considerable amount of redundant information popularly referred to as temporal redundancy between two successive frames of a video as the physical movement of the objects within a span of 33 milliseconds will be very limited. The best example is that of a news anchor briefing the news in a news-room/studio, there will be no change in the background, chair, desk, etc due to non-movement of the objects, which is the reason for spatial redundancy between the frames of a video, whereas there will be a change of movement of lips, hands, head, shoulders, and eyes but to a very limited extent. So, this type of very low degree of movement leads to temporal redundancy. To encode and compress all such videos, both spatial and temporal redundancies should be exploited.

## 8.3 Image compression standards

During the latter part of the last century, the emergence and development of digital imagery led to increasing demand for digital transmission and storage of images. Identifying the necessity of suitability and interoperability between various image communications and loading devices, several standardization efforts for image compression have been initiated over the past few years by agencies of international standard such as the International Standards Organization (ISO), the International Telecommunications Union (ITU) and the International Electro-technical Commission (IEC). In promoting the growth of digital photography, these principles have played a major role. Various image compression standards are discussed in this section.

### 8.3.1 JPEG

JPEG refers to ‘Joint Photographic Experts Group’ and specifies the working group WG1 of the ISO/IEC Joint Technical Committee 1, Study Committee 29 (ISO/IEC JTC1/SC29/WG1). The WG1 committee had developed many image compression standards at the international level.

### 8.3.2 JPEG2000

The standardization process to improvise the standard of JPEG Compression, WG1 committee initiated the process in the latter part of 1990. The improved standardized features include 16 bits deep samples being supported, superior bit-rate performance (especially at low bit-rates), support for samples of up to 16 bits deep, advanced transmission, and single architecture supporting both lossless and lossy compression.
