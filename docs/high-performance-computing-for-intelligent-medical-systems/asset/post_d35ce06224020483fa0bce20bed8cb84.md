Saha et al [53] developed EMCNet for the classification of patients with COVID-19 and normal cases. They used 4600 total x-ray images (2300 COVID-19 patients and 2300 normal) collected from different online sources and resized all of them to 224 × 224 × 3, and then normalized the dataset. The entire dataset was split into 70% training, 20% validation, and 10% for the test set. The EMCNet is a combination of AlexNet, VGG16, Inception, and ResNet 50 to extract features from images. They used CNN, Decision Tree, SVM, AdaBoost, and random forest for classifying between two classes. They also used ensembling of all of these classifiers with five-fold cross-validation and hard voting. Their ensemble method achieved 100% precision, 97.82% recall, 98.91% accuracy, 98.889% F1-score, and 0.9894 AUC. They did not follow any specific protocol, and CI was not reported.

Panahi et al [54] created Fast COVID-19 Detector (FCOD) for classifying x-ray images of COVID from non-COVID subjects. They used an open-source dataset from GitHub (https://github.com/ieee8023/covid-chestxray-dataset) and then selected 940 x-ray images of COVID-19 and 940 x-ray images of non-COVID-19. They separated the dataset into two parts: 80% for training and 20% for test. Data augmentation was used, and the size of the training set was then increased to 3760 images. FCOD consisted of several depthwise separable convolutional layers. Following each layer, a max-pooling layer and a batch normalization layer were used, and a dropout layer was used at the end. They achieved 93% sensitivity, 97% specificity, 97% precision, 96% accuracy, and 96% F1-score. They did not follow any specific protocol, and CI was not reported.

Pham [55] used transfer learning for the classification of chest x-ray COVID-19 positive from normal subjects (2 classes) and chest x-ray COVID-19 positive from normal subjects and pneumonia (3 classes) (figure 5.8). Three publicly available datasets and then their six sub-datasets were used in this study. The pre-trained AlexNet, GoogleNet, and SqueezeNet were used for classification, and the following metrics were reported: sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1-score, precision, and AUC. The hold-out (50%) was used. No specific STARD standard was followed.

Di et al [56] classified chest CTs of COVID-19 from community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) with uncertainty-vertex hypergraph construction. They performed segmentation on each image for extracting the lesion area with deep learning-based networks like VB-Net and extracted features for each patient based on histogram distribution, mean and variance in the lesion area, number of infected lesion, and also demographic variables such as sex and age. Overall, 191 features were calculated for each patient. The dataset had 3330 CT images, among which 2148 belonged to COVID-19 patients, and the remaining 1182 were from CAP patients. They used 10-fold cross-validation. They achieved 89% accuracy, 93% sensitivity, 84% specificity, 90% PPV (precision), and 88% NPV.

Wu et al [57] proposed a new algorithm for classifying chest CT images from normal subjects and CAP patients. They used a deep active learning method in which 30% labeled data were used. They used a subset of the CC-CCII dataset that included 962 patients (304 COVID-19, 316 pneumonia, 342 normal). Different augmentation, including affine transformation and color jittering, was used. They used 70% of the data to form an unlabeled dataset and 10% for validation, and 20% for test. First, they extracted the lung section of each image, and then they fed their network with unlabeled data. When the network could not predict the image label, it asked for the label from human experts. Their network included multiple levels of a 3D and 2D convolutional layer, pooling, and dropout. Finally, they compared their method with the state-of-the-art. They achieved 86% accuracy and 96% AUC. They did not report other metrics and did not report any CI, and also, they did not follow any specific standard.

Karakanis et al [58] proposed two new models: classifying chest x-ray COVID-19 from healthy control and classifying chest x-ray COVID-19 from pneumonia and healthy control subjects. They followed the protocol proposed by Maguolo and Nanni [60] and explained all their method aspects. Their implementation was published on GitHub. They used public datasets. Synthetic COVID-19 images (#130) were also generated with conditional GAN. The analyzed dataset was 275 COVID-19, 275 pneumonia, and 270 normal subjects. 80% was used for training, while 20% was used for test/validation. They reported 98.7% accuracy, 98.3% specificity, 100% sensitivity for the binary classification and 98.3% accuracy, 98.1% specificity, and 99.3% sensitivity for multi (3 classes) classification. No CI was reported for the performance indices.

## 5.2.6 Critical issues

Not following the critical STARD, TRIPOD, or medical data mining checklist prevents the COVID-19 diagnosis and prognosis methods’ clinical application. Moreover, there are at least two other critical issues to consider.

Balancing the dataset has been used in the literature to improve the performance of the classifiers. The imbalanced datasets are very hard to deal with. For example, Ardakani et al [22] reported a comprehensive list of the performance indices and the hold-out validation framework (80% training, 20% test) for COVID-19 diagnosis based on the CT-image deep learning. 86 non-COVID-19 and 34 COVID-19 and patients participated in the study, and finally, 510 non-COVID-19 and 510 COVID-19 CT image patches were analyzed. In addition to the hold-out validation framework that is susceptible to testing hypotheses suggested by the data, the dataset was balanced. The disease’s prevalence was not similar to the population, resulting in decreased precision, a.k.a., Positive Predictive Value (PPV), in practice. This issue can be described using the Bayes’ Formula:

where Se and Sp are the sensitivity and specificity of the designed system and P is the prevalence of the disease in the population. In the study of Ardakani et al [22], the best method was ResNet-101 with the Se and Sp of 100% and 99.02%, which are very high. If the COVID-19 prevalence is considered 10%, PPV will be 92%. The parameter PPV is the probability that a subject has COVID-19, knowing the diagnosis method’s result is positive. Such high Se and Sp values are not usually seen in the literature. Usually, Se and Sp of 80% and 95% are considered appropriate. In a similar prevalence condition, PPV will be only 64%. As a result, when the dataset is balanced, the system’s performance must be very high in terms of Se and Sp. Otherwise, the system does not work as expected in practice. Such a balancing procedure was also used in other studies, such as [53].
