A hacker may launch a brute force attack on a website or a group of websites to profit financially from advertising commissions. Here are a few examples of common techniques:

- 2. Redirecting traffic from a lawful website to a commissioned ad site is illegal.
- 3. Infecting a website and its visitors with software that records their activities, such as spyware. The information gathered is subsequently sold to marketers without the user's knowledge or agreement.
- 4. **Obtaining Personal Information**

   Gaining access to a participant's accounts may reveal a ton of information, such as accounting data and account numbers, along with personal patient history. Business data compromise, whereby cybercriminals obtain access to a piece of main corporate information, can mean the loss of sensitive data and login information.

## Malware is spreading

The overwhelming majority of attacks are not aimed at a single individual. An attacker may just desire to cause chaos and demonstrate their malicious skills. They might do this by sending software through email, hiding spyware on a hacked webpage, or diverting blog users to malicious sites.

The attacker can obtain access to connected computers and applications and carry out larger attacks targeting businesses by compromising a user's workstation with spyware.

## Brute-Force Attack Tools

Guessing a user's password on a social networking page can take quite some time, especially if the password is complicated. Hackers have created tools and applications to make the job of cracking passwords easier. Password cracking tools can crack passcode combinations that would be practically impossible for a human to crack on their own. The following are examples of commonly used brute-force tools:

- **Aircrack-ng**: A collection of tools for analyzing Wi-Fi network security, monitoring, and exporting data, and attacking networks by spoofing access points and injecting packets.
- **John the Ripper**: A popular password cracking tool that can recover passwords for Windows, Ubuntu, and macOS, databases, online services, network traffic, encrypted authentication tokens, and document files using dozens of encryption and hash variations. By quickly guessing credential sequences, some programs can hack a variety of computer systems, cellular phones, and encrypted USB sticks.

A brute-force attack may need a significant amount of computing power. To cope with this, developers have invented hardware-accelerated approaches, including combining a computer's microprocessor with graphics chips (GPUs). Adding the GPU's data processing power allows the attacker to run multiple tasks at once, making password cracking much easier and faster for cybercriminals.

## How Can Brute Force Attacks Be Prevented?

The following are some of the best strategies for creating a stronger password:

- 1. **Create strong, unique, and non-obvious passwords**: As a general rule, passwords should be at least 5 to 10 characters long and include uppercase and lowercase letters, punctuation, and digits. Unless an attacker has access to a supercomputer, this drastically increases the amount of time needed to break a password, from a few minutes to several centuries.
- 2. **Make your passwords tougher**: Although adding extra characters to personal passwords is a smart option, many companies impose password strength requirements. As a corollary, to secure yourself from simple phishing scams, use complicated passwords. A passphrase is a collection of letters or characters with specific markings that make cracking extremely difficult.
- 3. **Create mnemonic passphrases**: Shortening phrases to make them sound nonsensical to people who see them is another creative password strategy. This is done by dropping syllables or only using the first letters of words, then constructing a passphrase from the incomplete words. "Hope" is written as "hp," whereas "blue" is reduced to "bl."
- 4. **Avoid using passwords that are commonly used**: Passwords that are often used, including a nickname, a sports team, or simply "password," are particularly dangerous. Attackers look for common words and phrases in people's passwords and devise methods to break into accounts using these terms.
- 5. **Create unique passphrases for each account**: Credential stuffing is the practice of cybercriminals using passwords that have already been leaked online to see whether they have been reused on other services. Regrettably, because individuals regularly reuse their passwords for email, social networking pages, and online publications, it is a big success. It is crucial not to use the same password for many websites or organizations.
- 6. **Use a password manager**: Password managers make it simple for individuals to set secure passphrases for all the services they visit. They create and store users' online account credentials for multiple hosts, including all your credentials, accessed through a single master password. With the aid of a password manager, users can construct long and complex credentials.

## Better Protect User Passwords

It is pointless for users to follow strong password best practices if their employer cannot safeguard their data from brute-force attacks. The company must also take steps to protect its users and strengthen network security, such as:

- 1. **Accept CAPTCHA verification**: By including a CAPTCHA window in the account setup, an attacker is prevented from brute-forcing their way into an account or network. For example, entering words from on-screen images, selecting various image squares, and identifying objects are all examples of CAPTCHA alternatives.
- 2. **Set up an IP allowlist**: It is a smart option to have an IP allowlist to secure a corporation's infrastructure, including consumers, against identified attackers. It is vital to keep this allowlist up to date to avoid new attacks.

## What does the term "encryption key" mean?

Personal information encryption is the method of scrambling data into a set of meaningless characters. The content can be deciphered with the right security password. Even the most powerful supercomputers are unable to decode a 256-bit encryption scheme, which would need 2 to the power of 128 possibilities. It is used by almost all websites and web browsers. Data security using 256-bit encryption is so strong that even a supercomputer capable of analyzing billions of possibilities every second would be unable to crack it. As a result, brute-force attacks cannot break 256-bit encryption.
