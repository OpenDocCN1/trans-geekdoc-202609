This issue is partially resolved by new technologies known as global positioning systems (GPS) and cosmic-ray neutron sensing (CRNS). According to Rahimzadeh-Bajgiran et al. (2013), there are several benefits to using soil moisture estimating equipment, including mobility, ease of installation, operation, and maintenance, relative maturity, and the capacity to make direct measurements at different depths with high temporal resolution. However, according to Zhang et al. (2014), these methods are labour-intensive, costly, advanced and some may also be destructive (e.g., gravimetric sampling).

For such uses, further contemporary techniques are required, such as electronic sensors that provide an instantaneous measurement of soil moisture. The most extensive range of soil moisture sensors available for purchase nowadays is based on the soil medium’s electromagnetic sensing. These sensors fall into three categories: capacitance-type devices, TDR, and electrical resistivity (ER) (Heng et al., 2002; Jones et al., 2002). To achieve accurate data, these sensors need to be calibrated properly. Although the scientific literature provides a variety of methods for calibrating dielectric soil moisture sensors, their usage in field investigations is limited due to factors impacting calibration, the technical difficulties of calibration, and a shortage of confidence in their efficacy.

Compared to capacitance-based sensors, Time Domain Reflectometry sensors have been demonstrated to have greater accuracy and to work at higher frequencies (Walker et al., 2004; Robinson et al., 2008). On the other hand, capacitance sensors are far less expensive despite having inferior accuracy. This makes it possible to employ more instruments and, thus, denser networks. Considering the significant temporal and spatial variability of soil moisture at comparatively small scales (Teuling and Troch, 2005), it has been recommended that some applications would benefit from selecting less expensive but less accurate sensors to reduce the sampling error caused by spatial variability (Teuling et al., 2006; Bogena et al., 2007; Robinson et al., 2008).

Remote sensing technologies comprises radar (microwaves), satellite, and other non-contact approaches. The assessment of electromagnetic energy reflected or emitted from the soil surfaces is necessary to determine the soil moisture by using remote sensing. Depending on the temperature of the soil, the dielectric characteristics, or a mix of the two, the intensity of this radiation with soil moisture may change. As moisture content affects the dielectric constant, it is possible to use the attenuation of microwave energy in active radar to determine the moisture content of porous material. The visible/infrared hyperspectral (VIR/NIR) and thermal infrared (TIR) remote sensing techniques are used to measure the surface soil moisture content. The soil water content of surface soils, which can be categorized into warm and cool soils, with temperatures ranging from 2°C to 50°C and −2°C to −50°C, respectively, is measured using thermal infrared (TIR) sensors. Only warm soils allow for the measurement of soil moisture using thermal infrared (TIR) sensors. On the other hand, cool soil moisture is measured by visible/infrared hyperspectral (VIR/NIR) sensors (Gerhards et al., 2019).
