## Introduction

Soil moisture (SM) is crucial for ecological processes and ecosystem services, impacting the hydrological cycle, climate regulation, nutrient availability, and primary productivity (Anagnostopoulos et al., 2017). It significantly influences land evapotranspiration, a vital process connecting water, energy, and carbon cycles (Green et al., 2019; Padrón et al., 2020). In agriculture, optimal soil moisture is essential for maximum crop productivity, making accurate SM data vital for effective water resource management to prevent drought and water stagnation (Li et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2019). SM is also critical for hydrological applications like flood prediction, as saturated soils increase flood risks (Kim et al., 2019). In forestry, it supports tree growth, forest health, and fire risk management (Krueger et al., 2022). Furthermore, SM is integral to climate models, influencing weather predictions, climate projections, and climate variability through its interaction with atmospheric conditions (Koster et al., 2010). Therefore, precise and feasible methods for soil moisture evaluation are essential given their diverse and significant roles.

Traditional soil moisture evaluation methods like gravimetric methods, neutron probes, and tensiometers are accurate but have notable limitations. Gravimetric methods, though precise, are labor-intensive and time-consuming, providing only point-based measurements. Neutron probes are costly, involve radioactive sources, and need careful handling, while tensiometers require regular maintenance and are effective only at specific moisture levels. These limitations—high cost, laboriousness, and limited spatial coverage—hinder their use for large-scale or real-time assessments (Ochsner et al., 2013). Consequently, remote sensing systems have been developed, significantly improving soil moisture predictions (Wang and Qu, 2009). Advances in remote sensing technology now enable the monitoring of spatial time-series soil moisture using optical, thermal infrared (TIR), and microwave observations. New methods and algorithms combining satellite-based optical/TIR or optical/microwave data have enhanced soil moisture retrieval. However, TIR and passive microwave observations, while useful for regional and global scales, have limitations in spatial and temporal resolution for detailed agricultural applications.

The integration of deep learning and advanced machine learning techniques has revolutionized the retrieval of soil parameters, surpassing traditional models. These modern methods are adept at modeling the complex, nonlinear relationships inherent in soil moisture (SM) estimation. For example, deep learning approaches, like the Unsupervised Self-Training Method (USTM), has demonstrated higher precision and accuracy in estimating SM using various indices and ground truth data compared to traditional techniques (Sedighi et al., 2024). Machine learning algorithms are now pivotal in SM estimation studies, effectively simulating patterns and deriving insights from scientific data. They handle the nonlinear and complex nature of SM relationships adeptly and are used in scale transition processes such as gap filling, downscaling, and upscaling, enhancing the spatial completeness and resolution of SM products.

AI and computer vision techniques further advance SM assessment by analyzing visual data from various sensors, including RGB, multispectral, and thermal cameras, with high accuracy and non-invasively (Zhang et al., 2024). Integrating AI and computer vision into SM evaluation supports precision agriculture and daily management practices by enabling site-specific management and optimizing resource use for higher agricultural productivity. These techniques offer deeper insights into water movement within the soil, informing water resource management and ecosystem conservation strategies. AI and computer vision bridge the gap between point measurements and remote sensing, providing essential information on the spatial and temporal variability of SM, crucial for informed decision-making to sustain water resources, ensure food security, and implement ecosystem conservation strategies.

The benefits of integrating AI and computer vision are numerous. AI algorithms can process vast amounts of data from various sensors, improving the accuracy of SM measurements and enabling real-time monitoring. These techniques assess SM without disturbing the soil, preserving ecosystem integrity, and can be applied across large agricultural fields, offering comprehensive SM data that informs irrigation practices and crop management. By automating data collection and analysis, AI and computer vision reduce the need for expensive and labor-intensive traditional methods. AI-driven SM data can seamlessly integrate into precision agriculture systems, optimizing water use and improving crop yields. Moreover, accurate SM data supports climate models and weather predictions, aiding climate adaptation strategies for agriculture and forestry. These technologies provide valuable insights into SM dynamics, supporting sustainable agriculture, forestry, and ecosystem management. Leveraging AI and computer vision allows for informed decisions to optimize water resources, ensure food security, and conserve ecosystems amidst changing climatic conditions. With this background, we will delve into various machine learning techniques, including their classification, underlying relationships, advanced deep learning approaches used in soil moisture (SM) evaluation, and their further applications with SM products.

## Machine Learning

### Supervised Learning Techniques for Soil Moisture Prediction

Supervised learning is a central element in the methodology of soil moisture prediction that involves training predictive models on datasets labeled with noise-free data (Karthikeyan et al., 2020). As in the aforementioned supervised learning techniques, training is performed using input features—temperature, humidity, soil type, and remote sensing—separating the indices and furthering the test on the target variable, in other terms, the soil moisture for every input data point. The last step in this process is the training of the dataset on which they want the model to learn; that is, the soil moisture results. There are various key steps in the process of implementing supervised learning for soil moisture prediction (Fig. 1). Supervised learning techniques provide potent tools for soil moisture prediction based on labeled datasets, enabling the training of models to effectively predict soil moisture levels associated with a vast array of input features. Some commonly used algorithms (Fig. 2), such as decision trees, random forests, and SVMs, generate robust and highly interpretable predictions, which are very useful for proper management in terms of agriculture practices, water resources, and environmental conservation methods.

### Classification

Classification models play a vital role in segregating soil moisture levels into different classes such as dry, moist, or wet. The models proved to be effective in classifying soil conditions for practical decision-making and were generally not suitable for predicting the exact numerical values of soil moisture. By categorizing continuous soil moisture data into meaningful classes, classification models provide more actionable information, especially in agriculture, where certain moisture thresholds must be closely monitored for irrigation and crop management.
