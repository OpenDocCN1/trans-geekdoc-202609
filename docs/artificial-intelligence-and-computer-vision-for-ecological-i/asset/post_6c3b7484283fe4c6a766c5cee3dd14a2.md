## Time Domain Reflectometry (TDR) Sensors

Time Domain Reflectometry sensors measure the travel time of an electromagnetic pulse along a waveguide inserted into the soil. Soil moisture levels influence the travel time, as the presence of water slows the pulse. This delay is calibrated and converted into volumetric soil moisture content using appropriate conversion functions. TDR sensors offer high-precision measurements at various soil depths but are expensive and require specialized equipment and expertise for data interpretation. Despite these challenges, they are extensively utilized in research, precision farming, and hydrological studies, providing a deeper understanding of soil moisture dynamics essential for scientific investigations (Kashyap et al., 2021).

## Frequency Domain Reflectometry (FDR) Sensors

The FDR sensors measure the dielectric constant of soil by analyzing the frequency response of the soil’s capacitive properties. Operating at higher frequencies, these sensors provide greater accuracy across varying depths. However, FDR sensors are expensive and require calibration for different soil types. Like TDR sensors, FDR sensors are utilized in research and precision agricultural applications, particularly in areas where detailed moisture profiling is essential. Zhu et al. (2019) conducted experiments comparing TDR and FDR sensors under varying temperature conditions and found that TDR sensors are more sensitive to temperature changes than FDR sensors.

## Gypsum Block Sensors

Gypsum block sensors measure the electrical resistance of a gypsum block in equilibrium with soil moisture. In this case, electrical resistance changes along with the degree of wetness surrounding the block. These sensors are cheaply made and require little skill to use but they have lower accuracy rates and become less effective over time. The use of these sensors in agriculture, particularly when cost is a major factor, is limited to basic soil moisture monitoring. Other methods are usually employed together with them for a comprehensive understanding of the amount and distribution of soil water. Using experimental results, Ezekiel et al. (2021) demonstrated that the mean difference between the Gravimetric method, compared to gypsum, Tensiometer and Sensor, was 6.30, 3.95 and 3.02, respectively. It could be concluded that the Sensor used with the Arduino board measures moisture content more accurately than the Gypsum and Tensiometer. Veeranna et al. (2016) developed the automated Wireless Gypsum Block Sensor Network System which performed extremely well with results highly correlated concerning actual soil moisture as 0.98 r-square value between them.

## Neutron Probe Sensors

Measuring hydrogen atom density in the soil using a neutron source at a point is how neutron probe sensors work. The slowdown of neutrons by hydrogen atoms and this change are measured to determine the moisture content of the soil. Neutron probe sensors require special handling because they have radioactive sources that need safety precautions but they give accurate readings as well as can measure various depth levels of soil moisture. Neutron probes find applications in research activities and critical monitoring of soil moisture. They are highly accurate and hence suitable for detailed hydrological investigations and large-scale agricultural schemes. Evett et al. (2009) determined spatiotemporal variability of electromagnetic (EM) and Neutron Probe Sensors to determine the soil profile water content. They stated that the EM sensor had low accuracy and high variability than the Neutron Probe Sensors with a standard comparison with gravimetric samples. ## Dielectric Soil Moisture Sensors

Using high-frequency electromagnetic waves, dielectric soil moisture sensors check the dielectric constant in the soils which provides real-time data regarding changing patterns in water wetness over time making it a continuous measurement by wave interaction with the soils. These sensors offer continuous measurements of soil moisture but are costly and sensitive to salinity levels in the ground. Plauborg et al. (2005) experimented with TDR and dielectric soil moisture sensors in drip-irrigated sandy soils and discovered that TDR produces better results with higher precision.

## Remote Sensing Techniques for Estimation of Soil Moisture

An essential factor in soil and water study is soil moisture (SM). SM is arguably the most significant link that interlinks water cycle and life—plant—and its significant effects on farming, biology and nature.

A thoroughly researched aspect of the terrestrial water cycle is land surface hydrology. In land-surface hydrology, the primary factors are streamflow, vegetation, precipitation, SM, and surface temperature. Currently, satellites are used to monitor surface temperature, vegetation, and precipitation. The only variable that is still either not observed is SM. For precisely this reason, satellite SM has seen an increase in use over the last ten years (Lakshmi et al., 2013).

### Overview of Remote Sensing Techniques for Estimation of SM

Determination of SM from remote sensing has been studied for many years and a growth has been observed in this field of study. The methods used during Thermal Infrared (TIR) remote sensing uses the relationship between SM and surface temperature. This results in complicated derivative processes and many approximations and hence valuable information of SM is extracted by combining optical and TIR remotely sensed data (Zhang et al., 2014).

The advancement in satellite technology offers quantitative estimations and a potent way to track the spatial-temporal variations of regional SM. Many specialists have developed different methods to obtain SM by examining the correlations between SM and land surface parameters obtained from satellites. Through the usage of Visible Infrared (VIR) band, deriving SM through the relation between different types of SM data or vegetative traits and soil reflectance that occur during water stress has been studied. Merging of remotely sensed data from Optical and TIR sensors yields accurate data. Techniques based on spatial and temporal information each have benefits and drawbacks. Therefore, it’s critical to figure out how to combine these techniques in a sensible way to produce SM that is accurate. Remotely Sensed techniques are used in estimation of SM (Petropoulos et al., 2015), providing a thorough summarization of various remote sensing methods employed to determine SM, highlighting their respective benefits and limitations.

Microwave remote sensing, particularly Synthetic Aperture RADAR (SAR) data, is useful data because it can penetrate cloud coverage and provide data irrespective of the weather condition. SAR systems emit microwave signals and measure the backscatter, which is influenced by soil's moisture content. These measurements are utilized to derive accurate SM levels across massive zones. The high resolution of SAR data allows for detailed spatial analysis, making it a valuable resource in precision agriculture and hydrology. Passive microwave sensors, employed from 1978 to 2015, have significantly advanced soil moisture (SM) estimation by providing a detailed understanding of sensor technologies and their applications. Over this period, sensors like SMMR, SSM/I, MSMR, SMOS, AMSR-E, TRMM, and SMAP have progressively enhanced SM monitoring through improvements in frequency range, swath width, polarization capabilities, and temporal resolution. These advancements have revolutionized the ability to capture accurate, large-scale SM data, contributing to critical applications in hydrology, agriculture, and environmental monitoring.
