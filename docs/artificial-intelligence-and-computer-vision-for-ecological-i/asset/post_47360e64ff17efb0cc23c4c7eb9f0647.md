- **1. Scanning Multichannel Microwave Radiometer (SMMR):**
  - Launch Date: 1978–1987.

  - Swath Width: 1,400 km.
  - Polarization: H & V.
  - Temporal Resolution: 1 day.

- **3. Multifrequency Scanning Microwave Radiometer (MSMR):**
  - Launch Date: 1999–2001.
  - Frequency: Bands include 6.6 GHz, 10.65 GHz, and 18.21 GHz.
  - Swath Width: 1,360 km.
  - Polarization: H & V (excluding 22.23 GHz).
  - Temporal Resolution: 2 days.

- **4. Soil Moisture and Ocean Salinity (SMOS):**
  - Launch Date: 2009.
  - Frequency: 1.4 GHz.
  - Swath Width: Not specified.
  - Polarization: H & V.
  - Temporal Resolution: 3 days.

- **5. Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer for EOS (AMSR-E):**
  - Launch Date: 2002–2011.
  - Frequency: Multiple bands, including 6.6 GHz, 10.65 GHz, 18.7 GHz, 23.8 GHz, 36.5 GHz, and 89 GHz.
  - Swath Width: 1,445 km.
  - Polarization: H & V.
  - Temporal Resolution: 2 days.

- **6. Tropical Rainfall Measuring Mission (TRMM):**
  - Launch Date: 1997–2001.
  - Frequency: 10.65 GHz, 19.35 GHz, 22.24 GHz, 37 GHz, and 85.50 GHz.
  - Swath Width: 880 km.
  - Polarization: H & V.
  - Temporal Resolution: 3 days.

- **7. Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP):**
  - Launch Date: 2015.
  - Frequency: 1.41 GHz.
  - Swath Width: 1,000 km.
  - Polarization: H & V.
  - Temporal Resolution: 4 days.

The evolution of these sensors highlights advancements in satellite remote sensing technology for SM monitoring. The increased precision in frequency, wider swath widths, and improved temporal resolutions have enhanced the accuracy of SM data, supporting large-scale hydrological and agricultural applications (Das et al., 2015).

## Airborne Sensing Platforms for Assessment of SM

Airborne remote sensing platforms play a crucial role in SM assessment, offering the capability to collect data over large areas quickly and efficiently. These platforms utilize various sensors and instruments capable of measuring electromagnetic radiation across different wavelengths, enabling them to capture detailed information about SM content. Mentioned below is a description of various remote sensing instruments used for Earth observation, highlighting their operational frequencies and primary usage in environmental monitoring and research.

- (a) The Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer 2 (AMSR-2) is a passive remote sensing instrument on the GCOM-W1 satellite that operates at frequencies ranging from 6.9 to 89 GHz. It is primarily used to measure precipitation, concentration of sea ice, and sea surface temperatures.

- (b) The Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer–EOS (AMSR-E) is a microwave radiometer mounted on the Aqua satellite that operates at frequencies between 6.9 and 89 GHz and is used to measure SM, sea surface temperatures, and precipitation.

- (c) Aquarius is a joint active and passive L-band microwave radiometer on the Aquarius satellite that operates at frequencies of 1.26 and 1.41 GHz. Aquarius primarily measures ocean surface salinity as well as sea surface temperature and wind speed.

- (d) Advanced Synthetic Aperture Radar (ASAR) is an active radar instrument on the ENVISAT satellite that operates at a frequency of 5.33 GHz. ASAR is used to measure sea and land surface features, such as sea ice cover and land deformation.

- (e) Advanced Scatterometer (ASCAT) is an active radar scatterometer on the MetOp satellite that operates at a frequency of 5.25 GHz. ASCAT is used to measure wind speed and direction over oceans and land surfaces.

- (f) Microwave Imaging Radiometer using Aperture Synthesis (MIRAS) is a passive microwave radiometer on the SMOS satellite that operates at a frequency of 1.4 GHz. MIRAS measures SM and sea surface salinity.

- (g) The NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) is an active radar instrument that will operate at L and S bands when launched. NISAR is designed to measure changes in the Earth's surface, including surface elevation and deformation.

- (h) Phased Array L-band Synthetic Aperture Radar (PALSAR) is an active radar instrument on the ALOS satellite that operates at a frequency of 1.27 GHz. PALSAR is used for forest mapping, disaster monitoring, and terrain analysis.

- (i) RADARSAT-1 and 2 are active radar instruments that operate at a frequency of 5.40 GHz and are primarily used for ice mapping, maritime surveillance, and environmental monitoring.

- (j) Tandem-L is an active radar instrument on the Tandem-L satellite that operates at a frequency of 1.2 GHz. It's used for terrain analysis, environmental monitoring, and disaster response.

- (k) Sentinel-1A and 1B are active radar instruments that operate at C-band. These are mainly used to monitor sea ice, surveillance of marine environments, and disaster response.

- (l) Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP) is a combined active-passive radiometer on the SMAP satellite that operates at frequencies of 1.41 and 1.26 GHz. It's used to measure the amount of vegetation water, freeze/thaw state, and SM.

- (m) Special Sensor Microwave Imager (SSM/I) is a passive radiometer that operates at a frequency of 19.35 GHz. SSM/I is used for measuring sea ice extent, atmospheric moisture content, and precipitation.

- (n) WindSat is a passive radiometer on the Coriolis satellite which operates at a frequency of 6.8–37 GHz. WindSat measures sea surface winds, temperature & water vapour (Mohanty et al., 2017).

## Case Studies Highlighting the Application of Remote Sensing in SM Monitoring

Zhou and Zhang (2016) demonstrated recent advancements in SM determination through RS (Remote Sensing). Their article addresses the evolution of retrieval techniques, the validation of SM products produced from satellites, and their applications in hydrology and agriculture. The study suggests that there has been a major increase in remote sensing to study SM, with the launch of dedicated satellite missions such as SMOS and SMAP. Validation studies are being conducted to assess the precision of geospatial SM products. New methods are now being explored to enhance resolution of these products. For example, unification of SAR data and Optical or TIR data, Single Spectral Analysis Method, Vegetation Index Method (VCI, AMTNDVI, NDWI, NMDI, PDI, MPDI). The study emphasizes the importance of developing comparable SM indices that can be normalized and standardized to allow for accurate comparisons across different data sources and time periods, which is crucial for monitoring true SM conditions.

Das and Paul (2016) discussed types of Microwave Remote Sensing and the factors that influence microwave remote sensing such as Dielectric constant (ε), Backscattering Coefficient (σ°), Surface Roughness, Bulk Density (ρb), Soil texture, Vegetation cover, Incident angle (θ), Bands, Polarization. The study mentions algorithms and models such as empirical models, physical models, and data assimilation techniques that convert microwave signals into SM estimates, integrate data from multiple sensors, and incorporate ancillary information (land cover, surface temperature) to enhance accuracy. The study emphasizes that SAR data is very beneficial to determine SM although both active and passive SAR sensors have advantages and disadvantages. Ongoing and future satellite missions, along with improved algorithms, can enhance accuracy and reliability of SM data.

Zhang et al. (2014) addresses the estimation of surface soil water content (SSWC) using thermal remote sensing as traditional SM estimation methods have limitations, prompting the need for innovative approaches. The study area chosen was the central area of Spain. The study discusses the computation of the SM index TRRVDI (Temperature Rising Rate Vegetation Dryness Index), and the Theoretical Dry Edge is determined.

$$TRRVDI = \frac{RT_{(i)} - RT_{wet}}{RT_{dry} - RT_{wet}}$$

Ground-based findings of SM were used to calibrate and verify the model. The study concludes by emphasizing the potential of using temporal variations in LST and the significance of Thermal Remote Sensing for monitoring and determination of SM content.

O'Neill et al. (1996) utilized a vegetation scattering model to estimate microwave backscatter comparable to radar measurements, employing the Peake (1959) formulation to express emissivity via bistatic scattering coefficients. This approach was applied to obtain SM using SAR data, validated through comparisons of modelled and measured radar backscatter. By inputting model-generated values for vegetation transmissivity (γ) and single scattering albedo (α) into equations along with field measurements, soil reflectivity was calculated, enabling SM estimation. The study used the MACHYDRO-90 test in Pennsylvania, demonstrating the model's effectiveness in accounting for vegetation effects on SM predictions. The estimated and measured SM in corn fields showed promising results upon comparison, validating the model's accuracy in practical field conditions (O'Neill et al., 1996).
