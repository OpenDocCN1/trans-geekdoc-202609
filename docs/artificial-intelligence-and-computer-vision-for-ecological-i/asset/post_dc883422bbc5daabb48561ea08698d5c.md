Remote sensing sensors pick up these changes in visible and near-infrared (Vis-NIR) portions of the electromagnetic spectra, which causes alterations in plants’ spectral characteristics (West et al., 2003). The stressed plants generally show lower spectral reflectance values at the NIR area of EM spectra as compared to healthy vegetation, and thus such spectral response is able to differentiate healthy plants from stressed plants (Govaerts and Verhulst, 2010). In practical applications, utilizing disease-specific spectral disease indices (SDI) derived from remote sensing (RS) proves more effective for the accurate detection of plant diseases compared to conventional indexes of the vegetation (i.e., NDVI, DVI, etc.) and others (AL-Saddik et al., 2017). While remote sensing (RS) technology employing visual (RGB), multispectral, and hyperspectral sensors has demonstrated effectiveness in disease monitoring, achieving early disease detection remains challenging. Consequently, experiments have revealed that thermal and fluorescence-based RS hold promise, particularly in early disease detection when symptoms are not yet apparent in plant parts. This potential is attributed to the tendency of diseased plants to exhibit elevated temperatures compared to healthy ones (Mahlein, 2016).

## Crop Yield Prediction

In the current context, crop yield is typically assessed and mapped using a yield monitor attached to a combine harvester, which usually consolidates yield data from numerous crop rows during harvesting (Bern et al., 2019). However, low resolution is a defining feature of these yield maps. Hence, remote sensing imagery integrated with various machine learning-based models has been utilized to create high-resolution yield maps, allowing for the evaluation of yield variations within fields and across landscapes (Khanal et al., 2018). This enables a more comprehensive understanding of the field’s overall agricultural production. The RS data is commonly employed to predict crucial crop biological and physical factors (i.e., Leaf Area Index ‘LAI’; biomass; etc.) which serve as essential indicators of crop health and development across various crops (Ali et al., 2020). These data are integrated into yield forecasting models to facilitate timely and accurate yield prediction.

## Crop Residue Management

Efficient management of crop residues holds significant importance in different regions such as India, whereas residue burning is common across small to large-scale farm holdings. This practice aims to enhance soil quality and crop productivity while mitigating the adverse environmental effects caused by excessive burning of crop residues in the fields (Shah et al., 2017). Many studies have illustrated the capability of both multispectral and hyperspectral RS techniques to accurately map the spatial distribution of plants’ residues in the site (Galloza et al., 2013; Sharma et al., 2016). Sharma et al., 2016 developed RS indexes (i.e., normalized difference tillage index ‘NDTI’ which is a multispectral index that can be applied to Landsat ETM+ and OLI imageries). Their findings indicated that NDTI could effectively distinguish the degree of residue cover associated with several practices of the tillage process in various crop fields located in a part of the USA (Sharma et al., 2016). Galloza and colleagues found that hyperspectral indices outperformed multispectral indices in distinguishing crop residue covers in the field (Galloza et al., 2013). For example, compared to Vis-NIR imagery, Thermal Infrared (TIR) from NASA’s Airborne Terrestrial Application Sensor (ATLAS) is more useful in identifying variations in temperature differences among crop residues and soil systems (Sullivan et al., 2004). In recent times, scientists have turned their attention to site-specific tillage practices for managing residues, utilizing drone technology to gain a deeper insight into the impact of managing the crop residues through the water, nutrients, and soil dynamics (Khanal et al., 2020).

## Identification of Plant Species

Different plant species can also be identified by integrating remote sensing technology with machine learning and deep learning techniques. A team of researchers employed a convolutional neural network (CNN), a deep learning algorithm, to identify six different plants based on their morphological characteristics (Chouhan et al., 2024).

## Challenges and Future Direction

Remote sensing technologies and data-driven approaches have emerged as powerful tools in modern agriculture, offering unprecedented insights into crop health, soil conditions, and environmental factors. However, their implementation faces several challenges and technological constraints that need to be addressed for their widespread adoption and effective utilization.

Based on research conducted by The World Bank, it has been identified that around 65% of individuals working in agriculture are situated below the poverty threshold, equating to less than US $3.10/day (Castaneda et al., 2016). This creates a significant barrier for many smallholder farmers to embrace advanced technologies like satellite technologies, UAVs for precision agriculture on their farms, as the considerable cost disparity between one agricultural UAV and its financial benefits was unfeasible for them to afford such technology (Fernández et al., 2019). For small farm owners in places like India and Africa, even if they could buy such a device, they would still encounter problems with costs because the services and working costs for these instruments are already quite costly. Additionally, research indicates that previously utilized PA techniques like mapping of yield; GPS; and variable rate mechanisms had minimal influence on total returns. Yield mapping led to approximately a 2.5% increase, GPS guidance systems to a 2% increase, and variable-rate systems to a 1.1% increase in yield (Schimmelpfennig, 2016). Therefore, for many farmers, particularly those who own smaller-scale farms, it may be challenging to justify investing a substantial amount of time in setting up the initial capital required for these technologies. Bridging this technological gap requires innovative pricing models, capacity-building initiatives, and government support to make remote sensing technologies more affordable and user-friendly for farmers worldwide.

The next strong struggle is the complexity and variability of agricultural landscapes. Different regions exhibit diverse topographies, soil types, and crop varieties, making it challenging to develop universal remote sensing models that accurately capture agricultural dynamics across various environments (Hosseini et al., 2020). ## Conclusion

In this chapter, we have examined current developments in remote sensing technology and how they apply to agriculture. The integration of remote sensing techniques has significantly transformed agricultural practices, offering new insights and solutions to address various challenges faced by the agricultural sector. From the comprehensive analysis of current developments, it is evident that remote sensing technologies, including space-based satellite imagery, airborne imageries, and hyperspectral imaging, have revolutionized crop monitoring, yield prediction, and precision agriculture. These technologies have empowered farmers to make well-informed decisions, optimize resource use, and boost productivity while reducing environmental impact. Additionally, combining remote sensing with new technologies like data analytics and machine learning has opened up new ways to interpret data, resulting in more accurate and timely decisions in agriculture. The integration of these technologies has facilitated the development of innovative solutions for pest and disease management, soil mapping, and irrigation optimization.

However, despite the remarkable progress, several challenges remain to be addressed. These include difficulty in data interpretation, restricted access to modern technologies in rural places, and the need for continuous research and development to enhance the capabilities of remote sensing systems. Additionally, there is a growing need for capacity building and educational programs to equip farmers with the technical knowhow and skills required for effective utilization of advanced technologies.

Looking ahead, the future of remote sensing in agriculture holds great promise. As technology continues to evolve, we anticipate the emergence of more advanced sensors, platforms, and analytical tools that will improve the accuracy, efficiency, and sustainability of farming methods. Collaboration between stakeholders, including researchers, policymakers, industry players, and farmers, will be essential to drive innovation and ensure the widespread adoption of remote sensing technologies in agriculture.

In summary, using remote sensing technologies marks a major change in agriculture, providing new opportunities to tackle global food security issues, mitigate climate change impacts, and promote sustainable agricultural development. With continued investment, innovation, and collaboration, remote sensing is poised to play a central role in shaping the future of agriculture, ultimately leading to more resilient and productive agricultural systems.
