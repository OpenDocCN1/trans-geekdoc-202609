
## Computer Vision (CV) Technologies in Environmental Monitoring

### Understanding Computer Vision and Image Processing

The artificial intelligence discipline of CV makes it possible for computers to analyze and comprehend visual data from their environment. Fundamentally, CV entails the retrieval of significant data from pictures and videos to carry out operations that would typically need human vision. A key element of CV is image processing, which includes a range of methods for improving, interpreting, and modifying images (Liu et al., 2024). Filtering, segmentation, feature extraction, and pattern recognition are some of these approaches that together enable the conversion of unprocessed visual data into meaningful insights.

Image processing begins with the acquisition of images through cameras, satellites, drones, or other sensors. These images often undergo preprocessing steps, such as noise reduction, contrast enhancement, and normalization, to improve their quality and suitability for further analysis. Segmentation divides an image into meaningful regions or objects, making it easier to analyze specific features (Valipoor and de Antonio, 2023). Feature extraction identifies relevant attributes, such as edges, textures, or shapes, that can be used for classification or recognition tasks. Pattern recognition, often powered by machine learning algorithms, enables the identification and categorization of objects or phenomena within the images.

Deep learning has transformed CV by dramatically increasing the precision and effectiveness of image analysis, especially when convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are employed. CNNs are very good at tasks like object identification, picture classification, and semantic segmentation because of their ability to automatically and adaptively learn spatial hierarchies of characteristics from images. These abilities have wide-ranging applications in many fields, including autonomous driving, environmental monitoring, and medical imaging (Liu et al., 2024) (Liu et al., 2023).

### Role of CV in Water Quality Assessment

The use of deep learning has transformed CV by dramatically increasing the precision and effectiveness of image analysis, especially when convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are employed. CNNs are very good at tasks like object identification, picture classification, and semantic segmentation because of their ability to automatically and adaptively learn spatial hierarchies of characteristics from images. These abilities have wide-ranging applications in several fields, including autonomous driving, environmental monitoring, and medical imaging (Rawat et al., 2023).

The identification and tracking of turbidity is one of CV’s main functions in the evaluation of water quality. Elevated levels of turbidity, frequently resulting from suspended particulates including silt, algae, and microbes, can negatively impact aquatic ecosystems by diminishing light penetration and interfering with photosynthesis. To precisely measure turbidity levels, CV algorithms examine how light is scattered and absorbed in photographs of water (Adelani et al., 2024). Compared to conventional manual sample techniques, this automated analysis is more effective and covers a wider region, giving real-time insights into the clarity of the water and possible pollution sources.

Additionally, CV is essential for monitoring algal blooms, which can negatively impact aquatic life and water quality. Toxins produced by harmful algal blooms (HABs), which are frequently caused by nutrient pollution, pose a risk to both human health and animals. CV systems can identify and monitor the degree and evolution of algal blooms throughout time through the use of satellite imagery and aerial photography. These systems analyze concentrations of several forms of algae and distinguish between them using machine learning and pattern recognition techniques, one of the frameworks has been illustrated in Fig. 4. Prompt management efforts to lessen the effects of HABs are made possible through early discovery and ongoing monitoring (Tang et al., 2022).

Identifying contaminants and other items in water bodies is also made easier by CV technologies. Utilizing image processing techniques, for example, plastic garbage and other detritus can be visually identified and categorized. By identifying and measuring the amount of contaminants present in images captured by surface and underwater cameras, CV systems can provide important information for strategies aimed at preventing pollution and organizing cleanup operations (Punithavathi et al., 2022). CV can also assist in a thorough evaluation of the water quality by keeping an eye on variations in the color of the water, which may be a sign of dissolved materials or chemical contaminants. In conclusion, computer vision technologies are now essential to assessing the quality of modern water since they provide strong instruments for ongoing, extensive, and real-time monitoring. Through the use of sophisticated image processing and deep learning techniques, CV improves our capacity to identify, evaluate, and address a range of water quality concerns, ultimately leading to improved aquatic environment management and protection.

### Integrating AI and CV for Water Quality Assessment

#### Data Collection and Preprocessing

Robust data collection and preprocessing are essential for the accuracy and dependability of subsequent studies, and they are the first steps in an effective evaluation of water quality. Information is gathered from a variety of sources, such as underwater cameras, satellite pictures, in situ sensors, remote sensing technology, and aerial photography, during the data collection process (Nti et al., 2023). Temperature, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, pH, and other physical and chemical characteristics can all be continuously and real-time measured with *in situ* sensors. Large-scale data are available through satellite imaging and remote sensing, which can record changes in water quality across wide areas both spatially and temporally.

To guarantee the acquired data’s quality and usefulness for analysis, preprocessing is a must. Preprocessing procedures for sensor data could involve calibration, noise reduction, and handling of missing readings. The accuracy and consistency of the sensor readings with accepted criteria are guaranteed by calibration (Taşan, 2023). Random oscillations and outliers in the data can be eliminated with the use of noise reduction techniques like filtering. With a view to ensuring that the dataset is full for analysis, handling missing values requires the use of interpolation or imputation techniques to estimate the missing data.

Preprocessing for image-based data from drones and satellites entails multiple processes. These include normalization to standardize the data range, radiometric correction to correct the pixel values for atmospheric effects and sensor noise, and geometric correction to correct distortions caused by the perspective of the sensor (Al-Janabi and Al-Barmani, 2023). Techniques for image segmentation and enhancement, like contrast adjustment and edge recognition, are used to draw attention to essential characteristics and make key details easier to see. Preprocessing checks that the data is correct, clean, and prepared for AI and Computer Vision (CV) model analysis.

#### AI Models for Water Quality Prediction

Predicting water quality is becoming more and more common with AI models, especially those that make use of machine learning and deep learning techniques. These models are capable of pattern recognition, in-depth dataset analysis, and future water quality forecasting. In water quality evaluation, supervised learning algorithms including random forests, decision trees, and support vector machines (SVM) are frequently employed for regression and classification tasks. Through training on historical data, these models discover the correlations between water quality indicators (such as pollutant concentrations and algal bloom occurrences) and input factors (such as temperature, pH, and nutrient levels) (Chhipi-Shrestha et al., 2023).

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are two examples of deep learning models that provide sophisticated processing capabilities for handling big, unstructured datasets. CNNs are especially good in identifying and categorizing characteristics linked to water quality, like turbidity levels or algal blooms, by analyzing spatial data, such as satellite photography (Iqbal et al., 2023). Because RNNs can handle sequential data, they are a good fit for time-series predictions. This means that water quality trends can be predicted using sensor data from the past. This enhances the interpretability and performance of the model. Furthermore, a lot of the time, ensemble learning techniques are used, which integrate several models to improve prediction accuracy.
