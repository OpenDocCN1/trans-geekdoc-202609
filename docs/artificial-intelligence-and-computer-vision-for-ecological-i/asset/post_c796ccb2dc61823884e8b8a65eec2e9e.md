## Types of Sensors Used in Agricultural Mapping
Different types of sensors are utilized in RS to capture a wide range of data relevant to agricultural mapping. These sensors can be broadly categorized based on the data they collect from optical, thermal, radar, and LiDAR sensors.

### Optical Sensors
Optical sensors detect light reflected from the Earth’s surface across numerous wavelengths, such as visible, near-infrared (NIR), and shortwave infrared (SWIR) (Nurdin et al., 2022). These sensors frequently gauge plant vitality, soil characteristics, and crop status. Instruments that record data across several distinct spectral ranges are often employed to compute plant health indicators, like the NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) (Nurdin et al., 2022). Hyperspectral sensors, which capture information across numerous fine spectral divisions offer intricate spectral data, enabling crop identification, nutrient shortage detection, and stress monitoring in plants. These instruments find use in scientific studies and precision farming, providing sophisticated tools for in-depth examination and informed choices (Rahimi et al., 2021).

### Thermal Sensors
Thermal sensors calculate the thermal infrared radiation emitted by the Earth’s surface, providing information about surface temperature. In agriculture, thermal sensors monitor soil moisture levels, detect plant water stress, and manage irrigation systems efficiently (Kluger et al., 2021). For example, sensors on the Landsat 8 satellite provide thermal data applicable in assessing crop water use and manage water resources. Thermal imagery helps farmers optimize irrigation schedules, minimize water consumption, and enhance crop yields by guaranteeing that plants get a sufficient amount of water in a timely manner (Kumalawati et al., 2021).

### Radar Sensors
Radar sensors, or Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR), emit microwave signals and measure the reflected signals to capture Earth’s surface information (Javaid et al., 2023). Radar sensors can see through cloud cover and function around the clock, making them crucial for uninterrupted surveillance (Vyas et al., 2021). In agriculture, SAR sensors measure soil moisture, monitor crop growth, and detect changes in field conditions. Examples include the Sentinel-1 satellites, which provide high-resolution radar imagery. The ability to obtain data under all weather conditions and during both day and night makes radar sensors a reliable source of information for agricultural monitoring, particularly in regions with frequent cloud cover (Anuradha, 2024).

## Ground-Based Sensors
Ground-based sensors, such as spectroradiometers and LIDAR, provide highly accurate and detailed data for agricultural mapping (Ullo and Sinha, 2021). These sensors typically gather information on soil properties, crop growth stages, and plant health. The main advantage of ground-based sensors is their precision, which can complement data from aerial and satellite sources. However, their spatial coverage is limited, making them suitable for smaller areas or validating data obtained from other RS methods. The high equipment cost and the labor-intensive nature of ground-based data collection are notable challenges (“RS in Applications of Geoinformation,” 2022).

## Challenges in Data Acquisition

### Data Quality
Ensuring high-quality data is crucial for accurate agricultural mapping. Common issues include noise, missing values, and inconsistencies across different datasets (Khanal et al., 2020). Noise can result from sensor errors or environmental factors, while missing values may arise due to gaps in data collection. Inconsistent data formats and resolutions from various sources can complicate integration and analysis. ### Data Volume
RS generates large volumes of data, especially when using high-resolution images and frequent temporal observations (Bei et al., 2022). Managing and processing this data requires substantial storage capacity and computational power. Efficient data handling techniques and robust infrastructure are necessary to cope with the sheer volume of information, ensuring timely and accurate analysis for agricultural applications (Khanal et al., 2020).

### Data Integration
Combining data from multiple sources, such as satellite images, aerial photographs, and ground-based sensors, can enhance the overall accuracy and detail of agricultural mapping (Kashyap and Kumar, 2021a). However, integrating these diverse datasets poses significant challenges due to differences in spatial resolution, temporal frequency, and data formats. Harmonizing these datasets requires sophisticated algorithms and careful preprocessing to ensure compatibility and reliability of the integrated information (Yan et al., 2023).

### Environmental Factors
Environmental conditions such as weather, seasonal variations, and atmospheric disturbances can significantly impact the quality and availability of RS data (Kashyap and Kumar, 2021b). Cloud cover can obstruct satellite and aerial imagery, while atmospheric conditions can introduce distortions. Seasonal changes in vegetation and soil moisture also affect data consistency. Addressing these challenges requires advanced correction techniques and adaptive data acquisition strategies (Steven and Clark, 1990).

## Data Preprocessing Steps

### Data Cleaning
Data cleaning is the initial step in preprocessing, aimed at removing noise, correcting errors, and handling missing values (Liu, 2020). Noise can be reduced using filtering techniques, while errors are corrected by comparing data against known standards or reference datasets. Missing values can be addressed through interpolation or imputation methods. Effective data cleaning ensures the reliability and accuracy of subsequent analyses (Zakari et al., 2024).

### Geometric Correction
Geometric correction adjusts for distortions in RS data caused by sensor movement, Earth’s rotation, and terrain variations. Ground Control Points (GCPs) and orthorectification techniques are commonly used to align images accurately with geographic coordinates. This step is crucial for ensuring that spatial relationships in the data are accurate, enabling precise mapping and analysis.

### Radiometric Correction
Radiometric correction involves adjusting pixel values for sensor noise, atmospheric conditions, and lighting variations. Techniques like dark object subtraction, atmospheric models, and radiometric calibration standardize the reflectance values (Escandón-Panchana et al., 2024). This correction ensures the data accurately represents the observed features’ true spectral characteristics, facilitating reliable interpretation and analysis (Afxentiou et al., 2021).

### Image Enhancement
Image enhancement techniques improve the visual appearance of RS data, making it easier to identify and analyze specific features (Zhang et al., 2023a). Histogram equalization, contrast adjustment, and edge enhancement are commonly used. Enhanced images can reveal details that are not readily visible in raw data, aiding in detecting and interpreting regional features (Escandón-Panchana et al., 2024).

### Image Classification
Image classification categorizes pixels into different land cover classes based on spectral properties (Sachithra and Subhashini, 2023). Supervised classification techniques, such as Random Forest and Support Vector Machines (SVM), require labeled training data to guide the classification process (Singh et al., 2022). Unsupervised classification methods, like k-means clustering, group pixels into classes based on similarities in their spectral signatures without prior labeling. DL approaches, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), offer advanced capabilities for automated classification with high accuracy (Afxentiou et al., 2021).

### Data Fusion
Data fusion integrates information from multiple sources to enhance the overall accuracy and completeness of agricultural mapping (Omyatov et al., 2023). Techniques such as multisensor fusion combine data from different sensors, temporal fusion integrates observations over time, and spatial fusion merges data from various spatial resolutions. Effective data fusion provides a comprehensive view of agricultural conditions, supporting informed decision-making (Escandón-Panchana et al., 2024).
