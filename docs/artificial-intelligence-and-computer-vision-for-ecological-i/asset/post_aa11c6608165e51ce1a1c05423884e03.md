Despite this advantage, its use in precision agriculture is still limited due to its coarse spatial resolution, which hinders its suitability for specific agricultural operations at the field level (Stafford, 2000). Another drawback of satellite-based measurements is their reliance on weather and atmospheric conditions, which can result in the attenuation of electromagnetic signals traveling from the object to the satellite sensor (Rees, 2013). Despite encountering numerous challenges, satellite imagery remains effective for large-scale mapping of diverse phenomena, including mapping of land uses, and land cover, mapping of soil erosion, desertification evaluation, regional yield estimation, and monitoring climate change (Jafarabiglu and Pourreza, 2022). This effectiveness stems from its ability to offer a comparatively low-priced “macro” perspective of the terrain (Barnes, 2018). Advanced satellite systems with high resolution include a red-edge band to monitor crop growth and are specifically designed for agricultural applications. RapidEye data has been used to evaluate nitrogen levels in wheat crops cultivated in pre-defined management zones (Basso et al., 2013; Basso et al., 2016).

## Aerial Platforms

Unlike satellites, unmanned aerial systems (UAS) can capture and deliver high-resolution data, making them especially well-suited for agricultural operations in small and confined fields. However, the primary limitation lies in their restricted payload capacity and brief flight duration, hindering their extensive application. Conversely, manned aerial vehicles occupy an intermediate position, capable of covering larger areas than UAS and providing better resolution than satellites. In the 1920s, agrochemicals were applied to crops using manned aerial vehicles or piloted aircraft, marking the first use of airborne remote sensing in agriculture. As per Rees (2013), the commencement of analog aerial image capture and vegetation mapping began with the progress in aerial platforms such as airplanes and photographic equipment. During the 1970s, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in the United States emerged as a leading entity in utilizing aircraft for aerial remote sensing. They effectively mapped soil moisture for agricultural management by employing the P-3A aircraft, which was outfitted with various remote sensing sensors and operated at approximately 300 meters above ground level (AGL) (Jafarabiglu and Pourreza, 2022). Nevertheless, the utilization of aircraft remote sensing for research endeavors remains restricted, primarily owing to numerous drawbacks including the elevated expenses associated with sensors and platforms, requisite structure and skilled aviators, the operative intricacy of airlifts, and diminished reproducibility owing to the absence of detailed automatic pilot systems (Barnes, 2018). However, both private and governmental sectors have conducted research utilizing aerial platforms and sensors, yielding encouraging findings. While aerial photography provides excellent spatial resolution, its spectral resolution remains a concern due to the availability of only three basic bands: red (R), green (G), and blue (B). If imaging spectrometers, also known as hyperspectral sensors, were used in aerial platforms instead of multispectral sensors, they could enable the identification of plants’ biochemical composition due to their numerous contiguous bands that capture the reflectance spectrum of each pixel (Asner and Martin, 2016). An essential aerial remote sensing platform is AVIRIS (Airborne Visible InfraRed Imaging Spectrometer), and its latest iteration, AVIRIS-NG (AVIRIS-Next Generation), has recently been utilized in diverse applications including geology, ecological research, and agriculture (Lundeen and Gowey, 2021). AVIRIS is capable of capturing 224 consecutive calibrated spectral bands (ranging from 400 to 2500 nm) at varying altitudes across regions encompassing the United States, Canada, and Europe. On the other hand, AVIRIS-NG is a hyperspectral sensor equipped with 425 consecutive hyperspectral bands spanning the visible to near-infrared spectrum (ranging from 380 to 2500 nm), with spatial resolution varying from 3 meters to 18 meters. Recently, the AVIRIS-NG sensor has been extensively utilized in India, covering an area of 22,840 square kilometers across 57 sites during the initial phase of an airborne science campaign. This campaign took place over 84 days, from December 16, 2015, to March 6, 2016, as part of a joint initiative between the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) and NASA known as the HYperSpectral Imaging (HYSI) program. To back the campaign, organized fieldwork and on-site measurements were conducted at these sites, covering a range of topics such as crops, soil, forests, geology, coastal regions, oceans, river water, snow, urban areas, and beyond (Bhattacharya et al., 2019).

Given the drawbacks associated with manned aircraft, researchers are increasingly turning to unmanned aerial systems (UAS) for agricultural applications. Researchers have long documented the real strength of UAS for agrarian uses. Initially, UAS were primarily used for military purposes. Over time, their applications have expanded, and they are now widely utilized in the domain of precision farming. The use of Small UAS, commonly referred to as drones, weighing under 25 kg (55 lbs), began in agricultural operations in the early 2000s (Simpson et al., 2003). Its popularity surged rapidly due to its ability to provide high-resolution imagery, even at the centimeter scale. Eventually, in 2014, MIT’s Technology Review acknowledged it as one of the top ten innovative technologies (MIT Technology Review, 2014). The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) reported in March 2018 that by 2022, there will be 451,800 commercial drones, four times as many as there were 110,604 in 2017. The report also suggests that drone technologies are projected to generate services valued at $127 billion (Mazur et al., 2016). In addition to having high spatial resolution capabilities, farmers or growers can deploy drones as needed to survey fields regularly based on the intended temporal resolution, whether monthly, weekly, daily, or even hourly (Zhao, 2018). Moreover, drone-based remote sensing shows promise in offering comprehensive information on both inter and intra-field crop variability, aiding in the optimization of resource utilization based on the variability within fields. This includes assisting in irrigation scheduling, thereby enhancing water use efficiency (Barnes, 2018). The types of drones commonly used in agriculture include fixed-wing, single-rotor, and multirotor models. The vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) ability, along with the capability of staying in one place in the air without moving up or down rotary drones, have become highly popular in agriculture. ## RS Sensors

Remote sensing sensors vary according to the spatial, spectral, radiometric, and temporal resolution they provide. The spatial resolution of an RS-based sensor is determined by the pixel size of an image, which corresponds to a specific part on the ground. Sensors characterized by high spatial resolution typically have smaller footprints, while those with larger footprints usually exhibit lower spatial resolution. The number of bands present within a specific region of the EMR spectrum defines the spectral resolution of an RS-based sensor (Nowatzki et al., 2004). While multispectral sensors provide information across an inadequate number of spectral bands, hyperspectral sensors provide an extensive array of contiguous bands, often with narrow widths (< 20 nm), ranging from 10 to over 400 bands, such as those found in AVIRIS-NG sensors, enabling detailed object analysis (Teke et al., 2013). Various machine learning and deep learning methods, such as random forest and neural networks, are employed to decrease the dimensionality of hyperspectral data while preserving its original variability. This aids in extracting valuable insights about soil and vegetation conditions for modeling purposes (Chang et al., 2020). Radiometric resolution in remote sensing pertains to the sensor’s capability to detect and quantify minor variations in electromagnetic radiation intensity, indicating the level of detail it can discern in the captured data. Temporal resolution denotes a specific time interval in which the satellites can complete their movement in the orbital plane and revisit the same place after that certain period. Space-based platforms like satellites and airborne vehicles such as aircraft or UAVs commonly utilize passive sensors, meaning they rely on external light sources rather than generating their own energy. Nevertheless, certain satellites like ERS are equipped with active sensors, specifically the active microwave sensor. Green Seeker and Crop Circle are examples of ground-based platforms equipped with active sensors, and they are integral components of variable rate applicators. As fluctuations in daylight or sunlight intensity minimally affect the reflectance values measured by these active sensors, they can furnish more precise and consistent vegetation indices (VI) (Table 1) enhancing the assessment of crop nutrient status effectively (Sishodia et al., 2020).

### Visual Digital or Panchromatic Sensors

A digital camera serves as an excellent illustration of a visual sensor, featuring a color filter array like the Bayer pattern, which overlays a chip made up of silicon. In this setup, each pixel is surrounded by two different colors from the filter array (Hunt Jr and Daughtry, 2018). To create an image, the raw image file needs to undergo demosaicing to extract the three primary spectral bands, specifically the RGB bands representing Red (R), Green (G), and Blue (B), i.e., the panchromatic bands. Typically, the camera implements several adjustments such as correcting sensitivity for light, white balancing, removing noise from the dark coloured pixel, etc., to produce an image that is visually appealing to human trichromatic vision.
