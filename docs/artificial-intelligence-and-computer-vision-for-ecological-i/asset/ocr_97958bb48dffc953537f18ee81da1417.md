# Cost and Resource Constraints

The adoption and utilization of RS technologies in agriculture can be hindered by cost and resource constraints. Acquiring high-resolution imagery and advanced RS products can be costly, particularly for small-scale farmers or organizations with limited budgets (Wu et al., 2023). Furthermore, specialized software and hardware for data processing, analysis, and visualization can be expensive and require regular upgrades or maintenance (Kalinaki et al., 2023). Skilled personnel with expertise in RS, image analysis, and agricultural applications can be scarce and costly to hire and retain, especially in resource-constrained regions. Limited access to computing infrastructure, such as high-performance computing clusters or cloud-based platforms, can also hinder the ability to efficiently process and analyze large volumes of RS data.

# Technical and Operational Challenges

RS in agriculture involves various technical and operational challenges. Data processing and analysis often require significant computational resources and specialized software, which can be resource-intensive and challenging to maintain, especially in remote or rural areas with limited infrastructure (Kalinaki et al., 2023). Integrating RS data with other sources of information, such as weather data, soil data, and agricultural management practices, is crucial for comprehensive decision-making but can be complex and require sophisticated data fusion techniques (Ahmad et al., 2022). Automating the detection and classification of crop types, growth stages, and stress conditions from RS data is another challenge attributed to the variability in crop characteristics and environmental conditions (Wu et al., 2023). Additionally, operational challenges, such as timely data acquisition, processing, and information dissemination to farmers, are complex and require robust workflows and communication channels (Hariri and Goli, 2024).

# Scalability and Generalization

RS techniques developed for specific agricultural regions or crop types may not be easily scalable or generalizable to other areas or crops. The diversity of farming practices, environmental conditions, and crop characteristics across different geographic regions can pose significant challenges in applying RS methods universally (Barbosa Júnior et al., 2024). Factors such as soil types, climate patterns, and farming practices can vary substantially, requiring adaptations or new approaches to utilize RS data effectively. Additionally, the transferability of algorithms and models trained on specific datasets to new regions or crop varieties can be limited, leading to potential inaccuracies or biases in the analysis (Rushák et al., 2023).
