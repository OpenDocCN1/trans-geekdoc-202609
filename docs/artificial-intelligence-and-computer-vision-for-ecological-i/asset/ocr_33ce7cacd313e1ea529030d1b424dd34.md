# Multispectral Sensors

Multispectral sensors generally feature wide bands with large wavelength ranges, typically spanning from 50 to 100 nanometres in width. Multispectral remote sensing sensors employed in agriculture are specialized instruments designed to capture data across specific bands of the electromagnetic spectrum relevant to agricultural applications. These sensors typically collect information in several predetermined spectral bands, such as visible, near-infrared, and sometimes infrared wavelengths. By analyzing the data captured by these sensors, farmers and researchers can gather insights into various agricultural parameters, including crop health, vegetation vigor, moisture levels, and nutrient content of the soil. A multispectral sensor with narrower wavelength bands can offer an enhanced understanding of vegetation conditions by computing vegetation indices, such as the red-edge band responsible for chlorophyll absorption. This capability enables the multispectral sensor to provide reliable insights into the chlorophyll content of plants, similar to hyperspectral sensors (Hunt Jr and Daughtry, 2018). Some multispectral sensors such as Landsat, SPOT, and Sentinel-2 carried on satellite-based platforms have been employed in agricultural applications. After the year 2000, several other spacecraft like GeoEye-1 SkySat-2, and Superview-1 were launched in the year of 2008, 2014, and 2018 respectively. These are examples of nanosatellites and other spacecraft capable of capturing high-resolution multispectral images with a resolution of 2 meters or less. These satellites can be revisited daily or even less frequently.

# Hyperspectral Sensors

Hyperspectral sensors are furnished with narrow width, contiguous spectral bands, typically with a spectral width of 10–20 nm. As a result, hyperspectral sensors like Hyperion and spectroradiometers (e.g., Headwall), which contain numerous or even hundreds of bands, are capable of capturing hyperspectral images and providing highly detailed spectral responses of various objects. This capability surpasses that of multispectral images, enabling hyperspectral sensors to more effectively detect detailed variations in surface covers and their changes over time. Therefore, it enables more precise and prompt identification of crop physiological conditions and vegetation parameters, including leaf area index (LAI) estimation, discrimination between crop types, retrieval of crop biomass, and assessment of leaf nitrogen content (Sun et al., 2017).
