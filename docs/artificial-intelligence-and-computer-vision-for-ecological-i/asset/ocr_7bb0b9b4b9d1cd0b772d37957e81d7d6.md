Remote sensing sensors pick up these changes in visible and near-infrared (Vis-NIR) portions of the electromagnetic spectra, which causes alterations in plants’ spectral characteristics (West et al., 2003). The stressed plants generally show lower spectral reflectance values at the NIR area of EM spectra as compared to healthy vegetation, and thus such spectral response can able to differentiate healthy plants from stressed plants (Govaerts and Verhulst, 2010). In practical applications, utilizing disease-specific spectral disease indices (SDI) derived from remote sensing (RS) proves more effective for the accurate detection of plant diseases compared to conventional indexes of the vegetation (i.e., NDVI, DVI, etc.) and others (AL-Saddik et al., 2017). While remote sensing (RS) technology employing visual (RGB), multispectral, and hyperspectral sensors has demonstrated effectiveness in disease monitoring, achieving early disease detection remains challenging. Consequently, experiments have revealed that thermal and fluorescence-based RS hold promise, particularly in early disease detection when symptoms are not yet apparent in plant parts. This potential is attributed to the tendency of diseased plants to exhibit elevated temperatures compared to healthy ones (Mahlein, 2016).

# Crop Yield Prediction

In the current context, crop yield is typically assessed and mapped using a yield monitor attached to a combine harvester, which usually consolidates yield data from numerous crop rows during harvesting (Bern et al., 2019). However, low resolution is a defining feature of these yield maps. Hence, remote sensing imagery integrated with various machine learning-based models has been utilized to create high-resolution yield maps, allowing for the evaluation of yield variations within fields and across landscapes (Khanal et al., 2018). This enables a more comprehensive understanding of the field’s overall agricultural production. The RS data is commonly employed to predict crucial crop biological and physical factors (i.e., Leaf Area Index ‘LAI’; biomass; etc.) which serve as essential indicators of crop health and development across various crops (Ali et al., 2020). These data are integrated into yield forecasting models to facilitate timely and accurate yield prediction.

# Crop Residue Management

Efficient management of crop residues holds significant importance in different regions such as India, whereas residue burning is common across small to large-scale farm holdings. This practice aims to enhance soil quality and crop productivity while mitigating the adverse environmental effects caused by excessive burning of crop residues in the fields (Shah et al., 2017). Many studies have illustrated the capability of both multispectral and hyperspectral RS techniques to accurately map the spatial distribution of plants’ residues in the site (Galloza et al., 2013; Sharma et al., 2016). Sharma and colleagues 2016 developed RS indexes (i.e., normalized difference tillage index ‘NDTI’ which is a multispectral index that can be applied to Landsat ETM+ and OLI imageries). Their findings indicated that NDTI could effectively distinguish the degree of residue cover associated with several practices of the tillage process in various crop fields located in a part of the USA (Sharma et al., 2016).
