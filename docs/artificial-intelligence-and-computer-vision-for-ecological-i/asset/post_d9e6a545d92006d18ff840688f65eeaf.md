## Multispectral Sensors

Multispectral sensors generally feature wide bands with large wavelength ranges, typically spanning from 50 to 100 nanometres in width. Multispectral remote sensing sensors employed in agriculture are specialized instruments designed to capture data across specific bands of the electromagnetic spectrum relevant to agricultural applications. These sensors typically collect information in several predetermined spectral bands, such as visible, near-infrared, and sometimes infrared wavelengths. By analyzing the data captured by these sensors, farmers and researchers can gather insights into various agricultural parameters, including crop health, vegetation vigor, moisture levels, and nutrient content of the soil. A multispectral sensor with narrower wavelength bands can offer an enhanced understanding of vegetation conditions by computing vegetation indices, such as the red-edge band responsible for chlorophyll absorption. This capability enables the multispectral sensor to provide reliable insights into the chlorophyll content of plants, similar to hyperspectral sensors (Hunt Jr and Daughtry, 2018). Some multispectral sensors such as Landsat, SPOT, and Sentinel-2 carried on satellite-based platforms have been employed in agricultural applications. After the year 2000, several other spacecraft like GeoEye-1, SkySat-2, and Superview-1 were launched in the years 2008, 2014, and 2018, respectively. These are examples of nanosatellites and other spacecraft capable of capturing high-resolution multispectral images with a resolution of 2 meters or less. These satellites can be revisited daily or even less frequently.

## Hyperspectral Sensors

Hyperspectral sensors are furnished with narrow width, contiguous spectral bands, typically with a spectral width of 10–20 nm. As a result, hyperspectral sensors like Hyperion and spectroradiometers (e.g., Headwall), which contain numerous or even hundreds of bands, are capable of capturing hyperspectral images and providing highly detailed spectral responses of various objects. This capability surpasses that of multispectral images, enabling hyperspectral sensors to more effectively detect detailed variations in surface covers and their changes over time. Therefore, it enables more precise and prompt identification of crop physiological conditions and vegetation parameters, including leaf area index (LAI) estimation, discrimination between crop types, retrieval of crop biomass, and assessment of leaf nitrogen content (Sun et al., 2017). Various platforms including space-based (satellite), airborne (UAS), and ground can be used as bases on which hyperspectral sensors can be installed, to effectively capture images having different resolutions including spatial and temporal resolutions. The space-based hyperspectral sensor Hyperion is predominantly used in agricultural applications. With its 30-meter spatial resolution and 10-nanometer spectral resolution, Hyperion collects data across a broad spectrum, including visible, near-infrared, and shortwave-infrared regions, making it highly effective for agricultural applications (Lu et al., 2020). The first airborne hyperspectral sensor, the Airborne Visible/Infrared Imaging Spectrometer (AVIRIS), introduced in 1987, had 224 bands spanning from visible to shortwave infrared. It was used for various agricultural applications, including assessing vegetation properties like canopy area, chlorophyll, and water content, as well as analyzing soil characteristics (Estep et al., 2004). Compact hyperspectral imaging sensors such as the Headwall company’s Micro and Nano Hyperspec VIS-NIR sensors, UHD 185-Firefly, and HySpex VNIR, which feature hundreds of bands covering a broad spectral range (VIS-NIR), can be mounted on UAVs for use in agricultural and forestry tasks (Saari et al., 2011). In recent times, close-range hyperspectral imaging sensors (HySpex hyperspectral camera, hyperspectral imaging scanner of Pika XC, etc.), comprising ground or laboratory-based systems, have become increasingly popular due to their ability to capture hyperspectral imagery at super-high spatial resolutions, reaching levels as fine as centimeters or even sub-centimeters. This capability enables the investigation of fine-scale vegetation features, such as those at the canopy stages, thereby significantly helping to perceive initial symptoms of vegetation stress conditions, such as disease incidence, weed infestation, or nutritional deficits (Malmir et al., 2019).

## Thermal and Microwave Sensors

Nowadays, advanced satellites are equipped with two additional sensors—thermal infrared sensors and microwave sensors—whose use in agriculture is increasing daily because of their longer wavelengths. Thermal infrared sensors can detect the thermal energy emitted by target objects such as crops or soils. The temperature data collected by these sensors can then be used to estimate various parameters, including water stress conditions in both soil and plants, the rate of evapotranspiration (ET), and the water requirements for irrigation (Khanal et al., 2017). Thermal sensors yield data comprising a blend of canopy, shadow, and bare soil temperatures. To assess water stress, compare the canopy temperature of crops with that of well-irrigated crops (Hunt Jr and Daughtry, 2018). However, thermal sensors are typically either precise yet costly or imprecise yet affordable. As a result, their application in agriculture is relatively limited compared to other types of sensors.

Microwave sensors function like thermal sensors in measuring the emitted energy and/or backpropagated pulse from the land surface, albeit using longer microwave wavelengths. Microwave sensors are primarily utilized for estimating soil moisture levels and crop water usage across extensive regions. One notable advantage they offer over other sensor types is their ability to operate day and night and under any weather conditions, as microwaves can penetrate through clouds (Palazzi et al., 2019).
