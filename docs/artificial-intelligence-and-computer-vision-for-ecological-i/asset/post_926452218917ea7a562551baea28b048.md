The process of field sampling involves gathering water samples by hand from different depths and places. These samples are then subjected to laboratory analysis utilizing methods like spectrophotometry, chromatography, and titration. These techniques offer accurate measurements of water quality indicators such as microbiological content and chemical concentrations, but they are generally restricted in terms of their temporal and spatial coverage, necessitating a large investment of both financial and human resources.

On the other hand, contemporary methods make use of cutting-edge technologies to improve the effectiveness, scope, and real-time capabilities of water quality evaluation. For example, automated in situ sensors are placed in water bodies to continuously check for elements including temperature, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, and pH. Because these sensors give real-time data, fewer human samplings are required, and problems with water quality can be identified right away. Observing long-term, large-scale changes in water quality through satellite imaging and aerial photography is another way that remote sensing and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) broaden the scope of monitoring. The integration of spatial and temporal data made possible by GIS technology enables thorough study and visualization of pollution sources and trends over large geographic areas.

Another advancement is the incorporation of machine learning (ML) and AI into the evaluation of water quality. Large datasets can be analyzed by AI algorithms, which can then be used more effectively and accurately than traditional statistical approaches to find patterns, forecast trends, and spot abnormalities. Trained on previous data, machine learning models are able to predict future water quality conditions, offering useful insights for proactive management.

Water quality testing is made more thorough and efficient by combining old techniques with new technology. For specific pollutants and biological assessments in particular, traditional approaches continue to be crucial for getting accurate and comprehensive results. Modern methods, on the other hand, provide larger temporal and spatial coverage, allowing for large-scale, ongoing monitoring. Together, these techniques provide a more comprehensive understanding of water quality, facilitating improved decision-making and water resource management. In addition to improving assessment efficiency and accuracy, this integrated approach aids in the creation of more effective policies and interventions that preserve and protect aquatic habitats.

## Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

### Basics of AI and Machine Learning

AI is the term used to describe how technology, especially computer systems, can simulate human intelligence processes. Learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and language comprehension are some of these processes. AI can be divided into two categories: broad AI, which can carry out any intellectual work that a human can, and narrow AI, which is made to accomplish a specific task (like facial recognition or internet searches). Machine learning (ML) is a subfield of AI that focuses on creating statistical models and algorithms that let computers analyze, forecast, and make decisions based on data (Singh et al., 2024).

The ability of models to get better at a task via practice, or by processing more data, is what defines machine learning. Three primary categories of machine learning algorithms exist: reinforcement learning, unsupervised learning, and supervised learning. A labeled dataset is used to train the algorithm in supervised learning, where each training example has an output label associated with it. The programme is supposed to be able to anticipate new, unobserved data with accuracy (Singh et al., 2023). The objective of unsupervised learning is to find underlying structures or hidden patterns in the input data by training on data without labeled replies. By rewarding good behaviors and penalizing undesired ones, reinforcement learning allows an algorithm to learn the best course of action through trial and error.

A kind of machine learning known as “deep learning” has become a potent tool in the field of artificial intelligence. It uses multi-layered neural networks, hence the name “deep” learning. Because these neural networks are made to resemble the structure and operation of the human brain, they can analyze and learn in complicated ways from vast amounts of data. Deep learning is especially helpful for applications like picture recognition, natural language processing, and speech recognition because it performs well on tasks involving unstructured data, such as text, audio, and photos (Singh et al., 2024). While recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are better suited for sequential data analysis, such time series or language modeling, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), a sort of deep learning model, are especially good at image-related tasks. Deep learning’s progress has greatly improved AI systems’ capabilities, enabling them to achieve previously unheard-of levels of accuracy and efficiency across a wide range of applications. Deep learning and AI have the potential to revolutionize many industries, including environmental sciences, as computing power and algorithmic complexity increase.

## Applications of AI in Environmental Sciences

There are now more options for tracking, controlling, and reducing environmental problems thanks to the use of AI in environmental sciences. AI finds widespread use in environmental monitoring, where algorithms analyze massive amounts of data from several sources to spot patterns and abnormalities. AI is capable of analyzing remote sensing data and satellite pictures, for instance, to track the health of coral reefs, monitor deforestation, and identify illicit fishing activities, some of the applications are depicted in Fig. 3. By using past pollution data and climatic variables, machine learning algorithms can forecast air quality levels and issue early warnings for days with dangerously low air quality (Zhong et al., 2021).

Artificial Intelligence is revolutionary in the field of water quality evaluation. AI systems can continuously monitor water quality indicators including pH, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, and the presence of pollutants by analyzing data from in situ sensors, satellite photos, and historical records (Gupta et al., 2021). Proprietary analysis techniques may overlook some patterns and correlations in the data, but machine learning algorithms can, making it possible to anticipate changes in water quality more precisely. For example, by examining environmental variables like temperature, light availability, and nutrient levels, AI models can predict dangerous algal blooms. This makes it possible to protect aquatic ecosystems and public health in time.

Resource management optimization is a key area in which artificial intelligence is being used in environmental sciences. By evaluating crop requirements, soil moisture content, and weather predictions, AI systems can optimize the use of water resources in agriculture by suggesting effective irrigation schedules. This increases agricultural yields while simultaneously conserving water. AI is used in wildlife conservation to examine data from acoustic sensors, drones, and video traps to track the movements of endangered species and keep an eye on animal populations (Zhong et al., 2021) (Sanchez et al., 2022). AI facilitates the creation of efficient conservation methods and mitigates the loss of biodiversity by processing massive datasets efficiently and accurately.

To sum up, artificial intelligence and machine learning have emerged as essential instruments in the field of environmental sciences, providing robust approaches to the observation, evaluation, and administration of natural resources. AI has the potential to handle enormous volumes of data, identify patterns, and make predictions. These capabilities will help us better understand complex environmental systems and respond to environmental concerns. The application of AI technology in environmental sciences is anticipated to grow as it develops, making a greater contribution to the sustainability and preservation of our natural world (Jain et al., 2020).
