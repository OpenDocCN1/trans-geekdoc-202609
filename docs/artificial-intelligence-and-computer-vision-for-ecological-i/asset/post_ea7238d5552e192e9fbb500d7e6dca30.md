## Historical Overview

The integration of remote sensing technology into agriculture has a rich historical background marked by significant milestones and advancements. Early endeavours in remote sensing for agricultural purposes can be traced back to the 1960s and 1970s, with the launch of pioneering satellite missions such as Landsat. Landsat-1, launched in 1972, provided the first multispectral imagery of Earth’s surface, laying the foundation for land cover mapping and crop monitoring (Campbell and Wynne, 2011). Subsequent Landsat missions, including Landsat-2 and Landsat-3, continued to contribute valuable data for agricultural applications, albeit with limited spectral and spatial resolution capabilities.

The 1980s witnessed a paradigm shift in remote sensing technology with the launch of more advanced satellites like SPOT (Satellite Pour Observation de la Terre) by France and the launch of the first synthetic aperture radar (SAR) satellite, Seasat, by NASA. These satellites offered higher spatial resolution and specialized sensors, enabling more detailed observations of agricultural landscapes, crop types, and vegetation health (Merchant, 2000).

The 1990s marked a period of significant technological advancement and diversification in remote sensing applications for agriculture. The launch of the Advanced Very High-Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) on NOAA satellites provided global coverage for monitoring vegetation dynamics, land surface temperatures, and drought conditions (Justice et al., 1998). Additionally, the development of hyperspectral imaging sensors allowed for more precise spectral analysis of agricultural fields, leading to improved crop health assessments and disease detection capabilities (Thenkabail et al., 2000).

In the 21st century, the proliferation of commercial satellite providers, along with advancements in unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) or drones, revolutionized the accessibility and affordability of high-resolution remote sensing data for farmers and agricultural researchers. The launch of satellites with multispectral and hyperspectral sensors, such as Sentinel-2 and WorldView series, further enhanced the capabilities of remote sensing in agriculture, enabling detailed crop monitoring, soil analysis, and precision farming practices (Yang et al., 2018).

Overall, the historical evolution of remote sensing in agriculture reflects a trajectory of continuous innovation and technological refinement, from early satellite missions to the current era of high-resolution multispectral and hyperspectral imaging, empowering stakeholders in the agricultural sector with unprecedented insights for sustainable and efficient farming practices.

## Principles of Remote Sensing in Agriculture

Remote sensing in agriculture relies on the fundamental principles of electromagnetic radiation, sensor technology, and data interpretation to gather and analyse information about agricultural landscapes. The following principles are key to understanding how remote sensing is applied in agricultural contexts:

- 1. **Electromagnetic Spectrum:** Remote sensing sensors detect electromagnetic energy produced or absorbed by entities on the globe's surfaces. Different wavelengths within the electromagnetic spectrum provide unique information about agricultural features such as vegetation health, soil properties, and water content (Merchant, 2000).
- 2. **Sensor Technology:** Remote sensing platforms, including satellites, drones, and airborne sensors, carry specialized instruments such as multispectral and hyperspectral cameras, thermal sensors, and radar systems. These sensors capture data across various spectral bands, allowing for the analysis of specific agricultural parameters (Thenkabail et al., 2000).
- 3. **Spectral Resolution:** The spectral resolution of remote sensing instruments refers to their ability to distinguish between different wavelengths of electromagnetic radiation. Higher spectral resolution enables detailed analysis of vegetation indices, soil composition, and other agricultural variables (Campbell and Wynne, 2011).
- 4. **Spatial Resolution:** Spatial resolution enhances the amount of detail obtained in remote sensing images. High spatial resolution is crucial for identifying individual crops, monitoring field boundaries, and assessing crop health at a localized level (Merchant, 2000).
- 5. **Temporal Resolution:** Temporal resolution refers to the frequency at which remote sensing data is acquired over time. Regular data collection allows for monitoring seasonal changes, crop growth cycles, and dynamic agricultural processes (Campbell and Wynne, 2011).

## How Remote Sensing Works

Satellite imagery is a powerful tool for acquiring data regarding entities or places from far away, usually by aviation or spacecraft. The process involves several key steps, each critical to acquiring, processing, and interpreting the data needed for various applications, including agricultural mapping. Here’s an overview of how remote sensing works:

- 1. **Energy Source and Illumination:** Remote sensing relies on electromagnetic energy, usually sunlight or emitted radiation, as the primary source. The energy interacts with the Earth’s surface and is either reflected, absorbed, or transmitted. Active remote sensing systems, such as radar and LiDAR, emit their energy and measure its reflection from the target (Yang, 2018).

- 2. **Interaction with the Target:** The energy interacts with the objects on the Earth’s surface. Different materials reflect, absorb, and emit energy differently across various wavelengths of the electromagnetic spectrum. Vegetation, for example, has distinct reflectance properties in the visible and near-infrared regions, which are critical for assessing plant health (Thenkabail et al., 2011).
- 3. **Detection and Recording:** Sensors onboard satellites, aircraft, or drones detect the reflected or emitted energy. These sensors can be passive (measuring natural energy) or active (emitting energy and measuring the return). They record the energy in multiple spectral bands, creating multispectral or hyperspectral images (Campbell and Wynne, 2011).
- 4. **Transmission and Reception:** The collected data is transmitted to ground stations for processing. Modern satellites often use high-speed data links to ensure timely delivery of the large volumes of data they collect (Lillesand et al., 2015).
- 5. **Processing and Analysis:** The raw data received is processed to correct for distortions and extract meaningful information. This includes radiometric correction (to adjust for sensor noise and atmospheric effects), geometric correction (to align images with geographical coordinates), and spectral analysis (to identify specific features based on their spectral signatures) (Yang, 2018).
