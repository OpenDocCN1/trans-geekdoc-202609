## Applications of RS in Precision Agriculture: Current Progress

The following section includes the exploration of the diverse applications of RS technologies in agriculture, spanning from field planning to harvest. These applications encompass tasks such as generating field topographical maps, mapping soil moisture levels, managing water resources, monitoring pests and diseases, predicting crop yields, managing crop residues, and numerous other applications.

### Field Topographical Maps Generation

Topographical characteristics of a field, including slope and elevation, significantly influence preseason management decisions. These decisions involve determining the optimal timing of field activities and practices, selecting appropriate crop types, and calculating rates of seed planting. Hence, accurately defining field topography using Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) is essential. This delineation can be achieved through grade surveying by utilizing the Global Positioning System (GPS) instrument, which is integrated into farm machinery, and remote sensing (RS) imagery (Khanal et al., 2020).

### Soil Moisture Map Preparation

Preparation of a high-resolution field map of soil moisture is highly useful for taking management choices including crop sowing or transplanting, and particularly in irrigation scheduling for the survival of crops. Optical, thermal, and microwave types of remote sensing data are extensively utilized worldwide for determining soil moisture (Verstraeten et al., 2008). Optical and thermal data commonly employ methodologies like the “triangle” or “trapezoid” method, as well as the land surface temperature-vegetation index (LST-VI) approach. These approaches rely on the physical contact of temperature on the land surface (also latent heat fluxes) and characteristics of vegetation areas to estimate and map soil moisture and evapotranspiration (ET). Microwave sensors gauge the dielectric characteristics of soil for estimating soil moisture levels, relying on either land surface emissivity or scattering. Multiple satellites equipped with microwave active and passive sensors, for instance, ‘Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer-Earth Observing System (AMSR-E)’; ‘Soil Moisture and Ocean Salinity (SMOS), Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP)’; ‘and Sentinel-1’. These examples of microwave sensors are utilized to monitor soil moisture purposes (Babaeian et al., 2018).

### Irrigation Water Management

Utilizing a suitable quantity of irrigation water at a suitable time is crucial in alleviating the water stress in growing crops and ensuring optimal growth and yield of these crops. Many farmers rely on their experience for field irrigation, while commercial farms may use soil moisture sensors to irrigate crops. However, neither of these methods accounts for the variability in soil moisture levels across the field during irrigation (Thompson et al., 2007; Holt et al., 2019). The RS data assist in identifying differences at the ground, enabling the implementation of variable-rate irrigation using conventional irrigation systems like center pivots. Remote sensing images, gathered at multiple intervals throughout a developing time, are utilized to assess different indicators related to the water consumption of the crops, including evapotranspiration, levels of moisture levels, and drought stress conditions of crops. Those factors are then employed in accurately calculate the demand for water and accordingly irrigation scheduling for the crops.

### Crop Nutrient Management

Promoting healthy crop development and gains while avoiding environmental hazards by preventing nutrient loss and contamination of both surface and groundwater resources requires making sure that fertilizer nutrients are applied to crops in suitable quantities and at the right times. However, soil nutrient levels exhibit variability in both spatial and temporal dimensions, as well as in terms of crop fertilizer requirements, owing to variations in soil conditions, topography, hydrological conditions, and activities of management (Hendricks et al., 2019). Mapping of spatial as well as temporal variations of those nutrients using conventional tools poses significant challenges for their applications in PA. Hence, the mapping of RS based on various indexes like difference vegetation index (DVI) and NDVI, holds promise for comprehending and delineating the spatial variability of nutrients. This is due to the notable correlation between vegetation indices and parameters such as plant chlorophyll, activity of the photosynthetic process, and the productivity of the growing crops. Certain commercially available remote sensors, like Green Seeker, Yara N-sensor, and Crop Circle, whether handheld or tractor-mounted, hold promise in measuring crop and soil spectral reflectance values. Subsequently, this data can aid in implementing spatially variable and real-time fertilizer applications (Ali et al., 2017). Remote sensing technology also assists in generating spatially variable or digital maps of the different nutrients of the soil such as the soil organic carbon, and available macronutrients (nitrogen, and phosphorus), facilitating tailored nutrient management at specific sites (Blasch et al., 2015; Kalambukattu et al., 2018). Malmir et al. (2019) utilized laboratory-based hyperspectral imagery acquired through a Pika XC2 hyperspectral camera mounted on a linear stage to accurately predict the soil macronutrients as well as the micronutrients, enabling site-specific soil nutrient management.

### Crop Disease Detection

Given the widely recognized detrimental impact of pest and disease infestations on crop yields and agricultural profits, early identification of diseases is essential to resist the spreading of these diseases and maximize economic returns. The conventional approach to disease monitoring, such as field scouting, proves ineffective in detecting pest infestations until visible symptoms appear on plant parts. Furthermore, this traditional method consumes time, is laborious, needs experienced hands, and is economically inefficient (Ehsani and Maja, 2013). Remote sensing technology effectively circumvents these limitations and enables the earlier detection and monitoring of disease infestations compared to conventional methods.
