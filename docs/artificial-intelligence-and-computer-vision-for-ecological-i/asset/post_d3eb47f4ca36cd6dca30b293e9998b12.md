## Factors Influencing Soil Moisture Variability

The variability of soil moisture is influenced by multiple factors, including soil texture, topography, crop cover, and irrigation practices. Soil texture, defined by the proportions of sand, silt, and clay, governs water retention and infiltration capacities. This classification is based on the composition of mineral particles within the soil, evaluated using diverse retrieval systems. Topography plays a crucial role in water distribution and movement: slopes facilitate runoff, while low-lying areas encourage water accumulation. The interaction of soil texture, topography, crop cover, and irrigation practices results in significant spatial and temporal variations in soil moisture (Hendrickx et al., 2024). The soil textural triangle provides a standardized method for classifying soils based on the heterogeneous sizes of soil particles.

## Soil Moisture Sensors and their Application in Agriculture

Soil water sensors (SWS) are among the most effective tools for implementing scientific irrigation scheduling (Brant et al., 2004). Accurate monitoring of soil water content is critical for precision agriculture, enabling optimized productivity, resource conservation, and environmental protection (Saleem et al., 2024). SWS estimates water availability through various methods, providing essential data for irrigation management, drought monitoring, and soil health assessment. In recent decades, a plethora of techniques have emerged to estimate volumetric soil water content (θv). These include direct methods like gravimetry and volumetry, while in indirect methods such as neutron scattering, tensiometers, frequency domain reflectometry (FDR) sensors, resistive sensors, time-domain reflectometry (TDR), and capacitance-type devices (Umarfarooque et al., 2011). These devices offer precise measurements of soil moisture levels, thereby supporting effective water management strategies. Soil moisture sensor placement and the optimum number of sensors are also important factors that significantly affect sensor-based surface irrigation automation, performance and cost-effectiveness. This section focuses on different types of soil moisture sensors, their principles of working and their respective strengths and limitations.

Gaikwad (2015) describes a variety of soil moisture sensors, each with its own set of characteristics and applications. The study introduced a potentiometric sensor that is affordable, lightweight, and probe size independent, making it ideal for on-site investigation. Kojima et al. (2016), developed a low-cost soil moisture profile probe utilizing thin-film capacitors and a capacitive touch sensor. Soil moisture content was measured through the capacitance changes in the capacitors, constructed from electrode probes serving as electrode plates. Yamamoto et al. (2002), presented sensors that utilize the thermal characteristics of soil and water, offering high sensitivity in low moisture concentrations and minimal ion effects. You et al. (2013) developed a handheld microwave reflectometer-sensor system for precise moisture monitoring in various soil types. Placidi (2020) focused on the experimental characterization of a low-cost capacitive soil moisture sensor, demonstrating its reliability in determining soil moisture for IoT applications. These sensors collectively provide a range of options for soil moisture analysis, each with its own strengths and potential applications. Different types of soil moisture sensors are discussed below.

### Tensiometers

Tensiometers measure the matric potential (also known as soil water tension or soil suction) of water in the soil. The matric potential indicates how tightly water is held in the soil, which is an indirect measure of the soil moisture content. The device simulates the function of a plant’s root system by assessing the ease with which a plant can absorb water from the soil. It comprises a stem filled with distilled water, a porous ceramic tip at the base, and a pressure/vacuum gauge at the top, allowing precise measurement of soil water potential. Manjhi and Sarkar (2019) reported in their study on soil moisture variations that tensiometers and soil moisture meters showed deviations of 8.51% and 5.45%, respectively, compared to the gravimetric method. These findings underscore the importance of accurate soil moisture measurement for effective irrigation scheduling and crop yield improvement. While these sensors are invaluable for irrigation management due to their direct measurement of soil water potential, they do have limitations, such as a restricted measuring range and the need for regular maintenance to prevent air entry.

### Capacitive Soil Moisture Sensors

Capacitive sensors measure the dielectric permittivity of soil by leveraging the higher dielectric constant of water compared to dry soil. These sensors typically consist of two metallic plates forming a capacitor (Eller and Denoth, 1996). Variations in soil moisture alter the dielectric permittivity of the soil, which in turn changes the capacitance between these plates (Mander et al., 2014). This principle enables capacitive sensors to gauge soil moisture levels accurately. The durability, power efficiency, and affordability of capacitive sensors make them suitable for widespread application. Their ease of use, reliable measurement results, and cost-effectiveness make capacitive sensors ideal for various purposes, including agricultural and environmental monitoring.

The underlying mechanism is based on the proportionality of capacitance to the relative permittivity (εᵣ) of the medium between the capacitor’s electrodes. Air has a relative permittivity (εᵣ, air) of approximately 1, whereas water has a relative permittivity (εᵣ, H₂O) of about 81. Consequently, the theoretical maximum capacitance change between air and water is 81-fold, highlighting the sensor’s sensitivity to changes in soil moisture content. Soil and sensor form a capacitor where the capacitance varies according to the water content present in the soil. The capacitance is converted into voltage level basically from 1.2V to 3.0V maximum.

Despite their relatively high cost, capacitive sensors are extensively employed in Decision Support Systems (DSS) due to their compatibility with small processor units such as Arduino and Raspberry Pi. Their cost-effectiveness and user-friendliness make capacitive sensors a preferred choice over other sensing technologies, particularly in small projects and primary agricultural practices. They provide a reliable, low-power, and economical solution for monitoring soil moisture, facilitating effective soil management in small-scale gardening and farming activities.
