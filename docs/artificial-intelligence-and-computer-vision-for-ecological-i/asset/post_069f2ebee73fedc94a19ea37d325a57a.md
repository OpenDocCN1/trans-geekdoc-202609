## Logistic Regression

Logistic regression is generally used for classifying soil moisture due to its probabilistic approach. Unlike linear regression, logistic regression calculates the probability of an input fitting to a specific class using a logistic function (sigmoid function), transforming predictions into probabilities ranging from 0 to 1. The logistic function transforms the regression output into class probabilities, making them interpretable. It estimates the relationship between input features and the log odds of the target variable through an iterative optimization algorithm, fitting the model to the data. It can handle binary classification (e.g., “dry” vs. “not dry”) and multi-class classification using extensions like multinomial logistic regression to predict probabilities for multiple categories (e.g., “dry,” “moist,” “wet”). One of the strengths of logistic regression is its interpretability. The model coefficients indicate the strength and direction of the relationships between input features and class probabilities, helping analysts understand which features are significant for classifying soil moisture levels (De Figueiredo et al., 2021).

However, logistic regression assumes a linear connection between input features and the log odds of the target variable, which might not be valid for complex datasets with nonlinear relationships. Thus, it might not perform well on complex data. Despite this, logistic regression is commonly used in agriculture for soil moisture classification, aiding land managers and farmers in irrigation scheduling and crop management by predicting the probability of different soil moisture levels. It helps interpret the factors influencing soil moisture, leading to better management strategies.

## Deep Learning

### Neural Networks

Deep learning, a subset of machine learning, includes neural networks (NNs) that process large, high-dimensional data for soil moisture prediction (Shen, 2018). Neural networks excel at uncovering hidden patterns and relationships that traditional machine learning models often miss (Rakshitkhajuria et al., 2018). For instance, feedforward neural networks can be trained on diverse input features to capture the processes generating soil moisture values (Zhou et al., 2023). Inspired by the human brain, neural networks are well-suited for deep learning tasks in remote sensing (Fig. 8). These architectures, comprising multiple layers, learn hierarchical representations of input features, such as temperature, humidity, soil type, and remote sensing indices, to predict soil moisture content. An important advantage of deep learning is its ability to automatically generate features from raw data, capturing subtle and nonlinear interactions. This is crucial in soil science, where environmental interactions are complex.

However, deep learning requires large volumes of labelled data, which can be difficult and costly to obtain in soil science. Additionally, deep learning models are computationally intensive, requiring substantial resources for training and inference. Deep learning shows promise in integrating with remote sensing data. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been applied to satellite imagery to extract spatial patterns relevant to soil moisture, enhancing the spatial resolution and coverage of predictions and enabling comprehensive monitoring across large areas.

Researchers are developing new architectures and hybrid models that combine deep learning with physical process-based models to leverage their strengths. Efforts to collect and annotate large-scale soil-moisture datasets are underway, supporting the training of robust models to capture soil-vegetation-atmosphere dynamics. Overall, deep learning holds great potential for soil moisture prediction due to its ability to find hidden relationships in large datasets. Neural networks and advanced architectures enable accurate, high-resolution estimations of soil water dynamics, providing valuable input for environmental management, effective agricultural practices, and further research and development.

Hachani et al. (2019) used Sentinel-1 SAR data and artificial neural networks (ANNs), incorporating auxiliary information like digital elevation models, local incidence angles, and NDVI. They combined satellite measurements with electromagnetic model simulation data (based on the IEM model) for robust, location-independent training, achieving a high correlation (R = 0.77, RMSE = 0.0184). Li et al. (2020) found that a basic feedforward three-layer ANN could simulate and predict soil moisture with lower computational costs and less reliance on prior conditions than traditional physical models. ANNs excel in predicting soil moisture due to their autonomous learning capabilities (Zhang, 2023). Hou et al. (2016) showed strong concordance between ANN predictions and real measurements using meteorological data. Li et al. (2018) used an adaptive genetic ANN model with atmospheric forcing data, significantly improving soil moisture predictions.

## Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are powerful tools for spatial data analysis, making them particularly effective for soil moisture evaluation through remote sensing images (Chouhan et al., 2024). CNNs automatically and adaptively learn spatial feature hierarchies from input images through layers of convolutions and pooling operations, capturing spatial patterns and relationships crucial for tasks like soil moisture estimation.

CNNs excel in identifying and extracting important features from remote sensing images, such as vegetation indices (e.g., NDVI) or soil reflectance properties correlated with soil moisture levels. By processing these images through multiple convolutional and pooling layers, CNNs detect minor changes in pixel intensities and spatial arrangements, capturing essential spatial information related to soil moisture dynamics.

A significant strength of CNNs is their ability to perform image segmentation, partitioning images into relevant sections. This capability allows CNNs to create detailed maps illustrating soil moisture distribution within landscapes, aiding researchers and land managers in decision-making for agriculture, hydrology, and environmental management. Li et al. (2020) proposed a Convolutional Neural Network (ICNN) method combining multi-polarization radar and multispectral data to improve soil moisture retrieval, achieving a correlation coefficient of 0.934 and an RMSE of 0.0145, without needing separate vegetation and soil roughness estimations. Similarly, Roberts et al. (2022) used CNNs with GNSS reflection measurements to complement soil moisture data from the SMAP Mission, improving spatial resolution and coverage, and showing a strong correlation with SMAP values.

## Recurrent Neural Networks

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, have emerged as powerful tools for handling time-series data, including soil moisture readings collected over time. Unlike classical feedforward neural networks that neglect the order of input features, RNNs are designed to exploit time dependency, making them ideal for sequences like soil moisture data. LSTMs, a type of RNN, excel at modeling long-range dependencies and retaining information for extended periods, which is crucial for predicting soil moisture levels based on historical data.

LSTM networks capture the temporal dynamics and seasonality inherent in soil moisture time series. They can identify patterns such as diurnal variations, seasonal trends, and long-term fluctuations, resulting in accurate future soil moisture predictions. Additionally, LSTMs are robust against irregular sampling intervals and missing values, common issues in environmental datasets, ensuring their performance in complex data scenarios. The LSTM networks involve several key processes. They handle sequential data, with each time step corresponding to a specific point in the data. LSTMs use memory cells to store information over long periods, maintaining historical context crucial for soil moisture prediction (Fig. 9).
