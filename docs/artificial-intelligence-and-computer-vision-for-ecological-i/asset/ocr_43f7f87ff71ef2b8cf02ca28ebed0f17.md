# RS Sensors

Remote sensing sensors vary according to the spatial, spectral, radiometric, and temporal resolution they provide. The spatial resolution of an RS-based sensor is determined by the pixel size of an image, which corresponds to a specific part on the ground. Sensors characterized by high spatial resolution typically have smaller paths, while those with larger paths usually exhibit lower spatial resolution. The number of bands present within a specific region of the EMR spectrum defines the spectral resolution of an RS-based sensor (Nowatzki et al., 2004). While multispectral sensors provide information across an inadequate number of spectral bands, hyperspectral sensors provide an extensive array of contiguous bands, often with narrow widths (< 20 nm), ranging from 10 to over 400 bands, such as those found in AVIRIS-NG sensors, enabling detailed object analysis (Teke et al., 2013). Various machine learning and deep learning methods, such as random forest and neural networks, are employed to decrease the dimensionality of hyperspectral data while preserving its original variability. This aids in extracting valuable insights about soil and vegetation conditions for modeling purposes (Chang et al., 2020). Radiometric resolution in remote sensing pertains to the sensor’s capability to detect and quantify minor variations in electromagnetic radiation intensity, indicating the level of detail it can discern in the captured data. Temporal resolution denotes a specific time interval in which the satellites can complete their movement in the orbital plane and revisit the same place after that certain period. Space-based platforms like satellites and airborne vehicles such as aircraft or UAVs commonly utilize passive sensors, meaning they rely on external light sources rather than generating their own energy. Nevertheless, certain satellites like ERS are equipped with active sensors, specifically the active microwave sensor. Green Seeker and Crop Circle are examples of ground-based platforms equipped with active sensors, and they are integral components of variable rate applicators. As fluctuations in daylight or sunlight intensity minimally affect the reflectance values measured by these active sensors, they can furnish more precise and consistent vegetation indices (VI) (Table 1) enhancing the assessment of crop nutrient status effectively (Sishodia et al., 2020).

# Visual Digital or Panchromatic Sensors

A digital camera serves as an excellent illustration of a visual sensor, featuring a color filter array like the Bayer pattern, which overlays a chip made up of silicon. In this setup, each pixel is surrounded by two different colors from the filter array (Hunt Jr and Daughtry, 2018). To create an image, the raw image file needs to undergo demosaicing to extract the three primary spectral bands, specifically the RGB bands representing Red (R), Green (G), and Blue (B), i.e., the panchromatic bands. Typically, the camera implements several adjustments such as correcting sensitivity for light, balancing in white colour, removing noise from the dark coloured pixel, etc., to produce an image that is visually appealing to humanoid trichromatic visualization.
