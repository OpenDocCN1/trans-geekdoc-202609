## Introduction

Soil is an essential component of life, serving as a reservoir for the nutrients and water that support us. Air, water, and life can all be found in the complex combination of minerals and organic components that makes up healthy soil. Soil moisture is an important component of the climate system. It is also known as soil water content and refers to the total amount of water, including water vapour, in the soil’s active layer. Depending on the kind of soil and vegetation, the total thickness of this active layer varies, although it is usually in the first 1–2 meters (Robock et al., 2000). In addition to being an important factor in agricultural water security, soil moisture is a reliable indicator of crop output in the future (Tian et al., 2019). The total amount of water storage within the soil is the most important factor in soil moisture. For such uses, further contemporary techniques are required, such as electronic sensors that provide an instantaneous measurement of soil moisture. The most extensive range of soil moisture sensors available for purchase nowadays is based on the soil medium’s electromagnetic sensing. These sensors fall into three categories: capacitance-type devices, TDR, and electrical resistivity (ER) (Heng et al., 2002; Jones et al., 2002). To achieve accurate data, these sensors need to be calibrated properly. Although the scientific literature provides a variety of methods for calibrating dielectric soil moisture sensors, their usage in field investigations is limited due to factors impacting calibration, the technical difficulties of calibration, and a shortage of confidence in their efficacy.

Compared to capacitance-based sensors, Time Domain Reflectometry sensors have been demonstrated to have greater accuracy and to work at higher frequencies (Walker et al., 2004; Robinson et al., 2008). On the other hand, capacitance sensors are far less expensive despite having inferior accuracy. This makes it possible to employ more instruments and, thus, denser networks. Considering the significant temporal and spatial variability of soil moisture at comparatively small scales (Teuling and Troch, 2005), it has been recommended that some applications would benefit from selecting less expensive but less accurate sensors to reduce the sampling error caused by spatial variability (Teuling et al., 2006; Bogena et al., 2007; Robinson et al., 2008).

Remote sensing technologies comprise radar (microwaves), satellite, and other non-contact approaches. The assessment of electromagnetic energy reflected or emitted from the soil surfaces is necessary to determine the soil moisture by using remote sensing. Depending on the temperature of the soil, the dielectric characteristics, or a mix of the two, the intensity of this radiation with soil moisture may change. As moisture content affects the dielectric constant, it is possible to use the attenuation of microwave energy in active radar to determine the moisture content of porous material. The visible/infrared hyperspectral (VIR/NIR) and thermal infrared (TIR) remote sensing techniques are used to measure the surface soil moisture content. The soil water content of surface soils, which can be categorized into warm and cool soils, with temperatures ranging from 2°C to 50°C and −2°C to −50°C, respectively, is measured using thermal infrared (TIR) sensors. Only warm soils allow for the measurement of soil moisture using thermal infrared (TIR) sensors. On the other hand, cool soil moisture is measured by visible/infrared hyperspectral (VIR/NIR) sensors (Gerhards et al., 2019).

Artificial intelligence in the form of Deep Learning/Machine Learning Technique utilizes algorithms to address problems without explicit programming. This procedure is frequently rapid and more efficient than conventional ones. New algorithms that can precisely forecast the moisture content of soil have been created using machine learning. These algorithms can subsequently be utilized for irrigation or other applications (Ahmad et al. 2010). Using remotely sensed data, machine learning and deep learning algorithms have been successfully deployed to predict soil moisture (Prasad et al., 2018). Using deep learning and machine learning techniques, several vegetation indices and spectral bands along with meteorological, soil, and crop factors could be used as input variables for determining soil moisture (Li et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2021). Artificial neural networks and convolutional neural networks are two examples of machine learning techniques that have been effectively applied for this purpose in recent years (Yinglan et al., 2022). It has been shown that these algorithms outperform conventional rule-based methods (Song et al., 2016). High-performance soil moisture sensors that are resistant to weather fluctuations can be created using this method. Farmers will be able to save money on irrigation water, which is a valuable resource in dry areas (Cai et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2019; Sarwar et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2022).

These days, technology is a huge part of so many facets of our life. Agriculture might seem to be exempt from that, as all it involves is planting seeds and watching them grow, but that is definitely not the case. Farming is not an easy task. The population’s exponential increase presents extremely dynamic challenges to maintaining food supplies. To feed more people, food must be produced in greater quantities. However, an increase in population also means that more buildings and water use will occur, depleting the precious land and water resources required for food production. With fewer resources, the agriculture sector must rely on technical advancements to feed this expanding population. Technological advancements like Genetic engineering, GPS, aerial imaging, herbicide tolerance, robotics, soil sensors, and precision agriculture are just a few examples that have transformed farming to a high-level industry. Both irrigation fields and gardens for plants depend heavily on the soil’s moisture content. Over the past ten years, many farmers have found that implementing remote sensors to monitor a field’s soil moisture content is an essential management tool. By using precision approaches, it helps to minimize fertilizer runoff from over-saturation, irrigate crops precisely when they need it, and lower input costs.

## Soil Moisture Measurement Methods

The procedure of measuring soil moisture is not easy, yet it is crucial for much of the research on water management, including scheduling irrigation. Numerous approaches have been put out, and each has pros and cons of its own. Some of the methods most commonly used are discussed below:

### Traditional Soil Moisture Measurement Methods
### Gravimetric or Oven Drying Technique

Currently, the most common practical approach for determining soil moisture content (θm) is the gravimetric method (Kashyap et al., 2021). All indirect methods are calibrated using this most natural and traditional approach. Using an auger, gather a sample of soil from the field and weigh it to determine its wet and dry contents. Weighing the soil sample (M_s) at the moment of sampling yields the moist weight, and drying the soil sample in an oven at 105°C for 24 hours yields the dry weight. The soil water (M_w) is represented by the weight loss.

θm = Mw / Ms

This technique guarantees an accurate assessment of the moisture content regardless of the type and salinity of the soil. Nevertheless, a repeated measurement is not possible due to the method’s destructive character (Zazueta et al., 1994). However, because of the labour and time-intensive techniques, it is not appropriate for large-scale or real-time field studies. Stated differently, the gravimetric approach-based measurements of soil moisture are not very practical for agricultural or hydrological applications where a large number of in situ observations are required on a regular basis, nor for validating remote sensing-based products that necessitate sampling within a limited time window during satellite passes (Fig. 2).

### Volumetric Method

The volume of water in a particular amount of dry soil, typically in 1 m³ is known as the volumetric water content. VWC can be computed using volume (cm³/cm³) or mass (g/g). This moisture content percentage is displayed for output. With this method, a soil sample is taken from the field using a core sampler of known volume at appropriate root zone depth. Its wet and dry weights are then determined using a procedure similarly to the gravimetric water content method. The amount of water in the sample is shown by the difference between the wet and dry masses of soil.
