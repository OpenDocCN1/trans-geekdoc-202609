Large-scale data are available through satellite imaging and remote sensing, which can record changes in water quality across wide areas both spatially and temporally.

To guarantee the acquired data’s quality and usefulness for analysis, preprocessing is a must. Preprocessing procedures for sensor data could involve calibration, noise reduction, and handling of missing readings. The accuracy and consistency of the sensor readings with accepted criteria are guaranteed by calibration (Taşan, 2023). Random oscillations and outliers in the data can be eliminated with the use of noise reduction techniques like filtering. With a view to ensure that the dataset is full for analysis, handling missing values requires the use of interpolation or imputation techniques to estimate the missing data.

Preprocessing for image-based data from drones and satellites entails multiple processes. These include normalization to standardize the data range, radiometric correction to correct the pixel values for atmospheric effects and sensor noise, and geometric correction to correct distortions caused by the perspective of the sensor (Al-Janabi and Al-Barmani, 2023). Techniques for image segmentation and enhancement, like contrast adjustment and edge recognition, are used to draw attention to essential characteristics and make key details easier to see. Preprocessing checks that the data is correct, clean, and prepared for AI and Computer Vision (CV) model analysis.

# AI Models for Water Quality Prediction

Predicting water quality is becoming more and more common with AI models, especially those that make use of machine learning and deep learning techniques. These models are capable of pattern recognition, in-depth dataset analysis, and future water quality forecasting. In water quality evaluation, supervised learning algorithms including random forests, decision trees, and support vector machines (SVM) are frequently employed for regression and classification tasks. Through training on historical data, these models discover the correlations between water quality indicators (such as pollutant concentrations and algal bloom occurrences) and input factors (such as temperature, pH, and nutrient levels) (Chhipi-Shrestha et al., 2023).

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are two examples of deep learning models that provide sophisticated processing capabilities for handling big, unstructured datasets. CNNs are especially good in identifying and categorizing characteristics linked to water quality, like turbidity levels or algal blooms, by analyzing spatial data, such as satellite photography (Iqbal et al., 2023). Because RNNs can handle sequential data, they are a good fit for time-series predictions. This means that water quality trends can be predicted using sensor data from the past.
