Despite this advantage, its use in precision agriculture is still limited due to its coarse spatial resolution, which hinders its suitability for specific agricultural operations at the field level (Stafford, 2000). Another drawback of satellite-based measurements is their reliance on weather and atmospheric conditions, which can result in the attenuation of electromagnetic signals traveling from the object to the satellite sensor (Rees, 2013). Despite encountering numerous challenges, satellite imagery remains effective for large-scale mapping of diverse phenomena, including mapping of land uses, and land cover, mapping of soil erosion, desertification evaluation, regional yield estimation, and monitoring climate change (Jafarabiglu and Pourreza, 2022). This effectiveness stems from its ability to offer a comparatively low-priced “macro” perspective of the terrain (Barnes, 2018). Advanced satellite systems with high resolution include a red-edge band to monitor crop growth and are specifically designed for agricultural applications. RapidEye data has been used to evaluate nitrogen levels in wheat crops cultivated in pre-defined management zones (Basso et al., 2013; Basso et al., 2016).

# Aerial Platforms

Unlike satellites, unmanned aerial systems (UAS) can capture and deliver high-resolution data, making them especially well-suited for agricultural operations in small and confined fields. However, the primary limitation lies in their restricted payload capacity and brief flight duration, hindering their extensive application. Conversely, manned aerial vehicles occupy an intermediate position, capable of covering larger areas than UAS and providing better resolution than satellites. In the 1920s, agrochemicals were applied to crops using manned aerial vehicles or piloted aircraft, marking the first use of airborne remote sensing in agriculture. As per Rees (2013), the commencement of analog aerial image capture and vegetation mapping began with the progress in aerial platforms such as airplanes and photographic equipment. During the 1970s, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in the United States emerged as a leading entity in utilizing aircraft for aerial remote sensing. They effectively mapped soil moisture for agricultural management by employing the P-3A aircraft, which was outfitted with various remote sensing sensors and operated at approximately 300 meters above ground level (AGL) (Jafarabiglu and Pourreza, 2022). Nevertheless, the utilization of aircraft remote sensing for research endeavors remains restricted, primarily owing to numerous drawbacks including the elevated expenses associated with sensors and platforms, requisite structure and skilled aviators, the operative intricacy of airlifts, and diminished reproducibility owing to the absence of detailed automatic pilot systems (Barnes, 2018). However, both private and governmental sectors have conducted research utilizing aerial platforms and sensors, yielding encouraging findings. While aerial photography provides excellent spatial resolution, its spectral resolution remains a concern due to the availability of only three basic bands: red (R), green (G), and blue (B).
