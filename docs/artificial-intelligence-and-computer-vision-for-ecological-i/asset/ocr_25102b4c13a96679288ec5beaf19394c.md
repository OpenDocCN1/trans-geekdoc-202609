Das and Paul (2016) discussed types of Microwave Remote Sensing and the factors that influence microwave remote sensing such as Dielectric constant (ε), Backscattering Coefficient (σ°), Surface Roughness, Bulk Density (ρb), Soil texture, Vegetation cover, Incident angle (⊓), Bands, Polarization. The study mentions algorithms and models such as empirical models, physical models, and data assimilation techniques that convert microwave signals into SM estimates and integrate data from multiple sensors and incorporate ancillary information (land cover, surface temperature) to enhance accuracy. The study emphasizes that SAR data is very beneficial to determine SM although both active and passive SAR sensors have advantages and disadvantages. Ongoing and future satellite missions, along with improved algorithms can enhance accuracy and reliability of SM data.

Zhang et al. (2014) addresses the estimation of surface soil water content (SSWC) using thermal remote sensing as traditional SM estimation methods have limitations, prompting the need for innovative approaches. The study area chosen was the central area of Spain and discusses the computation of SM index TRRVDI (Temperature Rising Rate Vegetation Dryness Index), and Theoretical Dry Edge is determined.

$TRRVDI = \frac{RT_{(i)} - RT_{wet}}{RT_{dry} - RT_{wet}}$

Ground based findings of SM were used to calibrate and verify the model. The study concludes with emphasizing on the potential of using temporal variations in LST, significance of Thermal Remote Sensing for monitoring and determination of SM content.

O’Neill et al. (1996) utilized a vegetation scattering model to estimate microwave backscatter comparable to radar measurements, employing the Peake (1959) formulation to express emissivity via bistatic scattering coefficients. This approach was applied to obtain SM using SAR data, validated through comparisons of modelled and measured radar backscatter. By inputting model-generated values for vegetation transmissivity (γ) and single scattering albedo (α) into equations along with field measurements, soil reflectivity was calculated, enabling SM estimation. The study practiced MACHYDRO ‘90 test in Pennsylvania, demonstrating the model’s effectiveness in accounting for vegetation effects on SM predictions. The estimated and measured SM in corn fields showed promising results on comparison, validating the model’s accuracy in practical field conditions (O’Neill et al., 1996).
