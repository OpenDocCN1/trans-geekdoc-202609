## Fundamental Principles of RS
### Definition and Key Concepts
RS is the science of acquiring information about the Earth’s surface without direct physical contact. It involves the use of sensors to detect and measure the physical properties of objects or areas from a distance. Key concepts in RS include electromagnetic radiation, sensors, platforms, and data processing.

### RS Platforms
RS platforms are vehicles or devices that carry sensors and collect data. The leading platforms used in RS for agriculture are satellites, UAVs (Unmanned Aerial Vehicles), and aircraft (Okengwu et al., 2023). Satellites orbit the Earth and provide global coverage, making them ideal for monitoring large agricultural areas (Steven and Clark, 1990; Victor et al., 2024). UAVs are smaller, uncrewed aircraft that can fly at lower altitudes, providing higher-resolution imagery for smaller areas. Aircraft, including manned and unmanned options, are used for more specific or targeted data collection (Victor et al., 2024).

### Types of Remote Sensing
RS can be categorized into two main types: active and passive. Active RS involves signal transmission from the sensor to the target and measuring the reflected or backscattered signal (Ullo and Sinha, 2021). Examples include radar and lidar systems, often used for terrain mapping and vegetation analysis. Passive RS relies on detecting natural energy reflected or emitted from the Earth’s surface (Khanal et al., 2020). Passive sensors measure radiation intensity in different wavelengths, such as visible, infrared, and microwave. Examples include optical sensors, which capture reflected sunlight to create images, and thermal sensors, which detect emitted infrared radiation to assess temperature (Victor et al., 2024). ## Key Principles of RS Technology
### Electromagnetic Spectrum
RS relies on the interplay between the Earth’s surface and electromagnetic (EM) radiation (Okengwu et al., 2023). The EM spectrum encompasses various energy waves, spanning from compact gamma radiation to expansive radio signals. Specialized detectors are engineered to capture distinct wavelength ranges within this spectrum (Khanal et al., 2020).

### Energy Interactions
As EM waves interact with Earth’s terrain, they may be absorbed, pass through, or bounce back. The degree of energy retention or reflection varies based on the characteristics of the surface material encountered (Victor et al., 2024).

### Spatial Resolution
This refers to the level of detail in an image. Higher spatial resolution means more detail can be seen in the image. Spatial resolution is determined by the sensor’s capabilities and the altitude of the platform (Karnawat et al., 2020).

### Temporal Resolution
Temporal resolution refers to how often a sensor can revisit the same area to collect data. Higher temporal resolution allows for more frequent monitoring of changes over time (Okengwu et al., 2023).

### Spectral Resolution
Spectral precision denotes a detector’s capacity to differentiate various EM wave frequencies (Ullo and Sinha, 2021). More spectrally sensitive instruments can extract finer details about the makeup and state of observed features or regions (Shanmugapriya et al., 2019).

### Radiometric Resolution
Radiometric resolution refers to the sensitivity of a sensor to detect variations in radiation intensity. Higher radiometric resolution allows for more subtle differences in the reflectance or emission of energy to be detected (Jiang et al., 2022).

## Overview of RS Platforms
RS platforms are the carriers or vehicles that house the sensors used for collecting data. These platforms can be classified based on altitude, coverage area, and operational capabilities.

### Satellite Platforms (Space-based)
Satellite platforms orbit the Earth and provide extensive coverage, making them ideal for large-scale agricultural monitoring. Different types of orbits, such as geostationary and polar orbits, offer unique advantages for RS (Khanal et al., 2020). Geostationary satellites remain fixed over a single point on the Earth’s surface, providing continuous monitoring of the same area, which is helpful for weather monitoring and tracking large-scale agricultural trends (Oymatov et al., 2023). On the other hand, Polar-orbiting satellites pass over the Earth’s poles and cover the entire globe as the Earth rotates. They provide high-resolution data and revisit the same area regularly, which is crucial for monitoring changes in agricultural fields over time. Examples of agrarian satellite platforms include the Landsat series, Sentinel-2, and MODIS, which avail crucial data for crop monitoring, soil assessment, and land use planning (Jiang et al., 2022).

### Aerial Platforms
Aerial platforms, including manned aircraft, offer high-resolution data and can be deployed flexibly (Tang and Shao, 2015). Manned aircraft are suitable for covering large areas quickly. They can be equipped with various sensors to capture detailed images and data, making them ideal for regional agricultural surveys and mapping projects.

### Ground-Based Platforms
Ground-based platforms include fixed installations and mobile systems that provide highly detailed and accurate data at specific locations. Fixed installations, such as weather stations and ground-based spectroradiometers, continuously monitor specific parameters like soil moisture, temperature, and crop growth (Bougettaya et al., 2022). These provide valuable ground truth data that can be used to validate and calibrate RS data from other platforms. Mobile systems, mounted on vehicles or handheld devices, can be moved to different locations to collect data on-demand. They are helpful for detailed field surveys and validate RS data with high precision. Ground-based platforms offer the highest level of detail and accuracy, making them indispensable for specific agricultural applications, such as soil analysis, crop scouting, and localized environmental monitoring (Li et al., 2023). Other ground-based platforms include UAVs or drones which are increasingly becoming popular for precision agriculture (Kattenborn et al., 2021). They can fly at low altitudes, capturing high-resolution images and data and providing spatial information about crop health and field conditions.
