An essential aerial remote sensing platform is AVIRIS (Airborne Visible InfraRed Imaging Spectrometer), and its latest iteration, AVIRIS-NG (AVIRIS-Next Generation), has recently been utilized in diverse applications including geology, ecological research, and agriculture (Lundeen and Gowey, 2021). AVIRIS is capable of capturing 224 consecutive calibrated spectral bands (ranging from 400 to 2500 nm) at varying altitudes across regions encompassing the United States, Canada, and Europe. On the other hand, AVIRIS-NG is a hyperspectral sensor equipped with 425 consecutive hyperspectral bands spanning the visible to near-infrared spectrum (ranging from 380 to 2500 nm), with spatial resolution varying from 3 meters to 18 meters. Recently, the AVIRIS-NG sensor has been extensively utilized in India, covering an area of 22,840 square kilometers across 57 sites during the initial phase of an airborne science campaign. This campaign took place over 84 days, from December 16, 2015, to March 6, 2016, as part of a joint initiative between the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) and NASA known as the HYperSpectral Imaging (HYSI) program. To back the campaign, organized fieldwork and on-site measurements were conducted at these sites, covering a range of topics such as crops, soil, forests, geology, coastal regions, oceans, river water, snow, urban areas, and beyond (Bhattacharya et al., 2019).

Given the drawbacks associated with manned aircraft, researchers are increasingly turning to unmanned aerial systems (UAS) for agricultural applications. Researchers have long documented the real strength of UAS for agrarian uses. Initially, UAS were primarily used for military purposes. Over time, their applications have expanded, and they are now widely utilized in the domain of precision farming. The use of Small UAS, commonly referred to as drones, weighing under 25 kg (55 lbs), began in agricultural operations in the early 2000s (Simpson et al., 2003). Its popularity surged rapidly due to its ability to provide high-resolution imagery, even at the centimeter scale. Eventually, in 2014, MIT’s Technology Review acknowledged it as one of the top ten innovative technologies (MIT Technology Review, 2014). The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) reported in March 2018 that by 2022, there will be 451,800 commercial drones, four times as many as there were 110,604 in 2017. The report also suggests that drone technologies are projected to generate services valued at $127 billion (Mazur et al., 2016). In addition to having high spatial resolution capabilities, farmers or growers can deploy drones as needed to survey fields regularly based on the intended temporal resolution, whether monthly, weekly, daily, or even hourly (Zhao, 2018). Moreover, drone-based remote sensing shows promise in offering comprehensive information on both inter and intra-field crop variability, aiding in the optimization of resource utilization based on the variability within fields. This includes assisting in irrigation scheduling, thereby enhancing water use efficiency (Barnes, 2018). The types of drones commonly used in agriculture include fixed-wing, single-rotor, and multirotor models. The vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) ability, along with the capability of staying in one place in the air without moving up or down rotary drones, have become highly popular in agriculture. However, concerns persist regarding flight endurance and energy efficiency.
