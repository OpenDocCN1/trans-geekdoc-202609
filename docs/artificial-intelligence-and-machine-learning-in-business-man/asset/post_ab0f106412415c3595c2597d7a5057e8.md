## INTRODUCTION

Advent of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) has ushered us into an era that was once considered part of science fiction. Today, these applications address problems in sectors like medicine, defence, education, transport, manufacturing, retail, and many more. AI technologies have drastically improved our capability to process large amounts of data generated in today's sensor-enabled world. Insights from this data have the potential to transform human lives for good. However, like any other technology, AI is bound to go through a learning curve where unintended outcomes might negatively affect recipients. The community today is not just limited to scientists, developers and engineers, but also involves all stakeholders who are impacted by these solutions: citizens, industries, governments, lawmakers. In order to promote trustworthy and responsible development of this technology for the greater good of society, it is important that all stakeholders are sensitized to various ethical aspects. This chapter aims to highlight such ethical issues and approaches currently being undertaken.

### 14.2.1 POWER IBALANCE

Use of AI solutions has been proposed as a solution to many world problems under labels like 'AI for good'. However, access to sophisticated AI applications and their impact on society is seriously plagued by differential power distribution in society. Thoughtful use and deployment of AI/ML applications can lead to further widening of the gap between less powerful and more powerful. This issue can be seen from two perspectives:

#### 14.2.1.1 Existing Power Imbalance in Funding Agencies and Organizations Developing AI Solutions

The entire process of conceptualization, design, training and deployment of sophisticated AI solutions is often very costly. Organizations require highly skilled data scientists, software engineers, researchers and UX designers for AI solutions capable of addressing practical needs satisfactorily. This human power is often accessible to large, heavily funded organizations and corporations that have these resources from across the world. Access to human resource in developing or underdeveloped countries is often difficult for smaller companies and non-profit organizations, and even academia is facing this issue as quality researchers are often recruited by private firms, creating a gap in resource-constrained academic research efforts. Such concentration of key human resource under the hands of few powerful agencies can influence the kind of AI solutions that get attention. The area of research and development in AI can get impacted and can generate more profit compared to applications that may have a greater impact on society but have less earning capacity. The recent incident of Google's ethics researcher Timnit Gebru's termination from the company on differences in research focus is an example of such a scenario.

The latest AI applications employ deep learning algorithms with multiple network layers to process a large amount of data in parallel. To handle heavy computing requirements, specialized chips called GPUs or Graphics Processing Units may be required. These GPUs are not only costly to build and produce, but they also consume large amounts of data required to handle intense algorithms.

Furthermore, the amount of data required to train such algorithms is also very high. Data collection, pre-processing, and cleaning of such data is another layer of cost that organizations have to bear. Investment of such large scale is also affordable to large corporations, while smaller, privately owned or not-for-profit organizations may not be able to afford these costs. All costs can make AI technology accessible to only a few powerful stakeholders, adding to their productivity and profits and in turn further widening the economic and digital divide in the society.

For example, in AI interventions in agriculture, farmers backed up by large corporations have sufficient funds and backup to experiment with such solutions. Even when these solutions may not be immediately successful, these farmers are able to contain the sunk cost, as they have adequate financial support from the industry.
