## High-performing nanocatalyst screened via machine learning and confirmed experimentally

Qionghua Zhou¹, Qiang Li¹, and Jinlan Wang¹,*

In this issue of Chem Catalysis, Han and co-workers combine first-principles calculations with a machine-learning technique to search ternary electrocatalyst for oxygen reduction reaction. The predicted promising PtFeCu nanoparticles were validated by experimental tests, showing both higher activity and more durable stability than commercial Pt/C.

The proton exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cells provide a promising way of energy conversion based on electrochemical reactions, and the key point is the development for active and stable catalysts for oxygen reduction reaction (ORR). Typically, the noble Pt catalysts take the lead in the commercial grade, and other non-noble catalysts are still in an early age.1,2 To improve the activity and stability and maximize the atomic efficiency, various Pt alloy have been proposed,3,4 and some binary nanoparticles have shown better ORR performance than that of pure Pt due to synergic effect.5 However, the diversity, multi-composition, and various ratio changes of the alloy systems can constitute a big database, which imposes a big challenge for traditional trial-and-error methods. Machine learning is a technique within materials design that has risen rapidly within the past few years, used especially in massive chemical space screening.6,7 It is based on statistics of data instead of quantum mechanical calculations, which makes it thousands of times faster than first-principles calculations. It has been successfully applied in catalyst design like hydrogen evolution reaction8 and nitrogen fixation.9 In spite of its success, the results from this state-of-art approach usually still need strict validations.

In this issue of Chem Catalysis, Han and co-workers apply machine learning to find active and durable ternary electrocatalyst for ORR.10 By performing first-principles calculations, they prepared 44,884 atomic local environments of a PtFeCu nanoparticle with different morphology, size, composition, and configuration. Based on these accurate data, atomistic interaction potential data was parametrized by neural network, which is a deep-learning algorithm. Using this potential, the authors explored the stable configuration of a PtFeCu nanoparticle through its shell structures and alloying compositions. The mapped ternary phase diagram shows that Pt composition should be greater than 0.8 in order to form a Pt-skinned shell, upon which ORR is catalyzed by the surface Pt sites; meanwhile, Fe and Cu atoms located at the core can induce strain effect to enhance the activity of the Pt. The catalytic performance is evaluated based on computational hydrogen electrode model (free-energy diagrams) and correlated with d-band theory. It is suggested that by mixing different sized elements in a nanoparticle, the strain field can modulate the d bands of the active centers and, thus, reach the optimal range of the volcano plot. Consequently, PtFe high Cu low models, which show proper binding energies with oxygenate intermediates, are proposed to present high activity for ORR. To validate the machine-learning predictions, four different compositions of alloying nanoparticles were synthesized and tested. Consistent with the theoretical predictions, the Pt0.78Fe0.15Cu0.07 (776 μA cm−2Pt and 0.67 A mg−1Pt) exhibits activities three times bigger and more specific than commercial Pt/C (248 μA cm−2Pt and 0.21 A mg−1Pt). In regard to the stability, the calculated segregation energies and the distribution of the alloying elements indicate that the limited amount of Cu can improve chemical stability. Again, the accelerated stress test (AST) also shows that the additional Cu enhances the durability of PtFeCu catalyst. Thus, the authors proposed that their ORR electrocatalyst Pt0.78Fe0.15Cu0.07 surpassed the 2020 US Department of Energy's target for both activity and stability.

This work shows a systematic study of theory-guided experiment and a good example of how machine learning helps optimization of the alloy ratio of nanocatalysts, not only for accelerating the screening of large chemical space but also for bridging the gap between atomic-scale calculations and real-scale experiments. It is especially critical for catalyst design because the reaction happens in atomic scale, and the observations are relatively macroscopic. In this work, the massive data brought by machine learning help the authors to map the complex relationship among composition ratio and configuration of three shells in the PtFeCu nanoparticle, thereby screening out the best region of the phase diagram. In addition, the detailed analysis clearly demonstrates that the hetero-elements play a critical role in the activity and stability of the designed catalysts, including surface strains and metal segregation. It can then be applied to other catalytic reactions on the promotion effect by multi-element alloying. With the help of the database, the structure-activity relationships can be analyzed by a large number of catalytic models, and the in-depth modulation mechanisms for electronic structures and catalytic activities can be unveiled. The successful approach of this work reveals a promising future of data-driven catalyst design.

## Organometallic reagents primed for peptide modification

Cecile Elgindy¹ and Lara R. Malins¹,*

In this issue of Chem Catalysis, Walczak and co-workers introduce a carbastannatrane alanine derivative that facilitates amino acid side-chain functionalizations via C-C, C-S, and C-Se bond formation using Stille cross-coupling reactions. Applications in late-stage peptide modifications and macrocyclizations further exemplify the value of this novel building block.

Elucidating the structural and biological consequences of post-translational modifications (PTMs) has led to an improved appreciation for structure-activity relationships of peptides and proteins as well as an expanded potential for their therapeutic application. However, given the broad functional diversity of PTMs, many of these modifications remain poorly understood, with current limitations marked by difficulties in replicating their highly selective, enzymatic installation using chemical methods. Ideally, such methods should be site selective and residue specific, utilize mild reaction conditions (ambient temperature and physiological pH), and be applicable to peptides and larger protein structures—a difficult task without the assistance of specialized enzymes. The appeal of efficient chemical approaches to both native and bespoke peptide modifications has fuelled considerable interest in the development of new methods.
