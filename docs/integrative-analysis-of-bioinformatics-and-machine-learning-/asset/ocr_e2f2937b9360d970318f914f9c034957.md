In recent years, revolutionary breakthroughs in immunotherapy have led to advances in lung cancer, owing to the increasing recognition and focus of tumor microenvironment (TIME), which contains tumor cells, mesenchymal cells, and others. These diverse components and their interactions through multiple ligand and molecular mechanisms further lead to their vital and multifaceted impacts in different phases of tumor, including tumorigenesis, development, progression, metastasis, and antitumor immunity.

Development of immunotherapy in biology has been hindered by the heterogeneity and unique characteristics of TIME or multicomic genetic expression profiles. This has facilitated precision medicine and identifies candidates that properly respond to immunotherapy, parameters are extracted from the entire data source or limited to small-sample spatial relationships among lung cell types.

Some of the aforementioned studies are those that evaluate spatial data, as they learned from information about treatment response and functional mechanisms. Thus, they are rarely applied to clinical practice.

Immunotherapy, specifically the use of checkpoint inhibitors (ICs) that block programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-L1) or programmed cell death protein 2 (PD-1) and T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), has improved the survival rates of patients with locally advanced or metastatic, non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC).

As the first-line treatment in the advanced/metastatic setting, clinical trials have shown that ICs may generate a sustained immune response and clinical efficacy. Patients in situ have high immune infiltration and relatively mechanisms for the activation of T cells to target tumor cells and metastases. Unfortunately, only few subsets of patients with LUAD benefit from ICs, due to de novo or acquired resistance.

Costimulatory molecules are made of B7-CD8 and tumor necrosis receptor family (TNF). The B7-CD8 family (CD80, CD86, PD-L1, PD-2, inducible T cell ligand (H-B17, H-B3a, and HERV-LR antigen 2) belonging to the B7 family (CD74, CTLA-4, inducible T cell checkpoint, PD-1, and TIMD2) belonging to the TNF family.

The family contains 19 molecules from the TNF ligand superfamily (TNSF) and 29 members from the TNF receptor superfamily. These costimulatory molecules provide signal for T cells, and participate in cell proliferation, differentiation, activation, and function. Hence, they serve as potential targets for current immunotherapy, which have been explored in prostate cancer and head neck squamous carcinoma.

However, PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4, the majority of molecules are still poorly understood in LUAD. The exploration of costimulatory genes in patients with LUAD is presented here.

We utilized the transcriptional profiles of patients with LUAD obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Gene Expression Omnibus database to classify tumor subtypes ('cold' and 'hot'). A series of bioinformatics and machine learning algorithms were used to establish a novel signature based on CMGs in patients with LUAD.

This performance was validated with internal and external cohorts. Moreover, a small-sample immunohistochemistry (IHC) explored the correlation between the expression level of identified CMG biomarkers and the response to immunotherapy.
