**Theorem 5.1.** Consider the converging Gibbs chain $\mathbf{x}_1 \Rightarrow \mathbf{h}_1 \Rightarrow \mathbf{x}_2 \h2 \dots$ starting at data point $\mathbf{x}_1$. The log-likelihood gradient can be

```
$$\frac{\log P(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta} = -\frac{\partial FreeEnergy(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta} + E\left[\frac{\partial FreeEnergy(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta}}\right] + E\left[\frac{\partial P(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta}}\right] (5.28)$
```

and the final term converges to zero as $t$ goes to infinity.

Since the final term becomes small as $t$ increases, truncating the chain to $k$ steps in the Markov chain, using the approximation-

```
$$\frac{\log P(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta} \simeq -\frac{\partial FreeEnergy(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta} + E\left[\frac{\partial FreeEnergy(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})}{\partial \theta}}\right]$
```

is exactly the CD-k update (Equation (5.27)) when we replace the expectation with a single sample $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}_{k+1}$. This tells us that the bias of CD-k is $E[\frac{\partial P(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})}{\partial \theta}]$. Experiments and theory support the idea that CD-k yields better and faster convergence (in terms of number of iterations) than CD$(k-1)$, due to smaller bias (though the computational overhead might not be worth it). although experiments show that the CD-k bias can be large when $k$ is small, empirically the update rule of CD-k still moves the model's parameters in the same quadrant as log-likelihood gradient [12]. This is in agreement with the good results that can be obtained even with $k=1$. An intuitive picture that may help to understand the phenomenon is the following: when the input example $\mathbf{x}_1$ is used to initialize the chain, each Markov chain step (to $\mathbf{x}_2$) tends to be in the right direction compared to $\mathbf{x}_1$, i.e., roughly going down the energy landscape from $\mathbf{x}_1$.
