
# Energy-Based Models and Boltzmann Machines

demonstrated by [12], shows how one can expand the log-likelihood gradient for any $t \ge 1$.

**Theorem 5.1.** Consider the converging Gibbs chain $\mathbf{x}_1 \Rightarrow \mathbf{h}_1 \Rightarrow \mathbf{x}_2 \to \dots$ starting at data point $\mathbf{x}_1$. The log-likelihood gradient can be

$$\frac{\log P(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta} = -\frac{\partial FreeEnergy(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta} + E\left[\frac{\partial FreeEnergy(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta}\right] + E\left[\frac{\partial P(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta}\right] (5.28)$$

and the final term converges to zero as $t$ goes to infinity.

Since the final term becomes small as $t$ increases, truncating the chain to $k$ steps in the Markov chain, using the approximation-

$$\frac{\log P(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta} \simeq -\frac{\partial FreeEnergy(\mathbf{x}_1)}{\partial \theta} + E\left[\frac{\partial FreeEnergy(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})}{\partial \theta}\right]$$

is exactly the CD-k update (Equation (5.27)) when we replace the expectation with a single sample $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}_{k+1}$. This tells us that the bias of CD-k is $E[\frac{\partial P(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})}{\partial \theta}]$. Experiments and theory support the idea that CD-k yields better and faster convergence (in terms of number of iterations) than CD$(k-1)$, due to smaller bias (though the computational overhead might not be worth it). although experiments show that the CD-k bias can be large when $k$ is small, empirically the update rule of CD-k still moves the model's parameters in the same quadrant as log-likelihood gradient [12]. This is in agreement with the good results that can be obtained even with $k=1$. An intuitive picture that may help to understand the phenomenon is the following: when the input example $\mathbf{x}_1$ is used to initialize the chain, each Markov chain step (to $\mathbf{x}_2$) tends to be in the right direction compared to $\mathbf{x}_1$, i.e., roughly going down the energy landscape from $\mathbf{x}_1$. Since the gradient depends on the change between $\mathbf{x}_2$ and $\mathbf{x}_1$, we tend to get the direction of the gradient

So CD-1 corresponds to truncating the chain after two samples (one from $\mathbf{h}_1$, and one from $\mathbf{x}_2|\mathbf{h}_1$). What about stopping after the first (i.e., $\mathbf{h}_1|\mathbf{x}_1$)? It can be analyzed from the following log-likelihood gradient expansion [12]:

$$\frac{\partial \log P(\mathbf{x}_1|\mathbf{h}_1)}{\partial \theta} = E \left[\frac{\partial \log P(\mathbf{x}_1|\mathbf{h}_1)}{\partial \theta}\right] - E \left[\frac{\partial \log P(\mathbf{h}_1)}{\partial \theta}\right]. (5.29)$$

Let us consider a mean-field approximation of the first expectation, and where instead of the average over all $\mathbf{h}_1$ configurations according to $P(\mathbf{h}_1|\mathbf{x}_1)$ by its average configuration $\mathbf{h}_1 = E[\mathbf{h}_1|\mathbf{x}_1]$, yielding:

$$\frac{\partial \log P(\mathbf{x}_1|\mathbf{h}_1)}{\partial \theta} \approx E\left[\frac{\partial \log P(\mathbf{x}_1|\mathbf{h}_1)}{\partial \theta}\right]. (5.30)$$

If, as CD, we then ignore the expectation in Equation (5.29) (incurring an additional bias in the estimation of the log-likelihood gradient), we then obtain the right-hand side of Equation (5.30) as an update direction, which is minus the gradient of the *reconstruction* error,

$-\log P(\mathbf{x}_1|\mathbf{h}_1)$

typically to train auto-encoders (see Equation (4.7) with $\mathbf{c} = E[\mathbf{h}_1]$^4).

So we have found that the truncation of the Gibbs chain gives rise to first approximation (one sample) to reconstruction error (through a biased mean-field approximation), with slightly better approximation (two samples) to CD-1 (approximating the expectation by a sample), and with more terms to CD-k (still approximating expectations by samples). Note that reconstruction error is deterministically computed and correlated with log-likelihood, which is why it has been used to track progress when training RBMs with CD.

> ^4It is debatable whether or not one would take into account the fact that $\mathbf{h}_1$ depends on $\theta$ when computing the gradient in the mean-field approximation of Equation (5.30), but it must be the case to draw a direct link with auto-encoders.
