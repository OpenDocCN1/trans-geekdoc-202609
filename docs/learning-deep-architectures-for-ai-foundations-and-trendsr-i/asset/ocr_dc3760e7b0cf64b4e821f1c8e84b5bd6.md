Automatically learning features at multiple levels of abstraction allow a system to learn complex functions mapping the input to output directly from data, without depending completely on human-crafted features. This is especially important for higher-level abstractions, which humans often do not know how to specify in terms of raw sensory input. The ability to automatically learn powerful features will become increasingly important as the amount of data and range of applications to learning methods continues to grow.

Depth of architecture refers to the number of levels of composition of non-linear operations in the function learned. Whereas most current learning algorithms correspond to shallow architectures (1, 2 or 3 levels), the mammalian brain is organized in a deep architecture [173] with given input present at multiple levels of abstraction, each level corresponding to a different area of cortex. Humans often describe such concepts in hierarchical ways, with multiple levels of abstraction. The brain also appears to process information through multiple stages of transformation and representation. This is particular clear in the primate visual system [173], with its sequence of processing stages: detection of edges, primitive shapes, and moving up to gradually complex visual shapes.

Inspired by the architectural depth of the brain, neural network researchers have wanted for decades to train deep multi-layer neural networks [19, 191], but no successful attempts were reported before 2006: researchers posted experimental results with typically two or three levels (i.e., one or two hidden layers), but training deeper consistently yielded poorer results. Something that can be considered a breakthrough happened in 2006: Hinton et al. at University of Toronto introduced Deep Belief Networks (DBNs) [73], with a learning algorithm that greedily trains one layer at a time, exploiting an unsupervised learning algorithm for each layer, a Restricted Boltzmann Autoencoder (RBM) [51].
