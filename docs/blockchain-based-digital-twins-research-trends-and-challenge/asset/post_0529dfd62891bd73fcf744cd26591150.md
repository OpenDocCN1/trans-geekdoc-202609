# ABSTRACT

The term “industrial internet of things” (IIoT) is used to describe the interconnection of various sensors, instruments, and other equipment over computer networks used in many industrial contexts, such as not limited to production and energy management. The ability to gather, share, and analyze data is a key enabler of productivity and competency gains, among other economic advantages. Cyber-physical systems, the cloud, big data analytics, Artificial Intelligence (AI), and Machine Learning (ML) are just some of the technologies that make the IIoT possible. With the help of the IoT, AI, ML, SA, and spatial network graphs, digital twins may create dynamic simulation models that evolve and adapt in real-time to the state of their physical counterparts. A digital twin is a copy of anything that is continually learning and updating its characteristics based on data from a diversity of causes to reflect the near real-time state of the original thing. This system is capable of learning from a variety of sources, including sensor data expressing numerous facets of its functioning state, anthropological professionals like technologists with extensive and pertinent business field acquaintance, alike machineries, related task forces of machineries, and grand arrangements and situations. A digital twin is a programmed depiction by means of a machine that also includes the use of data from the machine’s prior lives. For manufacturers, digital twins mean cutting expenses, keeping tabs on assets, improving maintenance, cutting down on downtime, and opening the door to the development of linked goods. This chapter analyzes the various technologies, architecture, and industrial revolutions that makeup IIoT.

# 7.1 INTRODUCTION

The era of revolution in industry first began in the 1780s with the expansion of systems to generate electricity from thermal energy, hydropower, and biofuels. During the 1870s’ second industrial revolution, electrical energy was favored by factories and other industrial establishments. During the era of the 3rd uprising of industry, which began in the 1970s, embedded hardware, and computing technologies were used to introduce the notion of automation to the manufacturing industries. Industry 3.0, also known as the computerized age, was widely adopted as a result of technological advancements starting from 1970. # 7.1.1 BLOCKCHAIN TECHNOLOGY

When neither verification audit methods are present, the issue of confidence in information systems becomes incredibly complex, especially when they deal with sensitive data like financial transactions involving volatile currencies. Blockchain is the system that enables a collection of untrustworthy parties to verify transactions [13]. It provides a decentralized, public, auditable, and unalterable ledger. All transactions made from the system’s inception are available on the blockchain, and they can be verified and assembled at any time by any authorized party. The blockchain protocol organizes data into a series of blocks, each of which contains a collection of Bitcoin transactions that took place at a specific moment. A reference to the preceding block forms a link between blocks, making a chain.

A smart contract is a set of procedures of a system or code that allows a contract to be inevitably completed or obligated based on a set of agreed-upon conditions. In the case of Bitcoin transactions, for instance, intelligent control defines the underlying application logic that must be implemented. Since smart contracts enable the specification of criteria and functions that go beyond the switchover of cryptocurrency as the endorsement of assets in a particular choice of non-monetary associates, they are the appropriate component for expanding the usage of blockchain technology into new industries.
