1. Limited Adoption: Digital twin technologies have not yet been fully incorporated into clinical practice routines. The use of technology in healthcare facilities (such as hospitals and labs) should be increased to improve clinical procedures, digital simulations, and patient care in general. However, despite the growing usage of digital twins in the healthcare system, it is suggested that they will continue to be pricey and out of reach for most people. Digital twin technology will start to be seen as a perk available only to those with more financial means, leading to inequalities in the healthcare system.

2. Data Quality: Artificially intelligent digital twins can adapt from the available biological data, but they are constantly aware that the quality of the data may be low because it was gathered by for-profit organizations. As a result, it is difficult to analyze and portray such data. In the end, this has a severe impact on the models, which also has a bad impact on the model’s dependability throughout the diagnostic and treatment procedures.

3. Data Privacy: As a result of the deployment of digital twins, data on each individual will need to be gathered more often by healthcare and insurance companies. These health organizations progressively get a full awareness of an individual’s biological, genetic, physiological, and lifestyle-related information. Such unique data may be exploited for commercial objectives as opposed to people’s interests. One example would be an insurance company using the data to identify individuals with greater accuracy.
