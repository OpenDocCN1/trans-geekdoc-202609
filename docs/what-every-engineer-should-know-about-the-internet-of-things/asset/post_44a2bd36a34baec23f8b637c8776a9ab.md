
## Networks of ‘Things’

### 2.5.1 Open, Closed

NoTs can be open, closed, or somewhere in between. For example, an automobile can have hundreds of sensors; wired Central Processing Unit (CPUs); databases such as maps, wired communication channels through the car, and without any wireless access between any ‘thing’ in the car to the outside. This illustrates a closed NoT. Such a NoT mitigates nearly all wireless security concerns such as remotely controlling a car; however, could there still be concerns of malware and counterfeit ‘things’ that could reduce in safety. (There are issues related to wireless transmission ranges that we ignore for simplicity.) A fully open system would essentially be any ‘thing’ interoperating with any ‘thing’, any way, and at any time. From a “trustworthiness” standpoint, is impossible to assure since the NoT is unbounded.

Most NoTs will be between these extremes since a continuum will likely exist. The primitives serve as a guidepost to where reliability and security concerns require additional mitigation, e.g., testing and other technologies related to validation and verification.

### 2.5.2 Patterns

We envision a future demand for design patterns that allow larger NoTs to be built from smaller NoTs, similar to design patterns in object-oriented systems. In essence, these smaller entities are sub-NoTs. Sub-Notes could speed up IoT adoption for organizations seeking to develop IoT-based systems by having access to sub-NoT catalogues. Further, the topology of sub-NoT could impact the security and performance of composite NoTs.

## Networks of 'Things'

### 2.5.2 Environment

As NoS are likely to rely on factors from outside (e.g., temperature sensors), it is equally logical to assume that some form of actuation may be in place to influence that environment. This raises the larger question of how a NoT and its environment interact, not only at the element level but also, significantly, at the NoT's operating level.

### 2.6 Reliability and Security scenarios

The elements lay out contextual issues related to trustworthiness of a specific NoT. The primitives are the building blocks of NoS. Because trustworthiness is such a broad concept, this document has mainly focused on two "ities” related to the primitives: security and reliability. People often ask for simple examples of real or hypothetical use cases relating these “ities” to each primitive. The following are examples of simple, hypothetical reliability and security scenarios associated with each primitive.

- Sensor Reliability: A modern car's speed sensor is exposed to heat, water, and dust (environment). Years later, it starts providing inconsistent readings due to fatigue that induces sensor drift. This is an example of malfunctions caused by environmental conditions.
- Sensor Security: A smart building's temperature sensors are easily accessible, and this particular system doesn't provide a means for validating the firmware's authenticity. An attacker substitutes the firmware with a botnet and can respond to commands. These sensors then become part of a botnet and can contribute to distributed-of-service (DDoS) attacks. This is an example of typical tampering and altering firmware.
- Aggregator Reliability: In a smart city environment, thousands of sensors transmit data to a series of smart gateways that compress several gigabytes of raw data into meaningful information. A blackout that occurred in part of the city creates an unexpected condition that results in division by zero, which causes the application to crash for the entire duration of the blackout. This is an example of unpredictable conditions.
- Aggregator Security: An attacker introduces a rogue sensor into a network that produces false readings. These readings are passed as inputs to the aggregator function without any validation. The attacker launches a buffer overflow attack to gain root access to the entire middleware infrastructure (gateway). This is an example of an injection attack or buffer overflow.
