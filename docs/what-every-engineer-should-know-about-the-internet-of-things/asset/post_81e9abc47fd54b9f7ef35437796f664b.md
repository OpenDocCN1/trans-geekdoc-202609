For example, an effective approach is to create learning modules that address specific learning objectives. The modules would highlight CPS/IoT concepts using appropriate tools and hands-on exercises that could easily fit into existing courses as embedded systems, systems administration, computer security, software architecture, and software construction. Virginia Tech created CPS-focused learning modules for this purpose (Deshmukh, Patterson & Baumann, 2016). Another approach is to create elective courses where students implement practical CPS or IoT systems as a project mission similar to these desired.

Accordingly, the path of least resistance to create new programs by modifying existing programs may already exist at the institution and only require slight modifications. This appears to be the most efficient way to create new CPS/IoT educational program that is relevant, timely, and available.

The burden and challenge is on higher education to prepare the next generation of engineers with the skill necessary to build these complex systems. Universities are being tasked to provide working knowledge of a new engineering discipline that adapts the IoT and CPS differences in existing engineering disciplines. This is not an easy mission as it is challenging for educators to keep up with the rapid technology development as well as students (Zhu et al., 2014).

## 9. IoT in Healthcare

### 9.1 INTRODUCTION

Healthcare is a data-intensive discipline (Patil & Seshadri, 2014) in which large-scale data is generated, disseminated, stored, and accessed daily. In 2017, 16.5 million patients globally (41% growth from 2016), and this panorama has the potential to reach 50.2 million by 2020 (Mack, 2017). Also, since January 1, 2018, the US Centers for Medicare & Services developed new incentives to promote (Daniel & Uppal, 2017).

Healthcare applications that are connected to the Internet, also referred to as Internet of Things (IoT) applications, have been widely forecast, investigated, and even deployed on small scale. For example, hospitals have been implementing “smart beds” that can detect when they are occupied and when a patient is attempting to get up, sending this information over the network to nurses (Babu & Jayashree, 2015). The beds also self-adjust to ensure appropriate pressure and support is applied to the patient without having to be manually adjusted by the users. Another area where smart technology is being discussed as an asset is coupled with medication dispensers to automatically upload data to the cloud when medication is not taken or any other indicators for which the care team should be alerted (Chauffani, 2016).

Despite these concerns, polls show that 90% of Americans still value online access to their health records (FDA, 2013). It is easy to perceive that IoT contribute to enhancing the quality of caregiving for patients at a reduced cost. The nature of healthcare and the computational and physical technologies and constraints present several challenges to system designers and implementers. These challenges are complex and include the following:

- Physical (e.g., available technology)
- Logical (e.g., analytics, languages, tools)
- Political (e.g., funding, mandates)
- Behavioral (i.e., desired functionality)
- Communications (e.g., available channels)
- Ethical (e.g., governmental privacy protection standards)
- Structural (e.g., patterns of architecture and design)
