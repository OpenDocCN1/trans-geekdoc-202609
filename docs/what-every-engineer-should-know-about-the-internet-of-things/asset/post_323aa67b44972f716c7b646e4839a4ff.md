
## 7 IoT in Education

### 7.1 INTRODUCTION

According to analyst firm Gartner, 8.4 billion ‘things’ were connected to the Internet in 2017; excluding the laptops, computers, tablets, and mobile phones (Gartner, 2017). In addition, an estimate predicts this number to increase to 64 billion Internet of Things (IoT) devices worldwide by 2025 (Petrov, 2020). Regardless of the exact number of devices, spending in this market is expected to increase substantially. The International Data Corporation (IDC) is forecasting worldwide IoT spending to reach $1.1 trillion in 2023 (IDC, 2019).

As we have discussed throughout the book, IoT applications are already leveraged in diverse domains such as the medical services, small retail, customer service, smart homes, environmental monitoring, and industrial internet. Now, because of their unique nature, academic institutions are looking to incorporate IoT in educational activities to benefit students, instructors, and the entire education system.

For example, because IoT can monitor student attendance and in-class activities in-Alotaibi (2015) and Jiang (2016). However, monitoring can also be applied in other ways. Some studies focus on monitoring the students and objects in online laboratories’ settings (e.g., Fernandez et al., 2015; Lamri et al., 2014; Bisták, 2014; Yin et al., 2012; Bin, 2012; Srivastava & Yammijavar, 2016; Shi et al., 2010). IoT is also being deployed for more pragmatic scenarios in education. For example, Valpreda & Zonda (2016), an IoT-based system was proposed to increase children’s knowledge about agricultural food production and consumption. Other studies on deploying IoT to educate students with special needs exist (e.g., Sula et al. 2013, 2014) for children with autism spectrum disorder.

Educators in the USA (GSMA, 2012), Japan (Fuse, Ozawa & Miura, 2012), and the UK (UNICEF, 2024) already have been applying IoT technologies in their pedagogical processes. Also, several academic institutions (e.g., University of San Francisco (Nie, 2013)) have incorporated IoT technology to enhance campus safety. The information technology and networking giant Cisco has launched several IoT projects for the education sector through engaging schools and students in a smart academic forum (Cisco, 2013). Despite the several studies available on the inclusion of IoT into the education domain, there is a lack of consolidated and coherent views on this subject. On the other hand, although beneficial, the adoption of IoT technologies in education also poses new challenges to their realization.

With the increased use of IoT in the education domain, it is of utmost importance to study how this technology with its distinguished systems functions, as sensing and decision-making, can support and challenge the pedagogical processes for all interacted actors (faculty, students, and staff) as well as all involved assets (e.g., libraries, classrooms, laboratories).

Although there are several benefits of the inclusion of IoT into the education domain, there is still lack of consolidated and coherent views on this subject. Hence, the discussions in this chapter aim to close the gap of knowledge and embark on mapping the published studies available. Results of a systematic literature review focused on the benefits and the challenges faced in education integrating IoT into the curriculum and educational environments will be presented. Differing mapping views of the extracted studies are provided as well as a summary of the already implemented tools and a list of gap research questions yet to be investigated. We also provide a tool for an adaptive learning experience in response to a remote learner's emotions. This chapter will answer the following questions:

To answer the above questions, we used the systematic literature review (SLR) method (Keleet, 2007) to identify, extract, evaluate, and synthesize the articles on the symbiosis of IoT in education. The results show that we identified a significant variety of positive contributions for IoT technologies on education. In particular, we identified three dimensions to classify emerging scenarios of IoT in education: delivery mode, perception, and learning principles (Figure 7.1). While the focus of this chapter is answering the above questions, we also raise a set of research questions that we discuss in Section 7.5, and also provide a demonstration of a tool we developed to provide an adaptive learning experience in response to a remote learner's emotions in Section 7.4.

The presentation in this chapter will be mostly in the format of reporting on the results from the SLR we followed. Nevertheless, the description of the SLR process conducted is beyond scope of this chapter. ## 7.2 WHAT ARE THE BENEFITS OF THE ADAPTED SCENARIOS OF IoT IN EDUCATION?

### 7.2.1 APPLYING IoT IN DIFFERENT EDUCATION LEVELS AND SUBJECTS

The literature suggests that IoT can be incorporated at all education levels. We provide a mapping of the primary selected studies into the educational levels in: https://bit.ly/2XCZFI5K. We also find that IoT incorporation is not limited to particular education subjects. “Computer, Electrical and Electronics Engineering” education, “Science”, “language” education, “Physical education”, and “Business” education are mentioned.

### 7.2.2 PERCEPTION

Perception is a way of understanding, or interacting with something. More specifically, in the context of this chapter, perception refers to a role that can provide services. We identified three different stakeholders: instructor, student, and staff.

### 7.2.3 What Every Engineer Should Know About IoT

For the instructor, IoT can help in managing and tracking attendance, participation, and performance. It can also assist in identifying and tracking fixed and portable resources (e.g., library, cafeteria, dormitory). The collected data can be further utilized to automatically calculate patterns and find inefficiencies.

For the student, IoT can help in providing adapted learning resources based on location, time, and interaction. It also provides support for students with special needs.

For the staff members, IoT will play a designated role in managing and tracking fixed and portable resources. For example, using a noise sensor, one classroom can communicate automatically to a neighbor classroom and inform them if the noise level exceeds a certain level. A warning message could be displayed on the LCD screen in the noisy room.
