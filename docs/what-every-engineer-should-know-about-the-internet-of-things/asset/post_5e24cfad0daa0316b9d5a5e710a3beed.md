There is nothing like IoT. Events and computations can occur anywhere, be transferred at “any time”, and occur at differing levels of speed and performance. The desired result is that all events and computations converge towards a single decision (output). The key concern is “any time”, because these transactions can take place geographically anywhere, at the microsecond level, and with clear understanding of what the clock in one geographic region means with respect to the clock in another geographic region.

There is no universal time-stamping mechanism for practical use in many or most IoT applications. The Global Positioning System (GPS) can provide very precise, accurate up to 100 ns with most devices. Unfortunately, GPS devices have two formidable limitations in use IoT. First, GPS requires line-of-sight access to satellite signals. Many IoT devices are designed to work where a GPS receiver could not receive a signal, such as indoors or enclosed in walls or other obstructions. Additionally, if an IoT device is placed where satellite reception is available, GPS power demands are significant. Many IoT devices have drastically battery life or power access, requiring carefully planned communication. Adding the comparatively high-power demands of devices to such a system could cripple it, so in general GPS may not be practical for use in many networks of things.

Consider a scenario where a sensor in location v is supposed to release data at time x. There is an aggregator in location z waiting to receive this sensor’s data concurrently with other sensors. Note that v and z are geographically apart and the local time x in location v does not agree, at a global level, with what time it is at z. If there existed a universal time-stamping mechanism, local clocks could be avoided altogether, and this problem would go away. With universal time-stamping, the time of every event and computation in a network of ‘things’ could be agreed upon by using a central time-stamping authority that would produce time stamps for all computations that request them. Because time is a vital component needed to trust distributed computations, such an authority would be beneficial. However, such an authority does not exist (Stravou & Voas, 2017). Research is warranted.

## 10.10 LACK OF MEASUREMENT

Standards are intended to offer levels of trust, comparisons of commonality, and predictions of certainty. Standards are needed for nearly everything, but without metrics and measures, standards become more difficult to write and determine compliance against. Metrics and measures are classified in many ways.

Measurement allows for determination of one of two things: (1) what currently exists, and (2) what is predicted and expected in the future. The first is generally easier to measure. One example is counting. For example, one can count the number of coffee beans in a bag. Another approach is estimation. Estimation approximates what you have. By using the coffee example and having millions of beans to count, it might be easier weighing the beans and using that to estimate an approximate count.

Prediction is different than estimation, although estimation can be used for prediction. For example, an estimate of the current reliability of a system, given a fixed environment, context, and in time might be 99%. The key word is point in time. In comparison, a prediction would say something like: based on an estimate of 99% reliability today, it is believed that the reliability will also be 99% tomorrow, but after tomorrow, the reliability might change. The reason is simple: As time moves forward, components usually wear out, thus reducing system reliability. Or as time moves forward, the environment may change such that the system is under less stress, increasing predicted reliability. In IoT, as 'things' may be swapped in and out on a continuous basis, and predictions and estimations of an 'ility' such as reliability will be difficult.

To date, there are ways to measure IoT systems other than by 'things' or dynamic testing. Counting is a static approach. Testing is a dynamic approach when the network is executed. (Note that there are static approaches that do not require execution, e.g., a walkthrough of the network architecture.) To date, counting and dynamic approaches such as estimating reliability and performance are reasonable candidates. Static testing (e.g., code checking) can also be used to show that certain classes of IoT vulnerabilities are likely not present. IoT methodology is an open question.

## 10.11 PREDICTABILITY

The ability to design useful IoT systems depends at a fundamental level on predictability, assurance that components will provide the resources, performance, and functions that are specified when they are needed. This is hard enough to establish in a conventional system, but an extensive body of knowledge in queuing theory and related subjects has developed. IoT systems will provide an even greater challenge, since more components will interact in different ways and possibly not at consistent times.

Two properties of IoT networks have a major impact on predictability: (1) a larger set of communication protocols may be involved in a single network, and (2) the network configuration changes rapidly. Communication protocols for networks of 'things' include at least 13 data links, 3 network layer routing, 5 session layers, and 2 management standards (Salman). Data aggregators in the network must be able to communicate with devices that have widely varying, throughput, and storage characteristics. Since many small devices have limited battery life, data transmission times must be rationed, so devices are not always online. For example, Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) devices can be configured to broadcast their presence for periods ranging from 0 to 2 seconds.

In addition to constantly changing configuration, another issue stems from the embedding of devices with the physical world. Even more than conventional systems, humans are part of IoT systems, and necessarily affect the availability of services in unexpected ways. Consider the story of a driver who took advantage of a cell phone that interacts with his vehicle's onboard network. Though probably not considered by the user, the car started, then later parked in a mountainous area, only to discover it was impossible to restart the car because there was no cell signal (Neumann, 2018).

This story illustrates a basic predictability problem for IoT networks – node location and signal strength may be constantly changing. How do you know the network will continue to function adequately and remain safe? Properties such as performance and capacity are affected as configuration evolves, but you need to be able to predict these. Modeling and simulation become essential for understanding system behavior in a changing environment, but trusting requires some assurance that it incorporates all features of interest and accurately represents the environment. Beyond this, it must be possible to adequately analyze system interactions with the physical world, including potentially rare combinations of events.

Recommendations for design principles for this new environment will take time before users are able to trust systems of assorted components. Here, the importance of a central theme of this document is reshown: to be able to trust a system, it must be bounded, and IoT by its nature may defy any ability to bound the problem.

## 10.12 FEW IoT-SPECIFIC TESTING AND ASSURANCE APPROACHES

To have any networks of 'things' acting together, assurance will need to be much better than it is today. A network of 'things' presents a number of testing challenges beyond encountered with conventional systems. Some of the more significant include the following:

- Communication among Large Numbers of Devices: Conventional Internetbased systems usually include one or more servers responding to short communications from users. There may be thousands of users, but the communication is typically one to one, with possibly a few servers cooperating to produce a response to users. Networks of 'things' may have several tens to hundreds of devices.
- Significant Latency and Asynchrony: Low power devices may conserve power by communicating only on a periodic basis, and it may not be possible to synchronize communications.
- More Sources of Failure: Inexpensive, low power devices may be more likely to fail, and interoperability problems may also occur among devices with slightly different protocol implementations. Since the devices may have limited storage and processing power, software errors in memory management or timing may be more common.
