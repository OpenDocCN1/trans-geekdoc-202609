Regarding privacy, in particular, a smart city processes personal, enter-, and governmental data 24/7 an unauthorized person should not be able to access. Despite cryptography, anonymization, and access control, four factors are determinants for a comprehensive privacy solution: time and space (Arruda & Bulcão-Neto 2019; Perera et al., 2020). Access to the elderly’s data is immediately granted to an emergency service when a call occurs and remains until his/her admission at the hospital. From this moment, the patient’s location is notified of emergency contacts, whereas her/her health conditions are available only by health professionals in charge during the hospitalization period. The closer an IoT device is to the end user (e.g., a smartphone), the more challenging it is to protect the privacy of consumers before their sensitive data “leaves” (Hom et al., 2018).

These concerns relate to directing, domain-specific, and other redundant standardization efforts. The P2413 incorporates a collection of architecture viewpoints elaborated to form the framework description body: a reference model, a reference architecture, and a blueprint for data abstraction.

An active project of the same IEEE working group leveraged the IEEE P2413 is the standardization of a Reference Architecture for Smart Cities (RASC; IEEE, 2019b). RASC’s goals include defining different vertical applications in the smart city (e.g., water and waste management, environment monitoring, smart buildings, and eHealth), communications between these vertical applications, and an intelligent and integrated operations center in a smart city use case. Besides, RASC also provides a four-tiered architecture for smart cities (device, communication network, IoT platform, and application) and defines the relationships with attributes specific to cloud computing technologies and Big Data analytics. By this writing, RASC is still an IEEE draft standard, under regular revisions.

Software-Engineering Smart Cities: It is common sense that a considerable investment in networking infrastructure is needed to make a smart city reality. Nevertheless, networking is not enough (Weyl & Ebert, 2016). Software is mass pervasive in smart cities, covering data acquisition, communication, representation, storage, reasoning, retrieval, and visualization, among others. Furthermore, the multidisciplinary nature of IoT and the nature of stakeholders in a smart city challenge current software engineering practices (Santana et al., 2018; Motta, de Oliveira & Travassos, 2019).
