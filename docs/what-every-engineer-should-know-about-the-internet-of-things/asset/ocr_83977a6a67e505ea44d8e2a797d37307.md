Limited interfaces may extend to be unavoidable with small devices but go against secure systems paring with Kerckhoffs' rules for crypto from the 19th century (Kerckhoffs, 1883) and later extended for IT systems (Saltzer & Schroeder, 1975). Among these is the principle that a secure system must be to use and not require users to remember complex tasks. IoT systems run counter to this principle by their nature. Today, device makers are inventing user interfaces that often vary wildly from device to device and manufacturer to manufacturer, almost ensuring difficulty in merging the right steps for a given device.

One of the challenges of designing for IoT usability is the asynchronous operation imposed by device processing and battery limitations. Devices may only be able to communicate periodically, with possibly minutes to hours between transmissions, conditions at a given time may be different than the last data received from a device. Since multiple devices, it is likely that decisions may be based on at some currently invalid values, or actions may be delayed by the system waits for updated values. In the worst case, badly implemented IoT can “make the real world feel very broken” (Treseler, 2014), when flipping a light switch results in nothing for some time as devices communicate.

In efforts to reduce usability problems, manufacturers have turned to AI to allow users to interact with their devices. One of the most popular uses of AI is for smart speakers, which allows users to simply talk to the device to control household appliances or order products. But with much IoT, building in one can have significant implications for others. Implementing the desirable response-capability requires engineering trade-offs that inherently limit the ability to build-in security. In one well-known example, a smart speaker responded to an accidental sequence of words to a conversation, then to someone else (Soper, 2018). The functions required for such a system. The most obvious implication is course that the device must be constantly listening to be able to respond without a user flipping an ON switch. Since small devices do not have the processing capacity and databases for voice recognition, data must be sent to a larger processor in a cloud or similar service. Consequently, there is an always-active listening device with a connection to the Internet, clearly a security risk that will be challenging to defend against. Added to this the need to prevent the system from misinterpreting user directions that it hears (consider how difficult it is sometimes to misunderstand between two people communicating). It is easy to see how building in usability can lead to real challenges in providing effectiveness.

One recommendation to improve usability for devices is to provide consistency among user interfaces. Standardized approaches will need to be developed, similar to what occurred for graphical user interfaces (GUIs) in the 1980s–1990s. Prior to the time of the 1980s, computer interfaces were typically limited to keyboards and text displays with some basic graphical capabilities.
