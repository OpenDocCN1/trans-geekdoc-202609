These problems are compounded due to the economies behind IoT; the barrier to constructing an IoT device is low, meaning that the market contains many different devices and models from many different manufacturers, with very few authoritative bodies attending the security of any of these devices. While these problems extend into the traditional computing market i.e., laptops and personal computers – the market mechanics have driven most products towards consolidated products and features, making it easier for consumers to evaluate and understand the security offered by the devices and manufacturers.

Nonetheless, there is no central entity regulating the security of IoT devices, recent progress has been seen as regulatory participants consider how they want to address this complex problem. As an example, the Internet of Things Cybersecurity Improvement Act (Weaver, 2017) was introduced in 2017 with the goal of setting standards for IoT devices specifically installed in government networks. The bill contains several important stipulations, requiring devices to abandon fixed default passwords and devices that must not have any known vulnerabilities. The act also relaxes other acts that could be used to prosecute researchers looking to test the safety of these devices.

The mandates of several agencies border with the IoT security space. A good example of this is the Federal Trade Commission (FTC). In January 2018, the VTech agreed to settle charges by the FTC that they violated not a security law, but US children's privacy law, collecting private information from children, without obtaining consent, and failing to take reasonable steps to secure the data (Federal Trade Commission, 2018). The key phrase is VTech's products were the connected-toys – i.e., IoT devices – which collected personal information, and due to security risks in how these devices handled and managed data, the company was fined. This case shows that if IoT devices do not have reason security, a manufacturer may be held liable.

The US Consumer Product Safety Commission has called for more collaboration between lawyers and experts in the area (American Bar Association, 2017). Outside of the United States, the European Union for Network and Information Security (ENISA) has published security guidelines for IoT (ENISA, 2017). As more calls for security and recommendations occur, standardization and regulation may flow, increasing the security and safety of deployed IoT systems.

Regulations offer a serious means with which can help increase the security and safety of IoT systems, as evidenced by their successes in other industries such as vehicle manufacturing. While some improvements have been noticed as some agencies and organizations attempt to influence in IoT regulation, it has not been seen where one central organization mandates rules regarding the use and development of IoT systems. Such an organization could have significant impact on the security and safety of IoT systems and consumers' lives.

To complete this model, we define six elements, namely, environment, cost, geographic-location, owner, Device_ID, and snapshot, although that are not primitives, are key players in trusting NoTs. These elements play a major role in fostering the degree of trustworthiness that a specific NoT provides.

1. Environment: The environment of a specific NoT operates; this is particularly important. The environment of a NoT is the universe in which it operates. This includes the physical and logical environments. The environment of a NoT can be compromised, which would affect the trustworthiness of the device.

2. Cost: The costs of building and operating a NoT, in terms of money and resources. If the cost of security is too high, it may lead to reduced security, which would affect the trustworthiness of the device.

3. Geographic Location: The geographic location of a NoT is the physical place where it resides. This location can change, and it may affect the trustworthiness of the device. The geographic location of a NoT should be protected.

4. Owner: The owner of a NoT is the person or organization that owns and controls it. The ownership of a NoT can be transferred or compromised, which would affect the trustworthiness of the device.

5. Device_ID: The Device_ID is a unique identifier for a NoT. If the Device_ID can be forged or spoofed, it would affect the trustworthiness of the device.

6. Snapshot: A snapshot is an instant in time. Snapshots of a NoT can be tampered with, which would affect the trustworthiness of the device.

Trustworthiness includes attributes: own- security, reliability, availability, and performance

Snapshots may be aligned to a clock (NIST, 2015). A global clock may be burdensome for sensor networks. Others, however, argue in favor of a global clock (Li, 2004). This publication does not endorse either scheme at the time of writing.
