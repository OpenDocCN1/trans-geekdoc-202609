Complex systems are those with many independent and interdependent components, which interact in nonlinear ways (behavior cannot be expressed as the direct conjunction of the activity of individual components) and have interdependencies that are difficult to describe, predict, and design (Alpampi & Pardo, 2014). The IoT includes many physical objects (e.g., sensors and actuators) that interact autonomously and heavily depend on their capabilities, such as state, processing, and memory (Khanna & Kaur, 2020). Both smart cities and IoT-based systems are composed of several communicating systems that form large networks that can be hard to manage, which characterizes a complex system.

Second, every IoT may demand a tremendous amount of infrastructure (e.g., sensing resources) to ease a smart city’s deployment (Shen et al., 2013). Highly interrelated with IoT, the cloud computing paradigm has already shown that consuming resources as a service (platform, infrastructure, or software) is highly efficient, scalable, and easy to use. This is an emerging but already recurrent paradigm as a service, with functionalities (e.g., sensing and analytics) that are offered to be invoked by other systems and applications via well-defined interfaces (Tanna, Kumar & Karthika, 2017). This can be clearly observed in Figure 3.1, where the emergency service is available via the elderly’s smart home environment and the emergency service.

Third, both cities are spatially structured, they have geographical coverage and can be spread along a region, forming alliances, as the network formed by the smart hospital, the emergency service, and the ambulance.

Regarding the temporal dimension, the IoT infrastructure in a smart city may manage many parallel and concurrent events due to the vast number of interactions. In smart cities, real-time data processing is crucial when a system must be notified immediately after a significant event occurs, such as the interaction between the smart home environment and the emergency service. In both, space and time rule the different types of events taking place in every IoT use case, such as smart cities (Arroyo & Bulcão-Neto, 2019).

Moreover, IoT and smart cities provide intelligent services, i.e., could use AI Analytics to process the volume of data that forms a Big Data structure. In that sense, Big Data analytics contributes to making the smart city’s constituent elements beneficial to its citizens. In our scenario, traffic sensors spread across the city continuously feed the traffic control system that analyzes such data to guide the ambulance through the safest paths until the hospital.

Finally, for both, an architecture emerges, i.e., a composition of physical objects with their software counterparts that, together with their connections, assure a structure that can be analyzed and monitored in a safe and scalable fashion.

For establishing the relation between IoT and smart cities, we need to have two main ideas in mind: the key functional features addressed in an IoT architecture and the anatomy of a smart city application (Weyrich & Eber, 2016; Lynn et al., 2020).
