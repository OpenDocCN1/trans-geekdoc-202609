Before leaving the problem of having no universally accepted and acceptable definition for IoT, it should be stated that IoT is increasingly associated with artificial intelligence (AI), automation and 'smart' objects. So, is "IoT" any you can attach the adjective "smart" to, e.g., smartphone, smart car, smart appliance, toy smart home, smart watch, smart grid, smart city, smart tv, smart suitcase, smart clothes? No answer is offered here, but it is something to consider, the overuse of the adjective 'smart' adds confusion to what IoT is about.

Now consider the question: what is meant by 'trust'? No formal definition is suggested in this publication but a variation on the classical definition of reliability. Here, trust is the probability that the intended behavior and the actual behavior are equivalent, given a context, fixed environment, and fixed point in time. Trust should be viewed as a level of confidence. For example, cars have a trusted set of behaviors when operating on a roadway cannot be expected when the car is sunken in a lake. This informal trust definition works well when discussing 'things' and networks of 'things'.

The value of knowing intended behaviors can be dismissed when attempting to establish trust. Lack of access to a specification for intended behaviors is a trust concern. If there is little difficulty giving 'things' to other 'things', that only addresses a network of 'things' architecture and is one of determining trust. Correct architecture does not ensure that the actual behavior of the 'things' will exhibit the intended behavior. Hardware and software components may not work well when integrated, depending on whether they were the right components to be selected, whether they had the proper levels of 'ilities', such as security and reliability built-in, and whether the architecture and specification for the composition was correct.

The Internet (I) is rarely associated with the term 'trust' or 'trustable'. Identity theft, false information, the dark web, breakdown in personal privacy, and other negative features of (I) have caused some people to avoid the Internet altogether. But for most, avoidance is not an option. Similar trust concerns occur for (T) because 'things' carry their own trust and the interactions between 'things' can exacerbate these concerns. From a trust standpoint, the Internet should be viewed as untrustworthy backbone with untrustworthy things attached– that becomes a perfect storm. Here, there are three categories of IoT trust that must be addressed: (1) trust in a 'thing'; (2) trust in a network of 'things'; and (3) the environment and context that the network will operate in and context, and at a specific point in time.

Understanding what IoT is and what trust means is the first step in confidently relying on IoT. IoT is a complex, distributed system with temporal constraints.
