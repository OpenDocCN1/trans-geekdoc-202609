Healthcare: IoT is transforming the healthcare domain in very beneficial ways. IoT tools such as needed for healthcare monitoring and compliance have been and are developing. Smart healthcare is any health service using technology to collect and store information that can be dynamically accessed by the patient, provider, or another device. A great example is a device that prevents a healthcare-associated infection (HAI). On any given day, 1 out 3 hospital patients acquires an HAI (Centers for Disease Control). Several studies reported that a simple and takes only a few seconds to clean their hands with an alcohol-based rub prevents HAI and saves lives, reduces morbidity, and minimizes costs (Haque et al., 2018). However, the hand cleaning was not consistent. A tool that automatically detects whether staff members wash their hands as they enter and exit a patient's room was developed. In addition, this tool provides the compliance data needed for infection preventors. Here is how it works: The soap/gel dispensers have embedded software that detects the Wi-Fi band worn by the healthcare professional - then the hand hygiene data is reported. An individual report will include the hand hygiene transactions (where, when, who) of the healthcare professionals. The managers can then determine hygiene gaps that need to be addressed to improve the hygiene process. Other IoT healthcare products track the location of mobile equipment and systems for the pharmaceutical and biotechnology industry to remotely monitor and provide notification for network outages and when the temperature, humidity, and CO2 levels exceed desired parameters.

Another significant healthcare category are wearable devices. Some are used for location monitoring to ensure the safety of seniors and babies during hospital stays. The best example of a patient-wearable medical device is a closed-loop insulin delivery system (a.k. artificial pancreas) for a person with type 1 diabetes (TID). TID is a chronic insulin autoimmune disease where the patient is *dependent*. The challenge is the amount of insulin a patient needs varies depending on many factors.

Without devices, type 1 patients use insulin therapy, and they require two types of insulin: long-acting (also called “basal”) and fast-acting (also called “bolus”). A closed-loop insulin delivery system uses an IoT architecture to control insulin delivery. To appreciate this IoT device, and the risk of automating care, some knowledge of TID care is necessary. The greatest risk is that if too much insulin is used, the patient may experience low blood sugar which can be life-threatening. If the patient does not receive enough insulin, the risk is vital organ damage as well as an increased risk of other autoimmune diseases. The challenge is the amount of insulin each patient needs varies and depends on many factors. To keep blood sugar level in a normal range, the amount depends on the food (and type), body size, hormone levels, activity, current health status, time of day, current amount of insulin in the body, and even the weather at times (https://www.diabetes.co.uk/).

An insulin pump still requires manual input to dose when food is consumed or to correct high blood sugar. In other words, the user needs to manually enter the blood sugar reading from a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) (or a finger prick) into the pump.
