# 10.20 VISIBILITY AND DISCOVERABILITY

More than else, IoT represents the merger of information and technologies with the physical world. This is an enormous change in the way that humans relate to technology, whose full implications will be not understood for many years. As many aspects of technology, the change has been occurring gradually for some time but has now reached an exponential growth phase. However, by this merger of information technology with the physical world is not always obvious. Mark Weiser, who coined the Ubiquitous Computing 30 years ago, said “The most profound technologies are those that disappear. They weave themselves into the fabric of everyday life until they are indistinguishable from it” (Weiser, 1991). Today this vision is coming true, as IoT devices proliferate into every aspect of daily life. According to one study, in 4 years there will be more than 500 IoT devices in an average household (Gartner), so that they are beginning to disappear.

But is this disappearance uniformly a good thing? If a technology is invisible, then users will not be aware of its presence, or what it is doing. Trust issues related to this new technology world when news reports suggest that smart televisions were “eavesdropping” on users (Toyukawa, 2015; Chung et al., 2017). Voiceoperated remote controls in smart televisions can only work if the televisions are always “listening”, but the trust implications are obvious. To resolve concerns in cases like this, applications need to be configurable for users to balance convenience with their personal security and privacy requirements, and device capabilities need to be visible with clear explanation of implications.

A different set of concerns is involved with technical aspects of device discovery in networks of ‘things’. The Internet was built almost entirely on the TCP/IP protocol suite, with HTML for web sites running on top of TCP/IP. Standardized communication protocols and internationally agreed web names enable consistent operation regardless of the computer or router manufacturer. Smartphones have the Bluetooth protocol for devices. This structure has not been extended to IoT devices, because they generally do not have the processing power to support it. Instead, a proliferation of protocol families has developed by different companies and consortia, including BLE, ZigBee, Digital Enhanced Cordless Telecommunications Ultra Low Energy (DECT ULE), and collection of proprietary technologies for Low Power Wide Area Networks (LPWAN). These many technologies result in a vast number of possible interactions across various versions of software and hardware from many different sources.

Most computer users are familiar with problems that arise when some business application or other software will not run because other software was changed on the system, and the two packages are no longer compatible. At least with PCs and mainframes, a person generally has a good idea of what is running on the systems.
