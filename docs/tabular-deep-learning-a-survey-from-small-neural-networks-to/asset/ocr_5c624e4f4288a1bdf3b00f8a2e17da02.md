Missing data can be randomly distributed and unrelated to other dataset variables (Missing completely at MCAR), nonrandomly distributed and related to other variables (Missing at MAR), or caused issues related to the values themselves (Missing at random, MNAR) [283]. Missing values reduce prediction power, especially in small datasets, introducing sampling bias. While statistical methods for imputing missing values have been traditionally used, in this section we will mainly discuss generative methods. Traditional methods are based on dropping others with null values, simple imputation, or using summary statistics (mean imputation, median, and so on) [283]. Imputation is especially important for neural networks because its output varies with the number of missing values (variable imputation). Multivariate imputation, on the other hand, exploits the other variables (the information contained in) to produce a prediction (classification if categorical, regression if conditional) that replace the missing input [12, 167]. Traditionally, gradient-boosting decision tree (GBDT) models have outperformed deep learning (DL) models on tabular data. Recent advances in architectures have narrowed the gap, making DL models competitive with GBDT, especially when effectively handling categorical variables. While DL models have strong performance in classification and regression, with regression, neural networks often fail to model complex target functions effectively. Well tuned neural networks can compete with GBDT in specific real-world scenarios or domain-specific applications. Notably, even small networks can be competitive, reducing the need for data augmentation and regularization techniques to mitigate overfitting. However, GBDT models require significantly less to perform well. own remains the best-performing GBDT model. Many previous benchmarks suffer from data leakage, outdated datasets, or oversampled datasets, highlighting the need for more, representative benchmarks.
