You and Kohli [182] highlight how dataset characteristics impact model performance, and benchmarks vary accordingly. TabNet provides a continuously maintained tabular benchmark platform where new models can be tested, datasets incorporated, and new protocols used. The authors 1000 datasets to build a benchmark of 31 datasets covering finance, health, and policy, and so on. This platform also features an automatic evaluation system to avoid bias, data leakage, and other problems in evaluation. Moreover, they not only tested the models but also in ensemble scenarios. The deep learning models are not consistently superior (but are the GBDTs anyway), and the model task is specific. In addition, the fragility of benchmarks has a strong impact on ranking (the various issues that classification results usually change). Ensembling models and automatic tuning seem to be the best increase performance significantly. ChemicalBench [244] contains a benchmark on medical tabular data, featuring de-novo-key property prediction for clinical decision making. In ChemicalBench, they use machine learning methods and 14 general-purpose LLMs and 8 conditional LLMs. The study shows that model performance perform well when features are fewer than 50% of the original dataset. Also, there are no differences between LLMs that are general-purpose and those that have been fine-tuned to medical tasks (LLMs in general show high rates of false positives or false negatives and a general bias).
