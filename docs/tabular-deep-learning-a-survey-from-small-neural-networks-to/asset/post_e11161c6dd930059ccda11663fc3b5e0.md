# 7.3. Regularization

The fact that neural networks can approximate any function means that they only fit the training data. This flexibility also accounts for their sensitivity to overfitting and the impact of a single feature. Furthermore, the possibility of introducing constraints to improve performance has been suggested. Some techniques are a kind of "common sense" that could be introduced by intuition, or by data-driven approach. Regularization has the advantage of reducing variance (and thus, model complexity), which makes the models less sensitive to noise and overfitting ([70]) (Tab 5 and Fig. 8). L1/L2 regularization is the most commonly used form of regularization to reduce the size of the weights, and is easily implemented in the loss function. Early stopping and dropout can also be seen as another form of regularization to reduce variance and make the model more robust to rare events ([264]. Label smoothing or noise injection is another way to make the model more resistant to noise, with the advantage of improving the calibration of the model ([417, 245].

Dropout is the most widely used form of regularization. Dropout randomly drops a fraction of neurons during training to prevent overfitting. It is also a form of noise; in addition to regularization, it prevents the co-adaptation of neurons and forcing a more sparse representation (like L1 regularization) ([317]). Several variants have been proposed such as inverted dropout (scaling at the end of the minibatch), dropout of layers instead of neurons, DropConnect (which uses the same units, weights are regularized), Adaptive Dropout (the dropout probability is different for each unit), and same range ([142, 34, 9, 105]). Stochastic depth can be achieved by combination of dropout in which some units of a layer or even whole blocks (e.g. convolutional blocks) are dropped ([144]. Early stopping (stopping when there is no more weight update or improvement) can also be considered a regularization technique, the simplest but also the most effective ([19]). Early stopping can be considered as an implicit regularization technique, equivalent to L2 regularization for each model or an hyperparameter selection method. It has the advantage of reducing training time and improving generalization without overfitting the model.

# 7.4. Specialized architectures

Over time, specialized architectures have been proposed to enhance or replace MLPs (Tab. 6 and 9). Some strategies are directed at enhancing the capabilities of MLPs in handling tabular data, while others create structures to overcome the limitations of classical models.

**Alternatives to MLP.**get Neural Networks (PNNs) [275] are a type of neural network architecture designed to enhance the modeling of feature interactions, especially in structured data like tabular data. While classical MLPs primarily rely on additive transformations followed by activations, PNNs incorporate multiplicative interactions between features. MLPs have difficulty in being able to capture complex feature interactions that are often present in tabular data, which are higher-order interactions. PNNs, therefore, use additive and multiplicative interactions between features to better capture local interactions (especially categorical). Kolmogorov-Arnold networks (KANS) are a new type of neural network based on Kolmogorov-Arnold representation. While neural networks are based on the universal approximation theorem, Kolmogorov representation states that any multivariate function can be represented as a finite composition of univariate functions. KANS try to overcome the activation functions of MLPs by using B-splines instead of activation on each node whose weights are replaced by a set of coefficients (edges of the network) [280, 224]. In KANS you can adjust the grid size (the number of nodes in the B-spline) to capture more details in the data, including regularization (parameterization and pruning) to make the training smoother and symbolic representation with known symbolic functions (like sine, cosine) or to improve the interpretability! According to the authors, KANS are more interpretable, expressive, and work well for continued regression. In addition, spectral bias analysis shows that KANS, as opposed to MLPs, have reduced bias (which is useful for tabular learning). Although there, KANS are not yet optimized, especially in computational tasks [280, 314, 141, 388].

The rationale is to scale the model able to handle different distributions in tabular data, and adaptively filter feature importance informative or diminishing less relevant ones.
