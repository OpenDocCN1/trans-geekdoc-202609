Correlation does not imply causality, and causal inference is the process of deriving a causal relationship about the conditions for an effect to occur [372]. The process of causal inference is complicated by the fact that not possible cases may be causal. Considering a target variable, there may be potential variables (the cause) and the dependent (effect), which are commonly called confounders (if we want to measure the effect of exercise on weight loss, diet is a potential confounder). Our idea is to compare what happened with what would have happened under other circumstances, or counterfactual thinking (what treatment would have been for a patient?). In medicine, randomized controlled trials are used to establish causality. We must be able to confirm that the cause (e.g., a new drug) is the cause (in reduction of symptoms). Causal ML tries to find an association where we cannot randomize trials. Causal ML tries to answer the questions: what would happen if we changed a feature, what would be the effect observed, what would happen if things were different (counterfactual outcome). There are several challenges to causal ML, such as collection bias (observations are not randomized), hidden confounders (some confounders may only be measurable), generalization problems, and computational cost (ML often requires large datasets). In any case, causal ML has having relevance in healthcare, marketing, finance, public policy, and Causal Trees [11] are examples of causal ML that focus on partitioning the data into subgroups where treatment effects can be estimated more accurately (decision trees minimize prediction error). On the deep learning, there are some examples, such as CAT-MIWAE [12] causal-aware asymmetric variational autoencoder for imputing missing values in data generated under causal intervention. CAT-MIWAE is more interpretable and seems to perform better in imputation since it assumes an MCAR and MAR missing mechanism. Miracle [18] is another method for imputing missing values that are at the same time as learning the causal model of data generation process. The strategy of these methods is not only to use variables for imputation, but also to try to derive causal links to the variables that needs imputation. Causal Structure Learning (CASTLE) [19] learns an optimal causal structure and tries to perform in the main task (e.g., classification) and discover the structure of the data-generating process. Bayesian networks [23] are a type of neural network that is used to represent dependencies between variables, and this dependency is expressed by conditional probability. Causal learning is an interesting perspective, as it allows us to gain insights into our data. We would like to emphasize some questions that remain open in causal ML and tabular data: how we can efficiently perform selection and calculation in tabular datasets? When the datasets are small in size or are there a lot of features? We can reliably identify and adjust confounders in real-world tabular datasets, especially in high-stakes fields like healthcare and deep learning architecture? How can we integrate causal reasoning while maintaining performance? Can we integrate these methods into ML pipelines for large datasets? Can we integrate causal reasoning with other types of interpretability? What are the risks of using causal models in tables, and how can we design safeguards (e.g., uncertainty quantification, confidence intervals, expert validation) to ensure reliable use in decision-making?

# 14. Conclusions

Tabular data remain dominant in real-world applications (finance, healthcare, cybersecurity, ecommerce). However, this remains the last domain where neural models have not achieved dominance over traditional machine learning. This stems from several challenges that make it complex to design a winning architecture. Our various models have attempted to address the limitations of neural networks in the tabular domain, significantly narrowing the gap between neural and traditional models. To no doubt, unlike computer vision, tabular data is difficult to process. Furthermore, large-scale preprocessing techniques that generalize across different datasets are one of the most interesting areas of research. At the same time the dominance of small/medium datasets is often explored. Since these datasets come from sensitive fields, there is a strong interest in terms of their interpretability. Additionally, unsupervised and undersupervised learning for tabular data remains underrepresented, presenting a promising direction for future updates of this field. As such, the tabular domain is still evolving, and there remains room for improvement and research. Some open questions remain, such as better inductive biases, efficiency and interpretability, which may be intriguing research questions for future studies.
