# 8. Benchmark and comparative studies

There have been several studies in recent years that have shown advantages of tree-based models over neural networks. (Tab. 8) [31] In their experiments, XGB-Boost, LightGBM, and CatBoost outperformed us in tabular data. Our results are reiterated in [2022]. For Grinzstein, the causes of this gap: the well-suited inductive biases of decision trees, the deficiency of neural networks in learning complex functions biased towards overly smooth sensitivity to noise. In addition, MLPs tend to overfit and ignore bias. Neural networks thus appear to be competitive only when the features or datasets are large enough to allow learning the patterns for generalization [46]. In addition, regularization techniques or designing neural networks for specific domains makes them competitive with tree-based models [90, 294, 287]. Shmoil [306] conducts an in-depth comparison of 20 datasets on more than one hundred datasets, favoring datasets with categorical features (which are prevalent in the real world). In survey, tree-based models perform best (CatBoost and LightGBM), which effectively handle categorical variables. XGBoost sometimes leads bias. But based models generally require less tuning to reach strong performance, whereas deep learning (DL) models often require more optimization to achieve. Their analysis of benchmark aligns with predictions better in regression, where the target function is often irregular. A notable finding from their study is that small datasets with many columns or high kurtosis which indicates a high number of extreme values or outliers. This contrasts with others that suggested DL models with small-to-medium datasets or datasets with many non-informative features. Authors argue that perhaps benchmarks may have underestimated DL performance due to dataset selection or the use of architectures. DL methods implement performance engineering, making complex interactions that tabular models might not capture under the conditions which. Also, Frisch [237] suggests that when neural networks (NNs) outperform gradient-based trees (GBDTs) on tabular data, learning and deep learning algorithms across 176 classification datasets from OpenML, revealing that 98 datasets overperform others. The study reveals that no single algorithm consistently outperforms others except GBDTs, particularly CatBoost, generally demonstrate superior performance. Among deep learning, TabNN shows notable results, though their computational cost scales quadratically with the number of features. Their analysis indicates that NNs tend to underperform on larger datasets or with non-informative features, with exceptions like TabNet performing well on larger ones. Additionally, GBDTs outperform on datasets with categorical, high-variance features that are skewed, tailed, or those that are highly irregular. Rachevlev [295] critiques tabular deep learning benchmarks for datasets plagued by issues such as data leakage, degradation (e.g., flattened images), and limited features or data points. Moreover, these benchmarks often neglect time-splits for temporal data. To address shortcomings, the authors introduce TabReD—a collection of inherently tabular datasets sourced from Kaggle and other industrial platforms, designed with industry-standard feature management practices. Their evaluation reveals that gradient-boosted models and MLPs with numerical feature ensembles surpass in performance. While FT-Transformer exhibits commendable results, it demands higher computational resources. Additionally, models like SNN, DCNv2, ResNet, and Prompt, which have excelled in other domains, do not excel in this context. Tabular [284] shows that even the newest models perform best in this impact model. In other words, although the advantages of neural models are automatic feature engineering, different processing pipelines can outperform the latest models under the same feature engineering. In addition, if specific data processing is conducted, the differences in performance are reduced. You and Kohli [182] highlight how dataset characteristics impact model performance, and benchmarks vary accordingly. TabNet provides a continuously maintained benchmark platform where new models can be tested, datasets incorporated, and new protocols used. The authors thousands of datasets to build a benchmark of 31 datasets covering finance, health, and policy, and so on. This platform also features an automatic evaluation system to avoid bias, data leakage, and other problems in evaluation. Moreover, they not only tested the models but also in ensemble scenarios. The deep learning models are not consistently superior (but are the GBDTs anyway), and the model task is specific. In addition, the fragility of benchmarks has a strong impact on ranking (the various issues that classification results usually change). Ensembling models and automatic tuning seem to be the best increase performance significantly. ChemicalBench [244] contains a benchmark on medical tabular data, featuring de-novo-key property prediction for clinical decision making. In ChemicalBench, they use machine learning methods and 14 general-purpose LLMs and 8 conditional LLMs. The study shows that model performance perform well when features are fewer than 50% of the original dataset. Also, there are no differences between LLMs that are general-purpose and those that have been fine-tuned to medical tasks (LLMs in general show high rates of false positives or false negatives and a general bias).
