In contrast, tree-based models create sharp, decision boundaries by recursively partitioning the feature space, making them well-suited for problems with irregular decision boundaries. Moreover, these models insert discontinuities in the data, even when there are abrupt, working hierarchically and locally. # 3.7. Lack of a foundation model

Recent advances in computer vision (CV) and natural language processing (NLP) have benefited from large volumes of data and available architectures to learn from. For example, ImageNet (with images) has been instrumental in being able to develop efficient architectures that learn at scale. Similarly, the transformer used for NLP models trained on these datasets learn general patterns that can be transferred a paradigm for transferring knowledge gained from one model to another. A new paradigm has emerged, where a large model when data is scarce.

To date, a foundation model for tabular data is limited due to the lack of generalizing patterns among tabular data. In fact, for homogeneous data, it is possible to define a of patterns that can be generalized by a model (e.g., angles, textures, and objects in vision). Given the heterogeneous, a model struggles to identify patterns in small amounts of data, and patterns in one dataset are not generable to another [404, 154]. In fact, the architectures used for other data, where their high number of parameters is an advantage, are disadvantaged and experience overfitting when applied to tabular data.

# 3.8. Robustness to distribution shifts and domain drift

In real-world applications, tabular data are rarely stable. Models trained on a distribution often encounter difficulties resulting in performance degradation [101, 175]. The source of this shift is called distribution drift. This problem can result from temporal changes (e.g., evolving consumer behavior), geographical variation, policy updates, and or data acquisition biases. Tabular models are particularly vulnerable to this challenge since they lack structural priors (like spatial location in images or

sequential order in that) could anchor generalization. In addition, even small changes in co-variates (such as change in income or age distribution) can impact decision boundaries. In addition, labels can be outdated or used inconsistently across domains (nomenclature problems, changing in name, and so on). Another determinant that may be changes in the target variable, such as changes in proportions or collection in the real-world data during the training data). Deep learning methods assume i.i.d. and can overfit to the specific feature-label relationships present in data. In comparison to tree-based models, deep learning models are more robust when they encounter unfamiliar or dissimilar patterns.

# 3.9. Bias in tabular models

As machine learning are increasingly deployed in high-stakes settings (such as credit scoring, healthcare, justice), fairness has become a fundamental requirement [239, 191, 97]. Tabular data are not one of the main formats used in these domains, and they present unique issues that have been less explored than other datasets [239, 191]. Specifically, fairness in machine learning refers to the absence of unfair direct or indirect treatment of individuals or groups based on attributes such as gender, race, age, socioeconomic status. In tabular data, these attributes are explicit in the contexts of the datasets (for example, names indicating race or include of a group of people), allowing one to observe (and then correct) but at the same time making it to train models. Bias in tabular models can manifest itself in different ways such as for certain datasets (e.g., zip code) with sensitive features, or through historical bias. In contrast to images and text, it is much easier to define such features. In addition, the lack of inductive bias makes deep learning models at risk of learning unintended correlations.
