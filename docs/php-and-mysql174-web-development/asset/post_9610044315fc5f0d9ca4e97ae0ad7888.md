
Other common uses of triggers are to reformat data, or to log an audit trail recording what changes were made, when, and by whom.

## Identifying the Threats We Face

We will begin by looking at the specific security threats facing a modern web application. The first step in building a secure application is to understand the nature of the risks, so that we can think about how to protect against these risks.

## Access to Sensitive Data

Part of our job as web application designers and programmers is to ensure that any data we entrust to users is safe, as are any data we get from other departments. When we expose parts of this information to users of our web application, it must be in such a way that they see only the information that they are permitted to see, and they most cannot see information for other users.

If we are writing a front end for an online stock or mutual funds trading system, people who can get access to our account tables might be able to find out such information as users' taxpayer identification numbers (Social Security Numbers, or SSN, in the United States), personal information as to what the users hold and how much of each, and in extreme cases, even bank account information for users.

Even the exposure of a table full of names and addresses is a serious violation of security. Customers value their privacy very highly, and a large list of names and addresses, plus some inferred information about them (such as “all thousand of these people like to shop at online tobacco stores”) creates a potential sell item to marketing firms that do not play by the rules, spammers, and so on.

All of these are terrible scenarios, but the most commonly seen that cause huge problems are leakage of credit card numbers, and leakage of passwords.

The value of credit card numbers is obvious—anyone obtaining a list of valid numbers along expiration dates, cardholder names, and so on, can either use them themselves, or more commonly, sell a list of card numbers to the highest bidder.

Passwords may be less interesting. Once an attacker has access to sensitive data inside your application, you may be asking passwords who would have any future. The username and password John Smith used to sign up for your photo sharing app stands a good chance of being the same password as what he uses for his online banking.

In some cases engineers put a lot of attention to protecting personally identifiable information, but miss more leakage of data. One good example of this which has been seen a few times is companies sharing logs, typically for one person to do research or market research.

 Usage data this can be mined for kinds of interesting facts. If IPs are associated with the logs, you can uniquely identify patterns of a particular user and a good guess as to their location. If web server contains URLs, as they generally do, these URLs contain usernames, passwords, or information about what (potentially private) endpoints are available on the website.

It goes without saying that any of the data leaks will damage your reputation: you lose customers who are unwilling to trust you after a security incident.

## Modification of Data

Although the loss of data could be damaging, modification can be worse. If somebody obtained access to your system and modified files? Although wholesale deletion will probably be noticed and can be remedied from your backup, how long will it take to notice modification?

Modifications to files could include changes to data or executable files. An attacker's motivation for altering data might be to defeat your site or obtain fraudulent benefits. Replacing executable files with sabotaged versions might give an attacker who has gained access on a single occasion a secret backdoor for future visits or a mechanism to gain higher privileges on the system.

You can protect data from modification as it travels over the network by computing a signature. This approach does not stop modifying the data, but if the recipient detects that the signature matches when the file arrives, she will know whether the file has been modified. If the data is encrypted to protect it from unauthorized viewing, the signature will make it very difficult to modify in route.

Protecting files on your server requires modification that you use the file permissions your operating system provides and protect the system from unauthorized access. Using file permissions, users can be authorized to use the system but not be given free rein to modify system files and other users' files.

Detecting modification can be difficult. If, at some point, you realize that your system's security has been breached, how will you know however have been modified? Some files, such as the data files that store your databases, are intended to change over time. Many others are intended to stay the same as the file you install, unless you deliberately upgrade them. Modification of both programs and data can be insidious, although programs can be restored if you suspect modification, you cannot know which version of your data was "clean".

File integrity assessment software, such as Trip, records information about important files in a known safe state, provides immediately after installation, and can be used at a later time to verify that files are unchanged.
