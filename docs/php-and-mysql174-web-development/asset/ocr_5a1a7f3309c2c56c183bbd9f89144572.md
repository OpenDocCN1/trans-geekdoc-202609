Losing data can be more costly for you than having it revealed. If you have spent months building your site, gathering user data and orders, how much would it cost you in time, reputation, and dollars to lose all that information? If you had no backups of your data, you would need to rewrite the website in a hurry and start from scratch. You would also be dissatisfied customers and fraudsters claiming that they ordered something that never arrived.

It is possible that crackers will break into your site and destroy your data. It is fairly likely that a programmer or administrator will at some point delete something by accident, but it is almost certain that you will lose a hard disk drive. Hard disk drives read and write thousands of times per minute, and, occasionally, they fail. Murphy's Law would tell you the one that fails will be the most important one, long after you made a backup.

# Reducing the Risk

You can take various measures to reduce the chance of data loss. Secure your servers against crackers. Keep the number of staff with your machine to a minimum. Hire only competent, capable people. Buy quality data. Use Redundant Array of Inexpensive Disks (RAID) so that multiple drives can act like one, more reliable drive.

Regardless of its cause, you have one real protection against data loss: Backing up data is not science. On the contrary, it is tedious, dull, and—hope—useless, but it is vital. Make sure that your data is regularly backed up and make sure that you have tested your backup procedure to certain that you can recover. Make sure that your backups are stored away from your computers. Although the chances that your promises will fail or suffer some other catastrophic failure are unlikely, storing a backup onsite is a fairly cheap insurance policy.

# Denial of Service

One of the most threats to guard against is denial of service. Denial of service (DoS) occurs when somebody's actions make it difficult or impossible for users to access a service, or delay their access to a time-critical service.

Having your servers rendered useless for hours, if not longer, can be a serious burden from which to recover. If you consider how ubiquitous of the major sites on the Internet are and how you expect them to be there, any downtime is a problem.

Again, with some of the other threats, a DoS can come from forces other than malicious. A misconfigured network or an influx of users (say, your application featured on a popular blog) can have the same effect.

In early 2013, a series of distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks were made against US financial institutions such as American Express and Wells Fargo. These sites are accused of having high levels of traffic, and have excellent security teams working on them, but they are still vulnerable to being shut down for hours by a DoS attack.
