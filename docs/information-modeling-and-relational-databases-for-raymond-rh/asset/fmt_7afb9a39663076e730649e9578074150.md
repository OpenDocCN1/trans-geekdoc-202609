
Now consider [Fig. 14.13](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0070)(a), where again Country plays another role in the global schema, but now its medal-winning role is mandatory. The mandatory, frequency, uniqueness, and value constraints together ensure that each country must have its medal tally recorded for each medal kind, so [Fig. 14.13](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0070)(b) has three mandatory roles. This example illustrates the following fifth corollary to theorem PSG1:

![Fig. 14.13 The impact of adding mandatory role and frequency constraints.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-13-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.13](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0070) The impact of adding mandatory role and frequency constraints.

```
If R is a ternary with a UC spanning just B’s role and one other role, then adding a mandatory role constraint and frequency constraint of n (the number of possible values for B) to this other role is equivalent to making each specialized version of that role mandatory.
```

This corollary is illustrated in [Fig. 14.14](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0075). It is implied by the previous two corollaries because an equality constraint across a set of disjunctively mandatory roles implies that each of these roles is mandatory.

![Fig. 14.14 Fifth corollary to theorem PSG1.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-14-9780443237904.jpg)

### Other Kinds of Predicate Specialization/Generalization

As an introduction to a second equivalence theorem, consider [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080). The two schemas provide alternative models for a fragment of a car rally application. Each car in the rally has two drivers (a main driver and a backup driver), and each person drives exactly one car. The schema in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(a) is transformed into the schema in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(b) by absorbing the object type `DriverStatus` into the `drives` predicate, specializing this into the `main driver` and `backup driver` predicates. The reverse transformation generalizes the specific driver predicates into the general one by extracting the object type `DriverStatus`. Because this object type appears in a different fact type, this equivalence does not fit the pattern of PSG1.

![Fig. 14.15 The drives predicate is specialized by absorbing DriverStatus.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-15-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0080) The `drives` predicate is specialized by absorbing `DriverStatus`.

Note how the constraints are transformed. The external uniqueness constraint in (a) says that each car has at most one main driver and at most one backup driver. This is captured in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(b) by the uniqueness constraints on the roles of `Car`. The uniqueness constraint on the `drives` predicate in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(a) corresponds in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(b) to the uniqueness constraints on the roles of `Driver`. The uniqueness constraint on the `driver status` predicate in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(a) is captured by the exclusion constraint in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(b).

The mandatory and frequency constraints on `Car`’s role in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(a) require the two mandatory role constraints on `Car` in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(b). Finally, the mandatory role constraints on `Driver` in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(a) are catered for in [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080)(b) by the inclusive-or constraint (shown explicitly here as part of the exclusive-or constraint).

This example illustrates our *second predicate specialization/generalization theorem* (PSG2) as well as four of its corollaries (see [Fig. 14.16](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0085)). The terms “LHS” and “RHS” abbreviate “left-hand schema” and “right-hand schema”. In our example, *A, B,* and *C* correspond to `Driver`, `DriverStatus`, and `Car`, respectively; the equality constraint is implied by the two mandatory role constraints on `Driver`.

![Fig. 14.16 PSG2: R may be specialized into S1…Sn by absorbing B.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-16-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.16](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0085) PSG2: *R* may be specialized into *S*[1]…*S*[*n*] by absorbing *B*.

Theorem PSG2 can be derived from earlier results. For example, adding an exclusion constraint across *A*’s roles in the RHS of [Fig. 14.6](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0035) makes *A*’s role unique in the LHS, causing this compound fact type to split into two binaries, as in the LHS of [Fig. 14.16](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0085). [Section 14.4](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#s0040) considers two more general versions of this theorem.

Sometimes, we may wish to transform a schema into another that is not quite equivalent. For example, suppose that in our car rally application, we limit each car to at most two drivers, but do not classify the drivers in any meaningful way (e.g., as main or backup drivers). Let us also remove the constraint that drivers may drive only one car. This situation is schematized in [Fig. 14.17](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0090).

![Fig. 14.17 Can the predicate be specialized?](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-17-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.17](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0090) Can the predicate be specialized?

Although no `DriverStatus` object type is present, the frequency constraint in [Fig. 14.17](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0090) says that each car has at most two drivers. This enables us to introduce an artificial distinction to specialize the predicate into two cases, as in [Fig. 14.18](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0095).

![Fig. 14.18 Two ways of strengthening the schema in Fig. 14.17.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-18-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.18](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0095) Two ways of strengthening the schema in [Fig. 14.17](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0090).

Because this distinction is not present in the original schema, the alternatives shown in [Fig. 14.18](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0095) are *not equivalent* to the original. They are, in fact, *stronger*—each implies the original schema, but is not implied by it.

Notice the use of *hyphens* in the predicates of [Fig. 14.18](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0095) to bind adjectival phrases to their object terms. This improves verbalization of the fact types and constraints. The four fact types are `Car has Driver-A`, `Car has Driver-B`, `Car has first-Driver`, and `Car has second-Driver`. When the `NORMA` tool generates constraint verbalization, it binds the adjective to the object noun. For example, the upper uniqueness constraint in [Fig. 14.18](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0095)(b) verbalizes as “**Each** Car has **at most one** first Driver”. Without the hyphen, the verbalization defaults to “**Each** Car has first **at most one** Driver”.
