
Recall that a composite key in a predicate involves a UC spanning two or more roles. Because the mapping algorithm maps predicates with composite keys to separate tables, a basic strategy is to reduce the number of (relevant) compositely keyed fact types in the conceptual schema. There are two main situations with the potential for achieving this: compatible keys that may be unified and nonfunctional roles that may be replaced by functional roles. Let’s consider these in turn.

### Step 1

**Step 1** aims to unify compatible, composite keys (see [Fig. 14.42](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0215)). As preparation, step 1.1 includes two moves. As an example of its second move, consider a schema with the following fact types: `Company in State has staff of Gender {‘m’, ‘f’} in Quantity`; `Company in State has Budget`. Compatible keys based on `Company–State` are formed by absorbing `Gender` to specialize the quaternary into two ternaries: `Company in State has male staff in Quantity`; `Company in State has female staff in Quantity`.

![Fig. 14.42 Optimization step 1: unifying compatible, composite keys.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-42-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.42](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0215) Optimization step 1: unifying compatible, composite keys.

Steps 1.2 and 1.3 ensure that each pattern involving *n* compatible, composite keys is replaced by a single predicate (*n* ≥ 2). Several examples were discussed in previous sections. Whereas the original pattern maps to *n* tables, the nested version maps to just one table.

If nesting leads to a loss of clarity in the conceptual schema, the modeler may prefer not to nest. Ideally, the modeler may then choose whether to have nesting performed automatically as an invisible, preprocessing stage to `Rmap`. Any nested object type may be recast as a coreferenced object type if this seems more natural.

### Step 2

**Step 2** examines *object types with both a functional and a nonfunctional role*, and typically attempts to replace the latter by functional roles that can be grouped into the same table as the former (see [Fig. 14.43](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0220)).

![Fig. 14.43 Optimization step 2.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-43-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.43](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0220) Optimization step 2.

Most of step 2.1 amounts to predicate specialization using enumerated object types or frequency constraints. Several examples were discussed earlier. The predicate generalization case with a restricted UC is rare (e.g., recall [Fig. 14.10](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0055)).

The first case of step 2.2 for pattern (a) uses `PSG3` with its second corollary (see [Fig. 14.20](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0105)). The second case for pattern (a) is extremely rare and was ignored in earlier transformations.

As an example, consider the fact type `Official holds Position {‘president’, ‘secretary’, ‘treasurer’}`. We may transform to three unaries: `Official is president`; `Official is secretary`; `Official is treasurer`. Assuming that each position is held by at most one official, we also need to add a role cardinality constraint declaring `# ≤ 1` on each unary.

The final stage of step 2.2 (pattern (b)) uses `PSG2` and its second corollary. As an example, consider the main and backup driver UoD of [Fig. 14.15](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0080) and add the functional fact type `Car is of CarModel`. Specializing the `drives` predicate enables all the information to be mapped to a single `Car` table instead of a `Driver` and a `Car` table.

As an exercise, draw the schemas and perform the mappings. Step 2.2 completes the optimization strategy to reduce the number of mapped tables.

### Consolidating Steps 1 and 2

Before going on to step 3, let’s consolidate the first two steps by considering an example for which substantial optimization is possible. [Fig. 14.44](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0225) depicts a conceptual schema for postgraduate coursework in a computer science department. This UoD was first introduced in [Exercise 4.2](#Ch004_111-158_B9780443237904000232.xhtml#s0020).

![Fig. 14.44 This suboptimal schema maps to six tables.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-44-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.44](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0225) This suboptimal schema maps to six tables.

The pair-subset constraint reflects the fact that after students enroll in a subject, they choose a topic for it. Each topic has a lecturer in charge, and at most two (other) colecturers.

As an exercise, map the schema in [Fig. 14.44](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0225) to a relational schema and then check your solution with the one in [Fig. 14.45](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0230).

![Fig. 14.45 The relational schema mapped from Fig. 14.44.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-45-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.45](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0230) The relational schema mapped from [Fig. 14.44](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0225).

If you did this correctly, you should have obtained six tables. Notice the intertable constraints. The “⊙” superimposed on “⊗” in the relational schema denotes a partition of `PGtopic.topicCode` (i.e., `PGtopic.topicCode` is the disjoint union of `ActualPrerequsite.topicCode` and `PrefPrerequisite.topicCode`). The ordinary “⊗” indicates that the topic-lecturer pairs in `Colecturing` and `PGtopic` are exclusive. The other constraints are straightforward.

Suppose a focused query for this domain is the following: list all topics (code and title) together with their main lecturers, colecturers, actual prerequisites, and preferred prerequisites. Let another focused query be the following: list students, the subjects in which they are enrolled, and the topics chosen by them (if any) for those subjects. With the relational schema of [Fig. 14.45](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0230), the first query involves a join of four tables, and the second query involves a composite outer join of two tables. So, these queries will be slow to run.

Applying optimization steps 1 and 2 to the conceptual schema of [Fig. 14.44](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0225) results in three main changes, as shown in [Fig. 14.46](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0235).

![Fig. 14.46 The conceptual schema obtained by optimizing Fig. 14.44.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-46-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.46](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0235) The conceptual schema obtained by optimizing [Fig. 14.44](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0225).

Step 1.2 generalizes the two prerequisite binaries to a ternary by extracting the object type `PrereqKind {“actual”, “preferred”}`. Step 1.3 uses the overlap algorithm to nest the enrollment and topic fact types. Step 2.1 specializes the colecturer predicate into two exclusive binaries simply keyed on `Topic`. If we also demand that a topic has `colecturer2` it must have a `colecturer1`, then this may be added as a subset constraint. As an exercise, `Rmap` the schema in [Fig. 14.46](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0235), then check your solution with that shown in [Fig. 14.47](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0240).

![Fig. 14.47 The relational schema mapped from Fig. 14.46.](images/B9780443237904000189/f14-47-9780443237904.jpg)

[Fig. 14.47](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#B-f0240) The relational schema mapped from [Fig. 14.46](#Ch014_681-771_B9780443237904000189.xhtml#f0235).

The optimized relational schema has just three tables: one for the ternary, one for the nesting, and one for the five functional binaries of `Topic`. The “≠” symbol means that on the same row, any nonnull values for `headLecturer`, `colecturer1`, and `colecturer2` must differ. This relational schema leads to much faster execution of the focused queries as well as simpler constraint enforcement on updates.
