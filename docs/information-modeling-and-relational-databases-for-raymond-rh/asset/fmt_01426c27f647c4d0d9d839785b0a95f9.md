
**Schema migration**. As the usage of a database evolves, it may become apparent that the schema originally envisioned is no longer appropriate. Migration to an alternative schema is a complex process that can be aided by automation.

**Advanced analytics**. The business-relevant information in the data can be processed to uncover patterns and trends that are not obvious through traditional queries.

The kinds of AI applications mentioned above often rely on a response to a prompt defining the necessary focus for the AI. Creating the appropriate prompt is at present a skill, but a skill that itself will surely become amenable to AI in time. The one area that cannot be determined by AI is what is, or is not, relevant to the user (often referred to as the `UoD` in an ORM context), and the user’s view of how the objects in the `UoD` are related. In some sense, the `UoD` could be seen as the ultimate prompt that cannot be reduced to anything else and cannot be defined without user knowledge. Clearly, an AI agent must understand the `UoD` to be effective, but how can that information be supplied? An obvious solution is provided by an `ORM` schema. It is certainly possible to describe a `UoD` through an informal discourse, but such descriptions are plagued by well-known ambiguities. Creating an `ORM` schema can sometimes be a painful process for a business (internal politics, unclear direction, etc.), but the eventual prize is an unambiguous description of the business intent. Once this is adopted by the business, it can be used as the basis for many facets of subsequent AI processing.

## 17.7 Summary

Relational databases first appeared in the 1970s and became the dominant form of data management during the 1980s and 1990s. Apart from any intrinsic technological merits, their wide adoption was greatly aided by the similarities among the offerings of different vendors. This made investment in the technology a low-risk option, both in terms of deployment by companies and careers for individuals. Since their introduction, there have been many proposals for alternative approaches, but the technology and its associated language, `SQL`, have continued to evolve. The `SQL` standard has expanded to include features from many competing approaches to meet the perceived demand for alternatives.

However, there is one intrinsic feature of a relational database that cannot easily change, and that is its use of a client–server model with a single server providing resources to many clients. Increasing the size of the database requires increasing the size of the server, often at a disproportionally higher cost. Spreading a database over multiple servers is not practical unless the data can be partitioned into noninteracting subsets. When relational databases first appeared, this was not a serious problem because data volumes were generally low. In recent years, large datasets have become common, and this is one of the main driving factors behind the growth of the `NoSQL` movement.

A secondary factor has been the emergence of many niche applications where there is some advantage to be gained by a specialized architecture. In a sense, this is a reaction against the application-neutral approach of relational systems. Optimization of one factor is not always an advantage because it always involves deoptimization of other factors, and so seeking increased performance usually involves narrowing the applicability of the solution.

Today, many organizations use a mixture of database technologies, usually including one or more relational databases but supplemented by other approaches for selected application areas. This has been greatly facilitated by the growth of cloud-based facilities that reduce the barriers to deploying and managing a range of diverse data resources. We can expect this trend to continue for the foreseeable future.
