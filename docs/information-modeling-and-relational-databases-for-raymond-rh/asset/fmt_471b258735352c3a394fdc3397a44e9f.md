# Foreword

Herman Balsters, by Dr., ^()Partner at NorthChain, Groningen, The Netherlands.

I first became acquainted with ORM in 2005 at an ORM Workshop in Cyprus as part of the OTM Confederated International Workshops and Conference. I had just presented a paper on data federations at the OODB conference and out of pure curiosity, I paid a short visit to the ORM Workshop to learn a bit more about the ORM language. When I wandered in, Terry Halpin was offering a presentation. I was amazed with the lively discussions that went on during this presentation, and was surprised not only by the enthusiasm of the workshop participants but also by the high quality of comments and questions. Instead of paying only a short visit, I stayed at the ORM Workshop during the complete remainder of my stay in Cyprus, and I have been a great fan of ORM ever since.

During my work as a researcher and associate professor at the University of Groningen, I was heavily involved with languages such as unified modeling language (UML) and business process model and notation (BPMN), trying to capture the essence of information modeling. I had a constant struggle with these languages, which were considered state of the art, because I just could not get a full grasp of what models written in these languages really meant. I have a background in pure mathematics (in particular, mathematical logic) and computer science, and I wanted to have a clear idea of the semantics and completeness of the data and process models that I was trying to specify. I also wanted to see a clear connection between a model and how that model is implemented. In both aspects, I felt uncomfortable with the prevailing modeling languages of that time. And it was not only in research that I felt this lack of precision and completeness, but also in teaching. Nothing is harder to explain to students than something that you as a teacher do not completely understand.

All that changed the day I entered that ORM Workshop in Cyprus. It took me a while to master all the details of ORM, but after that, I was able to actually write models with complete understanding and with such precision that the models could directly be tied to implementations. Even today, I still use ORM with deep respect and satisfaction.

ORM is a so-called fact-oriented data modeling language. In ORM, you write facts about the world surrounding us. This means that you specify objects and relations between these objects, resulting in certain statements about the world. ORM does this in such a way that even nontechnical domain experts will be able to model facts about their domain of expertise. This has to do with the close resemblance that ORM has with natural language specifications about the world surrounding us. Yet ORM also does that in such a way that you can model facts with an extremely high level of detail and (mathematical) precision. ORM is a graphical language, with an intuitively understandable notation. ORM is also equipped with a powerful tool (NORMA) that allows for the graphical editing of ORM models, and validation checks. Another appealing aspect of ORM is the possibility of compiling ORM models directly into the relational data model, and after that, compilation to SQL. Hence, in ORM you can go from modeling directly to an implementation.

In 2008, authors Terry Halpin and Tony Morgan released the second edition of this wonderful book. I have used it ever since. Over the years, I have taught ORM to hundreds of students, and have supervised both Masters and Ph.D. students in their research involving ORM. The book excels in its simple and clear writing, covering almost every topic in information modeling. Also, the abundance of examples explaining the theory and practice of modeling using ORM makes this book essential for anyone wanting to understand all the details pertaining to information modeling.

This book is both technical and philosophical. It is technical because it provides for an abundance of material concerning theories, notations, examples, and tools to tackle almost anything that is thrown to an information modeler. As a bonus, we are shown how to technically implement those models as a relational database. But in a sense, the book is also philosophical because it takes you into an arena of challenging problems concerning the representation of the world surrounding us. ORM is all about using language to capture that world, and what the best language to do that job should look like.

As a university professor, I had the pleasure of using ORM to teach modeling to students, to write research papers, and to supervise Masters and PhD students in information modeling. Since leaving academia in 2018, I have been involved in information modeling on a more industrial level, where I have been confronted with some extremely challenging modeling problems in actual practice. Also in these complex situations involving modeling issues pertaining to data, business rules, and business processes, ORM provided solutions that I am sure I would otherwise not have had.
