# Following the Money

When investigators describe cryptocurrency investigations, they often follow some type of illicit payments from a source address to a destination. Because of the pseudo-anonymous nature of cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin with its 34-character Base58 addressing, this is complex to achieve. What I describe "address likeness" can quickly set in as you click from transaction to transaction.

To move from transaction to transaction is slightly different in all various blockchain explorers. Using Blockchain.info you use the highlighted “Spent” link on the output side of the move to the input side of the transaction, and rather confusingly click the “Output” link on the input side of the transaction to move back to the previous transaction (Figure 1.5).

We need to ascertain if one of the addresses is a payment address. In the vast majority of transactions that are one-to-many or many-to-many addresses, one of the outputs is a change address. The reason is that each Bitcoin is made up of 100 million satoshis, so the chances that several addresses will add up to exactly the input amount is tiny. In this example, the payment is from a "3" wallet. Bitcoin addresses that start with the number 3 are used for multisignature or more complex transactions than a straight address-to-address payment. The outputs are to a 3 and to a 1. It is significantly more likely that the change address is also a 3 and the 1 is the payment. Hence we can infer that the 3 is the change and the 1 is the payment.

Other techniques exist for discerning which address is the change address. For example, in Figure 1.6 any of the 3 inputs would have paid for the 3Hffw payment, but all three are needed for change to 3B5KC. This helps us conclude that the change address is the 3Hffw and hence we need to find the other one.

Once we have discovered the address or addresses that are likely payments, we can follow them to the next transaction by clicking the Spent button. In this example, both outputs are Unspent so we are at a dead end.

Clicking to the next or previous transaction will provide another list of inputs and outputs, and the investigator can get lost immediately. I recommend stopping after each and researching the addresses, and build the picture before moving again. Once you have moved to two transactions from your start point, experience shows that you are often beyond the scope of the investigation. Unless your suspect is using mixers and tumblers to split the coins, it is extremely hard to follow.

# Identifying Exchanges and Traders

Moving from transaction to transaction is straightforward but meaningless unless we can identify KYC data. Several websites identify belonging to exchanges and traders; the most reliable are walletexorer.com and bitcoinwho.com. The latter site also has a reporting function and also searches the web for mentions of the addresses.

Walletexorer.com attempts to use clustering techniques to identify one address and then ownership of other addresses owned by the same entity. The process is simple: make a deposit into an exchange and you will see the input address. You now have two addresses you know owned by the exchange.

Next, use clustering techniques and algorithms to locate other addresses owned by the same entity. A full discussion of clustering is beside this introduction, but as an example, look for transactions where the known address and the input address are shared.
