# 14 Chapter 1 Getting Started

The metadata about an case can be very interesting as it can help build up a picture of the use of this address. For example, how many transactions has this address involved in? How much Bitcoin has been received by this address over time and how much is left now? The values also have a graph option. This will show the movement of coins over time. When was the first payment made and what patterns do we see? Are coins received immediately or moved on the day?

Why are these questions important? Let's take the example of a scam where a victim's computer has been infected with a virus that encrypts all the data, and a Bitcoin ransom demanded for an unlock code. The questions could tell us when the scam started, how many victims paid the scam's address, how much money has been made, whether the coins are moved on or retained, and so on. In the case of the Petya/NoPetya ransomware, the coins were retained in the address for several days before victims paid before they were moved.

This can clearly be seen by searching for the address used by the scammers: 1Mz7153HMuxTu2R1t78mcSdaAtNbBWX. By taking a look at the graph for the balance and highlighting the obvious peak in June 2017, it is straightforward to ascertain when victims started, how much was received, and when the coins were moved.

It is clear from the graph that payments started on June 27, 2017, and primary payments were finished by June 28, 2017. Coins stayed in the address until July 4, 2017, when they were transferred. This helps us to understand the life cycle of the scam; victims paid a short few days before they were moved. No other significant payments were made. This is a good example of how metadata can help us to build a picture of an address used in a crime.
