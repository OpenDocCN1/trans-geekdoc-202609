## Prologue

One of the more recent investigations I worked on involved the hack of a multi-billion dollar organization. Their stolen data was posted for sale in private circles, and upon finding this out, I immediately contacted the organization. The organization had many questions, and given my prior investigative work, I was able to reach out to the threat actor on their behavior and obtain information on how the breach occurred.

The following text is a portion of the writeup provided by NSFW, a threat actor we will be covering in much greater detail throughout this book, where he describes, in detail, how he was able to hack this organization's network. The process he used was sophisticated, and by no means a run-of-the-mill drive-by hack. This was very well planned and executed.

All identifying information has been changed.

## HACK WRITEUP: NSFWW

Firstly I realized that GitHub is adding new device verification within the week, there I tried to identify it as many developers as possible and sign into their GitHub account to access organization private repos.

I identified software developers for Company using LinkedIn. Partially poaching each one to obtain Gmail accounts, I found Bob.

Performing database lookups in hope for password reuse (or rules to be applied to their previous password) in order to login with valid credentials.

The way I got into the GitHub was due to Bob, who reused the password "BobsTiger66" (Which GitHub had told was insecure with a red banner, yet he chose to ignore it), and reused on multiple private databases and one public database (ArmorGames).

Once logged in I had to act to avoid GitHub's new ML algorithm to lock accounts out of using new IPs, so I immediately used ssh-keygen to add a new public SSH key to the user profile, I realised I had added Okta SSO preventing the clone of private repos, or to bypass this I looked at potential integrations. 

Circle is a CI/CD tool which is inherently linked to organizations via either SSH linkage or PAT's, I realised the build processes could be exploited to obtain private repos however this was not needed. You guys had added a weird implementation of Okta with AWS, producing time-limited tokens, therefore I realised these spawned whenever a new process was triggered, therefore I accessed Circle debug and managed to extract these time limited tokens, and used them to download your internal details. 

Unfortunately these tokens were not given any further privileges, therefore you got lucky or I would have gained access to RDS via CLI and a snapshot.

When I read this, I was immediately impressed by the level of effort he put into the hack. And despite the outcome, the client was, too. In the end, this breach had a happy ending, because I was able to provide intel to the customer that allowed them to identify how the breach happened, and also to put in proper safeguards to ensure that this did not happen again. That's ultimately the point, right?

Not to provide customers with a useless rewrite of generic TTPs (tactics, techniques, and procedures) regarding assumed threat actors—which is so many intelligence companies do—but to actually provide useful text for how threat actor breaches their systems.

So many companies just rely on providing existing reports on threat actor groups and never actually got to the core of how an attack happened. Some-times actually hunting down the threat actors and speaking to them directly. They are usually pretty open and willing to brag how they did it, because on some level all hackers want to be famous; and as we will see in future chapters, vanity always trumps OPSEC (operational security).

In this particular case, I was already speaking to NSFW about several other hacks he is associated with, so it there was no issue to ask how he was able to pull this off.

And if you are paying attention, you will have noticed missings and important associated with his rewrite. Common misspellings or even regional differences in spelling (e.g., organization vs organization) can be very important investigative clues that we will discuss in future chapters.
