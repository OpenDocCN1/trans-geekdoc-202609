## Big Data

In 2001, Gartner defined Big data as "Data that contains greater variety arriving in increasing volumes and with ever-higher velocity." This led to the formulation of the "three V's." Big data refers to an avalanche of structured and unstructured data that is endlessly flooding and from a variety of endless data sources. These data sets are too large to be analyzed with traditional analytical tools and technologies but have a plethora of valuable insights hiding underneath

### The "Vs" of Big data

- **Volume**: To be classified as big data, the volume of the given data set must be large. Big data refers to an avalanche of structured and unstructured data that is endlessly flooding and from a variety of endless data sources. These data sets are too large to be analyzed with traditional analytical tools and technologies but have a plethora of valuable insights hiding underneath.
- **Velocity**: The speed at which data can be gathered and acted upon.
- **Variety**: Traditionally, data sets would contain mostly structured data, but the advent of big data has given rise to new data types such as video, text, and audio.
- **Veracity**: Another "V" that must be considered for big data analysis is veracity. This refers to the "trustworthiness or the quality" of the data. For example, social media platforms containing all kinds of errors significantly reduce the reliability and accuracy of the data.
- **Value**: Data has evolved as a currency of its own. Just like traditional monetary currencies, the ultimate value of the big data is directly proportional to the insight gathered from it.

## History of Big Data

The origin of large volumes of data can be traced back to the 1960s and 1970s when the Third Industrial Revolution had just started to kick in, and the development of relational databases had begun along with the construction of data centers. The concept of big data has recently taken center stage primarily since the availability of free search engines like Google and Yahoo, free online entertainment services like YouTube, and social media platforms like Facebook. In 2005, businesses started to recognize the incredible amount of user data generated through these platforms and services, and in the same year, an open-source framework called "Hadoop" was developed to gather and analyze these large dumps available to the companies. During the same period, a non-relational or distributed database "NoSQL" started to gain popularity due to its ability to store and extract unstructured data. "Hadoop" made it possible for the companies to work with big data with high ease and at a relatively low cost. Today, with the rise of cutting edge technology, not only humans but machines also generate data. The smart device technologies like "Internet of things" (IoT) and "Internet of systems" (iOS) have skyrocketed the value of big data. Our everyday household objects and smart devices are connected to the Internet and able to track and record our usage patterns as well as our interactions with these products and feeds all data directly into the big data. The advent of machine learning technology has further increased the volume of data generated on a daily basis.
