
## 5.5 RDF vs. LPG: Choosing the Right Approach

It is not a question of which approach wins over the other, as both have their own pros and cons that lead to different strengths and weaknesses for different types of use cases. As **RDF** is an atomic, universal, and standardized representation of graph data, it is very interoperable across different **RDF** graph instances and **RDF** graph data systems. Also, **RDF** has richer capabilities in terms of semantic expressivity and inference, allowing a higher level of machine-operable semantic information to be directly incorporated in and derived from **RDF** graphs. Therefore, for use cases where interchangeability is important and for applications requiring more explicit semantic information capturing, processing, and inferencing, **RDF** may be a better choice.

On the other hand, the object-oriented and higher-level abstractions of the graph data model with **LPGs** can be more intuitive, more efficient, and easier to work with. An analogy in computer programming is that while binary arithmetic operations are atomic, universal, and interoperable, we do not need to represent every computing task as a series of such operations and program binary arithmetic operations directly. The **LPG** data structures help to make the graph data representation more compact, and graph query and analytics more efficient, saving storage and time. It is also simpler and takes less time to prepare in terms of setting up a graph database and getting acquainted with the analytics software. So, the **LPG** approach may be preferred for use cases where large-scale graph analytics are involved, where semantics and/or inferences are not in high demand, and where agility and time to production are important considerations.

### Integration of RDF and LPG

An important note to mention is that there has been some progress to integrate these two approaches to reap the benefits associated with the strengths of both graph data models. As **RDF** is more atomic and semantically more expressive than **LPG**, **LPGs** can in theory be converted to **RDF** without loss of information for graph data interchange, integration, and inference, for example. However, since **LPGs** generally do not embed a high level of semantics internally, the exported **RDF** graphs will not capture the knowledge external to **LPGs**, although inference may help to some extent.

In the other direction, frameworks and software applications have been developed to convert **RDF** graphs to **LPG** graphs without loss of information. For example, the **LPG** platform **Neo4j** has developed **neosemantics (n10s)**, a plug-in that enables the use of **RDF** in **Neo4j**, and integrated **GraphScale** to make queries more performant through more expressive reasoning. A **Neo4j** study [[3]](#chap5.xhtml#rref3) showed that this combination had faster query performance compared to **RDF**-based reasoning through **SPARQL**. Of course, there would be some additional cost to convert between **RDF** and **LPG**. **TigerGraph** can also import **RDF** graphs and ontologies for **KG** construction on their **LPG** platform.

With the existing technologies available, conversions between **RDFs** and **LPGs** can be done in both directions without loss of information, as long as the vocabularies and ontologies are well mapped between the two. There is enough flexibility in vocabulary or ontology design for **LPGs** to map all **RDF** vocabularies and ontologies in theory, and vice versa. However, whether users want or need to do so and whether vendors opt to provide full conversion functionalities are separate questions. Ultimately, **RDF/RDF\*** can serve as the common denominator for graph data exchange and graph data integration across different types of graphs — **RDF** graphs or **LPGs** (after converting to **RDF/RDF\*** representation, of course).

Recently, a **Semantic Property Graph (SPG)** [[4]](#chap5.xhtml#rref4) approach was proposed to take advantage of the best of both worlds by restructuring and converting certain **RDF** nodes and edges into attributes of a property graph, in a way that **RDF** functionalities can still be preserved and the resulting **SPG** becomes more compact in size (with respect to memory and storage) and more efficient in terms of scalable graph analytics.

### Application to Knowledge Graphs

With respect to applications to knowledge graphs specifically, the **KG** can be built using either graph data model, although the term "knowledge graph" traditionally and still commonly refers to **RDF**-based **KGs**. However, **RDF** may be more suitable to serve as a strategic tool for large-scale **EKG**, while **LPG** may be better suited as a tactical tool for more agile and localized **KG** use cases. As Jesús Barrasa of **Neo4j** put it, we need to "balance between expressivity and performance without falling into unneeded complexity". Ultimately, there are tools available to convert, connect, and integrate **LPG**-based **KGs** into a broader **EKG**.

Currently, the graph database field, in Google search interest terms (which may not represent actual usage), is dominated by **LPG** thanks to its intuitive design, faster graph traversal/pattern matching, and ease of use [[5]](#chap5.xhtml#rref5). However, for large-scale **KG** and **EKG** applications, standardization and exchangeability can be important, and semantic or reasoning capabilities can be helpful as well. That is why industry-scale and web-scale **KGs** and **EKGs** for semantic data or information integration are typically built based on **RDF** standards. It is absolutely fine to begin the **KG**-building journey with **LPG** to smoothen out the learning curve and for faster delivery, but inconsistencies due to non-standard data models, vocabularies, and query languages will accumulate as technical debt that may come back to bite you later at the time of knowledge integration across **KGs**. Therefore, **LPG**-based **KGs** need to be designed carefully across different parts of the organization in terms of identifiers, vocabulary, and ontology. Otherwise, we are just creating isolated **KGs** that may be difficult to integrate in the future. The **LPG** community needs to learn or borrow from the **RDF** community with respect to standardization, not just on a common query language but also on data structure abstraction and entity references.

## 5.6 The Role of Machine Learning

Deductive reasoning reaches specific conclusions from generalities. Inductive reasoning makes broad generalizations from specific observations. Human intelligence combines both deductive and inductive reasoning. There is no reason why machine intelligence, also known as **AI**, should be effectuated differently. For machine deductive reasoning, the **KG** is an effective tool to capture domain terminologies, taxonomies, hypotheses, and logics via vocabularies, ontologies, and inference. For machine inductive reasoning, data-driven analytics and modeling, in particular **Machine Learning (ML)**, fit the objective well. The academic community also defines a third type of reasoning for making the likeliest possible explanation from an incomplete set of observations, termed abductive reasoning. For the sake of this discussion, we group it with inductive reasoning, as machine learning answers to both.
