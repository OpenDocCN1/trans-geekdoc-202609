
## Third: Sustainable and Green Finance

From the sustainable and green finance point of view [[6]](#chap1.xhtml#rref6), FIs shall build the additional capabilities to “incorporate Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) considerations into financing decisions and drive long-term sustainable economic growth” according to MAS.^([6](#chap1.xhtml#rfn6)) FinTech companies often aim for targets at reducing cost and information asymmetry and increasing efficiency, and therefore, it is natural for financial sustainability. Some FinTech firms and projects work directly for carbon-neutrality. For instance, there are some ongoing green projects led by FinTech firms, e.g., the Ant Forest by Ant Group has planted more than 100 million trees in China and expanded to GCash Forest in the Philippines. Some firms target at carbon offsetting “RegTech” via high-resolution satellite imagery to monitor projects and/or Big Data analytics to rate projects. InsurTech firms can then use parametric solutions for the dynamic pricing of climate-related projects.

## Fourth: Unequal Access and Service Fairness

Unequal access to FinTech services may generate a new request for service fairness. Adoption of new technologies heavily depends on the economic status, age, education, and other social factors. Even when WeChat acts as the largest social communicator in China, there are still many people outside of its coverage, which presents innovation opportunities for niche markets, e.g., longevity banking optimizing wealth for those aged 60 and above, young adult financial management with a total plan covering education and career, and so on.

## Fifth: Innovation Opportunities in Mature Fields

Even in some “matured” fields, there are still innovation opportunities due to entry barriers. For example, digital payment is considered as one of the most successful innovations in FinTech in the past 20 years. It covers many financial aspects, such as digital onboarding, real-time transaction flow, personalized customer engagement, preferred payment, real-time fraud analysis, robotic process automation (`RPA`) for compliance automation, etc. However, cross-border transactions are still under development in many countries, as it requires collaborations among multiple countries. During the pandemic, payment failures are rising which meant the infrastructure still needs improvements, and many different payment methods are not compatible to each other. New innovation opportunities are still possible, e.g., the “Buy Now, Pay Later” and the omnichannel payment. Another example relates to payroll in which FinTech may make salary more flexibly accessible via a unique pay experience.

## Sixth: Demand for Personalization

Customers have strong demands for personalization. For example, personalized engagement across customer journey can provide unique experiences to customers, when they are attracted from different media sources such as social media, digital marketing, and mobile app messaging, etc. This factor drives at least three innovation aspects: (i) the refined customer profiling via `AI` and big data, (ii) customized advertising in the omnichannel, and (iii) personalized UI design for engagement.

## Seventh: IT Infrastructure and Unified Data Platform

From the IT infrastructure perspective, a unified data platform can serve as the foundation for innovation, and is a long-term goal to conquer at many FIs. When “Cloud-first” becomes a strategy at many large organizations, the so-called financial `PaaS` becomes even more important. Particularly, when many FIs select multiple cloud providers as their major suppliers, the hybrid financial `PaaS` platforms shall be considered seriously.

## Eighth: Emerging Technologies

Emerging technologies, such as quantum computing, metaverse and digital twins, brain machine interface, deoxyribonucleic acid (`DNA`) computing, and storage and hyper-automation, can generate tons of new business opportunities. For example, a digital bank based on metaverse can provide customers a unique experience in the virtual reality with their digital identities, which may lead to a boost of digital-only neobanks. Quantum computing may ask the FIs to redesign their security and `AI` systems.

## Ninth: System-wide Risk and Regulation

Last but not the least, the potentially system-wide risk associated with FinTech’s expansion could have a huge impact on financial stability. Regulations are changing rapidly to address the economic and financial needs, both locally and globally. Different countries are unavoidable to be diverse based their own economic and financial market conditions. With strong regulatory support, FinTech is booming in countries such as the US, China, the UK, Singapore, and many other countries in Asia, Africa and South America. For example, the Chinese centralized government has given a strong push in investing and defining long-term strategies via multiple 5-year plans, and China has always been at the forefront of FinTech with its huge population, talent pool, and modern infrastructures. Particularly, a stable Sino-US bilateral relation is also one key factor for the sustainable development of FinTech. `RegTech` creates innovative regulatory-driven transformations for both regulators and FIs.

## Appendix 1.1: General Banking Structure

Some big banks may divide their internal departments into two parts: (i) the group business (`GB`) and (ii) group functions (`GF`). `GB` normally contains the retail banking (may also include private banking and wealth management sometimes), wholesale banking (may also include corporate, commercial, and institutional banking), and investment banking. `GF` usually refers to all support functions, such as legal, audit, compliance/risk, HR, finance, IT, and logistics. [Figure 1.11](#chap1.xhtml#fig1-11) illustrates a common bank structure with four major business and various support functions.

![](images/p30.jpg)

**Figure 1.11.** General banking architecture

Retail banking focuses on individuals who are mainly on retail clients, while wholesale banking mainly deals with the large group of individuals or organizations or companies (e.g., other banks and FIs, government agencies, large corporations, and real estate companies). Sometimes, banks may use wholesale or corporate banking for relatively big organizations while commercial banking is used for relatively small organizations. As some small companies have similar service requests to individuals, banks may put a portion of commercial banking (e.g., self-employed businesses) into retail banking. Banks may also have institutional banking offering a comprehensive suite of products and services for large institutions. To further reduce the cost, some banks may merge part functionalities of retail and wholesale banking.
