
Looking back, the banking industry has evolved with the development of technology. Brett King outlined the evolution of banking in his book "Bank 4.0". In his view, Bank 1.0 relied primarily on physical branches as the main access point. With the introduction of ATMs and online banking services, Bank 2.0 provided banking services beyond banking hours, emphasizing the concept of self-service banking, where customers can conduct banking business anytime. When smartphones became ubiquitous, banking services also shifted to mobile devices, bringing new business models such as mobile payments and P2P. This is Bank 3.0, where customers can not only bank anytime but also anywhere.

In Bank 4.0, the focus is on the ubiquity of banking services and frictionless interaction. Customers may not interact with banks directly, but they use banking services everywhere. This is precisely the open banking business model we introduce here. Therefore, open banking not only opens up bank data and functions but also opens a new chapter for the banking industry.

## 15.3 Strategy Evolution

Since the EU first introduced open banking, regulators have been rooted in open banking. At the same time, API providers and API consumers have adopted new strategies to respond to the evolution of open banking.

### 15.3.1 Regulators

Since challenging the status quo is a significant outcome of open banking and has the potential to drive industry development, regulators naturally become core participants in driving open banking transformation. Although different regulators may view open banking from different perspectives, their responses to open banking usually fall into the following three categories: (i) enacting legislation with mandatory open banking regulations, (ii) guiding banks or exploring open banking jointly with the domestic banking industry, or (iii) letting the market lead the development of open banking on its own.

The EU, UK, and Australia have incorporated open banking into legislation, mandating banks in these countries to adopt corresponding open banking strategies. Singapore and Hong Kong have adopted a guidance and collaboration approach by issuing the API Playbook and the High-Level Open API Framework, respectively. On the other hand, regulators in China and the US have not enacted direct laws, leaving huge room for autonomous exploration by banking institutions and other technology companies.

#### EU

Open banking serves as a component of the EU's creation of a level playing field for payment service providers. On January 12, 2016, the revised Payment Services Directive (PSD2) came into force, becoming key EU legislation aimed at promoting a more integrated and efficient European payments market. It is a revision of the first Payment Services Directive (PSD1), taking emerging and new types of payment services into account. A key enhancement of PSD2 is creating a level playing field for Third Party Providers (TPPs) providing account information and/or payment initiation services to customers, achieved by mandating banks to share customer data via APIs and open payment gateways to authorized TPPs, i.e., the EU version of open banking. European Economic Area countries (28 EU member states and 3 EFTA countries) were mandated to comply with the directive and had to transpose PSD2 into their local legislation by January 13, 2018. PSD2 was planned to be fully implemented across the EEA in 2019.

#### UK

One of the most important functions of UK open banking is to maintain full market competition in the banking and financial industry. In 2016, the UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) published a report on the UK retail banking sector, concluding that larger, older banks do not have to work as hard for consumers' business as smaller, newer banks.^[1] Therefore, open banking was chosen as one of the strategies to boost competition in UK retail banking. The Open Banking Implementation Entity (OBIE) was established to drive open banking, and the nine largest UK banks by market share were required to build and adopt a common open banking service. Regarding PSD2, although the UK confirmed Brexit after the referendum on June 23, 2016, the UK chose to continue complying with PSD2 by introducing the Payment Services Regulations 2017, which came fully into force in January 2018.

#### Australia

Open banking is one of the first applications of the Consumer Data Right introduced by the Australian government. Following the Federal Treasurer's announcement that the government would introduce open banking in the 2017-2018 Budget, an Open Banking Review was conducted to recommend the best way to implement open banking in Australia. The review was published in late 2017, themed around giving customers choice, convenience, and confidence. The Australian government subsequently released a draft Consumer Data Right (CDR) Rules Framework^[2] for consultation in September 2018. It has been determined that CDR will be applied first to the banking sector. The Australian four major banks were required to provide data according to the implementation timeline, and all other banks must follow up 12 months after the dates set for the four major banks. Other sectors such as energy and telecommunications have also been proposed to follow suit.

#### Singapore

Singapore adopts a supportive, guidance-based approach to open banking. In November 2014, an initiative to drive digitalization and smart technology adoption was proposed as one of the Smart Nation strategies, marking the start of open data and payments. Subsequently in 2016, the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS), together with the Association of Banks in Singapore (ABS), released a detailed roadmap titled "Finance-as-a-Service: API Playbook", covering recommended APIs, API standards, governance, and use cases. MAS also established the Financial Industry API Register as the first landing page for open APIs from financial institutions.^[3] The API Exchange (APIX) was launched in November 2018 as a collaboration platform to bridge financial institutions and fintech companies. However, there is no legislated or mandated timeline for open banking adoption yet.

#### Hong Kong

The Hong Kong government adopts a collaborative and phased approach to advance open banking. The Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) announced its commitment to a new era of smart banking in September 2017. Open banking is part of this long-term strategy. After 6 months of consultation and receiving 41 submissions, HKMA released the "Open API Framework for the Hong Kong Banking Sector". The framework outlines 4 phases for implementing open banking in Hong Kong and provides guidance on API selection, implementation, and governance. Banks are required to provide open API implementation roadmaps to HKMA.
