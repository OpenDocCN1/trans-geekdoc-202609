
# 8.7 Conclusion

Big Data is the lifeblood for AI/ML, and an effective and efficient data management system is crucial for AI/ML application development. AI/ML in turn can offer tremendous help for data management at scale. They go hand-in-hand. Large data volume and high data velocity require scale, while variety and veracity of data require something more than rule-based systems. AI/ML fits the bill well. There are already many successful AI/ML applications for data management in the real world, bringing tangible and material efficiency/effectiveness gains. Looking into the future, there is no reason to doubt even wider adoptions. The future is bright for applying AI/ML for data management.

## Challenges in Cultivating a Data-Driven Culture

Perhaps the biggest potential challenge is culture related. Cultivating a data-driven culture within an organization already takes a lot of effort, and on top of that, we need to have another layer of mental preparedness for applying cutting-edge technologies. There are certain human behavioral factors and short-term corporate imperatives that contribute to organizational inertia in innovation. Sometimes, we may be just too accustomed to traditional ways of doing things, or in the mindset that “if something is not broken, why fix it.” Another school of thought prefers building up the data-driven culture first then worrying about more advanced technologies. The problem here is that the rest of the world is moving ahead fast and will not wait for us to catch up. If we are not proactively starting to build up the capability and seeking out the most effective and efficient solutions to the clear and present Big Data management problems we are facing today, we will never get ready for the future, let alone leading the way to shape the future.

## Data Availability and Usability for AI/ML

Even for data management tasks involving Big Data, we may still face limited data availability and usability for AI/ML purposes, which restrict our ability to train highly performant ML models. For both supervised and unsupervised learning tasks, we may need to first use data integration tools to unify training data across silos. For supervised learning, which typically requires large amounts of labeled data, there are some strategies to deal with inadequate labeled data, for example, through crowdsourcing, synthetic data, semi-supervised learning, weakly supervised learning, and active learning with a feedback loop. Transfer learning is also proven very helpful to materially reduce the number of labeled training samples for text, image, audio, and video data applications. We also need to be data frugal and establish long-term data collection strategies to proactively and holistically assess what new data items the organization needs to collect, including data that can help in the future but with no immediate use for the moment, and start accumulating such data on a day-to-day basis. We need to be careful about throwing away raw data. For example, with the traditional ETL approach for data warehousing, if we only store the final transformed data, we may lose some information embedded in the raw data, which may be costly to recover or is not recoverable at all. Historically, we keep only transformed data to reduce storage costs, but as such costs become cheaper, we need to carefully weigh storage costs against the opportunity costs of losing potentially valuable information. In addition to data records, other data assets such as metadata, analytics, charts, dashboards, BI reports, models, data APIs, and metadata on models/APIs can also be included in the collection basket. All of these may be helpful for data cataloging, lineage tracing, and ML model training in addition to any regulatory retention requirements.

## Bridging the AI/ML Knowledge Gap

The lack of understanding of AI/ML technologies by data operation and business operation teams is a legitimate concern. DataOps and business experts usually do not have the technical know-how to fully grasp the inner workings of the underlying ML algorithms. This is where data scientists on the team can help to educate other colleagues on the basics, select the right algorithms, train performant models, understand limitations, assess proper use, and monitor model performance. Additionally, as discussed previously, there are already many applications that have AI/ML built-in and are made easy/ready to use for anyone, not just the data scientists. The drive to democratize AI/ML continues by technology companies, academia research institutes, and open-source communities, and we should expect more user-friendly AI/ML tools targeted for a variety of business domains. It is a misconception that AI/ML is just for the technically privileged few. It is up to us to keep abreast of latest developments and to try them out. As many of these AI/ML applications require user feedback to improve (for example, chatbots), the more we use them and the more feedback we provide, the better their performance can be. Continuous user feedback also helps to alleviate data availability problems.

## Limitations of ML Algorithms in Data Management

The quality, accuracy, and capability of existing ML algorithms may not satisfy the requirements of all relevant data management tasks. Generally speaking, labeled data (and supervised learning) are more informative than unlabeled data (and unsupervised learning). However, for many (if not most) data management tasks, labeled data are hard to come by. Even with labeled data available, for different data labeling mechanisms and different levels of label accuracy, different machine learning approaches are required. Some general frameworks are available to handle inadequate labeled data, such as semi-supervised learning and weakly supervised learning, but they may need to adapt to specific use cases. The quality of ML algorithms for data management is subject to ongoing research and improvement. In particular, challenges remain in leveraging AI/ML to embed domain knowledge in the data wrangling process, and it may not be easy to teach machines what data to keep, change, or reformat as competently as human experts can. Knowledge graphs, to some extent, can help to provide better context, reveal more connections, and broaden the machine’s ability to find additional patterns and rules to process data more autonomously. However, the data munging process to establish data transformation rules remains primarily a human-intensive effort.

## Resource Constraints and Budget Allocation

The lack of adequate resources may impede operations teams’ ability to embark on AI/ML-assisted data management initiatives. If this is due to budget constraints, then budget allocation decisions can be informed by a cost/benefit analysis, taking into account future enterprise needs, not just the lowest cost solutions for current needs. Within a limited budget, we can start with efficiency quick-wins through some easy-to-implement AI/ML-assisted process optimization and/or automation to achieve initial resource savings. This will allow operational teams to have more time to focus on further innovation and so forth. In this way, the efficiency gained can feed on itself through a virtuous circle.

## Integration with Enterprise Systems

Data management cannot be done in isolation. It needs to be tightly integrated with business operations and risk management. AI/ML-assisted data management systems also need support from other advanced technologies to better materialize their potentials. Enterprise systems, including data management systems, need to talk to each other through technologies such as APIs, microservices, and messaging systems (e.g., `Google Cloud Pub/Sub` and `Apache Kafka`). Connections between data assets within data systems and connections among enterprise systems can be captured by enterprise knowledge graphs, which can themselves be developed and supported in combination with other AI/ML tools.
