The reasonable logic to follow is to keep $\beta$ large at the beginning of the simulation while slowly suppressing it towards zero as simulation progresses. A further improvement to the MCTS algorithm relates to the use of heuristics, which affects the moves in Selection or Expansion steps mentioned above. The motivation for such improvement is to make the simulation more efficient and potentially to cut the situation time. In Bayesian formulation, this means that Bayesian priors can be introduced into the formula for $UCT$. Alternatively, we may modify the Expansion probability. It is essential to keep in mind that such extension be done in a way that still keeps the MCTS algorithm as well while making moves, which are locally suboptimal. This is the way the algorithm can overcome the human player and find innovative strategies.

We can roughly compare the MCTS to the ways we are thinking. The human player explores the viability of a move in the game similarly as the MCTS: playing several moves placing herself into a position of the other and thus responding to her moves. The best players can form strategies several ahead. The human brain, however, does not allow to keep in memory the full tree and thus often only the best move in a given layer of the tree is kept in mind. The human builds a tree which is very narrow, not exploring all options.

On the other hand, the human relies heavily on heuristics. The ability of the MCTS to explore the suboptimal moves presents a significant improvement against human players. ## 28.3 MONTE CARLO TREE SEARCH IMPLEMENTATION – TIC-TAC-TOE

In this section, we illustrate the MCTS algorithm on the game tic-tac-toe. The game dates back to Roman times. The rules of the tic-tac-toe are straightforward. The game is played by two players on the square of $3 \times 3$. Players are taking turns, where each player places her stone on the board in every turn. We assume Player 1 is placing and Player 2 is placing x. Whoever horizontal, vertical or diagonal wins the game. The game allows for a draw, where none of the players achieves the criteria to win all the places on the board are covered.

The game is trivial as the optimal strategy from both players results in a draw. The statistical account is, however, suitable for explanation and is easy to programme. The detailed statistical account of the game as well as various extensions can be found in Beck and Beck (2008). Let us review a few numbers characterising mathematical properties of the game. The game has 9 possible playouts, in total 362,880. This can be obtained because the first player has 9 possible places to place her stone, the second player only possible moves. Let us point out that we do not consider symmetries. If the symmetries would be considered, the number is much lower. Namely, when symmetries are taken into account for calculations, there are three possible initial moves: the centre, the middle of a side and a corner.
