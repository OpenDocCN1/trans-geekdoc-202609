In the previous chapters, we have been mainly working with traditional machine learning algorithms. The focus has been on methods which learn from experience. In this section, we take a step further and focus on elements of the machine learning literature leading to algorithms denoted as AI, or Artificial Intelligence. Each of the algorithms explored in this section aims to learn on the fly from its own experience, from its successes and mistakes, rather than from the provided data set.

The key concept for this chapter is the distinction between exploration and exploitation. The machine learning methods introduced so far have been constructed such that they exploit the knowledge learnt from the training data set. Exploration steps, on the other hand, for actions done by the algorithm, which explores new paths. The objective is to learn a profitable path and explore it. The outcome of the exploration step can be, however, a choice which is not optimal and will be avoided in the following steps. The outcome of exploration is thus helps the algorithms to deal with complex/or changing environments and learn their characteristics. Exploitation then follows the algorithm gains knowledge about the environment.

We start with a multi-armed bandit problem, which represents a textbook example of finding an optimal choice of slot machines in the casino without having prior knowledge about their performance. Then, we extend the introduced concepts and implement the Monte Carlo Tree search, which is an algorithm behind many game engines. We implement a particular method for tic-tac-toe. Further, we discuss the extension of the Monte Carlo Tree Search leading to AlphaGo engine.

The multi-armed bandit problem is defined as follows. Imagine that you are at a casino where are some slot machines. You can play one slot machine at a time. You do not see the other players using the slot machines and their outcome. Each slot machine is different and may provide different pay-out. A question has to answer is the optimal strategy to follow once you decide to play. You can play every game on a randomly chosen slot machine, or stick to one slot machine at the time. The question is whether there exists a more profitable strategy to follow.

Let us practise the problem mathematically. Let us consider K distribution functions $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_K$ over real numbers. We define the probability distribution functions with random variables $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_K$. Each random variable $X_i$ has a mean $\mu_i$ and is assumed to be $\sigma^2$-Gaussian:

```
$E[exp(\lambda(X_i - \mu_i))] \le \exp(\left(\frac{\lambda^2\sigma^2}{2}\right)) \quad (28.1)$
```

## 28.1.1 Analytic Solutions

First, let us mention a few theoretical achievements in this field. Lai and Robbins (1985) considered the reward to be generated from an exponential family of distributions with one parameter and showed the existence of policies with the fastest convergence rate. Katekhakis and Robbins (1995) used the case of reward functions which are normally distributed. Further, literature focuses on the cases when reward functions have been affected by exogenous variables. Burnetas and Katekhakis (1996) considered the case of reward being driven by unknown variables and showed the strategies with maximum convergence towards optimal policies. This was further elaborated by Burnetas and Katekhakis (1997), Tewari and Bartlett (2008), or Honda and Takemura (2011), among others.
