The QR algorithm has an obvious weakness in the underlying assumption imposed on the matrix A. If the matrix does not have distinct eigenvalues, the method fails. # 16.2.3 Inverse Iteration

Let us assume that we are equipped with the knowledge of the complete set of distinct real eigenvalues. Our objective is to find corresponding eigenvectors. We will focus on the inverse (power) iteration algorithm. The algorithm is an iterative procedure for a given approximation of the eigenvalue finds the corresponding eigenvector, or its approximation. Let consider the square p× matrix A with true eigenvalues λ1,...,λp. Furthermore, denote the approximation of the eigenvalue by indexing the variable with bar, i.e. λ' being a closure to λ.

The following procedure requires to associate eigenvalue λ' to the matrix A.

+   * Calculate the updated vector: v_{k+1} = (A-λ'Ip)⁻¹v_k.
* The algorithm proceeds in iterative steps, with k denoting the iteration step, at k = 0:
* Normalise vector v_{k+1} by its norm: v_{k+1} = v_{k+1}/||v_{k+1}||
* Vector v_{k+1} converges to v'.

The numerical procedure requires us to calculate v_{k+1} = (A-λ'Ip)⁻¹v_k. This can be done either by solving

> (A-λ'Ip)v_k = v_k. (16.21)

or calculating the inverse of A-λ'Ip. Both approaches are valid; they differ by the numerical implementations.
Furthermore, the closer λ' is to λ, the faster the convergence. The algorithm can for good approximations of eigenvalue converge in few steps. If, on the other hand, the approximation is not good, the algorithm can converge to an eigenvector of another eigenvalue. The iteration also involves normalisation. We have the L1 norm, where other normalisations are possible as well. The normalisation ensures that convergence proceeds in terms of the orientation of the iterated vector and prevents explosion of the vector in its norm.
