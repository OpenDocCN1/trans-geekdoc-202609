## Outlier Detection

Outlier detection, or anomaly detection, is a data science technique aiming to identify observations which do not fit into the data set and are way too different from the “normal” data. In general, there are two reasons why such nonconformity in the data may exist. First, the anomalous data points can be produced by some technical error, where, for, the data recording technique failed, or the survey has not been correctly scanned, among others. A particular example can be limit order book data, where bid price is recorded as –999. Such a datum corresponds to a failed printer than to a situation where the technically possible in the particular case is negative. Such a transaction would not be technically possible in properly working venues.

Second, the more critical reason corresponds to actual anomalous behaviour in the data. This may correspond, for example, to cases of bank frauds which commit the unlawful act, often tries to make the transaction look unusual. However, it is not possible as the knowledge of transactions is not publicly available. The outliers may appear in the data analysis as records with the unusual size of the transaction, timing of trades, or their frequency do not fit into the common patterns.

The outlier detection is by its nature a data science technique, where the objective being “outlier” is rather vaguely defined. It is without surprise that outlier detection technique mimics the machine learning techniques. The non-supervised outlier detection works with unlabelled data. The majority of data is assumed to be valid, and the property of being an outlier is understood to the majority. Such a method can be illustrated by a PCA technique for dimensionality reduction. The reduced set of components replaces the data. Then, every observation recorded back from the reduced set, and the error of the reduction is recorded. The outliers are then observations with significant errors.

The supervised outlier detection assume that we have a labelled set, where data are labelled as “normal” or “outlier”. One can then formulate a model, either theoretical or econometric, for “being outlier” and estimate the parameters. The typical choice can be a logistic regression model, which is well suited for modelling binary dependent variables. The usual problem for such techniques rests in the need for correct classification and presence of outliers, which are often very rare. The techniques thus have to be able to deal with unbalanced data sets, where one of the classes is rare.

The local outlier factor (LOF) is an algorithm that measures the local density deviation of a given data point with respect to local densities of its neighbors. The local density of a point is the average density of its neighbors. The LOF of a point is the ratio of the average local density of its neighbors to the local density of the point itself. An LOF value greater than 1 indicates that the point has a lower density than its neighbors and is likely an outlier. An LOF value close to 1 indicates that the point has a similar density to its neighbors. An LOF value less than 1 indicates that the point is in a denser region than its neighbors and is not an outlier.

The LOF algorithm is particularly useful for detecting outliers in datasets with varying densities. Traditional global outlier detection methods may fail to identify outliers in low-density regions if they are not extreme enough compared to high-density regions. LOF, on the other hand, compares the density of a point to the density of its neighbors, making it capable of detecting outliers regardless of the local density.

The LOF algorithm is defined as follows:

1. Calculate the distance between all two points in the dataset.
2. Calculate the $k$-distance for each point, which is the distance to its $k$-th nearest neighbor (k-NN).
3. Calculate the reachability distance of point $A$ from point $B$:
   $reachability-dist(A, B) = \max(k\text{-distance}(B), dist(A, B))$
4. Calculate the local reachability density (LRD) of point $A$:
   $LRD(A) = 1 / (\frac{1}{|N(A)|} \sum_{B \in N(A)} reachability-dist(A, B))$, where $N(A)$ is the set of $k$-nearest neighbors of $A$.
5. Calculate the local outlier factor (LOF) of point $A$:
   $LOF(A) = \frac{\sum_{B \in N(A)} \frac{LRD(B)}{LRD(A)}}{|N(A)|}$

The LOF algorithm is widely used in various fields such as anomaly detection in network monitoring, fraud detection, and intrusion detection. Its ability to handle local outliers makes it a powerful tool in data science and analysis.
