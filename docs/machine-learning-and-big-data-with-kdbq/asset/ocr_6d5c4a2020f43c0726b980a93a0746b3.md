We can immediately say that $v$ is an eigenvector, any multiple of such a vector is an eigenvector with the well as the same eigenvalue, or

$Av = \lambda_p v \Rightarrow Aav = \lambda_p av.\quad (16.3)$

Further, eq. (16.2) can be rewritten as:

$(A - \lambda_p I)v = 0,\quad (16.4)$

where $I_p$ is an identity matrix. The eq. (16.4) has a non-trivial solution $v$ if the matrix $(A - \lambda_p I)$ is singular. For non-singular matrices, only trivial solutions can satisfy the equality.

To be precise, the matrix is said to be singular when it is not invertible. In other words, some of its columns can be written as a linear combination of the remaining columns. This implies that such a matrix is not of the full rank. An equivalent definition is that its determinant is equal to zero (see the Appendix to this chapter for more about determinants and their implication in q). We can say that for square matrix $A$ the vector $v$ is an eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda$ if

$|A - \lambda_p I| = 0,\quad (16.5)$

where $|A|$ stands for the determinant of the square matrix $A$. This equation is known as a characteristic polynomial. This comes from the fact that the determinant in eq. (16.5) is a polynomial of $p$ in $\lambda$.

It is due to the fundamental theorem of algebra which we can rewrite eq. (16.5) as a product of:

$|A - \lambda_p I| = (\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2) \cdots (\lambda - \lambda_p).\quad (16.6)$

where $\lambda_i$ are roots of the polynomial. The roots can be all distinct or degenerate, and they can take complex values. When the root is degenerate, i.e. there is sequence of $(\lambda - \lambda_i)^{p_i}$, we say that the root has non-trivial algebraic multiplicity, $p_i$. The sum of all multiplicities is equal to the rank of the matrix.

It is further to introduce the notion of null space. For each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, we define the subspace

$v_i : (A - \lambda_i I)v_i = 0.\quad (16.7)$

This space of all $v_i$'s is further known as eigenspace of $\lambda_i$. The eigenspace is closed under addition and multiplication as. Thus, if we have two vectors $v_i^1$ and $v_i^2$, eigenvectors of $\lambda_i$, then their linear combination is also an eigenvector of $\lambda_i$.

The consequence of this observation is that for square complex matrix A, the entire vector space (dimension p) can be written as a Cartesian product of all eigenspaces corresponding to eigenvalues. Furthermore, any $p$-dimensional vector can be written as a linear combination of eigenvectors of A. In other words, linearly independent eigenvectors of A form the eigenbasis of a $p$-dimensional vector space.
