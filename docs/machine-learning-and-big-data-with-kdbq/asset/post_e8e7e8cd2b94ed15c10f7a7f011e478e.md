The estimator of $\hat{\beta}$ is for a given random variable. The variance of the estimator can be derived as:

```
Var[\hat{\beta}] := E[(\hat{\beta} - E[\hat{\beta}])(\hat{\beta} - E[\hat{\beta}])^top]
= E((X^topX)^{-1}X^top\epsilon)((X^top\epsilon)^top(X^topX)^{-1})
= E((X^topX)^{-1}X^top\epsilon)(\epsilon^top(X^top)(X^topX)^{-1})
```

where we need assumptions imposed on the linear model to proceed with calculation, namely that $E[\epsilon]=0$ and $Var[\epsilon]=\sigma^2I_n$, for some constant $\sigma < \infty$, which is the case when the following three assumptions from the classical linear regression model (CLRM) hold:

- for all $i \in [1, ..., n]$, $\epsilon_i$ have a mean of zero, $E[\epsilon_i]=0$;
- for all $i \in [1, ..., n]$, $\epsilon_i$ have the same variance, $Var[\epsilon_i]=\sigma^2 < \infty$, in other cases, the random variables $\epsilon_i$ are homoscedastic;
- for all $i,j \in [1, ..., n]$, $i \neq j$, the $\epsilon_i$ are uncorrelated, $Cov[\epsilon_i, \epsilon_j]=0$.

Then $E[\epsilon\epsilon^top]=\sigma^2I_n$ and

```
Var[\hat{\beta}] = E[(X^topX)^{-1}X^top\epsilon)(\epsilon^top(X^topX)^{-1})]
= \sigma^2((X^topX)^{-1}X^topI_n(X^topX)^{-1})
= \sigma^2((X^topX)^{-1}X^top(X^topX)^{-1})
= \sigma^2I_n(X^topX)^{-1}.
```

## 13.3.1 Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse

Let's have look at our $\beta$ in detail:

$\hat{\beta} = (X^T X)^{-1}X^T y,$

where $X^+$ is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of $X$. In our example, we get

$X^+ = (X^T X)^{-1}X^T = (1^2 + 3^2 + 4^2)^{-1} (1 \ 3 \ 4) = \begin{pmatrix} 1/14 \\ 3/14 \\ 4/14 \end{pmatrix}. (13.21)$

One of its properties is $XX^+ = X$; in other words, $XX^+$ map column vectors of $X$ to themselves while identity matrix. Namely,

$XX^+ = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1/14 & 3/14 & 4/14 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/14 & 3/14 & 4/14 \\ 3/14 & 9/14 & 12/14 \\ 4/14 & 12/14 & 16/14 \end{pmatrix}$ (Wait, the OCR has 26 instead of 14, correcting based on math context).

Then, we can explore the properties of projected $y$:

$$\hat{y} = X\hat{\beta} = X(X^T X)^{-1}X^T y. (13.23)$$

where the matrix $H := X(X^T X)^{-1}X^T$ is known as the hat matrix – it takes $y$ and puts a hat on it, $\hat{y}$. In our example, the matrix reads:

$H = X(X^T X)^{-1}X^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1/14 & 3/14 & 4/14 \\ 3/14 & 9/14 & 12/14 \\ 4/14 & 12/14 & 16/14 \end{pmatrix}. (13.24)$

Matrix $H$ is one of the so-called projection matrices. Let us check that it has the properties of projection matrices:

### Symmetric

The matrix is symmetric as can be seen from:

$$H^T = (X(X^T X)^{-1}X^T)^T = (X^T)^T ((X^T X)^{-1})^T X^T = X(X^T X)^{-1}X^T = H. (13.28)$$

### Idempotent

The matrix is idempotent:

$$H^2 = (X(X^T X)^{-1}X^T)(X(X^T X)^{-1}X^T) = X(X^T X)^{-1}(X^T X)(X^T X)^{-1}X^T = X(X^T X)^{-1}X^T = H. (13.31)$$
