
During this phase, the choice between available options is made randomly using the uniform distribution. At the end of the exploration phase, the outcome is evaluated and the choice with the best outcome is chosen. The moves in the exploratory phase are chosen based on the best average outcome. The option chosen in the exploratory phase may not be constant as the performance of the best option at the end of the exploratory phase may deteriorate during the exploration phase.

## Random exploration greedy algorithm

The second algorithm extends the first one by placing the proportion of the exploratory choices randomly during the iteration. In particular, at every step, we make an exploratory step with probability $\epsilon$, and an exploration step other. The exploratory step is as described above. Distributing the exploratory phase across the entire strategy improves the ability of the strategy to escape local maxima and provides a check against heteroscedastic behaviour of the system, where choices are made.

## Decreasing exploration greedy algorithm

The exploration in the algorithms described in the preceding paragraphs represents learning. The rate $\epsilon$ can be understood as a learning rate of the algorithm to explore the possible options. It is then intuitive to formulate an algorithm where the learning rate decreases over time. Such an algorithm will explore more during the initial stages, and the learning rate will be preserved during the entire lifetime of the strategy to preserve the ability to escape the local maxima. The functional dependency of $\epsilon$ on the number of iterations passed is a matter of choice and object of study.

## Adaptive exploration greedy strategy

The change of the learning rate during the progress of the strategy as introduced in the previous algorithm depends solely on the number of draws already taken. It may be optimal, though, to make it depend on the outcome of the strategies as well. If the algorithm learns a stable solution, it may be a good to slow down the learning, i.e. less exploration, and exploit instead. The stable solution can be defined using the variability of the outcome so far realised. This is the basis of the VBDE algorithm in Tokic and Palim (2011).

## 28.1.3 Confidence-Based Algorithms

The popular group of algorithms to solve the multi-armed bandit problems are confidence-based algorithms with optimism. The idea can be outlined as follows. At any point in time, we believe that the true mean $\mu_i$ of each arm $i$ is:

$$\hat{\mu}_i \in [\mu_i - \xi_i, \mu_i + \xi_i], \quad (28.6)$$

where $\hat{\mu}_i$ is an estimator of the true mean and $\xi_i$ denotes the confidence interval bounds. The width of the confidence interval decreases with time, i.e. $\xi_i$ as $t \downarrow$. Then, the value of arm $i$ is optimistically $\mu_i + \xi_i$. At time $t$, when we need to decide between arms, we choose one with the highest optimistic value:

$$i = \arg \max_{i} \mu_i + \xi_i. (28.7)$$

Auer et al. (2002a) elaborated the outlined approach and introduced the Upper Confidence Bound algorithm. In order to formulate Upper Confidence Bound its convergence properties, we need to impose an assumption on the distribution of the individual arms: every distribution is $\sigma^2$-sub-Gaussian. Namely, we define the running average of the $i$-th arm at time $t$:

$$\mu_{i,t} = \frac{1}{\#(i,t)} \sum_{s\le t} X_{i,s}. (28.8)$$

where $(i,t)$ stands for the number of instances the strategy chosen has been $i$, and $X_{i,t}$ being the realisations of the random variable $X_i$ in such cases. The Upper Confidence Bound algorithms are based on the theory which says:

$$P \ \mu_{i,t} \ge \mu_i + \frac{\sigma^2 \log \frac{1}{\delta}}{\#(i,t)} \le \delta \ || P \ \mu_{i,t} \le \mu_i - \frac{\sigma^2 \log \frac{1}{\delta}}{\#(i,t)} \le \delta, (28.9)$$

for positive real $\delta$, if the bandit is played sufficiently many times, the probability we choose the one is going to zero. Auer et al. (2002a) for proofs. The algorithm then follows these lines:

- Provide $\sigma^2$ and a sequence of probabilities $\delta_1, \delta_2, \delta_3, \dots$
- Initialise the algorithm by choosing every arm $i$ once.
- Choose a strategy such that every time $t$, the index $i$ results from:

$$i = \arg \max_{i} \mu_{i,t} + \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2 \log \frac{1}{\delta_i}}{\#(i,t)}} (28.10)$$

Practically, if the reward of each arm is $[0,1]$, the parameter $\sigma^2=1/4$ be a suitable choice using Hoeffding's lemma. The convenient choice for the sequence of probabilities is:

$$\delta_t = \frac{1}{t^2}. (28.11)$$

Such a choice of parameters provides an algorithm that optimises the Upper Confidence Bound and is adaptive enough to avoid some pathological cases which may occur – for instance, if one arm dominates some other arms, then under certain circumstances the regret of the algorithm may be infinite. This will cause pathological behaviour as a difference between an optimal arm and any other arm will be infinite.

## 28.1.4 Bayesian Algorithms

Another class of algorithms to solve the multi-armed bandit problems are Bayesian algorithms. As the name suggests, the Bayesian strategies assume some prior knowledge about the bandits' distributions. This can be used to improve exploration strategies and work more accurately with information and regret.

In order to implement the strategy, we further assume that probability functions corresponding to bandits' arms are given as $P = \{P_{\theta\}_{\theta\in\Theta}\}$ with being parameter $\theta$. In the simple settings, it can correspond to a $K$-dimensional real vector. The prior distribution imposed on the parameter space is just by $\pi(\theta)$. In order to solve the problem, we define the Bayesian regret:

$$\text{Reg}(A, \lambda, \pi) = E_{\pi} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^{n} \lambda(A, \theta) - \lambda(A^*, \theta) \right], \quad (28.12)$$

where $A^*$ is the minimiser of the loss $\lambda$. We have denoted the expectations with index $\pi$ in order to stress the dependence on the prior distribution.

The textbook example for the bandit problem, which nicely illustrates the meaning of the parameter set and choice of $\lambda$. The Bernoulli bandit procedure is a setup where the probability functions modeling the reward of each arm $i$ are given by Bernoulli distributions with parameter $\theta_i$. The pay-off of arm $i$ is either 1, with probability $\theta_i$, or otherwise. To solve the Bernoulli multi-armed bandit problem means to find the arm with the highest $\hat{\theta}_i$. The loss function for the Bernoulli bandit can be chosen using $\lambda(i, \theta) = -\theta_i$.

The schema for the Bayesian algorithm then follows along the following lines:

- Provide a prior distribution $\pi$ for $\Theta$, connected to probabilities $P = \{P_{\theta\}_{\theta\in\Theta}$
- At each step $t$, choose prior distribution $\pi_t$ based on past realisations and corresponding losses.
- Draw $\hat{\theta}_i$, from the prior distribution $\pi_t$
- Choose an action that minimises the loss $\lambda(i, \hat{\theta}_i)$ over a set of admissible strategies.
- Obtain the outcome.

## Thompson sampling

The simple implementation of the Bayesian algorithm is to follow Thompson (1933) and take prior distribution $\pi$, equal to the posterior distribution of $\theta$, conditional on the history of all observed pay-offs:

$$\pi_t(\theta) = \pi(\theta|_{\{A_0, X_0, \dots, A_t, X_t\}). \quad (28.13)$$

The Thompson sampling procedure for the Bayesian algorithm at every time step $t$ reads:

- #(i, t, 1) the number of cases when arm $i$ was chosen and resulted in a pay-off of 1, and analogously, #(i, t, 0) the number of cases when arm $i$ was chosen and resulted in a pay-off 0.
- For each arm $i$, the parameter $\theta_{i,j}$ is drawn as: $\theta_{i,j} \sim \text{Beta}(#(i,t,1), \#(i,t,0))$.
- Choose an action $i$ that maximizes $\theta_{i,j}$.
- Observe the pay-off and update the counts.
