3. When performance on our raw data becomes a consideration – even when we opt-mise our historical queries and have a robust database schema – consider a separate database with cleaned or aggregate data.

4. Usually you should use an API even if it is a simple abstraction. The abstraction means that the data storage method or source can always change without impacting your data usage.

Elaborate on points 2 and 4 above, instead of writing complicated select statements with explicit column names or where clauses may change over time, and then building a ton of dependencies on your data processing/ml algorithms, simply always write a function whose implementation can change over time, but this calls always has the input/output you expect based on your use case and specification. An excellent way and we do not want to refactor/rewrite all your dependent code, is to use a dictionary as we allow some flexibility on the input and output of the API, while our needs expand and we follow. This is the approach we follow throughout this book. In many cases, we present implementations of a particular function where we aim to preserve the same form of input/output and add the keys into the dictionary whenever needed.

A final point in terms of design is the following: Keep your filter code and data access separate from the calculation and analysis logic. Combining the two mingles different concepts and causes both confusion and also maintenance headaches when the codebase grows or is shared with others.
