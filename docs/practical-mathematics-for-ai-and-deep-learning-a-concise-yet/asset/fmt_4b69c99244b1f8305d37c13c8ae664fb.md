
## 2. A family of deterministic function $f(z)$, parametrized by vector $\theta$.

## 3. $f$ is deterministic, but $z$ is a random variable, and hence, $f(z)$ represents a random variable.

We have to optimize parameter $\theta$ such that we can sample from a simple distribution $p(z)$ and then $f(z)$, with high probability resembles X. Therefore, we can represent the random variable $f(z)$ by the conditional notation X|Z because first we choose Z and then **X** is generated based on that. We can call Z as the *latent variable* or *hidden variables*.

By law of total probability $p(x) = \int p(x|z)p(z)dz$, where we can choose the output distribution to be Gaussian: $p(x|z) = \mathcal{N}(f(z), \sigma^2 I)$, the covariance equal to the identity matrix I times some scalar $\sigma^2$. This $\sigma$ can be chosen based on the factor we use in the transformation to add noise.

Now, for any complicated data distributions also, we can estimate the model parameters $\theta$ by maximizing $p(x)$. Instead of finding $f(z)$ by inspection, we let it be a neural network. Maximizing $p(x)$ is equivalent to minimizing $-\log p(x)$, that is, MLE estimation.

$$
p(x) = \int p(x|z)p(z)dz
$$

Like before, we can choose $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. We can estimate $P(X)$ approximately by taking very large sample of size N from Z say $\{z^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ and then averaging the probabilities as follows:
$$
p(x) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N p(x|z^{(i)})
$$

However, for most of the samples z from this distribution, the probability of generating a data point X is very low. To understand this better, let’s go back to the circular distribution example. Take a data point X from this distribution, as shown in *[Figure 12.5](#c12.xhtml#fig12_5)*. x can only be generated by z from the shaded region in the left. For all points z outside the shaded region, p(x|z) is very small. This will make the estimation process intractable as the log of these very small quantities will lead to computational overflow. Refer to the following figure:

![](images/Image49067.jpg)

**Figure 12.5:** *Subset of values of z that can generate a given x*

Given a data point X, is there a way we can restrict the choice of samples z? This can be done by defining the conditional probability distribution $q(z|x)$. Now, by Bayes rule, $p(z|x) = \frac{p(x|z)p(z)}{p(x)}$. This is computationally hard. Let’s approximate $p(z|x)$ by another distribution $q(z|x)$ which we can choose to be a tractable distribution. We can define the parameters of $q(z|x)$ such that they are very similar to $p(z|x)$ and use them to perform approximate inference of the intractable distribution.

The Kullback–Leibler divergence or KL divergence is a qualitative measure of how one probability distribution p(x) is different from another probability distribution q(x). Here, we can design $q(z|x)$ such that KL $(q(z|x) || p(z|x))$ is minimum.

$$
D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z|x)) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}[\log q(z|x) - \log p(z|x)]
$$

By product rule of probability, we have $p(x, z) = p(x|z)p(z)$. Substituting this in the preceding KL divergence equation, we have the following:

$$
D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z|x)) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}[\log q(z|x) - \log p(x|z) - \log p(z) + \log p(x)]
$$

Using properties of log function, $\log(AB) = \log(A) + \log(B)$ and $\log(1/A) = -\log(A)$, we have the following:

$$
D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z|x)) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}[\log q(z|x) - \log p(x|z) - \log p(z)] + \log p(x)
$$

Now, we can take out the terms independent of z outside the summation as a constant.

$$
D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z|x)) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}[\log q(z|x) - \log p(x|z) - \log p(z)] + \log p(x)
$$

As $q(z)$ is a probability distribution over z, $\int q(z|x) dz = 1$. Let’s represent $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}[\log p(x|z) - \log q(z|x) + \log p(z)]$ by the function $\mathcal{L}(x)$. So, we can rewrite the preceding equation as follows:

$$
\log p(x) - D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z|x)) = \mathcal{L}(x)
$$

Since $D_{KL} \ge 0$, $\mathcal{L}(x) \le \log p(x)$ and KL divergence is non-negative quantity, we can write $\mathcal{L}(x) \le \log p(x)$, that is, $\mathcal{L}(x)$ is a lower bound of $\log p(x)$.

So, maximizing $\mathcal{L}(x)$ means maximizing log likelihood of data: $\log(P(X))$. Now onward, we will try to simplify this lower bound function to derive our objective function.

We can write $\mathcal{L}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}[\log p(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z))$. Refer to the following figure:

![](images/Image49116.jpg)

**Figure 12.6:** $\mathcal{L}(x)$ is a lower bound of p(x). Maximizing $\mathcal{L}(x)$ means maximizing log likelihood of data: $\log(p(x))$.

Therefore:

$$
\mathcal{L}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}[\log p(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z))
$$

Therefore:

$$
\mathcal{L}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}[\log p(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z))
$$

Pictorially, we can represent equation (II) as shown in *[Figure 12.6](#c12.xhtml#fig12_6)*.

Here, *E* represents expectation of $\log p(x|z)$ over q(z). This technique of approximating intractable integrals the way we did is called Variational Bayesian method in general, and this loss function $\mathcal{L}(x)$ being a lower bound is known as the variational lower bound or **Evidence Lower Bound** (**ELBO**).

We can assume q(z) to be multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean $\mu$ and diagonal covariance matrix $\Sigma$, where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are n-dimensional vectors. Suppose we find a deterministic function $g_\phi(x)$ that transforms X to the mean and variance of the tractable distribution q(z). We could have directly generated z instead of mean and variance. However, as $g_\phi(x)$ is a deterministic function, we will always get the same fixed z for a given X. Having mean and variance will enable us to take many samples from this distribution of the latent variables z. $g_\phi(x)$ can be represented as a neural network with the parameter as shown in *[Figure 12.7](#c12.xhtml#fig12_7)*:

![](images/Image49147.jpg)

**Figure 12.7:** *Using DNN to learn latent distribution q(z|x)*

Here, q(z|x) will restrict the choice of z, and hence, we can estimate our required parameter $\theta$ by maximizing log likelihood $\log(p(x))$. Refer to the following figure:

![](images/Image49155.jpg)

**Figure 12.8:** VAE block diagram: here, we have changed $\mathcal{L}(x)$ for ease in computation, which is described later.

So, now we can jointly estimate both the parameters $\theta$ and $\phi$ of the neural net topology in *[Figure 12.8](#c12.xhtml#fig12_8)*. To train this topology, our objective function is $\mathcal{L}(x)$.

We saw that $\mathcal{L}(x)$ has two parts. The second part of $\mathcal{L}(x)$ is the KL divergence, which enforces the distribution q(z) to take a certain form. It represents KL-divergence between two multivariate Gaussian distributions $q(z|x)$ and $p(z)$.

In general, KL-divergence between two *k*-dimensional multivariate Gaussians $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \Sigma_1)$ and $\mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \Sigma_2)$ is given by the following:

Here, $\Sigma_1 = \text{diag}(\sigma_1^2)$, $\Sigma_2 = \text{diag}(\sigma_2^2)$

$\Sigma_1^{-1} = \text{diag}(1/\sigma_1^2)$ and $\Sigma_2 = \text{diag}(\sigma_2^2)$, and hence, $\text{tr}(\Sigma_2^{-1}\Sigma_1) = \sum \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_2^2}$

$(\mu_2 - \mu_1)^T \Sigma_2^{-1} (\mu_2 - \mu_1) = \sum \frac{(\mu_2 - \mu_1)^2}{\sigma_2^2}$

So, all terms have sum over *k*, and thus, the second term in simplifies to the following:

$$
D_{KL} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^k \left( \frac{\sigma_{1j}^2}{\sigma_{2j}^2} + \frac{(\mu_{2j} - \mu_{1j})^2}{\sigma_{2j}^2} - 1 + \log \frac{\sigma_{2j}^2}{\sigma_{1j}^2} \right)
$$

Implementing this is simple, as shown in the following code. Let `z_mean` and `z_log_var` represent the tensors $\mu$ and $\log \sigma^2$ (as shown in *[Figure 12.8](#c12.xhtml#fig12_8)*) of dimension *k*=10, initialized randomly; then:

```python
1. import tensorflow as tf
2. z_mean = tf.random.uniform([1,10])
3. z_log_var = tf.random.uniform([1,10])
4. kl_loss = tf.reduce_sum(
5.      -0.5 * (z_log_var - tf.exp(z_log_var) - tf.square(z_mean) + 1),
6.               axis=1)
7. kl_batch_loss = tf.reduce_mean(kl_loss)
```
