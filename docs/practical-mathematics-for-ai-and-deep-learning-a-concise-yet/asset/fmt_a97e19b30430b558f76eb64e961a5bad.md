
# Expectation of jointly distributed variables

We can represent $n$ jointly distributed random variables as a random vector. This random variable $X$ is vector valued and assumes values in the Euclidean space. The multivariate density of this random vector is represented by $f_X(x)$ or simply as $f(x)$, where $x$ is a vector in the Euclidean space.

We have defined the mathematical expectation of a single variable in the previous sections. For two jointly distributed random variables $X$ and $Y$, we can define the expectation of a function $g(X, Y)$ as follows:

$$E[g(X, Y)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x, y) f_{X,Y}(x, y) \, dx \, dy, \text{ if } X,Y \text{ are continuous}$$

And

$$E[g(X, Y)] = \sum_x \sum_y g(x, y) P(X=x, Y=y)$$

The joint central moment ($r^{th}$ central moment of $X$ and $s^{th}$ central moment of $Y$) is denoted by $\mu_{rs}$ and is defined as follows:

$$\mu_{rs} = E[(X - \mu_X)^r (Y - \mu_Y)^s]$$

1.  If $r=1, s=1$, we call it covariance and is denoted by $\text{COV}(X, Y)$. So:
    $$\text{COV}(X, Y) = E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)]$$

2.  If $r=2, s=0$, the central joint moment reduces to variance of $X$, that is, $\mu_{20} = \text{Var}(X)$

3.  If $r=0, s=2$, the central joint moment reduces to variance of $Y$, that is, $\mu_{02} = \text{Var}(Y)$

The $\text{COV}(X,Y)$ can be equivalently written as $\text{COV}(X,Y) = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]$. The proof of this is simple.
$\text{COV}(X,Y) = E[(XY - XE[Y] - YE[X] + E[X]E[Y])] = E[XY] - E(X)E[Y] - E[Y]E[X] + E[X]E[Y] = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]$.

**Example**: Let's consider two random variables $X$ and $Y$ having joint density function:

$$f_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-\rho^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\left[x^2 - 2\rho xy + y^2\right]\right)$$

The density is plotted in [Figure 4.18](#c04.xhtml#fig4_18), where each line represents contour of constant density parallel to the line $y = x$. The lighter shades represent high density regions. As we move away from the origin, the density decreases parallel to the line $y = -x$. (However, the white space between lines doesn't not represent high density regions. The density varies parallel to the lines there as well.) Refer to the following figure:

Let's now calculate the marginal probability density functions:

$$f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y) \, dy = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}$$
$$f_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y) \, dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-y^2/2}$$

We can also calculate the conditional distributions $f_{Y|X}(y|x)$ and $f_{X|Y}(x|y)$:

$$f_{Y|X}(y|x) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)}$$

Knowing the marginal distributions, we can also calculate the expectations of the individual variables:

$$E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f_X(x) \, dx = 0$$

Similarly, $E[Y] = 0$. Now, we can calculate the variance of the two variables as follows:

$$\text{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = 1$$

To compute the covariance of the two variables, we need $E[XY]$:

$$E[XY] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} xy f_{X,Y}(x,y) \, dx \, dy = \rho$$

Hence, we can now calculate the covariance using the following:

$$\text{Cov}(X,Y) = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y] = \rho$$

**Theorem**: Two independent random variables are uncorrelated. $X, Y$ are independent implies $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x)f_Y(y)$. However, the converse is not true, that is, two uncorrelated variables need not be independent.

Example: $X \in \{-4, -3, -1, 1, 3, 4\}$, $Y = X^2$. Here $P(X=x) = 1/6$.

However, $E[XY] = \frac{1}{6} \times [-4 \times 16 - 3 \times 9 - 1 \times 1 + 1 \times 1 + 3 \times 9 + 4 \times 16] = 0$ and $E[X] = 0$.

Therefore, $\text{COV}(X,Y) = 0$, and hence, $\rho_{XY} = 0$, but $X$, $Y$ are not independent as $Y = X^2$.

# Transformation of a random variable

Let $X$ be a random variable and $g(.)$ be a function; then, $Y = g(X)$ is also a random variable. Here, $X$ and $Y$ are random vectors, and hence, $g$ must be vector field. We can represent $g$ as $g = (g_1, g_2, ..., g_d)$ such that:

$$Y_i = g_i(X_1, X_2, ..., X_d) \quad \text{for } i = 1, ..., d$$

Where, $g_i$ for all $i$ are continuous differentiable functions.

If $f_X(x)$ represents the joint density function of $X$, then the joint distribution function of $Y$ is given by $f_Y(y) = f_X(g^{-1}(y)) |J|$. Here, $J$ denotes the Jacobian matrix of the random variables, and $|J|$ represents the determinant of the Jacobian, and hence, is a polynomial.

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial g_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial g_1}{\partial x_d} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial g_d}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial g_d}{\partial x_d} \end{bmatrix}$$

If $X$ and $Y$ are real valued random variables, then the Jacobian simplifies to simple derivative; we write:

$$f_Y(y) = f_X(g^{-1}(y)) \left| \frac{d}{dy} g^{-1}(y) \right|$$

**Example**: Let $Y = X^2$, where $X$ is a random variable distributed as $N(0,1)$. We have:

$$f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}, \quad x = \pm \sqrt{y}$$

Hence, the density function of the random variable $Y$ is given by $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi y}} e^{-y/2}, \quad y > 0$

**Example**: Let $Z = X + Y$ and $W = X - Y$, that is, $X = \frac{Z+W}{2}, Y = \frac{Z-W}{2}$; we have $|J| = \frac{1}{2}$. The distribution of $Z$ is $f_Z(z) = \int f_{X,Y}(\frac{z+w}{2}, \frac{z-w}{2}) \frac{1}{2} dw$

# Multivariate distributions

Now, let's look at a few important multivariate distributions that will be used in defining many ML models in the later chapters. We will also look at the marginal distributions for some of these multivariate distributions.

## Multinomial distribution

Consider a sequence of **n** independent categorical trials with categorical probability vector $p = (p_1, ..., p_K)$, where $\sum_{k=1}^K p_k = 1$, which are constants for each trial. For example, in the ball and the urn experiment, an urn contains many balls of $K$ different colors. The color of the ball drawn is categorically distributed, and the sequence of $n$ such trials with replacement will be multinomial distributed. In $n$ trials, if $X_k$ represents the count of $k^{th}$ category obtained, then we must have $\sum_{k=1}^K X_k = n$. These different categories can be obtained in $\frac{n!}{X_1! \cdots X_K!}$ ways, and the probability of obtaining this category distribution is $\frac{n!}{X_1! \cdots X_K!} p_1^{X_1} \cdots p_K^{X_K}$. Let $X$ be multinomially distributed $M(n, p)$. Here, $X$ is a vector: $X = (X_1, ..., X_K)$ and we write $X \sim M(n, p)$.

$$P(X_1=x_1, ..., X_K=x_K) = \frac{n!}{x_1! \cdots x_K!} p_1^{x_1} \cdots p_K^{x_K}, \text{ where } \sum x_k = n$$

Text data can be modelled as a multinomial distribution over the words. Assuming that the text is a collection of words, given a set of text documents, we can define a vocabulary set of $K$ distinct words occurring in the entire document collection. Based on the number of times a word occurs in each document, we can represent each document as a categorical variable.

## Multivariate gaussian distribution

For a vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the multivariate Gaussian distribution takes the following form:

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} |\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (x - \mu)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)\right)$$

where $\mu$ is $d$-dimensional mean vector, $\Sigma$ is a $d \times d$ covariance matrix, and $|\Sigma|$ is the determinant of $\Sigma$. This is denoted by the notation $X \sim N(\mu, \Sigma)$.

We will encounter this form of Gaussian distribution in several topics across the book. So, we must understand this in detail. First, we will start with the geometric interpretation of this density function, which will give us an idea of how Gaussian distributed vectors are placed relative to each other in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Also, we will prove that $p(x)$ indeed represents a density, that is:

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p(x) \, dx = 1$$

Representing $dx_1 \cdots dx_d$ as a vector $dx$, we have a succinct representation of the normalization equation, that is, $\int p(x) \, dx = 1$

The power in exponent term $(x - \mu)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)$ is a quadratic form since $\Sigma$, being the covariance matrix, can be taken to be symmetric; hence, its inverse is also symmetric. Here, $\Delta$ is called the *Mahalanobis distance* from $\mu$ to $x$, and it reduces to the Euclidean distance when $\Sigma$ is the identity matrix.
