
In this chapter, we introduced the core theory and some applications of deep learning-based generative models. In the latest GAN and VAE applications, we may find that the generator/decoder has attempted different architectures, which may be suitable for specific problem domains. However, the loss functions for training such networks are still certain derivatives of the known loss functions discussed here, such as adversarial loss, reconstruction loss, ELBO, feature matching, cycle consistency loss, and Wasserstein loss. This chapter should enable readers to explore cutting-edge papers in generative modeling with cool applications and apply them to solve their own business problems.

# Key Takeaways

*   Generative models are primarily unsupervised machine learning models.
*   Generative models generate new data instances by learning the latent probability distribution of the data, while discriminative models distinguish between different kinds of data instances by learning class-conditional probabilities.
*   VAEs and VQ-VAEs are easier to train compared to GANs; additionally, the encoder part of a VAE can be used as a dimensionality reduction technique to represent data in a low-dimensional latent space.
*   Training GANs using adversarial loss is equivalent to minimizing the JS divergence, while training VAEs amounts to minimizing the KL divergence between the real data distribution and the generator's data distribution.
*   We have theoretically proven the existence of the GAN equilibrium point, but it is difficult to reach this equilibrium point numerically when training with adversarial loss. We may fall into many *local* Nash equilibria, which leads to mode collapse and training instability. These local equilibria are far from the global equilibrium, so we should always use some of the tricks discussed here, such as feature matching, minibatch discrimination, and conditional GANs, to mitigate these challenges.
*   If we have sequential data, it is better to use sequential generative models, such as HMMs or RNN-based models (e.g., C-RNN-GAN).
