# Digital Image Formation

Light is part of the electromagnetic spectrum that is sensed by the eye, which is further divided into violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, and red. Colors perceived (by eye) by looking at the object are determined by the category of light reflected by an object. An object is perceived as white when it reflects all wavelengths of light equally. An object appearing green reflects green light and absorbs all other wavelengths of light. A black object absorbs all wavelengths of light. Now, let us understand how this information is captured in digital images.

# Capture the light

Electromagnetic radiation reflected or emitted by the object is usually captured through a two-dimensional array of sensors. Response of each sensor is proportional to the integral of radiation energy projected onto the surface of the sensor. Analog circuitry analyzes sensor output to produce an analog signal. Figure 9.1 shows capture of an ellipse-shaped object by analog circuitry. This analog signal is then digitized to produce a digital image. Digitizing involves two processes: sampling and quantization. Refer to the following figure:

# Sampling and quantization

Output of most sensors are continuous voltage waveforms whose amplitude and spatial variation is related to electromagnetic waves sensed that are reflected by the object. Two processes, sampling and quantization, are necessary to convert these analog signals to digital. Consider line AB on the captured object, as shown in Figure 9.2(a). Intensity of pixels along this line gradually increases and decreases as shown in Figure 9.2(b):

![](images/9.2.jpg)

**Figure 9.2:** ***(a)*** *A line on the object is considered* ***(b)*** *Intensity variation along line* ***(c)*** *Consider equally spaced samples along line* ***(d)*** *Sampled values along line*

As the AB line is continuous, we need to sample a few points along the line AB. The number of samples required would depend on how close you need the digital representation to be with respect to analog, as shown in Figure 9.2(c). The higher the number of samples, the closer would be the representation. This process of representing an image with sampled points is known as **sampling**.

Now let's represent the complete ellipse with six sample points along the x-axis and four along the y-axis with a total of 24 sample points, as shown in Figure 9.3(a). Each of these 24 sample points will be represented with integer intensity values in range [0, 255], as shown in Figure 9.3(b). This is known as *quantization* of sample values. Outcome of sampling and quantization on the ellipse would look like in Figure 9.3(c):

![](images/Image45770.jpg)

**Figure 9.3:** ***(a)*** *sampling of intensity in 2D* ***(b)*** *Quantization of values* ***(c)*** *Resulting digitized image*

In digitization of an image, each of these cells that represented a sampled value of the real image are called *pixels*. In this example, the ellipse image is represented using *24 pixels* with *6* & *4* pixels along *x* and *y* axes, respectively. Count of the pixels used to represent an image is called its resolution. In this case, resolution of the ellipse image is mentioned as *6*×*4*.

# Pixels

In the digital world, images are presented using pixels. A pixel, also called a picture element, can be understood as a minute area of illumination on a display screen. Higher count of pixels results in higher resolution of the image, and higher resolution results in better representation. Each pixel of the image will denote a value of the intensity in that position. Intensity values are represented with integer values in range [0, 255]. To represent one pixel, one byte is sufficient. For the grey ellipse image in the preceding example, *24* = 6*4 bytes would be sufficient for digital representation of 24 sampled values. Programmatically, a 2-dimensional unsigned byte array of size 6x4 is sufficient to represent this image.
