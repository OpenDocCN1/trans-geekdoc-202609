Since the parameters `ξ` are not updated via the backpropagation scheme, the computed gradients at each iteration step are propagated only in the student branch. This is denoted by the "sg" symbol that indicates a "stop gradient" propagation operation. It turns out, empirically, that using this training strategy, BYOL avoids collapsed solutions.

In [66], different convolutional residual networks were used to implement the encoders. For the case of ResNet-50 with width multiplier 1 ([71]), the encoded output, `h`, was a 2048-dimensional vector. The representation vectors were then projected to a lower dimensional space by the projection networks, `g`. The latter was implemented as a multilayer perceptron consisting of a linear layer with 4096 nodes, followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation functions, and the last layer was a linear one with 256 output nodes. The network `q` had the same architecture as `g`.

Note that BYOL introduces two types of *asymmetry* between the two branches. One is the architectural asymmetry between the online and the target branches, due to the presence of the predictor in the upper branch. The other comes from the different parameter updating mechanisms that are used in each one of the branches. These asymmetries contribute to overcoming collapsed solutions.

The previous rationale for avoiding collapsed solutions has given birth to a number of variants that employ different loss objectives and/or different architectures, e.g., the so-called *data2vec* scheme proposed in [10]. In [145], a study of EMA is performed and the findings point out that the algorithm enjoys certain stability properties. Moreover, in this paper, a number of methods that evolve around the EMA scheme are investigated and compared.

### SimSIAM

In [34], the so-called *simple siamese* (SimSIAM) network is proposed. It retains the main idea of the two branches as in BYOL, but instead of adopting the EMA algorithm to update the target branch, it forces both branches to share parameters. However, at each iteration of the backpropagation algorithm a stop gradient operation is employed to freeze the adaptation of the parameters in one of the branches and the corresponding output is treated as a constant. This simple modification turns out to contribute to the robustness against the collapsing phenomenon.

In [182], an attempt was made to understand the dynamics of BYOL and SimSIAM schemes with respect to their robustness against collapsing.

### DINO

The *distillation with no label* (DINO) algorithm was proposed in [32] and it is a variant of BYOL. The EMA scheme is retained. The difference lies in that the output of each branch is *discretized* to a, say, `K`-dimensional vector and the outputs of both branches are transformed to `K` probabilities via the use of our familiar softmax operation. Then, similarity between the two branches is quantified via the cross-entropy loss function.

The DINO scheme is the evolution of a line of research in this topic, see, e.g., [30], [31]. The underlying idea is to discretize the generated output representations and then resort to clustering arguments. For example, in [31], the *swapping assignments between multiple views* (SwAV) scheme is proposed. The outputs of the two branches are discretized, forming a code book with, say, `K` prototypes, and then a clustering operation is performed *in parallel* with the parameters' learning. Similarity between the two branches is achieved by encouraging the cluster assignments in both branches to be similar. Collapse is discouraged by employing appropriate regularization that promotes the formed clusters to be populated as uniformly as possible.

### 19.6.7 Correlation-based techniques

The main idea in all the techniques that we have treated so far is that of enforcing similarity/dissimilarity, in one way or another (depending on the specific method), among data that originate in two different branches. In the last category of this subsection the notion of correlation is mobilized as a measure of similarity. This leads to slightly different task formulation needs, and we treat such schemes as a different category.

The stage of our discussion is that of the canonical correlation method (CCA), which is treated in more detail in Section [20.4](#Chp020_1103-1179_B9780443292385000263.xhtml). The main idea that underpins CCA is that if two sets of random variables (two random vectors) are involved, the value of their correlation depends on the coordinate system in which the random vectors are represented. The goal behind CCA is to seek a pair of linear transformations, one for each set of variables, such that after the transformation, the resulting transformed variables are maximally correlated. The main idea of CCA has been generalized to involve nonlinear transforms. For example, in [5], the objective becomes that of *jointly* learning the parameters of two networks such as their outputs are maximally correlated.

### Barlow twins

This is a typical example of this family of schemes. The *Barlow twins* architecture, [202], follows the philosophy of the two identical (parameter sharing) branches, as that of [Fig. 19.19](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0100).
