
In contrast, the estimator associated with Eq. [(14.28)](#Chp014_717-754_B9780443292385000202.xhtml#deq50), being the result of a ratio, is unbiased only asymptotically and it is a `biased` one for finite values of `N` (Problem [14.6](#Chp014_717-754_B9780443292385000202.xhtml#o0035)). Hence, if one would have the luxury of a very large number `N` of samples, Eq. [(14.28)](#Chp014_717-754_B9780443292385000202.xhtml#deq50) would be a good enough estimate. However, in practice, `N` cannot be made arbitrarily large and the resulting estimate may not be satisfactory.

If `q(x) ≈ p(x)`, or at least `q(x)` is a fairly good approximation of `ϕ(x)`, then Eq. [(14.28)](#Chp014_717-754_B9780443292385000202.xhtml#deq50) would approximate Eq. [(14.2)](#Chp014_717-754_B9780443292385000202.xhtml#deq2). However, for most practical cases, this is not easy to obtain, especially in high-dimensional spaces. If `q(x)` is not a good match to `ϕ(x)`, it is very likely that there will be regions where `ϕ(x)` is large while `q(x)` is much smaller. The corresponding weights will have large values, relative to those from other regions, and they will be the dominant ones in the summation (Eq. [(14.28)](#Chp014_717-754_B9780443292385000202.xhtml#deq50)).

The effect of it is equivalent to reducing the number, `N`, of samples. Moreover, it is also possible that `q(x)` takes very small values in some regions, which makes it very likely that samples from such regions are completely absent in Eq. [(14.28)](#Chp014_717-754_B9780443292385000202.xhtml#deq50) (see [Fig. 14.7](#Chp014_717-754_B9780443292385000202.xhtml#f0040)). In such cases, not only may the resulting estimate be wrong, but we will not be aware of it, and the variance of the weights, `w(x[n])` and `w(x[n])f(x[n])`, may exhibit low values. *These phenomena are accentuated in high-dimensional spaces* (see, e.g., \[26\] and Problem [14.7](#Chp014_717-754_B9780443292385000202.xhtml#o0040)).

To alleviate the previous shortcomings, a number of variants have been proposed to search for high-probability regions and make local approximations around the modes and use them in order to generate samples (see, e.g., \[30\] and the references therein).
