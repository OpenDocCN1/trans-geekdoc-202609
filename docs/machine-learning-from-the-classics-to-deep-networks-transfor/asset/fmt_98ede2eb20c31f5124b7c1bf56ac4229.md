
# 4.1 Introduction

Mean-square error (MSE) linear estimation is a topic of fundamental importance for parameter estimation in statistical learning. Besides historical reasons, which take us back to the pioneering works of Kolmogorov, Wiener, and Kalman, who laid the foundations of the optimal estimation field, understanding MSE estimation is a must, prior to studying more recent techniques. One always has to grasp the basics and learn the classics prior to getting involved with new “adventures.” Many of the concepts to be discussed here are also used in the next chapters.

Optimizing via a loss function that builds around the square of the error has a number of advantages, such as a single optimal value, which can be obtained via the solution of a linear set of equations; this is a very attractive feature in practice. Moreover, due to the relative simplicity of the resulting equations, the newcomer in the field can get a better feeling of the various notions associated with optimal parameter estimation. The elegant geometric interpretation of the MSE solution, via the orthogonality theorem, is presented and discussed and some typical application examples, such as deconvolution, equalization, and interference cancellation are considered for clarifying the main concept that underlies the MSE estimation task. In the chapter, emphasis is also given to computational complexity issues while solving for the optimal solution. Levinson's algorithm and its lattice-ladder interpretation are presented as elegant and typical examples of efficient solvers of the classical normal equations. The Gauss–Markov theorem of linear models is discussed and the chapter closes with presenting the celebrated Kalman filtering scheme.

The development of the chapter is around real-valued variables, something that will be true for most of the book. However, complex-valued variables are particularly useful in a number of areas, with communications being a typical example, and the generalization from the real to the complex domain may not always be trivial. In this context, the circularity conditions and the notion of widely linear estimation are introduced. The basic definitions concerning Wirtinger derivatives are provided and the associated optimality conditions for loss functions involving complex variables are presented.

# 4.2 Mean-square error linear estimation: the normal equations

The general estimation task has been introduced in Chapter [3](#Chp003_65-117_B9780443292385000093.xhtml). There, it was stated that given two dependent random vectors, `y` and `x`, the goal of the estimation task is to obtain a function, `g`, so as, given a value `x` of `x`, to be able to predict (estimate), in some optimal sense, the corresponding value `y` of `y`, or $\widehat{\mathbf{y}} = g(\mathbf{x})$. The MSE estimation was also presented in Chapter [3](#Chp003_65-117_B9780443292385000093.xhtml) and it was shown that the optimal MSE estimate of `y` given the value `x = x` is

$\widehat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}].$

In general, this is a nonlinear function. We now turn our attention to the case where `g` is *constrained* to be a linear function. For simplicity and in order to pay more attention to the concepts, we will restrict our discussion to the case of scalar dependent (output) variables. The more general case will be discussed later on.
