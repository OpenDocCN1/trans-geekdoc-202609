
## 19.6.5 Contrastive techniques

The rationale that underlies the so-called *contrastive* techniques is to *encourage* similarity between versions of semantic preserving transformations of an input. The main idea behind such methods can be traced back to [18] and [26]. In the former, the goal was to replace the external "teacher"/target used in a supervised learning task by internally derived "signals." Such signals can be generated by assuming that different parts of the perceptual input have common causes in the external world. Such a view changes the concept of training. Instead of "pushing" an output to "match" a corresponding externally provided target, the goal becomes that of "pushing" the outputs of two networks, whose inputs are fed with different views of the same input sample, to agree with each other. For example, the different views may be different modalities (e.g., vision and touch), or they can originate from the same modality at different times, or could be spatially adjacent parts of the same image.

### Various Ways of Dealing with Masked Image Patches

There are various ways in dealing with masked image patches in self-supervised learning. A straightforward path is to feed the encoder with the masked sequence of vectors, i.e., [Fig. 19.18](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0095)B (center). The outputs of the encoder are fed to a predictor that is trained to provide estimates of the masked patches, i.e., $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_1$, $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_6$, $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_8$. These steps are in line with the upper branch of [Fig. 19.16](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0085). The action of "Transform #1" is to mask the patches, according to some protocol. For example, a preselected percentage of patches can be randomly masked. The action of the "Transform #2" is to retain the original vectors of the masked patches, i.e., $\mathbf{x}_1$, $\mathbf{x}_6$, $\mathbf{x}_8$, which act as the targets in the cost function. A possible choice for the loss function can be the square Euclidean norm of the difference between pixel values in the original patches that were masked and the predicted ones, e.g., Eq. [(19.52)](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#deq123). Other norms can also be used or a combination of loss functions, see, e.g., [142].

### BEiT: BERT Pretraining of Image Transformers

In the *BERT pretraining of image transformers* (BEiT), [14], prior to the pretraining, an autoencoder is first used to encode image patches as *discrete* tokens. In other words, each one of the patches of an image is associated with a number in a discrete vocabulary. Subsequently, a BERT-like structure is trained so that the outputs that are associated with the masked patches predict the corresponding id number in the associated discrete vocabulary. Although the reported results indicate performance improvements with respect to previously published methods, the training, due to the tokenization step, poses certain challenges.

### SSAST: Self-Supervised Audio Spectrogram Transformer

The idea of predicting the pixel values of masked patches has been adopted to audio tasks in [59] (SSAST: Self-supervised audio spectrogram transformer). The inputs to the encoding transformer are the spectrograms that are computed from the input audio sequence.

### HuBERT: Hidden-Unit BERT

In [87] (HuBERT: Hidden-Unit BERT), the raw audio data are first coded via a CNN network to produce a sequence of coded frames that are fed as inputs to the transformer. After randomly masking some of the frames, the goal of the training during the pretext task is to predict the *cluster* indices of the masked frames. To this end, a clustering of the input frames is first performed, e.g., via the *k*-means algorithm. In other words, in this case, the pseudo labels consist of cluster indices.

### Masked Autoencoder (MAE)

In [73], the so-called *masked autoencoder* was introduced. The proposed architecture follows the general rationale shown in [Fig. 19.16](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0085). However, in this case, the input to the encoder transformer, $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot)$, is fed with the *unmasked* patches *only*. The masked patches are simply removed and they are *not* replaced by a mask symbol. Thus, the input consists of, say, $M < N$ vectorized patches, where $N$ is the total number of patches that result from the original image splitting. The input to the predictor module, $g_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\cdot)$, consists of the output of the encoder, augmented with the masked symbols placed at the positions that correspond to the removed patches in the input. That is, the decoder accepts as inputs $N$ tokens. The network is trained to reconstruct/predict the image patches. In [73], the cost function involves only the masked patches, similar to the BERT rationale. Up to 75% of the input patches can be masked, which also leads to substantial computational savings. The predictor $g_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\cdot)$ is also a transformer network, and the outputs of its last block are linearly transformed to match the dimensions of the image patches.

### Applications to Audio

The idea of masked autoencoders has been applied to spectrograms of audio signals in [91]. This idea is further enhanced in [199], where the multi-head-multi-window transformer is proposed. Each head in the decoder computes attention over windows of different sizes. The reason is that, in this way, one can cope better with the issue of local versus global information. It is known that in audio signals, some audio events are more localized than others, and this applies both to time as well as to the frequency domains.
