
Invertibility is imposed by judiciously selecting the layers (flows) of the network. Each one of the layers, *k* = 1, 2, …, *K*, implements a corresponding *invertible* transformation `f[k](·;θ[k])`. The overall functions, `f` and `f^(−1)`, result as the composition of the individual ones, i.e.,

```
f = f[K](f[K−1](…f[2](f[1]).…)), and f^(−1) = f[1]^(−1)(f[2]^(−1)(…f[K−1]^(−1)(f[K]^(−1))…))
```

and `θ` := [`θ[1]^(T)`, …, `θ[K]^(T)`]^(T). In other words, once learning of the parameters is completed, the approximation of the input distribution, `p[x](x)`, is achieved in a *step-wise* layer after layer fashion starting from, say, the standard Gaussian pdf. The critical point in designing normalizing flows is to model and select these invertible functions. To this end, various methods and techniques have been proposed. For more details see, e.g., [139] and the references therein.

## 19.3 Transformers

So far, our discussion has evolved around three modeling concepts as the basic tools to build deep networks: the fully connected feed-forward neural networks, the convolutional networks, and the recurrent neural networks. Now, we turn our attention to the most recent trend known as *transformers*. The main rationale behind the so-called transformer networks is the attention mechanism, which has already been introduced and used before in Section [18.13.2](#Chp018_885-995_B978044329238500024X.xhtml). At the time the current edition is being compiled, transformers constitute the state-of-the art in deep learning. To present the underlying basic idea and the related architectures for implementing such networks, we will follow a historical path, starting from the natural language processing (NLP) applications domain.

### 19.3.1 Natural language processing: a primer

The goal of this subsection is very humble and its purpose is to provide some very basic definitions and concepts related to the *natural language processing* (NLP) discipline that are needed for introducing transformers. After all, a more extended treatment of the NLP field would require a dedicated book, see, e.g., [58].

Natural language processing is broadly defined as the scientific area whose goal is the automatic manipulation (process, analyze, and synthesize) of natural languages, such as speech and text, via the use of computers. In Section [11.15](#Chp011_519-582_B9780443292385000172.xhtml), a case study on the closely related topic of authorship identification was discussed. There, a number of terms such as bag-of-words and n-grams were defined and used in the context of SVM classification.
