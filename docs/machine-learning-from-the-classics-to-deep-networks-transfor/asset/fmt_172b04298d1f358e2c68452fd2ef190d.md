
$q(y|\mathbf{x}_t) \propto \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_t|y)}{q(\mathbf{x}_t)}.$

![Equation](images/B9780443292385000251/si694.png)

In words, in the implicit classifier, we raise the previous ratio to the power of `γ`. Selecting the value of `γ`, in practice, is the outcome of a trade-off between sample variability and sample quality. While generating images, increasing `γ` decreases variability and increases sample fidelity, e.g., [86]. This is shown in [Fig. 19.25](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0140). The figure to the left corresponds to the unconditional model, `γ = 0` (high variability-low fidelity), the one in the middle to `γ = 1.5`, and the one to the right to `γ = 3` (low variability-high fidelity).^([^(147)](#fn147))

As we will soon see, conditional models based on classifier or classifier-free guidance have been extended and used in cases where the models are constrained not on class labels but on more general constrains, such as text captions.

### 19.7.7 Applications

Diffusion models have been applied to a wide range of applications and, currently, there is a fast growing trend to embrace such modeling in more and more disciplines.

In computer vision, they have been used for, e.g., image super-resolution, editing, impainting, restoration, and segmentation, besides image and video generation. In time series analysis, they have been applied to tasks such as imputation, forecasting, waveform generation. In NLP tasks, they have been used for sequence-to-sequence generation, e.g., [60]. For a related extensive literature review see, e.g., [41], [200], [115].

One of the most "trendy" and well-known applications (well beyond the scientific community) is the text-to-image generation, which is highlighted next.

#### Text-to-image generation

Text-to-image generation can be seen as an image generation task conditioned on a corresponding text description. The topic of text-to-image generation traces its origins back to [127], where the DRAW ([65]) generative model was extended to condition on image captions to generate novel visual scenes. Following this work, in [153], a GAN was employed that improved the generated image quality and could generate objects with recognizable properties. In [151], the text-to-image generation was performed based on a transformer that autoregressively modeled the text and image tokens as a single stream of data.^([^(148)](#fn148))

**CLIP**: Following the general rationale behind self-supervision, CLIP uses two branches to jointly train two encoders. See [Fig. 19.26](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0145) for a simplified block diagram. The input to the encoder of the upper branch is fed with the text description (caption) that describes an image, which feeds the input of the encoder in the lower branch. For example, if the image contains a dog that wears a hat (as in the figure), the corresponding description could be something like "dog wearing a hat." The text in the caption is encoded into a corresponding vector representation, after an appropriate embedding (see, e.g., Section [19.3.1](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#s0075)).
