
# Contrastive Loss and SimCLR

## Introduction to Contrastive Loss

In [26], the notion of *contrastive loss* was introduced. Two networks were employed and constrained to share all parameters. The goal was to recognize genuine from fake signatures. During training, parameters were adjusted to "push" the outputs of the two networks to be "close" when both modules were provided with genuine signatures. Otherwise, in the case of a forged signature, the respective outputs were "pushed" to be "far" (according to a distance measure).

## Self-Supervised Learning Context

In the context of self-supervised learning, since only unlabeled data are involved, to identify similar inputs we often form variants of a single input using known semantic preserving transformations. This process is also known as *data augmentation*, and we often refer to it as *joint-embedding self-supervised learning* (JESSL). The variants that originate from the *same* input sample are called *positive* pairs or examples. It is for such pairs that we wish to make the corresponding outputs of the two networks look "similar." Transformed versions that originate from *different* input samples are called *negatives*, and we wish to make the corresponding outputs "dissimilar." Note that contrastive techniques fall under the general scheme of [Fig. 19.16](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0085), where each branch corresponds to different variants of the same input sample.

## SimCLR: Simple Framework for Contrastive Learning

To unveil the main rationale behind these techniques, let us discuss one typical method known as *simple framework for contrastive learning* (SimCLR), presented in [35]. [Fig. 19.19](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0100) illustrates the idea schematically, assuming the input comprises single vectors.

For every input `x`, two variants are generated by randomly applying the same semantic preserving transformation family. The variants are denoted as `x̃_i` for one branch and `x̃_j` for the other. Examples of such semantic preserving transformations for images are random cropping followed by resizing, crop resize and flip, rotate, cut out, and adding noise; see [Fig. 19.20](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0105). In audio signals, such transformations could be altering the order of some frames in the sequence or masking some of them.

## SimCLR Architecture Details

In SimCLR, the two branches are identical. The two versions of the input sample feed the encoder, `f[θ](·)`, which in turn feeds the input of a nonlinear network, `g[ϕ](·)`. Going back to the generic scheme of [Fig. 19.16](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0085), the nonlinear module, `g[ξ]'(·)`, results as a serial concatenation of the `f` and `g` networks, whose parameters are constrained to be the same as those of the other branch.
