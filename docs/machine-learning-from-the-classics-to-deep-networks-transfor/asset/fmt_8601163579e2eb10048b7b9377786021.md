To maximize the bound, this term should be maximized for the respective observations, `x[n]`; this encourages the decoder to learn to *reconstruct* the data. The second term is non-positive (the KL divergence is a non-negative quantity, see [Problem 12.7](#Chp012_583-634_B9780443292385000184.xhtml)). To maximize the bound, the KL should be minimized; thus, the corresponding posterior will remain close to the prior; the latter has been chosen to be the standard multivariate Gaussian, `N(z|0, I)`. Hence, the prior acts as a regularizer that "pushes" the network's latent variables to match the standard Gaussian distribution as closely as possible.

The existence of the prior is very important. The regularizer "forces" the posterior distribution of the latent variables to be broad enough. We do not want, for example, two different images/samples from the same class to be mapped to two different and far away located regions in the latent space; i.e., we do not want their corresponding PDFs to be very narrow, with small variance, placed at mean values that are very different. Our desire is to map/code every input sample of the same class via latent variables that lie "close" enough within a small region in the latent space. Our desire is to construct a latent space that is smooth enough on a local scale for similar samples. As a matter of fact, our goal should be to construct encodings which are all as close as possible (dense packing) while still being distinct for different classes (cluster formation). This would facilitate smooth interpolation between different classes when generating new samples. Moreover, such a dense encoding packing, which is promoted by the KL term, would be highly helpful to sample the latent variables according to the standard Gaussian (`N(0, I)`) prior and the decoder to be able to decode and generate samples successfully.

Once the parametric forms of the involved distributions have been adopted, all one has to do is to maximize `F` with respect to the unknown parameters. Taking into account that the adopted models are feedforward neural networks, the necessary gradients for the optimization task are computed in the backpropagation rationale. To this end, the following "trick" comes into the game, see, e.g., [103].

### The reparameterization trick

The backpropagation algorithm requires the computation of the gradient of the loss function in Eq. [(19.22)](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#deq47). As we will soon see, the KL divergence term pauses no computational issues. In contrast, the first term on the right hand side requires more attention. The computation of the respective gradient, in every step of the backpropagation algorithm, demands an averaging operation with respect to the current estimate of the posterior PDF, `q`. However, it turns out that following standard arguments (e.g., [20]) in order to obtain estimates of the respective mean gradient values, via sampling from `q`, lead to estimates with high variances that render the method rather impractical, e.g., [103].

The previous difficulty is bypassed by the so-called reparameterization trick. The idea is to view each one of the latent variables as a function in terms of a) the parameters that define the respective distribution (Gaussian in this case) and b) of an independent auxiliary random variable that takes care of the associated randomness. For example, in the case of Gaussians, the defining parameters are the mean values and variances (covariance matrix). The auxiliary random variable can follow a simple PDF (e.g., Gaussian or uniform). It suffices for the function to be *differentiable*. In the current context, the implied transformation is a linear one and the involved random latent variables are expressed as

```
z[i] = μ[i] + σ[i]ε[i], i = 1, 2, …, m,
```

where `ε[i]` are the respective auxiliary random variables with zero mean and unit variance. Stacking together `ε[i], i = 1, 2, …, m`, into the random vector `ε ∈ ℝ^(m)`, the corresponding to `x[n]` latent random vector can be written as

```
z[n] = μ[n] + σ[n] ⊙ ε,
```
