
*   • In [13], a number of practical implementation recipes for self-supervised learning are discussed, such as hyperparameter tuning, choice of architectures, optimizers and batch sizes, practical tips and pitfalls, as well as performance evaluation techniques.

*   • Note that some of the generative techniques that have been discussed in Section [19.2](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#s0015) can also be considered as special cases of self-supervised learning. For example, the autoencoders/VAEs are trained so that the output of the decoder is to reconstruct the input of the encoder. In the GAN networks, a discriminator is trained to recognize fake from real samples, via the use of unlabeled data, see also, [117].

### 19.6.8 Self-supervised learning: some concluding remarks

In the previous subsections, the focus was on the main directions that are currently popular in self-supervised learning. The emphasis was on some typical methods for each category with the aim to shed light on the main idea and principles that underpin each one of the categories.

At the time the current edition of the book is written, self-supervised learning is a hot area of research and a large number of papers are published every year, concerning novel methods and algorithms, comparative performance results in various applications as well as tools for theoretical analysis of the respective methods. Despite the fast-moving research, the breadth of SSL methods and their notable successes, there are still many open problems and questions to be answered.

For example, one of the challenges concerns the data augmentation phase. It is not a surprise to say that what is learned by the SSL methods is determined by the choice of the type of data augmentation. Because the pretext task differs from the downstream tasks, there is an inherent *misalignment or pretraining bias*. As it is pointed out in [23], the use of the projector network can mitigate some of the effects of this bias, but only to some degree. The role of the projector is further analyzed in [24]. In [35], it is stated that no single transformation type, e.g., random cropping or random color distortion, suffices to learn good representations, even though the model can almost perfectly identify the positive pairs in the contrastive task.

In [50], it is shown that different augmentations lead to learning different type of invariances. Moreover, it turns out that some of them are better on some downstream tasks than others. In [48], an extensive comparative experimental study is carried out in the context of computer vision, using over 40 downstream tasks and 13 SSL models. The findings in the paper point out that no single self-supervised method dominates overall, suggesting that universal pretraining is still unsolved. Another interesting finding from this study is that self-supervised methods can match and, even, outperform supervised representation learning across most tasks considered.

Besides data augmentation, the size of the involved networks is also a challenge. Currently, this is also a line of research. How large or how small the dimensionality of the encoder and the projector networks should be? Are they interrelated? This interplay and how this influences the performance for some SSL models is studied in, e.g., [23].

In spite of the rapid developments of self-supervised learning and the continual emergence of novel methods, there is still a lack of widely accepted principled guidelines for evaluating the "goodness" of the obtained representations, at the output of the encoder, across a wide range of potential tasks. This is a drawback that may hinder their practical deployment. The reason is that for most of the methods, during the pretext training phase, there is no loss function to account for the input reconstruction, which could indicate successful or unsuccessful training. Masked methods are the exception, because they are forced to reproduce the missing parts. To this end, a number of techniques have been proposed.

One path for the quality assessment of the obtained representation at the output of the encoder is based on the performance of the downstream task. To this end, one popular protocol is to add a linear classifier (linear probe) on top of the encoder, which is trained via the corresponding labeled examples, e.g., [135]. The advantage of using a linear classifier is that its performance heavily relies on the quality of the representation since its discriminative power is low and, also, it is computationally cheap. Extensions to employ non-linear classifiers, such as feed-forward neural networks that comprise two or three layers or `k`-NN classifiers, have also been used, e.g., [197]. However, relying only on downstream tasks can limit our understanding of what information is retained in the representation of a given input.

Another path is to move away from labeled training. For example, in [22] a feature inversion technique is proposed for visualizing, in the input data space, the learned representations/features, for computer vision applications. However, such an approach suffers from the bias, introduced by the specific inversion method that is used, and it is not clear how these methods would perform on different data modalities other than computer vision.

In [56], the rank of the output representation matrix is used as a performance evaluation index. At the heart of this method lies Cover's theorem, [40] (see, also, Chapter [11](#Chp011_519-582_B9780443292385000172.xhtml)), which relates the training accuracy of a given embedding in linear regression or classification with the corresponding data matrix rank. The training accuracy increases with increasing rank. The rank criterion, called RankMe, is tightly related to what is known as *dimensional collapse* (e.g., [89], [97]). This phenomenon is different to what we called before as training collapse and it refers to learning rank-deficient (dimensional collapsed) embeddings. In other words, the information encoded across different dimensions of the representation at the output of the encoder is redundant. The RankMe criterion links dimensional collapse with the quality of the obtained representation.

In [2], the eigenspectrum of the empirical output representation covariance matrix is employed. It is demonstrated that the eigenspectrum decay can serve as a measure of representation quality.

In a nutshell, self-supervised learning is a highly promising and powerful learning method that, at the time the current edition of this book is being compiled, is dynamically evolving.
