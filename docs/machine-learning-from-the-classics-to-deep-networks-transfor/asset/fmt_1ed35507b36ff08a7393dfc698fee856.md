
### 13.12.1 Covariance Kernels

$$
\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}) = \left( 1 + \frac{{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|}^{2}}{2\alpha h^{2}} \right)^{-\alpha},\quad\alpha > 0,h > 0
$$

![Equation](images/B9780443292385000196/si647.png)

*   where *α* and *h* are known as the *scale mixture* and the *length scale* parameters.

Recall from Chapter [2](#Chp002_19-64_B9780443292385000081.xhtml), where random processes were first presented, that a stationary covariance function/kernel has as its Fourier transform the power spectrum of the respective random process; by definition, the power spectrum of a process is a nonnegative function in the frequency domain. This suggests a way of constructing kernels for random processes; that is, take the inverse Fourier transform of a positive function in the frequency domain. Moreover, in principle, all the rules for constructing kernels, which are discussed in Section [11.5.2](#Chp011_519-582_B9780443292385000172.xhtml), can also be applied to construct covariance functions. For example, a popular choice of a kernel for a Gaussian process is

$$
\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\mathbf{\theta}) = \theta_{1}\exp\left( - \sum\limits_{m = 1}^{M}\frac{{(x_{i} - x_{i}^{\prime})}^{2}}{2h_{i}^{2}} \right) + \theta_{2},
$$

![Equation](images/B9780443292385000196/si648.png)

where *θ*[1], *θ*[2] are hyperparameters, which define the process.

In [94], the notion of *spectral mixture* (SM) kernel is introduced. These kernels are derived by modeling a power spectral density, i.e., the Fourier transform of a kernel, with a Gaussian mixture in the frequency domain. However, accurate approximation of any stationary kernel requires a substantial number of mixture components. This increases the number of the involved hyperparameters that need to be estimated and poses serious computational challenges during the optimization task. In [99] and [42], the involved mean frequency and variance parameters in the Gaussian mixtures are appropriately fixed to pre-selected values in a grid and optimization takes place only with respect to the weights of the Gaussian components in the mixture expansion, giving rise to the *grid spectral mixture* (GSM) kernel. In [81], the GSM kernel is extended to handle multi-dimensional data.
