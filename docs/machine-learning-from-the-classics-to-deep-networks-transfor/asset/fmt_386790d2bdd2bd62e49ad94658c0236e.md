
The Occam factor penalizes those models which are finely tuned to the received observations. As an example, if two different models `ℳ[i]` and `ℳ[j]` have a similar range of values for their prior PDFs, then if, say, `Δθ[θ|y](ℳ[i]) ≪ Δθ[θ|y](ℳ[j])`, then `ℳ[i]` will be penalized more; only a small range of values for `θ` survive (i.e., correspond to high probability values) after the reception of `y`. So, if this fine-tuned (to the data) model `ℳ[i]` resulted in a large value of the ML term, it would not be certain that the evidence would be maximized for it, because the Occam factor would be small. Which model, between the two, finally wins it depends on the product of the two involved terms. Soon we will see that the Occam term is also related to the number of parameters, that is, to the complexity of the adopted model.
