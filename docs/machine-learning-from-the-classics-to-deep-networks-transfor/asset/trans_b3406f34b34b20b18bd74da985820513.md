
Speech recognition covers a wide range of application areas. Typical application scenarios of speech recognizers include automatic call handling in telephone networks, query-based information systems, data entry, speech dictation, robotics, and assistive technologies for people with special needs (e.g., the blind).

## Computer Vision

This is a discipline inspired by the human visual system. Typical tasks addressed in the field of computer vision include automatic edge extraction from images, representing objects as compositions of smaller structures, object detection and recognition, optical flow, motion estimation, shape inference from various cues (such as shading and texture), and 3D scene reconstruction from multiple images. Image morphing (i.e., transforming one image into another through seamless transitions) and image stitching (i.e., creating panoramic images from multiple images) are also research topics in computer vision. Image generation (e.g., text-to-image generation) is another popular trend, which will be discussed in Chapter [19](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml). In recent years, the interaction between the fields of computer vision and graphics has been increasing.

## Multimodal Data

Both speech recognition and computer vision process information originating from a single *modality*. However, humans perceive the natural world in a *multimodal* way through various senses (such as vision, hearing, and touch). Each modality involved contains complementary information, which the human brain utilizes to understand and perceive the surrounding world.

Inspired by this, *multimedia* or multimodal understanding achieved through *cross-media integration* has given rise to a related field whose goal is to enhance the performance of various scientific tasks involving multiple modalities. An example of modality fusion is the combination of images/video, speech/audio, and text. A relevant summary is given in Reference [9], which also involves human psychological processes such as *sensation*, *perception*, and *cognition*.

## Natural Language Processing

This is a discipline studying the use of computers to process language. An example of a Natural Language Processing (NLP) task is spam detection. Currently, the NLP field is a research hotspot; typical topics include the development of automatic translation algorithms and software, sentiment analysis, text summarization, author identification, and text generation. Speech recognition is closely related to NLP and, strictly speaking, can be considered a special sub-topic of NLP. The NLP field has recently made impressive progress in the context of so-called Large Language Models (LLMs), which will be discussed in Chapter [19](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml).

## Robotics

Robots are used in manufacturing to perform tasks, such as automotive production assembly lines, or by space agencies to move objects in space. In recent years, so-called social robots have been built to interact with people in their social environments. For example, social robots are used for the benefit of hospitalized children [10].

Robots have been employed in situations that are difficult or dangerous for humans, such as bomb disposal and operating in harsh and hazardous environments (e.g., high-temperature locations, deep sea, and high-radiation areas). Robots have also been developed for teaching.

Robotics is an interdisciplinary field that, in addition to machine learning, includes disciplines such as mechanical engineering, electrical engineering, computer science, computer vision, and speech recognition.

## Autonomous Vehicles

An autonomous vehicle is a vehicle capable of moving with little or no human intervention. Most of us have ridden autonomous trains at airports. However, these trains operate in highly controlled environments. Autonomous vehicles are intended to operate on city streets and highways. This field is also interdisciplinary in nature, with areas such as radar, LiDAR, computer vision, automatic control, sensor networks, and machine learning converging here. The use of autonomous vehicles is expected to reduce the number of accidents, since statistically most accidents are caused by human error, such as drunk driving, speeding, stress, fatigue, etc.

There are multiple levels of automation. In Level 0 (the category where most cars currently are), the driver has control, and built-in automated systems may issue warnings. The higher the level, the greater the autonomy. For example, at Level 4, the driver is first informed whether conditions are safe, and then the driver can decide to switch the vehicle to autonomous mode. At the highest Level 5, autonomous driving requires no human intervention whatsoever [21].

Besides the famous machine learning application examples mentioned above, machine learning is also applied in a wide range of other fields such as healthcare, bioinformatics, business, finance, education, law, and manufacturing.

## Future Challenges

Despite the impressive progress in machine learning, many challenges remain for the foreseeable future, in addition to the long-term challenges mentioned in the introduction to AI. The Berkeley Report [18] summarizes the following list of challenges:

*   Designing systems capable of *continuously* learning through interaction with *dynamic environments*, while making *timely*, *robust*, and *safe* decisions.
*   Designing systems that enable personalized applications and services without compromising user *privacy and security*.
*   Designing systems that can be trained on datasets owned by different organizations without leaking their *confidentiality*, and that provide AI capabilities across the boundaries of potentially competing organizations in the process.
*   Developing domain-specific architectures and software systems to meet the performance requirements of future applications, including custom chips, edge-cloud systems for efficient processing of edge data, and data abstraction and sampling techniques.

In addition to the aforementioned more technically oriented challenges, significant societal challenges also exist. New technologies increasingly influence our daily lives. In principle, they offer greater potential than ever before to manipulate and shape beliefs, opinions, interests, entertainment, customs, and culture, independent of specific societies. Furthermore, they offer the potential to access personal data, which may subsequently be used for economic, political, or other malicious purposes. As European Parliament member M. Schaake stated: "When algorithms affect human rights, public values, or public decision-making, we need oversight and transparency." However, the aforementioned views should not trigger a technophobic reaction. On the contrary, human civilization progresses through leaps in science and technology. What is needed is merely social sensitivity, awareness of potential dangers, and relevant legal "guardrails."

To put it simply in the words of Henri Bergson [2]: History is not deterministic. History is a *creative* evolution.
