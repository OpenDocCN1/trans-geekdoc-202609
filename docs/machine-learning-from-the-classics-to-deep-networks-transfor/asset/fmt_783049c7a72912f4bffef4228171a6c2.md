
However, problems arise when long phrases are considered. For example, it is very common in NLP tasks for a set of multiple related phrases to be involved. These phrases are *contextually* related and they feed the input of the encoder sequentially, one after the other. As it has already been discussed in Section [18.13.1](#Chp018_885-995_B978044329238500024X.xhtml), for such long sequences, RNNs are seriously challenged by the vanishing gradient phenomenon.

In addition to the above drawback, looking more closely at the concept of the state, around which a typical RNN evolves, it may be too much to “ask” from a network to encode the whole history in a single vector state! As a matter of fact, the use of attention tries to exploit the memory of the system in a complimentary fashion, via a weighted combination of the state vectors. Moreover, via such a weighting one can model *dependencies* without regard to their distance in the input or output sequences.

Besides the previous drawbacks, there are computational issues involved. The recurrent/sequential nature of a typical RNN, as discussed in Section [18.13](#Chp018_885-995_B978044329238500024X.xhtml), poses serious obstacles in parallelizing the involved computations.

In a breakthrough paper, \[189\], the authors came up with the idea of abolishing recurrence and retain only the attention mechanism in order to compute input and output representations. As they state in the title of the paper “Attention is all you need.”

### 19.3.3 The transformer network

A (full) transformer network comprises two parts, namely the encoder and the decoder. A corresponding block diagram is shown in [Fig. 19.9](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0050). Both the encoder as well as the decoder consist of a stack of structurally identical blocks. In general, the number of the involved blocks in the encoder, say `L`, may not be the same as the number of blocks in the decoder, say `L'`. As it is always the case, the role of the encoder is to output an information-rich representation/coding of its input sequence. This information is then passed on to the decoder blocks, as it is shown in the figure. Before we proceed to the more detailed description of the encoder and decoder blocks, let us make a few comments related to the input and output sequences in the encoder and the decoder, respectively.

In Section [19.3.1](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#s0075), we discussed that the first step in representing words by vectors is to assign an id number to each word of the vocabulary and then employ `l`-dimensional one-hot vector encodings, where `l` is the size of the vocabulary of the language corpus. In the sequel, each one of these vectors is projected to a lower dimensional space of appropriate dimensions, say `d[m]`, via the use of the so-called embedding matrix. We will refer to the resulting vectors as embedding vectors, tokens, or simply embeddings. We will soon discuss how embeddings are implemented in the context of transformers.

Note that in [Fig. 19.9](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#f0050), the outputs of the top block of the decoder, block #`L'`, are first linearly transformed and then passed through a softmax nonlinearity. The purpose of the linear transform is to project the output vectors of the top block of the decoder, of dimension `d[m]`, back to the vocabulary dimension `l`, and the goal of the softmax nonlinearity is to normalize the values of the elements of the resulting `l`-dimensional vectors so that they can be interpreted as probabilities. In other words, the elements of the output vectors in the decoder correspond to probabilities. What is the nature of these probabilities and how they are used in order to reach decisions will be explained soon.
