
### 19.2.4 Autoencoders

$$
\mathbf{g}: \mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{m} \longmapsto \widehat{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^{l},
$$

where

$$
\hat{x}[j] = g[j](\mathbf{h}) = \phi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}^{\prime T}\mathbf{h} + b_{j}^{\prime}), \quad j = 1, 2, \dots, l.
$$

The activation $\phi_{d}$ is usually taken to be either the identity (linear reconstruction) or the logistic sigmoid one. The task of training is to estimate the parameters

$$
\Theta := [\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}, \dots, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}], \mathbf{b}, \quad \Theta^{\prime} := [\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{\prime}, \dots, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{l}^{\prime}], \mathbf{b}^{\prime}.
$$

It is common to assume that $\Theta^{\prime} = \Theta^{T}$. The parameters are estimated so the reconstruction error, $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{x} - \widehat{\mathbf{x}}$, over the available input samples is minimum in some sense. Usually, the squared error cost is employed, but other choices are also possible. Regularized versions, involving a norm of the parameters, are also a possibility (for example, [155]). If the activation $\phi_{e}$ is chosen to be the identity (linear representation) and $m < l$ (to avoid triviality), the autoencoder is equivalent to the PCA technique [12]. PCA is treated in more detail in Chapter [20](#Chp020_1103-1179_B9780443292385000263.xhtml).

Another version of autoencoders results if during training one adds noise to the input [191, 192]. This is a stochastic counterpart, known as the *denoising autoencoder*. For reconstruction, the uncorrupted input is employed. The idea behind this version is that by trying to undo the effect of noise, one captures statistical dependencies between inputs. More specifically, in [191], the corruption process randomly sets some of the inputs (as many as half of them) to zero. Hence, the denoising autoencoder is forced to predict the missing values from the nonmissing ones, for randomly selected subsets of missing patterns.

Autoencoders have also been used for pretraining deep networks, in place of the RBMs discussed before (see, e.g., [81]). In the latter, autoencoders with many layers, instead of a single one, have been employed.

### 19.2.5 Generative adversarial networks

In Sections [19.2.1](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#s0020) and [19.2.3](#Chp019_997-1102_B9780443292385000251.xhtml#s0030), we considered generative probabilistic graphical models for probability distribution estimation as well as for data generation. The major drawback of such models comes from the computational intractability when maximizing the associated likelihood function and related costs in order to compute the unknown parameters that define the graphical model.
