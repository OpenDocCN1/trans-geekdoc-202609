
### Alternative Paths and Related Methods in Dictionary Learning

*   **Alternative paths to `k`-SVD:** Alternative paths to `k`-SVD for dictionary learning have been suggested. For example, in [73], a dictionary learning technique referred to as the Method of Optimal Directions (MOD) was proposed, which differs from `k`-SVD in the dictionary update step. In particular, the full dictionary is updated via direct minimization of the Frobenius norm.
*   **Probabilistic approaches:** In [126, 143], probabilistic arguments are employed, using a Laplacian prior to enforce sparsity. As discussed in Chapter 13 (Section 13.5), the involved integrations are not analytically tractable, and approximations are used to bypass this obstacle. In the former, the maximum value of the integrand is used; in the latter, a Gaussian approximation of the posterior is adopted to handle the integration.
*   **Variational techniques:** In [82], variational bound techniques are mobilized (see Section 13.9).
*   **Union of orthonormal bases:** The method proposed in [125] bears some similarities to `k`-SVD because it also revolves around SVD, but the dictionary is constrained to be a union of orthonormal bases. This can lead to computational advantages; on the other hand, `k`-SVD puts no constraints on the atoms of the dictionary, giving more freedom in modeling the input. Another difference lies in the column-by-column update introduced in `k`-SVD.
*   **Comparative study:** A more detailed comparative study of `k`-SVD with other methods is given in [4].
*   **Matrix factorization perspective:** Dictionary learning is essentially a matrix factorization problem where a certain type of constraint is imposed on the right matrix factor. This approach can be considered a manifestation of a wider class of constrained matrix factorization methods allowing several types of constraints. Such techniques include regularized PCA, where functional and/or sparsity constraints are imposed on the left and right factors [12, 190, 200], as well as structured sparse matrix factorization [14].
*   **Online and distributed schemes:** Besides the previously reported algorithms, a number of *online* and *distributed* dictionary learning schemes have been proposed.
    *   In [127], a distributed version of the `k`-SVD algorithm is developed.
    *   A cloud-based version of `k`-SVD is proposed in [149] with an emphasis on big data applications.
    *   A dictionary learning algorithm employing arguments inspired by the EXTRA optimization scheme (briefly discussed in Section 8.15) is described in [182].
    *   In [49], various agents/nodes learn different parts of the dictionary, an approach also suited for big data applications.
    *   An online algorithm for dictionary learning has been presented in [139], and an online version for a distributed setting with provable convergence to a stationary point has been proposed in [53].
    *   In [62], the decentralized case over time-varying digraphs involving a general set of constraints is considered, where a number of matrix factorization schemes result as special cases.

### Example 20.5: Image Denoising via Dictionary Learning

The goal of this example is to show the performance of the dictionary learning technique in the context of an image denoising task. In the case study of Section 9.10, image denoising based on a predetermined and fixed DCT dictionary was considered. Here, `k`-SVD is employed to learn the dictionary using information from the image *itself*.

The two 256 × 256 images, without and with noise corresponding to PSNR = 22, are shown in Figures 20.12A and 20.12B, respectively. The noisy image is divided into overlapping patches of size 12 × 12 (144), resulting in (256 − 12 + 1)² = 60,025 patches in total; these constitute the training data set used for learning the dictionary.

Specifically, the patches are sequentially extracted from the noisy image, vectorized in lexicographic order, and used as columns to define the 144 × 60,025 matrix `X`. Then, `k`-SVD is mobilized to train an overcomplete dictionary of size 144 × 196. The resulting atoms, reshaped to form 12 × 12 pixel patches, are shown in Figure 20.13. Compare the atoms of this dictionary with those of the fixed DCT dictionary in Figure 9.14.
