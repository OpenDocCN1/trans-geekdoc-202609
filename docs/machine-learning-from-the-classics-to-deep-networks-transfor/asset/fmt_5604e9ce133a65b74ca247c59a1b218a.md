### Nonconvexity of the cost function

Optimization of cost functions has been a recurrent theme that runs across this book. In Chapter [5](#Chp005_177-248_B9780443292385000111.xhtml), the gradient descent optimization algorithm was introduced and some of its convergence properties were outlined. In the sequel, the stochastic approximation framework for optimizing expectations of loss functions in an online, sample after sample, mode of operation was discussed. The gradient descent scheme and the online optimization rationale comprise the spine for a number of optimization algorithms that have been proposed and used for training feed-forward neural networks. The reason that we need to discuss these algorithms specifically in the context of neural networks is that the *multilayer* structure of these networks poses difficulties that have to be understood, prior to applying an off-the-shelf optimization scheme.

A major difficulty when dealing with the minimization of a cost function, such as in Eq. [(18.6)](#Chp018_885-995_B978044329238500024X.xhtml#deq14), in the framework of neural networks, is its *nonconvexity*. Convex functions were discussed in Chapter [8](#Chp008_343-416_B9780443292385000147.xhtml). There, it was pointed out that for the case of a convex function, every local minimum is also a global one. Recall, for example, a typical graph of a convex function in [Fig. 8.3](#Chp008_343-416_B9780443292385000147.xhtml). By the definition of a minimum of a function, we know that at this point the gradient (derivative in the simplest single-parameter space) becomes zero. Points where the gradient of a function becomes zero are known as *critical* or *stationary* points.

However, if the function is not convex, a stationary point can belong to one of the following three categories: to be (a) a local minimum (maximum), (b) a global minimum (maximum), or (c) a saddle point; see [Fig. 18.10](#Chp018_885-995_B978044329238500024X.xhtml#f0055) for the case of the one-dimensional space. All these stationary points are of importance in neural networks. A local minimum is the point where the value of the cost, `J(θ[l])`, becomes minimum within a region around `θ[l]`. The global minimum, `θ[g]`, is the point where `J(θ[g]) ≤ J(θ)`, ∀`θ` ∈ ℝ. A saddle point, `θ[s]`, is neither a minimum nor a maximum, yet the derivative (gradient in general) is equal to zero.

When adopting a gradient descent scheme to minimize a nonconvex cost function, the algorithm can converge to either a local or to a global minimum. Take, for example, the case of [Fig. 18.10](#Chp018_885-995_B978044329238500024X.xhtml#f0055). ```
