
### 20.5.2 ICA and higher-order cumulants

$p(\mathbf{s}) = \frac{1}{{(2\pi)}^{l/2}}\exp\left( - \frac{||\mathbf{s}||^{2}}{2} \right)$
![Equation](images/B9780443292385000263/si185.png)(20.46)

where for simplicity we have assumed that all the variables are normalized to unit variance. Let the mixing matrix, `A`, be an orthogonal one, that is, `A^(-1) = A^(T)`![Equation](images/B9780443292385000263/si186.png). Then the joint PDF of the mixtures is readily obtained as (see Eq. [(2.45)](#Chp002_19-64_B9780443292385000081.xhtml))

$p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{{(2\pi)}^{l/2}}\exp\left( - \frac{||A^{T}\mathbf{x}||^{2}}{2} \right)|\text{det}(A^{T})|$
![Equation](images/B9780443292385000263/si187.png)(20.47)

However, due to the orthogonality of `A`, we have `||A^(T)x||² = ||x||²`![Equation](images/B9780443292385000263/si188.png) and `|det(A^(T))| = 1`![Equation](images/B9780443292385000263/si189.png), which makes `p(s)`![Equation](images/B9780443292385000263/si190.png) indistinguishable from `p(x)`![Equation](images/B9780443292385000263/si191.png). That is, no conclusion about `A` can be drawn by observing `x`, as all related information has been lost. Seen from another point of view, the mixtures `x[i]`![Equation](images/B9780443292385000263/si76.png) are mutually uncorrelated, as `Σ[x] = I`![Equation](images/B9780443292385000263/si192.png), and ICA can provide no further information. This is a direct consequence of the fact that uncorrelatedness for jointly Gaussian variables is equivalent to independence (see Section [2.3.2](#Chp002_19-64_B9780443292385000081.xhtml)). In other words, if the latent variables are Gaussians, ICA cannot take us any further than PCA, because the latter provides uncorrelated components. That is, the mixing matrix, `A`, *is not identifiable* for Gaussian independent components. In a more general setting, in a case where some of the components are Gaussians and some are not, ICA can identify the non-Gaussian ones. Thus, for a matrix `A` to be identifiable, *at most one* of the independent components can be Gaussian.

From a mathematical point of view, the ICA task is ill-posed for Gaussian variables. Indeed, assume that a set of independent Gaussian components, `z`, have been obtained; then any linear transformation on `z` by a unitary matrix will also be a solution (as shown previously). Note that this problem is bypassed in PCA, because the latter imposes a specific structure on the transformation matrix.

In order to deal with independence one has to involve, in one way or another, higher-order statistical information. Second-order statistical information suffices for imposing uncorrelatedness, as is the case with PCA, but it is not enough for ICA. To this end, a large number of techniques and algorithms have been developed over the years, and reviewing all these techniques is far beyond the limits imposed on a book section. The goal here is to provide the reader with the essence behind these techniques and bring higher-order statistics into the game. The interested reader can delve deeper in this field from [55,58,81,100,120].
