
## BERT Embeddings in Fine-Tuning

The way the output BERT embeddings are utilized during fine-tuning depends on the nature of the corresponding task. For example, for classification-related tasks, such as entailment or sentiment analysis, the output `c ∈ ℝ^(d_m)` is utilized. Assuming that `K` classes are involved in the downstream task, the extra network that builds on top of `c` consists of:

a) A transformation matrix, say `W ∈ ℝ^(K × d_m)`, that outputs a `K`-dimensional vector (`Wc`), which is followed by
b) A softmax nonlinearity that acts on the elements of this vector (`Softmax(Wc)`) and transforms them into class probabilities.

On the other hand, for the question-answering task, the output vectors corresponding to the embeddings that comprise sentence "B" are utilized. The goal of this task is to extract that specific part from sentence "B" which, with high probability, is the answer to the question that comprises sentence "A." For more details, see [43].

## Remarks 19.9

*   **BERT Pretraining:** BERT was pretrained using the BookCorpus dataset and English Wikipedia. Two models of different sizes were trained:
    *   A small model comprising 110 million parameters (12 blocks, `d_m = 768`, and 12 attention heads).
    *   A large model comprising 340 million parameters (24 blocks, `d_m = 1024`, and 16 attention heads).
    At the time of publication, reported results showed significant performance improvements for a number of tasks compared to previous state-of-the-art models; see [43].

*   **BERT Analysis:** In [39], an analysis of the BERT network is performed to better understand what aspects and features of language these models learn. The analysis indicates that specific heads tend to specialize in particular relations. For example, there are heads that detect objects of verbs, determiners of nouns, objects of prepositions, and objects of possessive pronouns with relatively high accuracy. A summary of related results can be found in, e.g., [156].

*   **Vision Transformers (ViT & Swin):** Among the very first attempts to modify and apply transformers to computer vision are the ViT and Swin networks.
    *   The **Visual Transformer (ViT)**, proposed in [45], closely follows the BERT rationale. The image is split into patches, which are subsequently vectorized to form input tokens to the transformer. ViT employed patches of `16 × 16` pixels. In ViT, the supervised mode of pretraining resulted in better performance compared to masked self-supervision.
    *   The **Shifted Windows (Swin) Transformer** [116] is a variant of ViT that uses different patch sizes, starting from `4 × 4` to larger ones `16 × 16` as one moves upward to higher-level blocks. This hierarchical architecture has the flexibility to model at various scales. The proposed changes in the Swin transformer aim to cope with a major difference between language and vision: the large variations in the scale of visual entities and the high resolution of pixels in images compared to words in text.

### 19.5.3 Transformers: Some Concluding Remarks

Following the success of BERT and the GPT family of *large language models* (LLMs), a number of models and software packages appeared with similar capabilities (e.g., [150], [38], [187]). All these models have shown "admirable" state-of-the-art performance in a wide range of applications, such as question-answering, translation, word unscrambling, performing arithmetic operations, basic reasoning, program code generation, article learning, and text generation in prose often indistinguishable from that produced by an "average" person (e.g., [147], [90], [94]).

Besides NLP, transformers have been used in a wide range of applications including, but not limited to, computer vision (e.g., [99]), speech/audio processing and learning (e.g., [109]), biology (e.g., [64]), music generation (e.g., [205]), health (e.g., [113]), weather forecasting (e.g., [21]), and human life event prediction (e.g., [165]). In all these areas, their use has led to significant performance improvements compared to previous state-of-the-art techniques. Their learning power has ignited a new wave of applying such machine learning models in almost all aspects of human activity, ranging from science and technology to services and industry, and from gaming to education and human networking.

An interesting and important question is understanding and explaining why these models exhibit such impressive learning and performance capabilities. This is an ongoing, challenging research topic with many open questions. A detailed report on applying LLMs in various applications—pointing out capabilities, limitations, and possible future research directions—is given in, e.g., [28].

On the other hand, due to the previously reported capabilities, besides the advantages these models offer, they also pose a number of *social and ethical* threats related to misuse by malicious actors. Potentially, generated text can propagate stereotypes and prejudice related to gender, race, religion, political ideas, and human values. Moreover, such models can produce unreliable content containing wrong information; in other words, they suffer from "hallucinations" (see, e.g., [63]). Many related articles, statements, and discussions—often with opposing views—by prominent experts have been published in news and social media concerning potential dangers and possible measures to minimize these threats. Such measures could come from the technology itself and/or via an appropriate legal framework regulating the use of related platforms. A summary of opinions from world-renowned experts on such issues can be found in, e.g., [175]. An interesting poetic-like view compiled by two leading AI experts is given in [25].
