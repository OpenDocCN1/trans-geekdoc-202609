
$O_{t} = \mathbf{I} \ast \mathbf{H}_{t} = \sum\limits_{r = 1}^{d}h_{t}(1,1,r)I_{r},\ t = 1,2,\ldots,c.$

Stacking $O[t],\ t=1,2\ldots,c$ together, we obtain the volume $\mathbf{O}$ of dimension $k \times k \times c$. The operation is illustrated in [Fig. 18.28](#Chp018_885-995_B978044329238500024X.xhtml#f0150).

For example, we can employ $1 \times 1 \times 1$ convolution to reduce the size, by selecting $c < d$. In this way, although we reduce the depth, the elements of the new volume are weighted averages of the original $\mathbf{I}$. So, the original information is still retained within the new volume, in an averaging fashion. Often, once the new volume $\mathbf{O}$ is obtained, its elements are "pushed" through a nonlinearity, e.g., ReLU. Sometimes, the $1 \times 1 \times 1$ convolution followed by the nonlinearity is referred to as *network in network* operation and its purpose is to add an extra nonlinearity stage in the flow of operations through the network. Thus, in this context, if $c < d$, the network in network operation can be thought of as a nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique.

Looking at the $1 \times 1 \times 1$ convolution from a different viewpoint, it is nothing but a layer of a fully connected neural network, with $c$ nodes. The weights connecting the $t$th node with the $d$ input values are $h[t](1, 1, r),\ r=1, 2, \ldots, d$. This is the reason that we also call the operation network in network, in the sense that we embed a fully connected neural network between two successive convolution layers in the network. In this way, one can add an extra nonlinearity in the flow of operations through the network. In practice, the parameters of the respective $\mathbf{H}[t]$ tubes are learned during the training phase of the overall network.
