The previous algorithm is suboptimal. This is because, at each step, we optimize w.r. to a single quantity (dimension of the maximum value of a single vector), while keeping the previous decisions fixed. More elaborate schemes have been proposed and used in practice. For example, in the *beam search* algorithm, e.g., [177], more than one (conditional) probability values, e.g., the largest *K*, with *K* = 5 being a typical value, are selected at each stage and propagated through time. Although such an algorithm is still suboptimal, in this way, the final decision is based on information that relies on successive symbol combinations and not on each stage individually.

## Remarks 19.6

*   **The original transformer network** presented in [189] was trained on the WMT 2014 English-to-German and WMT 2014 English-to-French data sets and exhibited significant performance improvements compared to previous state-of-the art results at the time of publication. In that paper, two models were used: the base one, which comprised 65 million parameters, and the big one with 213 million parameters. More details on the adopted user-defined parameters can be found in the paper.

## 19.4 GPT: generative pretrained transformers

The publication of the transformer network in [189] ignited a new wave of research, both in theory as well as in the applications front, and a number of variants that spring from the underlying basic idea have been proposed. Among the very first attempts, in the framework of NLP tasks, were the GPT, [147], and the BERT, [43], models. Besides translation, which was treated before, these networks have been used in a wide range of other NLP tasks, such as textual entailment, question-answering, semantics similarity assessment, sentiment analysis, document classification, and text generation.

The first *generative pretrained transformer*, known as GPT-1, builds upon a fairly old idea, e.g., Section 19.2.2. First, one uses unlabeled data to pretrain a network. Once the pretraining phase has been completed, the learned parameters that describe the network are used as initial estimates for the next phase of supervised learning. During the latter, labeled data are used for the so-called fine-tuning to a specific task. Depending on the type of pretraining, different methods result. We will come to such techniques in more detail in Section 19.6.

In GPT, during pretraining, a transformer network is trained to learn to predict/generate the next word of an input text sequence of tokenized words, say, $\mathcal{U} = \{\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2, \dots, \mathbf{u}_N\}$, where in our current discussion *N* accounts for all tokens, including those of the special symbols. In contrast to the original transformer network, which involves two parts, i.e., the encoder and the decoder, in GPT only one stack of transformer blocks is employed. Each block comprises a) a masked self-attention layer, b) a residual "Add & Normalize" layer, c) a feed-forward fully connected network, and d) a final residual "Add & Normalize" layer, see Fig. 19.14. In other words, the structure is similar to that of the decoder block of the full transformer network of Fig. 19.13, by ignoring the encoder-decoder attention layer and the "Add & Normalize" layer above it. The goal in this section is to present the general idea behind GPT and not delving into details, which can be found in the related reference.
