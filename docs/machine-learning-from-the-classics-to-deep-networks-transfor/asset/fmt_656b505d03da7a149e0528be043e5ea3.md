
$$
\begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{x} & P_{x} \\ P_{x}^{*} & \Sigma_{x}^{*} \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{\theta}_{*} \\ \mathbf{v}_{*} \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p} \\ \mathbf{q}^{*} \end{bmatrix}
$$
(4.36)

where $\Sigma_{x}$ and $\mathbf{p}$ have been defined in Eqs. [(4.17)](#Chp004_119-175_B978044329238500010X.xhtml#deq26) and [(4.18)](#Chp004_119-175_B978044329238500010X.xhtml#deq27), respectively, and

$$
P_{x} := \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{T}], \quad \mathbf{q} := \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}y].
$$
(4.37)

The matrix $P_{x}$ is known as the *pseudocovariance/autocorrelation* matrix of $\mathbf{x}$. Note that Eq. [(4.36)](#Chp004_119-175_B978044329238500010X.xhtml#deq51) is the equivalent of Eq. [(4.28)](#Chp004_119-175_B978044329238500010X.xhtml#deq39); to obtain the widely linear estimator, one needs to solve one set of complex-valued equations whose number is double compared to that of the linear (complex) formulation.

Assume now that

$$
\Sigma_{x}^{*} = \Sigma_{x}, \quad P_{x} = 0.
$$
(4.38)

We say that in this case, the input–output variables are *jointly circular* and the input variables in $\mathbf{x}$ obey the (second-order) *circular* condition. It is readily observed that under the previous circularity assumptions, Eq. [(4.36)](#Chp004_119-175_B978044329238500010X.xhtml#deq51) leads to $\mathbf{v}_{*} = \mathbf{0}$ and the optimal $\mathbf{\theta}_{*}$ is given by the set of normal equations given in Eqs. [(4.16)](#Chp004_119-175_B978044329238500010X.xhtml#deq25)–[(4.18)](#Chp004_119-175_B978044329238500010X.xhtml#deq27), which govern the more restricted linear case. Thus, adopting the linear formulation leads to optimality only under certain conditions, which do not always hold true in practice; typical examples of such variables, which do not respect circularity, are met in fMRI imaging (see [1] and the references therein). It can be shown that the MSE achieved by a widely linear estimator is always less than or equal to that obtained via a linear one (Problem [4.9](#Chp004_119-175_B978044329238500010X.xhtml#o0050)).

The notions of circularity and of the widely linear estimation were treated in a series of fundamental papers [35,36]. A stronger condition for circularity is based on the PDF of a complex random variable: A random variable $x$ is circular (or strictly circular) if $x$ and $x e^{j\phi}$ are distributed according to the same PDF; that is, the PDF is *rotationally invariant* [35]. [Fig. 4.4](#Chp004_119-175_B978044329238500010X.xhtml#f0025)A shows the scatter plot of points generated by a circularly distributed variable, and [Fig. 4.4](#Chp004_119-175_B978044329238500010X.xhtml#f0025)B corresponds to a noncircular one. Strict circularity implies the second-order circularity, but the converse is not always true. For more on complex random variables, the interested reader may consult [3,37]. In [28], it is pointed out that the full second-order statistics of the error, without doubling the dimension, can be achieved if instead of the MSE one employs the Gaussian entropy criterion.
