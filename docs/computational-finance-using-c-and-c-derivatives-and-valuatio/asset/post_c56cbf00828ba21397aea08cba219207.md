## 9.6 SECURITISATION AND STRUCTURED PRODUCTS

In the United States Government Sponsored Enterprises (GSEs), Ginnie Mae, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were set up to provide larger residential mortgages and so guarantee the supply of mortgage credit that banks could extend to homebuyers. Initially the GSE held the mortgages in its portfolio or resell them to insurance companies, other investors. It profited from the difference (the spread) between the cost of funds and the interest paid on these mortgages.

By 1970 these GSEs also securitised the mortgages. A lender would assemble a pool of mortgages and issue securities backed by the mortgage pool. Those securities would be sold to investors, the GSE guaranteeing timely payment of principal and interest. In 1971, Freddie got into the business of buying mortgages, pooling them then selling mortgage-backed securities. Freddie collected fees from lenders for guaranteeing timely payment of principal and interest.

While GSEs had a near-monopoly on securitising fixed-rate mortgages that were within their timely loan limits, in the 1980s the markets began to securitise many types of loans, including adjustable-rate mortgages and other mortgages that the GSEs were not eligible or willing to buy. The mechanism used was: an investment bank, such as Lehman Brothers or Morgan Stanley, bundled loans from a bank or other lender into securities and sold them to investors, who received investment returned by the principal and interest payments from the loans. Investors held or traded these securities, which were often more complicated than the GSEs' basic mortgage-backed securities; the assets were not just leases, credit debt, car loans and housing loans. Over time, banks and securities’ firms used securitisation to mimic banking activities outside the regulatory framework for banks. For example, where banks traditionally took money from deposits to make loans and held them until maturity, banks now used money from the capital markets – often from money market funds to make loans, packaging them into securities to sell to investors.

Private securisations, or structured finance, had two key benefits to investors: pooling and tranching. If many loans were pooled into one security, a few defaults would have minimal impact. Structured finance could also be sliced and sold in portions – known as tranches – which lets buyers customised their payments. Risk-averse investors would buy tranches that pay off first in the event of default, but had lower yields. Return-oriented investors sought risk with higher yields. Bankers often compared it to a waterfall; the holders of the senior tranches – at the top of the waterfall – were paid before the junior tranches. If payments came below expectations, those at the bottom would be the first to be left and dry. Securitisation was designed to benefit lenders, investment banks and investors. Investment banks earned fees for issuing mortgage-backed securities. These securities fetched a higher price than if the underlying loans were sold individually, because the securities were customised to investors’ needs, were more diversified, and could be easily traded. ## 9.7 COLLATERALSED DEBT OBLIGATIONS

One problem with mortgage-backed securities was that, despite their relatively high returns, tranche other than triple-A could be hard to sell. If borrowers delinquent or defaulted, investors in these tranches would receive no payments because they were in the payment waterfalls. Solution to was the financial product called the collateralised debt obligation, or CDO. Bankers would take investment-grade tranches, largely rated BBB or A, from many mortgage-backed securities and repackage them into the CDOs. Approximately 80% of these CDO tranches would be rated triple-A despite the fact that they generally comprised the lower-rated tranches of mortgage-backed securities. CDO securities would be sold with their own waterfalls, with the risk-averse investors, again paid and the risk-seeking investors paid.
