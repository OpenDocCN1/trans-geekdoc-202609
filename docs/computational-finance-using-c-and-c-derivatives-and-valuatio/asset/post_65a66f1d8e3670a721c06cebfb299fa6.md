### 3.4 Generation of Multivariate Distributions

Let $\exp(Y)$ and $X$ have multivariate lognormal distribution which we by by $X \sim LN(\mu, \Sigma)$. We will represent the $p$ element mean vector of $X$ by $\tilde{m}$ and the $p \times p$ covariance matrix of $X$ by $S$. It can be shown

$$\Sigma_{i,j} = \log\left(1 + \frac{S_{i,j}}{\tilde{m}_i\tilde{m}_j}\right) \quad (3.4.7)$$

and

$$\mu_i = \log(\tilde{m}_i) - \frac{\Sigma_{i,i}}{2}, \quad i=1, \dots, p, j=1, \dots, p. \quad (3.4.8)$$

For the case of independent variables, we then have

$$\Sigma_{i,i} = \sigma_i^2 = \log\left(1 + \frac{S_{i,i}}{\tilde{m}_i^2}\right), \quad i=1, \dots, p \text{ and for } i \neq j, \Sigma_{i,j}=0,$$

### 4.1 INTRODUCTION

A European option taken out at current time gives the owner the right... could, for example, be the right to buy or sell at a particular price. If the option is worth $S_T$ at maturity, then the value of the put option at maturity is known as payoff. By contrast, a single asset vanilla call option, with strike price $E$ and expiry time $T$, gives the owner the right at time $T$ to buy an asset for $E$; the payoff for call is $\max(S_T-E)$. The owner of an American option has the right (but not the obligation) to exercise... options are more difficult to value than European options because of this extra flexibility. Even the single asset vanilla put has no analytic solution and requires finite difference and lattice methods to estimate its value. Many European options on the other hand take the form of relatively easy integral from which it is possible to compute a closed solution. The valuation of multi-asset European options, dependent on a large number of underlying assets, is more complicated but can conveniently achieved by using Monte Carlo simulation to compute the required multidimensional integral. The value of a single asset vanilla option will depend on the current asset price at time $t$, $S$, the duration of the option, $\tau = T-t$, the strike price, $E$, the riskless interest rate, $r$, and the probability density function of the underlying asset at maturity, $p(S_T)$.

### 4.2 PRICING DERIVATIVES USING A MARTINGALE MEASURE

In this section, we will briefly summarize the results of Harrison and Kreps (1979), and Harrison and Pliska (1981). We consider an economy over the time interval $[0,T]$ which consists of $n$ assets $S_i, i=1,\dots,n$, which can take the values $S_i, i=1,\dots,n,0\le t\le T$. Any asset $S_i$ which only takes values called a numeraire. Numeraires can be used to denom all the asset prices in the economy. So (for example) if $S^1$ is a numeraire, then the prices of the other assets denominated in units of $S_1$ are the relative prices $Z'_i=(S_i/S_1),i=2,\dots,n$.

One can find a unique probability measure $Q$ such that the relative prices $Z'_i,i=2,\dots,n$ are martingales. If either is free of arbitrage opportunities, then every payoff pattern $H_T$ can be represented as a linear combination of the asset values $S^T_i,i=1,\dots,n$, and in addition, the relative price process $(H_T/S^T_1)$ is a martingale. This means we can write

$$E^Q\frac{H_t}{S^T_t}=E^Q\frac{H_T}{S^T_T}, \quad \text{where } 0 \le t \le T.$$

The current (time $t$) value $V_t$ of payoff $H_T$ is

$$V_t=S^T_t\frac{H_T}{S^T_T}.$$

In general for a numeraire $N$ which takes the values $N_t, 0 \le t \le T$, we can write

$$V_t=N_te^Q\frac{H_T}{N_T}. \quad (4.2.1)$$

Equation (4.2.1) is very important because $V_t$ is the current (time $t$) price of a financial derivative with maturity $T$ and payoff $H_T$. It should be mentioned that the price of a financial derivative is independent of the martingale measure under which it is valued, and thus, the same price $V_t$ will be obtained for different numeraires $N$.
