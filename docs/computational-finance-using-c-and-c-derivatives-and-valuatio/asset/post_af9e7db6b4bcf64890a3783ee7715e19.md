
## 3.3.3 Student's $t$-Distribution

If $S_t(\mu,v)$ represents the Student's $t$-distribution with mean $\mu$ and number of degrees of freedom $v$, then $X \sim S_t(0,v)$ can be generated as follows:

```
$X \sim \frac{Z}{\sqrt{Y/v}} \qquad (3.3.15)
```

where $Z \sim N(0,1)$ and $Y \sim \chi_v^2$. The variance of $X$ is

```
$E[X^2] = \frac{\nu}{\nu-2}$.
```

Variates $X'$ from Student's $t$-distribution having $v$ degrees of freedom with mean $\mu$ and variance $s$ can be generated by modifying equation (3.3.15) as follows:

```
$X' \sim \mu + \frac{s^{1/2}}{\sqrt{v/(v-2)}}\sqrt{Y/v} \qquad (3.3.16)
```

The probability density function, $f(x)$, for $X'$

```
$f(x) = \frac{\Gamma((v+1)/2)}{\Gamma(v/2)^{-1/2}\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(v/2)} \left[ 1 + \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{s(v-2)} \right]^{-(v+1)/2} \qquad (3.3.17)
```

where $v > 2$.

## 3.4 GENERATION OF MULTIVARIATE DISTRIBUTIONS: CORRELATED VARIATES

In this section, we will show how to generate variates from a multivariate distribution with a known mean and a given covariance or correlation matrix. The methods described for covariance matrices are also applicable to correlation matrices, in which case the generated variates are normalized to have unit variance.

### 3.4.1 Estimation of Correlation and Covariance

Here, we show how to obtain a valid correlation matrix $C_R$ or covariance matrix $C$ from historic market data.

Let $\hat{X}$ be an $n \times p$ data matrix, with entries in the $i$-th row containing the $i$-th observation, and the $j$-th column containing the values of the $j$-th variable. If we create a new matrix $X$ such that the entries of the $j$-th column of $X$ are $X_{i,j} = \hat{X}_{i,j} - \mu_j, i=1,\dots,n$, where $\mu_j$ is the mean of the $j$-th column of $\hat{X}$, then the $p \times p$ matrix $C = X^T X$.

Further, if another matrix $\tilde{X}$ is defined such that $\tilde{X}_{i,j} = \frac{\hat{X}_{i,j}-\mu_j}{\sigma_j}, i=1,\dots,n$, where $\mu_j$ is the mean of the $j$-th column of $\hat{X}$, and $\sigma_j$ is the standard deviation of the $j$th column, then the $p$ by $p$ matrix $C_r = \tilde{X}^T \tilde{X}$ is the correlation matrix of $\tilde{X}$.

### Correlation matrix

Let us first consider the properties of a valid correlation matrix. They are the following:

- The matrix is symmetric with unit diagonal.
- The matrix has to be positive definite, that is, all the eigenvalues need to be positive.

We will now show that the $p$ by $p$ matrix $C_r$ is a valid correlation matrix if it can be factored as $C_r = X^T X$, where $X$ is a non-singular $n$ by $m$ data matrix.
The proof is as follows:

Since $X$ is non-singular, we can perform the singular value decomposition

```
$X = UKV^T$
```

where $U$ is $n$ by unitary matrix, $K$ is $n$ by $p$ matrix containing the (nonzero) singular values $\sigma_{i,1}=1\dots,p$, as the diagonal elements and zero elsewhere, and $V$ is $p$ by $p$ unitary matrix.
We have

```
$X^T X = (U K V^T)^T U V^T
= V K^T U^T U V^T
= V^T K^T K V^T
= V \Sigma V^T$
```

since $U^T U = UU^T = I_n$

Therefore,

```
$C_r = X^T X = V \Sigma V^T$
```

where $\Sigma$ is the $p$ by $p$ diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of $C_r$, and $V$ is the corresponding matrix of eigenvectors. since the $i$th eigenvalue satisfies $\lambda_i = \sigma_i^2$, it can be seen that all the eigenvalues are positive, and thus, $C_r$ must be positive definite.

If $C_r$ is positive definite, then we can perform the Cholesky decomposition $C_r = LL^T$ where $L$ is a lower triangular matrix.

### 3.4.2 Repairing Correlation and Covariance Matrices

There are situations when a supplied correlation matrix is not positive definite. Some of the reasons for this are the following:

- data. As a consequence, the elements in the correlation matrix may have been computed using pairwise correlations with a variety of sequence lengths. Under these circumstances, the equation $C_r = X^T X$ is no longer true, and so $C_r$ cannot be guaranteed to be positive definite.
- Manual adjustment of a correlation matrix may have occurred to reflect expected market conditions. This especially occurs when market crashes and certain stock prices become highly correlated. Under these circumstances, the best that can be done is to try and repair the correlation matrix $C_r$ into a valid correlation matrix $\hat{C}_r$.

When $C_r$ is not positive definite (the Cholesky decomposition fails), we use the eigen decomposition

```
$C_r = V\Sigma V^T,$
```

where

$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & & \lambda_2 & & \ddots & & \lambda_p \end{pmatrix};$$

then we form the matrix

```
$C_r^* = V K^T V^T,$
```

where the matrix $K$ is formed by taking the square root of the maximum of each eigenvalue and a very small number $\epsilon$ (say $\sim10^{-16}$). Thus,

$$K = \begin{pmatrix} \max(\lambda_1, \epsilon) & & \max(\lambda_2, \epsilon) & & \ddots & & \max(\lambda_p, \epsilon) \end{pmatrix};$$

The matrix $C_r^+$ is not acceptable as a correlation matrix because, although real, symmetric, and positive definite, its diagonal elements are not unity. It is possible to remedy this by pre- and post-multiplying $C_r^+$ by the diagonal matrix $F$.

$$\hat{C}_r = FC_r^+ F,$$

where $\hat{C}$ is the new correlation matrix, i.e., it is positive definite, symmetric. To achieve this, the diagonal elements of $F$ must be given by

$$F_{ii} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{C_{ii}}}.$$

We thus have

$$\hat{C}_r = F C_r^* F^T = F V K^T V^T F = (F V K)(F K)^T. \quad (3.4.1)$$

An optimally repaired matrix $C_r^*$, which minimizes the distance $||C_r - C_r^*||$, can be obtained via numerical optimization on the $n$-dimensional unitphere; this is described below. However it has been found that $\hat{C}_r$ is a very good approximation for the optimal estimate $C_r^*$.

Here, we provide details of how to obtain an optimally repaired matrix using hyperspherical coordinates, see Rebonato and Jäck (1999) – for a different approach see Higham (2002) or Qi and Sun (2006). The coordinates of the $i$th point on an $n$-dimensional hypersphere with radius $r$ can be shown to

$x_{i,1} = r \cos(\theta_{i,1}),$
$x_{i,2} = r \sin(\theta_{i,1})\cos(\theta_{i,2}),$
$x_{i,3} = r \sin(\theta_{i,1})\sin(\theta_{i,2})\cos(\theta_{i,3}),$
$x_{i,4} = r \sin(\theta_{i,1})\sin(\theta_{i,2})\sin(\theta_{i,3})\cos(\theta_{i,4}),$
$\dots$
$x_{i,n-1} = r \sin(\theta_{i,1})\sin(\theta_{i,2})\dots\sin(\theta_{i,n-2})\cos(\theta_{i,n-1}),$
$x_{i,n} = r \sin(\theta_{i,1})\sin(\theta_{i,2})\dots\sin(\theta_{i,n-2})\sin(\theta_{i,n-1}).$

where $\theta_{i,k}$ are hyperspherical coordinates and have the following constraints: $0 \le \theta_{i,k} \le \pi, k = 1, \dots, n-2$, and $0 \le \theta_{i,n-1} \le 2\pi$.

By construction, the radius of the sphere satisfies

```
$r^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (x_{i,k})^2.
```

This can be seen as follows:

```
$(B^T B)_{i,i} = r^2 \{ \cos^2(\theta_{i,1}) + \sin^2(\theta_{i,1}) (\cos^2(\theta_{i,2}) + \sin^2(\theta_{i,2})) + \cos^2(\theta_{i,3}) + \sin^2(\theta_{i,3})) \times ( \cos^4(\theta_{i,1}) + \sin^2(\theta_{i,n-2})\cos^2(\theta_{i,n-1}) + \sin^2(\theta_{i,n-1}) \dots ) } = r^2,
```

where we have used
$\cos^2(\theta_{i,k}) + \sin^2(\theta_{i,k}) = 1, k = 1, \dots, n-1.$

If $r = 1$, the coordinates of $n$-hyperspherical points are stored in the $n$ rows of the $n$ by $n$ matrix $B^T$, then

$B_{i,1}^T = \cos(\theta_{i,1}),$
$B_{i,j}^T = \cos(\theta_{i,j}) \sin^{j-1}(\theta_{i,k}) \quad j=2,\dots,n-1,$
$B_{i,n}^T = \sin^{n-1}(\theta_{i,k}), \quad n>1,$

$B^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & \dots & \dots \\ \cos(\theta_{2,1}) & \sin(\theta_{2,1}) & 0 & \dots & \dots & \dots \\ \cos(\theta_{3,1}) & \sin(\theta_{3,1}) & \cos(\theta_{3,2}) & 0 & \dots & \dots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \end{pmatrix}$

The Cholesky decomposition can be formed by setting the angles of the upper triangular elements of $B^T$ to zero, and this results in

```
$L^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & \dots & \dots \\ \cos(\theta_{2,1}) & \sin(\theta_{2,1}) & 0 & \dots & \dots & \dots \\ \cos(\theta_{3,1}) & \sin(\theta_{3,1}) & \cos(\theta_{3,2}) & 0 & \dots & \dots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \end{pmatrix}$
```

$L_{1,1}^T = 1,$
$L_{i,1}^T = \cos(\theta_{i,1}),$
$L_{i,j}^T = \cos(\theta_{i,j}) \sin^{j-1}(\theta_{i,k}) \quad j=2,\dots,i-1,$
$L_{i,j}^T = \sin^{i-1}(\theta_{i,k}), \quad i>1.$

We want to find the positive definite matrix $C_r^*$ which minimizes $||C_r - C_r^*||$.
This can be found by writing

```
$C_r^* = L^T L$.
```
