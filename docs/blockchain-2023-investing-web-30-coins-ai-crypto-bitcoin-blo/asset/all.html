What is Blockchain?

To comprehend Blockchain, you must know that a block is only a piece of data. Anything that may be stored digitally, like financial transactions, invoices, bills of lading, or hashes from the block previously added to the chain is included in that block.

What is a hash? Consider it a statistically determined fingerprint that identifies every piece of information in that block. The fingerprint of the previous block is included in every block, and this chaining of hashes gives the Blockchain its immutable characteristics. The hash of that block and all succeeding hashes will change if someone went back and changed a transaction in the chain.

What is Blockchain technology?

Blockchain technology stores data that makes it difficult or impossible for the system to be changed, hacked, or misused. A distributed ledger known as a Blockchain distributes and copies transactions among a system of computers.

Blockchain technology keeps public transactional records across numerous databases in a network connected by peer-to-peer nodes. These records are commonly referred to as "blocks," and the term "digital ledger" is widely used to describe this storage.

The owner's digital signature serves as a validation and fraud prevention mechanism for every transaction in this ledger and helps authenticate the transaction. The information in the digital ledger is, therefore, very secure.

The digital ledger can be compared to a network of computers sharing a Google spreadsheet, whereas transactional data are preserved per actual purchases. The data is available for public viewing, but it cannot be changed, which is a fascinating feature.

How Blockchain Started

The term "Blockchain" may conjure images of Bitcoin or other cryptocurrencies. This was the setting where the term was first introduced; but before all of that, there was the idea of a Blockchain.

David Chaum suggested a Blockchain-like system in his doctoral paper at the University of California, Berkeley, back in 1982.

Bellcore researchers submitted a report on using a Blockchain technique to ensure document timestamps nearly ten years later. They would later start a business called Surety (more on them in a second). Before 2008, a person or group, Satoshi Nakamoto, developed the Blockchain idea that would eventually power Bitcoin.

Relating to Surety, since 1995, it has been posting the most recent hash of each electronic document it has ever sealed in The New York Times Public Notices section. The fact that it is generally known taps into another approach that Blockchains can use. It would be challenging to covertly edit a block that has already been added to the chain, the more widely broadcast a hash is.

Since each participant has a copy of the most recent hash, this idea maintains integrity in public and consortium Blockchains. It ultimately evolved into Bitcoin's core, the well-known cryptocurrency that acts as a public record for all network transactions.

  3. A method of computing that can be used to store network transactions and records

Two keys make up a cryptography key: private and public keys. These secrets aid in the execution of successful transactions involving two parties. Blockchains come in four different varieties:

A single authority controls a private Blockchain network that functions for private corporations and organizations on closed networks. Private Blockchains allow businesses to set network characteristics, accessibility and permission choices, and other crucial security features.

Public Blockchain networks

Instead of being kept in one place, data is spread throughout a peer-to-peer network using DLT. Public Blockchains helped spread awareness of distributed ledger technology (DLT) and removed some difficulties and problems, including centralization and security weaknesses.

Permissioned Blockchain networks, sometimes called hybrid Blockchains, are private Blockchains that grant approved users exclusive access. Consortium Blockchains are also the best for working with various organizations. Similar to permissioned Blockchains, consortium Blockchains feature both public and private components. The Process of Transaction

The manner that Blockchain technology confirms and authorizes transactions is one of its defining features. For example, the first party would attach the transaction details to the second party's public key if they were to complete a transaction using their respective private and public keys. Then, this entire data is collated into a block.

The block comprises a timestamp, a digital signature, and other vital information. It should be emphasized that the block does not contain information on both parties' transaction identities. The block is then sent around the entire network, and the transaction is completed when the right user uses his private key to match it with the block.

Hash encryptions

Blockchain technology hashes and encrypts data, mainly using the SHA256 method to protect the data. The SHA256 method conveys information about the sender's address (public key), the recipient's address, the transaction, and the private key. After being verified, the hashed data is sent around the globe and put into the Blockchain in encrypted form. The sender and receiver's authentication is made more accessible by the SHA256 algorithm's virtually impenetrable hash encryption.

Proof of work

  • Each block in a Blockchain has four major headers.
  • Previous hash: addresses identifies the block before this one.
  • Transaction details: information about each transaction that must take place.
  • Nonce: in cryptography, an arbitrary integer is used to distinguish the block's hash address.
  • Block hash address: a hashing algorithm is used to send all the information mentioned above, including the transaction information and nonce. This produces an output that is the distinct "hash address," which is a 256-bit number with 64 characters in length. As a result, it is referred to as the block hash.
  • Using computer procedures, many people attempt to discover the appropriate hash value to satisfy predetermined criteria. When the predetermined condition is met, the transaction is finished. To put it simply, Blockchain miners attempt to solve a proof of work problem, a mathematical puzzle, and the first person to solve it earns a prize.

Mining

Adding transactional information to the current digital/public ledger is known as "mining" in Blockchain technology. Despite being linked to Bitcoin, the phrase is also used to describe other Blockchain technologies. Mining involves creating a block transaction hash that is difficult to forge. What Will Happen to Cryptocurrency After FTX Collapses?

Does the cryptocurrency market still have a bright future, or is the cryptocurrency bubble about to burst? A recent uptick in bankruptcy filings within the cryptocurrency industry has sparked worries about the long-term sustainability of digital assets and the industry.

First, think about the extent of the carnage: there has been a remarkable rise in the number of cryptocurrency market bankruptcy attributed to liquidity issues. Since their businesses could not continue, Three Arrows Capital, Alameda Research, Voyager Digital, FTX, Genesis BlockFi, and Celsius Network have either halted customer withdrawals or filed for bankruptcy.

The collapse of FTX, previously one of the biggest cryptocurrency exchanges in the world, was caused by a lack of assets on its balance sheet. Customers withdrew $650 million in assets on November 7 in response to rumors that the exchange may need to be properly liquidized. As a result, it was discovered that FTX was using customer funds to finance dangerous bets made by the affiliated trading company Alameda Research. The day before it failed, the exchange had only $900 million in liquid assets to offset its $9 billion in liabilities. The collapse of FTX, a market leader in the cryptocurrency space, was a big shock and diminished trust in digital assets.

Bitcoin owners have suffered significant losses: When you sum up the fall in the value of bitcoin, ether, and all the other digital currencies since they were at or around their peaks last year, you end up with a loss of more than $2 trillion in market cap.

Most of the current issues are on crypto lenders, an area of the cryptocurrency business that has experienced rapid growth in recent years. Crypto users who deposit their digital assets at a crypto lender or digital exchange also make money, just like customers of conventional banks do with interest rates on their savings. Due to historically low-interest rates, savings accounts at banks only provided minimal returns over the past couple of years. However, certain cryptocurrency lenders and exchanges provided substantially greater yields, frequently in the double digits and occasionally as high as 20%.

The truth about the unsustainable yields provided by some cryptocurrency exchanges and lenders has come to light. These cryptocurrency companies, like banks, make money from deposits by lending.

The fees that borrowers pay for the loan are a percentage, and cryptocurrency lenders earn from the difference between the interest payments made to depositors and the fees paid by borrowers. However, unlike conventional regulated lenders, crypto firms aren't under the control of banking regulators; as a result, few regulations are governing the capital they must retain or the things they may do with their clients' digital assets.

The current failure of these cryptocurrency lenders has also highlighted how intertwined many of these businesses are. FTX gave affiliated trading company Alameda Research billions of dollars, which were then used to finance dangerous trades. Alameda Research and Three Arrows Capital were on the list of debtors for the cryptocurrency lender BlockFi.

More cryptocurrency business failures appear likely. Additionally, other exchanges besides FTX were involved in shady trading, utilizing depositors' money when customers became concerned about the security of their digital currency deposits and wanted their money back.

Who is most at risk to the effects of crypto?

Thankfully, banks are secure. They each hold roughly $9 billion in cryptocurrencies. Banks paid attention to regulators' warnings to be cautious while investing in the asset class. Unexpectedly many retail investors possess cryptocurrency and experience some losses. Although broad, that exposure is very brief. In the US and Europe, 10% of homes have cryptocurrencies. The typical holding in the US is $1,000, and most European investors hold under $5,000.

Venture capital firms, which have staked much money on the cryptocurrency market, are the true bag holders. Only a few hundred of the 10,000 or so companies connected to the cryptocurrency industry are publicly traded. Venture capitalists, who funded the majority of the remainder, are now facing significant (and, in some cases, almost entirely) losses.

So, is the once-booming industry's end near?

Not exactly. Some more popular currencies, such as bitcoin and ether, appear resilient. For instance, Bitcoin is still four times higher than it was in December 2018 despite a 75% decline from its top in November 2021. The second-placed token, ether, has increased by more than 1,000%. But there is unquestionably a significant shrinking happening. Not all tokens will survive. Since their customers prefer to keep their cryptocurrency in "digital wallets," which often don't allow holders to earn a return on their cryptocurrency but ensure their assets stay safe, many exchanges and crypto lenders won't.

The exchanges that endure will have to put in a lot of effort to demonstrate to users that their money is safe and liquid rather than being the next FTX waiting to crash.

Washington will likely impose more regulations in the future to put an end to the cryptocurrency industry's ad hoc character. This shake-up is necessary for the industry's survival to eliminate the currencies and businesses that are nothing more than hype and to focus on the few promising players. It will be much tougher to demonstrate their usefulness and practical value now; gone are the days of easy riches.

BLOCKCHAIN-AS-A-SERVICE (BAAS)

Blockchain-as-a-Service (BaaS): what is it?

The term "Blockchain-as-a-service" (or "BaaS") refers to a cloud-managed platform that enables individuals and companies to create rapidly, host, and maintain Blockchain applications. The acronym "BaaS" is also frequently used to refer to "backend as a service."

The establishment and administration of cloud-based networks by a third party for businesses that develop Blockchain applications are known as Blockchain-as-a-service (BaaS). These third-party services are a recent innovation in the rapidly expanding field of Blockchain technology. The usage of Blockchain technology has expanded to include secure transactions of all kinds, going far beyond its most renowned use in bitcoin transactions.

With Blockchain-as-a-service, a third party manages the infrastructure and backend, decreasing the barrier to adoption for people and businesses. There is a need for hosting services as a result.

Learning about Blockchain-as-a-service (BaaS)

BaaS operates similarly to the software as a service (SaaS) concept and is, in fact, built on it. Using cloud-based solutions, customers can create, host, and manage their Blockchain apps and related Blockchain services. The cloud-based service provider keeps the infrastructure adaptable and functional simultaneously. BaaS is considered a breakthrough in the larger Blockchain ecosystem that will increase the business use of Blockchain.

Leading companies in the BaaS market include:

  • In 2015, Ethereum Blockchain-as-a-service was made available on Microsoft Azure thanks to a partnership between Microsoft and ConsenSys.
  • Amazon, which unveiled Amazon Managed Blockchain, a solution that "makes it easier to construct and administer large Blockchain networks" using open-source frameworks like Hyperledger Fabric and Ethereum.
  • R3, a collaboration of international financial institutions, created Corda, a distributed financial ledger.
  • PayStand, which focuses on transferring money between businesses.

Businesses and consumers alike are becoming more open to adopting Blockchain technology. However, the technical challenges and administrative burden associated with developing, establishing, running, and maintaining a Blockchain's infrastructure frequently serve as a barrier.

For a price, BaaS provides an outside service provider to establish all required Blockchain infrastructure and technologies. After it is constructed, the provider keeps taking care of the client's complicated backend processes. The BaaS provider often provides support services, including bandwidth management, appropriate resource allocation, hosting needs, and data security features. The BaaS provider frees the client to concentrate on the Blockchain's fundamental functions.

An illustration of Blockchain-as-a-service (BaaS)

The Hyperledger project's Blockchain-as-a-Service Hyperledger Cello, a BaaS-like Blockchain module toolkit and utility system. The function of a BaaS provider is comparable to that of a web hosting company. The website's content is created and maintained by its developers on their personal computers. They might engage support personnel or sign up with a third-party host like HostGator or Amazon Web Services. These outside companies handle the infrastructure and upkeep difficulties.

BaaS might be the impetus for a larger and deeper use of Blockchain technology across numerous business sectors and industry verticals. No matter how big or small, an organization may outsource the technically challenging job and concentrate on its core competencies rather than building and managing its own Blockchains.

What is Web 1.0?

Web 1.0 refers to the initial iteration of the internet. Think of the read-only or syntactic web as Web 1.0. The majority of participants were consumers of content. In contrast, most makers were web developers who created websites with content generally given in text or visual format. In general, Web 1.0 was around from 1991 until 2004.

In Web 1.0, static content was distributed via websites instead of dynamic HTML. A static file system was used instead of a database for data and content, and users could only do a little on the websites.

What is Web 2.0?

Most of us are only familiar with the web's current iteration, commonly referred to as Web 2.0, the interactive read-write and social web. In Web 2.0, you don't have to be a developer to participate in the development process. Many apps have been created so that anyone can make them.

Blockchain on the Verge of a Revolution in Investment

The term "Blockchain" was originally used in a written report published in 2008 by "Satoshi Nakamoto," the alleged pseudonymous individual or individuals who developed Bitcoin. At the time, "Bitcoin" was the name of the proposed peer-to-peer electronic cash system. With over 100 million users worldwide, Bitcoin has emerged as Blockchain technology's most popular application. However, because the underlying technology is rapidly proving to be a disruptive breakthrough, Blockchains can have many use cases beyond digital currency. A Blockchain is a distributed, decentralized public database that is cryptographically secure and maintains an immutable record of all previous transactions.

Database composition: traditional database vs. a distributed ledger

Technically speaking, a Blockchain is a type of distributed ledger technology (DLT) in which data is logged and kept in immovable units called blocks. The ledger is updated, and new blocks are added once a current block is filled. These blocks are safely connected (or chained) using cryptographic hashing. A powerful database is produced with a complete, verifiable, and immutable history of all previous transactions because each new chronological block of data references the previous one. Across an extensive network of computer systems or nodes, this database of transactions is freely transmitted and kept.

The databases throughout the entire network are likewise updated as blocks are added, and the ledger is updated, ensuring that every node has the same copy of the database.

Cryptographic proofs guarantee the data's accuracy and do away with the requirement for a centralized, trusted party like a bank. It is effectively "decentralized" because the data is spread across numerous nodes, and no single authority has control over or access to the database. There isn't a single point of failure as a result.

Blockchains make it possible to securely transfer digital assets without needing a centralized party to mediate the transaction. Although there are many different Blockchains and the technology is developing quickly. Generally, a Blockchain-based database is digital, decentralized, uses cryptography, has a consensus mechanism, and is immutable, real-time, and secure.

Association with cryptocurrency

Blockchain technology is being used for the first time in cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin. But there are now more applications for decentralized networks than just digital money. Not every Blockchain is a cryptocurrency, even though all cryptocurrencies are based on Blockchain technology. Because of its core characteristics, such as enabling untrusted parties to reach a consensus and enabling the safe proof of ownership of digital assets through encryption and digital signatures, Blockchain technology is the foundation for the existence of cryptocurrencies.

The inability to change or remove transactions from the Blockchain contributes to the rarity of digitally native assets and avoids double-spending. Cryptocurrencies would not be possible without Blockchain technology.

Bitcoin has drawbacks regarding transaction speed, fees, and volatility of its underlying value, despite being utilized by some as a sort of "peer-to-peer electronic cash" or medium of exchange. Due to its small block size and poor processing speed compared to high throughput payment networks like Visa or Mastercard, Bitcoin can only handle 7–10 transactions per second. Furthermore, the price of a Bitcoin transaction can frequently be more than a few dollars, depending on the demand for block space.

For big-value transactions, this is okay, but the costs are too costly for frequent, smaller-ticket purchases like those made through a credit card network. As a result, it is unlikely to be extensively used as a form of payment to exchange goods and services from various merchants.

We think that bitcoin's strongest application and the best product-market fit is a safe store of value. This idea is supported mainly by bitcoin's rarity, longevity, and adoption history. A total of 21,000,000 Bitcoins will be "mined" or created, and the Bitcoin network's encryption has been thoroughly tested over 13 years and found to be both secure and unbreakable. According to the "Lindy Effect" hypothesis, something's expected to last longer the longer it has been around. According to this standard, Bitcoin has established itself over a considerable amount of time, particularly by modern technology standards. It is likely to be increasingly recognized as an investible asset (like gold) and develop into a workable asset to store value for all investors worldwide.

Blockchain enables “smart contracts”

Ethereum and its native token, Ether, are the second most well-known uses of Blockchain technology. Ethereum is recognized as a second-generation application of the technology established with customization and programmability as a first premise if Bitcoin is seen as the first generation of Blockchain technology.

The broad programmability of Ethereum is far superior to Bitcoin's first-generation capability of straightforward balance transfers.

The idea of "smart contracts," or small computer programs that are distributed and run on a Blockchain network, was invented by Ethereum. These smart contracts, known as DApps (Decentralized Applications), make it possible for Ethereum to function as a "global internet computer" that never sleeps and can run various applications like your smartphone or home computer. While alternative Blockchain networks that support smart contracts and DApps have subsequently emerged, Ethereum continues to be the most widely used one, with over 80% of DApp activity taking place on top of the Ethereum network.

The greatest ecosystem of developers creating tools and programs is likewise focused on Ethereum. ERC-20 smart contracts, which take advantage of the security, data accessibility, and execution capabilities of the underlying Ethereum Blockchain, are present in many of the most frequently traded tokens.

Smart contracts are increasingly facilitating all kinds of financial transactions. Also known as DeFi, this group of on-chain applications and protocols offer financial services and are constructed on top of decentralized networks without centralized intermediaries (decentralized finance).

DeFi will replace some roles of the traditional financial intermediaries.

The field of traditional financial services, or "TradFi," is home to one of the most promising and potentially revolutionary Blockchain applications. The ecosystem of decentralized finance, also known as "DeFi," can completely rewire our financial system and give it significantly higher levels of efficiency, openness, justice, and inclusivity when compared to "TradFi." The current banking sector accounts for around 8% of the world's GDP, so DeFi is a considerable possibility.

Decentralization occurs in various financial services, including lending, borrowing, exchange trading, derivatives, yield farming, staking, insurance, and asset management. The goal of DeFi is to make financial services more widely available and to reduce the influence of centralized institutions. With all the advantages of a public Blockchain, which directly addresses many of Traditional Finance's major drawbacks, DeFi also aims to enhance "TradFi." DeFi stands in stark contrast to TradFi because it is decentralized, trustless (i.e., doesn't demand blind trust in a third party), permissionless, composable, and transparent. TradFi is centralized, requiring trust in third-party intermediaries, has disparate systems and regulatory hurdles, and is opaque.
