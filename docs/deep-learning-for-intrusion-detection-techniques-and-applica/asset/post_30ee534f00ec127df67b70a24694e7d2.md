
This dataset provided a rich set of real and simulated machine activities, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of IDS performance.

The DARPA 1998 dataset classifies attacks into five primary categories: probe, denial of service (DoS), remote to local (R2L), user to remote (U2R), and data attacks. Probe attacks, such as ipsweep, portsweep, and satan, involve scanning networks to gather information on valid IP addresses, open ports, operating systems, and known vulnerabilities. DoS attacks, like selfping, tcpreset, and arppoison, are designed to disrupt or crash system services by overwhelming the target with traffic or exploiting vulnerabilities. R2L attacks involve external attackers gaining unauthorized access to systems without valid accounts, using techniques like guest or dict for password guessing, or utilizing tools like ppmacro to exfiltrate data. U2R attacks, such as buffer overflow or privilege escalation techniques, allow local users to gain unauthorized administrative privileges. Data attacks involve the exfiltration of sensitive files from a system, such as ntfsdos or sqlattack, where files that should remain secure are illegally accessed and transferred. The dataset featured 24 attack types in the training phase, while the test data introduced 14 additional attack types, reinforcing the idea that many new cyberattacks are variations of existing ones and that their signatures could help in detecting emerging threats. The DARPA 1998 dataset significantly advanced the field of intrusion detection by providing a comprehensive, standardized testbed for evaluating IDSs. By including both normal network traffic and labeled attack data, it allowed researchers to assess IDS performance in detecting a wide variety of real and simulated attacks. The dataset contributed to the development of multiple IDS technologies, including signature-based and anomaly-detection methods, and helped establish objective evaluation metrics that are still used today.

Despite its widespread use, the DARPA IDS evaluation dataset has faced some criticism. One significant limitation is the lack of publicly available traffic generation software, which makes it difficult to assess the accuracy of the background traffic used in the evaluation. Additionally, the evaluation criteria did not consider factors such as system resource usage, ease of use, or the type of system being used, which are important for real-world applications. Critics like McHugh, Mahoney and Chan have pointed out other flaws, such as the use of synthetic background traffic that lacks the complexity of real-world network conditions, including packet storms and anomalous traffic. McHugh also criticized the reliance on scripts and programs for generating attacks, and the simple models used for background traffic, which do not adequately simulate real-world noise, potentially leading to an inflated detection rate. Mahoney and Chan further highlighted issues such as inconsistencies in packet TTL values between normal and attack traffic, which could make detection easier even for basic IDSs. The dataset’s isolation from the internet also means it misses anomalous but non-malicious traffic commonly seen in real-world scenarios, which could affect the realism of the dataset’s evaluations. While these limitations have led to ongoing discussions about the adequacy of the dataset for real-world testing, the DARPA dataset remains a foundational resource in IDS research. The inclusion of detailed system logs and file system snapshots in addition to network traffic allows for a more in-depth analysis of attack scenarios. As a result, the DARPA 1998 dataset remains a crucial resource for the continued advancement of network security, offering valuable insights for researchers and practitioners working to improve the accuracy and effectiveness of IDSs.

## 6.4.2 KDD 1999

The KDD Cup is an annual data science competition that has been running since 1997, organized by the knowledge, discovery, and data mining (KDD) community. The competition provides a platform for researchers and practitioners to apply ML and data mining techniques to real-world datasets, focusing on challenging, large-scale problems. The KDDCup99 is the dataset created in 1999, based on a DARPA intrusion detection dataset that simulated network traffic, including both normal and malicious (intrusive) activities. The competition aimed to develop models that could classify connections as either “good” or “bad.” The motivation behind the dataset’s creation was to improve the capability of IDSs.

To enhance the dataset’s utility, Stolfo et al. [7] introduced derived features that aid in distinguishing between normal and malicious connections. These features fall into several categories. Time-based traffic features, such as “same host” and “same service” features, analyze connections within a short time window (two seconds) to identify patterns related to protocol behavior and service use. Host-based traffic features, which examine connections to the same destination host over a larger window of 100 connections, address the needs of probing attacks that operate over extended intervals. Content-based features were also included to detect suspicious behaviors within the data portions of packets, such as failed login attempts, particularly useful for R2L and U2R attacks, which are often embedded in packet data rather than reflected in sequential patterns. This comprehensive approach to feature engineering made the DARPA dataset and its derivatives a valuable resource for intrusion detection research. However, challenges remain, particularly in mining unstructured data portions of packets and identifying subtle patterns in complex attacks. These limitations highlight the ongoing need for innovation in algorithm development and feature extraction, ensuring that IDSs can keep pace with the ever-evolving landscape of cyber threats.
