However, in the context of smart environments, many security threats are novel and cannot be predicted or explicitly labeled in advance. For example, a new type of malware might exploit a previously unknown vulnerability in an IoT device, and traditional systems would need a preexisting label to detect it, which is not possible without prior knowledge of the attack [16].

### 9.8.1.2 High-dimensional Data Processing: Learning Complex Patterns in Smart Environments

Smart environments are sources of a tremendous amount of data generated by a variety of interconnected devices, sensors, and systems. This is usually high-dimensional data, meaning that it is composed of a large number of variables that may interact in complex ways. For instance, in a smart home, temperature, humidity, motion, and energy consumption sensor readings are all interconnected and need to be considered together when identifying normal and anomalous behavior [18]. In industrial IoT systems, high-dimensional data could include sensor readings from hundreds of machines, operational logs, environmental factors, and more.

Deep learning models are particularly effective at processing this high-dimensional data because of their ability to learn complex, hierarchical features. Models such as autoencoders and generative adversarial networks (GANs) are well-suited for anomaly detection in such contexts as follows:

- **Autoencoders** are neural networks that learn to compress (encode) the input data into a lower-dimensional representation and then reconstruct (decode) the data back to its original form. The model is trained on normal data, and upon any anomaly, the reconstruction error increases because the model cannot reproduce the anomalous data from its lower-dimensional representation. For example, in an industrial IoT scenario, an autoencoder could learn the normal patterns of operation of machines. In the event that a machine starts to malfunction or is tampered with, the anomaly will appear in the form of an increased reconstruction error.

- **Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)** consist of two networks: the generator is responsible for producing synthetic data, and the discriminator decides how realistic the produced data is. GANs can be applied for anomaly detection by training the generator for normal data distributions and training the discriminator to recognize differences between real and synthetic data. If the data that the generator produces differs dramatically from the normal distribution or is an anomaly, then the discriminator flags this event as anomalous [19].

Autoencoders and GANs are particularly effective in learning the underlying structure of high-dimensional data without requiring explicit feature engineering, which makes them more suitable for use in smart environments where the data often is too complex for traditional methods.

## 9.8.2 Real-Time Analysis: Continuous Monitoring and Threat Detection

Real-time anomaly detection is required for smart environments because of their dynamic nature. Systems are always interacting, changing, and evolving. Smart environments, unlike static ones, are always in flux because new devices and systems are being added, and new behaviors keep emerging. Anomalies need to be detected in real time to prevent damage, address security breaches, and maintain operational efficiency. For example, a smart grid might need to detect unusual patterns of electricity consumption instantly to prevent a potential cyberattack or to isolate malfunctioning equipment before it affects the entire system.

Deep learning models, specifically RNNs and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, perform exceptionally well with time-series data, a common feature in smart environments. Time-series data involves data collected at consistent intervals, such as sensor readings over time, network traffic patterns, or user interactions with devices. RNNs and LSTMs are designed for sequential data processing and therefore have the ability to learn patterns over time.

- **Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)** are designed to account for sequential dependencies between data points. For instance, an RNN might examine traffic patterns in a smart city, where the behavior of vehicles at one time step is heavily dependent on previous time steps. If a vehicle starts moving in a pattern that is unusual for it (say, taking a different route than it usually does), the RNN can raise this as an anomaly.

- **Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks**, a kind of RNN, are particularly suited to discovering long-term dependencies in time series. For example, with regard to smart environments, LSTMs can learn complex temporal patterns, like user behavior in smart homes, device interaction in industrial settings, or environmental changes in smart cities. Because LSTMs can “remember” previous states over long periods of time, they are very valuable for detecting anomalies that creep up gradually or have long-run effects, such as gradual cyberattacks or system failure unfolding over time.

For instance, when it is a smart home, using an LSTM model, house members can be continuously monitoring their activity of turning off or on the lights, unlocking or locking doors, or adjusting the thermostats. Upon such a point where actions tend to behave far away from the previous normal trends of behavior—it may have sent an alarm in real time, the system having sensed that somebody has unlocked a door at inconvenient hours or somebody had turned off lights in an empty dwelling.

Real-time anomaly detection in smart environments is important not only for the identification of security threats but also for the continued operation of the system. A deep learning-based system capable of real-time analysis ensures that anomalies are detected and addressed as soon as they occur, preventing more serious issues from developing.

## 9.8.3 Intrusion Detection Systems (IDSs) Using Deep Learning

IDS stands for an intrusion detection system that monitors network traffic and system behavior to detect signs of unauthorized access or malicious activity [20]. Deep learning enhances traditional IDS through better accuracy and adaptability as detailed below:

- **Improved Detection Accuracy:** Traditional IDSs usually work on signature-based methods which are limited to known attack patterns. Deep learning models may identify novel attack patterns as they learn from large datasets of both normal and malicious activity.
- **Adaptability to New Threats:** Moreover, as smart environments are always adapting, new kinds of attacks emerge. Deep learning systems can learn and adapt in real time; therefore, their capability to recognize zero-day threats can also be improved.
- **Network Traffic Analysis:** Deep learning models can analyze network traffic at multiple layers of abstraction to detect high-level attack strategies, such as distributed DoS attacks, and low-level intrusions, like port scanning or malware.
