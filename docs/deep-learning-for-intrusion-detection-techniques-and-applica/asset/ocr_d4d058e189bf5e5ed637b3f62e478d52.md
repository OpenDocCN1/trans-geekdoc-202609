Various DL architectures are effective in zero-day attack detection. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are commonly used in image processing but can also be adapted for visualizing network traffic to identify spatial patterns indicative of malicious behavior [16]. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term memory networks (LSTMs) are tailored for sequential data, making them suitable for analyzing time-series data like network logs [17, 18]. Autoencoders, which learn compact representations of data, are ideal for anomaly detection, focusing on deviations from normal patterns [19]. Generative adversarial networks (GANs), comprising a generator and a discriminator, enhance detection by simulating potential attack scenarios [20]. Effective feature engineering is a cornerstone of DL-based intrusion detection. Important features can be extracted from network packets, including protocol types, payload sizes, and source-destination pairs. Flow-level features, such as duration and inter-arrival times, and system-level logs, capturing events and user activities, are crucial for building comprehensive models [21]. Preprocessing steps like normalization and encoding ensure data is in a suitable format for training [7].

Several datasets are used to train and evaluate intrusion detection models. NSL-KDD, CICIDS 2017, CICIDS 2018, and UNSW-NB15 provide diverse and realistic traffic patterns that include modern attack scenarios [8, 14, 21]. Custom datasets generated by organizations reflect environment-specific behaviors and improve model relevance. DL models for intrusion detection can be trained using supervised approaches, which rely on labeled data, or unsupervised methods that detect anomalies without labels. Hybrid models combining these methods offer robust performance for identifying zero-day attacks. Model training and optimization involve techniques such as hyperparameter tuning, regularization to prevent overfitting, and cross-validation for reliable evaluation. Performance metrics include accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1 score, which balance false positives and false negatives. Tools like receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) scores help visualize model performance [22]. Deploying DL models in real-world environments introduces additional considerations. Real-time processing capabilities, scalability to handle large data volumes, and mechanisms for continuous updating are critical. Despite their power, DL models face challenges, including data imbalance, adversarial attacks, and a lack of explainability. Innovations such as transfer learning, federated learning, and explainable AI are emerging to address these issues. DL offers significant advancements in detecting zero-day attacks by automating feature extraction, modeling complex patterns, and adapting to evolving threats. By integrating DL into IDS, organizations can enhance their cybersecurity posture and protect against sophisticated exploits. Ongoing research will continue to refine these techniques, making them more resilient, interpretable, and effective in safeguarding digital ecosystems.
