Another limitation in NBA systems is the delay in detecting attacks, as they depend on streaming data from network devices and routers. Data is sent to the IDS in batches over time, ranging from a minute to several hours. Due to this delay, if an attack occurs quickly during this period, it may go undetected or may have already compromised the system. Addressing this limitation necessitates a more powerful system capable of monitoring without relying on streaming data.

They are also referred to as knowledge-based detection or misuse-based systems. These systems depend on established repositories of attack information to classify incoming traffic as either normal or a recognized type of attack. Operating on pattern-matching techniques, they identify known attacks by comparing intrusion signatures with those stored in the database. When a match occurs, indicating a previously identified intrusion signature, an alarm signal is triggered. Examples of signatures include an attack attempt using the username "root," which poses a threat to network security, an email with the subject line "Free Programs" commonly associated with prevalent malware, or a system log indicating the disabling of host control in the operating system. In this system, logs of hosts are observed to identify actions or commands previously diagnosed as malware. The fundamental idea is to build a database of intrusion signatures and use it to assess ongoing actions by comparing them with existing signatures. If a match is found, an alert is generated. For instance, a regulation in the format of "if antecedent, then consequent" could result in labeling a scenario as an attack if the source IP address matches the destination IP address. Signature-based intrusion detection systems (SIDSS) are utilized in widely used tools like Snort and NetSTAT.

Traditional methods of detecting potential attacks in SIDSS typically involve examining network packets and comparing them to a database of predefined signatures. In cases where an attacker substitutes a malicious file named "prog.exe" with a different name, such as "prog2.exe," a signature searching for "prog.exe" would not detect the attack.
