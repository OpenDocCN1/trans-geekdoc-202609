# Dimensionality reduction

Let's say that we are interested in the marginal probability of selecting a participant at random from a specific age bracket. This can be written in general terms as $P(age_c) = rac{r_c}{n}$, i.e. the marginal probability of any age in the row $r_c$ as a fraction of all participants. For the probability of selecting a participant under 20, we therefore have $P(c < 20) = rac{2+1+3+5+14}{100} = rac{25}{100}$ (Fig. 5.9b (orange row)). If we are interested in the marginal probability that an individual suffers from heart disease, we instead need the column total (Fig. 5.9c); $P(H) = rac{1+3+4+8}{100} = rac{16}{100}$.

Joint probabilities summarize the chance of two more events co-occurring. For example, if we want the joint probability that an individual aged 60-80 with mental health illness, we need the total from the cell at intersection of these two events: $q$ (shown in Fig. 5.9d). The joint probability is then the value divided by the total number of samples: $P(60 - 80, M) = rac{4}{100}$.

Finally, conditional probability measures the probability of one event given another. For example, to calculate the probability of an individual suffering from mental illness that they know are 20–40, it is first necessary to take the subset of examples corresponding to participants of age 20–40 (Fig. 5.9e highlighted in orange) and then estimate the proportion of examples that have mental health illness relative to the row: $r_c$. Hence $P(M|20-40) = rac{6}{30} = 1/5$. If our interest was to include the conditional probability of age disease status, we would express the conditioning as the cell total relative to the column total, e.g., $P(20-40|M) = rac{6}{20}$.

In the case of mental health and age groups, we can see that the marginal probabilities of selecting an individual with mental health illness irrespective of age group are $P(M<20) = P(M|20-40) = P(M|40-60) = P(M|60-80) = P(M>80) = 1/5$. Further, the marginal probability of selecting an individual with mental health illness irrespective of age (Fig. 5.9f (blue column)) is $P(M) = rac{20}{100} = 1/5$. So, $P(M|20-40) = P(M)$—the addition doesn't impact our certainty at all. Another feature of independent events is that their joint probability is equal to the product of the individuals. This is derived from the product rule: $P(A, B) = P(A)P(B)$, where independent $P(A|B) = P(A)$, then $P(A, B) = P(A)P(B)$.
