## 5.5.7 Biomedical applications of ICA

A very common use of ICA is to isolate source signals from electrophysiological or functional recordings of the brain. Here, $X_M$ might represent neuronal recordings measured from electroencephalogram (EEG) records, or indirect measures of brain activity recorded from various locations in the brain, as done in functional MRI (fMRI). In the case of fMRI, most often spatial ICA is implemented, which means that the underlying assumption is that the source signals are nonoverlapping regions of the brain that share a common pattern of brain activity (Fig. 5.13). Then, the loading coefficients $A$ may be interpreted as the time series of patterns of activity. In this way, ICA can also be used to remove nuisance signals, e.g., generated through physiological processes (breathing, cardiac pulsation) or motion [41].

## 5.6. Manifold learning

In some circumstances, linear embeddings are insufficient to model the underlying distribution of the data. Take the toy example of Fig. 5.14. Here, a uniform distribution of points, originally generated in 2D (Fig. 5.14a), is subsequently rolled up into a 3D structure (Fig. 5.14b). We can see from points the black dashed arrow (Fig. 5.14b) that Euclidean distances shortcut between points that were originally distant on the plane. For instance, PCA and ICA embeddings will always fail to recover the data of nonlinearly distributed points: A 2D PCA embedding of the swiss roll dataset will severely collapse all points onto a plane that projects the points through the directions of variance through the data (Fig. 5.14c). What we need is to recover the geodesic distances (Fig. 5.14b (red dashed arrow)) that follows the curve of the manifold. Scikit-learn offers several approaches for this [2,3,11,39,46,47,51], each with slightly different techniques for approximating the underlying geodesic distances.^21 In this chapter, we will focus on Laplacian eigenmaps [2], which is known as spectral-embedding, which is one of the most intuitive to understand.

## 5.7. Laplacian eigenmaps

In essence, a manifold is a topological space for which Euclidean distances are only meaningful locally. Thus, one way is to construct a k-Nearest Neighbor (kNN) graph, which connects each point with its k closest points. In this way, geodesics along the manifold may be approximated by stepping from neighbour to neighbour, and summing the Euclidean distances between them. Fig. 5.15 shows a toy example for a simple function. Thus, Laplacian eigenmaps work by constructing and binarizing a kNN graph, otherwise known as adjacency matrix A. This is then used to calculate the graph Laplacian: $L = D - A$, where $D$ is the degree matrix calculated by summing the total number of neighbors of each node ($D = \sum A$). This represents a discrete approximation of the Laplace operator, whose eigenvectors represent the spectra (or harmonics) of the graph. One way to intuitively understand the links between the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian is to understand that the roots corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues reflect a random walk across the manifold, such that points having similar values must be close together on the manifold. In this way, it is possible to use the eigenvectors of the graph to ‘unwrap’ the manifold into a lower-dimensional space.

## 5.7.1 Calculation of the graph Laplacian

Estimation of the graph Laplacian may be broken down into the following steps:

![](img/d219b4c20ff50f4b97845dec7a9acecb_148_0.png)

![](img/d219b4c20ff50f4b97845dec7a9acecb_149_0.png)

**Figure 5.15** Geodesic distances through $k$-NN graph. Top: green edges connect the 3 nearest neighbors of every point; Middle: black arrows indicate the shortest path through the graph; Bottom: for choice of $k$ this will approximate a geodesic along the manifold.

- 1. Construction of the *adjacency* matrix through calculation of squared distances between all points: $SSD = \sum(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i)^2$;
- 2. Set all $k$ smallest distances in each row to zero; then all remaining distances to 1;
- 3. Calculate the *degree* matrix by summing the total number of neighbors of each node: $\mathbf{D} = \sum \mathbf{A}$;
- 4. Subtract the adjacency matrix from the degree matrix to return the graph Laplacian: $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{D} - \mathbf{A}$

For a set of $N$ examples, $\mathbf{A}$ should be a $N \times N$ shaped matrix, where each row represents each node and the entry indicates which examples are neighbors of that node. The first step in calculation is therefore to estimate the squared distances between all points (since we are interested only in the magnitude and not the direction). **A** is initialized with all zeros; then, each row is populated with 1 in columns that represent the closest neighbors of that node.^22 Finally, we need A to be symmetric, or otherwise the derivation of the Laplacian will not hold. Thus, the matrix A is symmetrized as $A_{ij}=A_{ji}$.

Let’s step through the following simple example for the data matrix $X$ with 6 examples, each with 2 features:

```python
X = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 & 1 & 3 & 4 & 2 & 2 & 3 & 0 & 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} (5.71)
```

To calculate the adjacency matrix A, we must first estimate the sum of square distances (SSD) between all points. Let us do this for the first data point. We write the calculation in the style of Python broadcasting as:

```python
x_0 - X = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 & 4 & 3 & 4 & 4 & 2 & 4 & 3 & 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 3 & 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 & 1 & 1 & 3 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} (5.72)
```

To calculate the SSD for this point, we need to square^23 and sum the distances:

```latex
$$\sum_j (x_0 - X_j)^2 = \sum_j \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 3 & 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 & 1 & 1 & 3 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}^2 = \sum_j \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 9 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 4 & 1 & 1 & 9 & 4 & 9 \end{pmatrix} = 5 \ (5.73)$$
```

> $^{22}$ Note that it is common practice to binarize A; however, weighted adjacency matrices can also be used.

> $^{23}$ * Represents element-wise multiplication.
