The result of early damage detection (pre-collapse prediction) is shown in Fig. 4.7, where the horizontal line is the threshold limit obtained from the standard confidence interval under 5% significance level.

As Fig. 4.7 appears, the novelty scores of the training data between the samples 1–4100 show serious variations implying the effects of the EOCs available in these indices. An important observation is that the pattern of variability in these novelty indices highly resembles the augmented displacement samples and their MAD values presented in Fig. 4.6a. Due to severe changes in the novelty scores of these samples, one cannot yield a reliable and correct decision on the status of the bridge in the first 41 images. To put it another way, even though we knew that the Tadcaster bridge operated normally on the dates of these images, it is expected that the MD values from HMC-MD should fall below the threshold. This means that the application of this technique is limited to other real-world problems with severe EOCs.

Regarding the novelty indices of the validation data points, it is seen that there is a gap and difference between them. Moreover, some novelty scores of these points incorrectly exceeded the threshold. On the other hand, although almost all the novelty indices of the last two images are over the threshold limit, it is observed that some indices have smaller values compared to the undamaged state. In other words, the distance quantities of some training and validation samples are greater than the corresponding samples of the last two images, especially Image 44. Thus, the outcome of this comparison confirms the importance of data normalization for mitigating the negative influences of the EOCs. Subsequently, the process of early damage detection is carried out by the proposed HMC-DAE-MD technique to demonstrate the positive effect of data normalization. For this purpose, it is necessary to train a DAE by the augmented training data X. According to the proposed hyperparameter optimization for this ANN (see Sect. 2.3.2.2), one should define some trials for the neuron sizes of the DAE hidden layers. In this regard, the sample neurons for the mapping ($N_{m_0}$), encoding ($N_{e_0}$), and de-mapping ($N_{d_0}$) layers are set as 30, 10, 30, respectively. According to the initialization of this hyperparameter optimization as mentioned in Algorithm 1, $N_{m_0} = N_{d_0}$ and $N_{m_0} > N_{e_0}$. The results of this hyperparameter optimization (the variance amounts of the MD) are presented in Table 4.1. In this regard, the optimal neuron numbers should force the HMC-DAE-MD method to provide smooth novelty indices for the training data with the lowest rate of variability. Therefore, the optimal neurons are those that have the minimum variance value. In Table 4.1, it can be observed that the minimum variance coincides with the 28th row and 4th column of the stored variance matrix (Step 7 of Algorithm 1). This means that the DAE with 28 neurons for the mapping and de-mapping layers and 4 neurons for the encoding layer can satisfy the condition of the lowest rate of variability and can properly mitigate the EOC effects. Therefore, one should design a DAE using the three hidden layers and the selected neuron sizes. It is worth remarking that the learning process of this DAE is based on the Levenberg–Marquardt backpropagation under 1000 epochs for estimating the model-parameters (i.e., the weight matrices and bias vectors).

Using the learned DAE, the augmented training and test matrices are fed into this neural network to extract their residual matrices E$_\mathbf{x}$ and E$_\mathbf{z}$, which are the main inputs of the MD-based novelty detector for feature discrimination. To better demonstrate the positive effect of data normalization by the DAE, the initial result of feature discrimination ignores any threshold limit. As a comparison, Fig. 4.8 evaluates the evolution of the novelty indices gained by HMC-MD and HMC-DAE-MD. As can be seen, despite high variability of the distance values in the training data points of Fig. 4.8a, the DAE highly assists HMC-MD in reducing the influences of the EOCs and providing smoother outputs with lower variability. This makes sense that HMC-DAE-MD significantly outperforms HMC-MD. The same conclusion can be achieved concerning the validation data. In Fig. 4.8b, one can discern that the sudden gap and difference is not available and both groups of validation data regarding Images 42 and 43 have similar novelty indices. In relation to the test data related to Images 44 and 45, it is seen that their distance values in Fig. 4.8b are more distinguishable from the distances of the undamaged condition (i.e., training and validation points) compared to Fig. 4.8a. Thus, it can be deduced that the DAE-aided data normalization not only mitigates the EOC effects but also helps the MD metric to increase the detectability of damage.
