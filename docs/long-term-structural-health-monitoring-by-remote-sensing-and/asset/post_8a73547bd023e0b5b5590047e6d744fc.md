To sample from the marginal density for x (i.e., p(x)), it needs to sample from p(x, y) and then ignore the y values. Since the generation of random samples from p(x, y) is not tractable, it is necessary to define a Markov chain to enable us to converge to that distribution. Given the current sample x, one should generate y from the conditional distribution of y given x, in which case one can sample the new x from the slice S = {x: y < f(x)}. Because the sample generation from the slice S is difficult due to the lack of exact information and form of S, one can exploit the algorithm proposed by Neal (2003), which updates x through a process that leaves the uniform distribution of S invariant. Given the current state x0, the next one is generated in the following steps:

- (1) Draw y0 uniformly from (0, f(x0)) and define the horizontal slice S = {x: y0 < f(x)}.
- (2) Find an interval that consists of all or much of S using the stepping-out procedure described in Neal (2003).
- (3) Generate and draw a new sample within the interval of Step 2.
- (4) Update the step i to i + 1 and repeat the previous steps to terminate the process by a desired number of samples.

## 2.3.2 Data Normalization by Deep Auto-encoder

In the proposed HMC-DAE-MD method, a data normalization strategy based on the DAE is added to the previously proposed HMC-MD method to enhance its performance in reducing the EOC effects. Data normalization in SHM is a technique for eliminating the level of changes in data caused by any kind of non-damage conditions; that is, environmental and operational, and sometimes noisy conditions. In most cases, one attempts to construct a computational/statistical model by using training data, which serves as an input applied to that model, and then predict it as the model output. On this basis, data normalization intends to perform a residual extraction process by computing the direct difference between the input and output. To better indicate the difference between the HMC-MD and HMC-DAE-MD, Fig. 2.4 depicts the general flowchart of SHM by these methods. It should be clarified that the main rationale for selecting each of them relies upon the extent of variability conditions in the augmented data. In the step of feature discrimination, the MD metric takes either the real augmented data points or their residual samples (i.e., normalized data points) after data normalization. From Fig. 2.4, one can realize that the process starts by using a limited number of displacement samples from a few images. In the next step, the HMC algorithm is employed to expand these samples and produce a larger dataset of displacements. As the EOCs depend strongly on choosing between HMC-MD and HMC-DAE-MD, an investigation is implemented to recognize the level of EOCs in the augmented data by graphical (e.g., Box plot) or numerical analyses. In case of low EOC, one can ignore the data normalization. In this circumstance, the expanded data is decomposed into the training, validation, and test subsets.

Using the augmented training data points, their mean vector and covariance matrix are estimated and considered the main elements of the MD-based novelty detector. In this regard, all augmented displacement data points are applied to this detector to calculate their distance quantities (i.e., novelty indices) and make a decision in the step of feature discrimination. In case of high EOC, it needs to use the data normalization, which is developed by a DAE and using the expanded training set. Hence, the main goal is to perform the residual extraction by calculating the direct difference between the DAE input and output (prediction). The trained DAE is then considered to extract the residuals of the augmented validation and test sets. Accordingly, one can obtain new training, validation, and test datasets, which are insensitive to the EOCs. These datasets are finally applied to the MD-based novelty detector to calculate their distance quantities.

Having considered the novelty indices or distance amounts of the augmented training set, a threshold is estimated to make the final decision on the unknown test data points. It should be mentioned that as the training and validation datasets belongs to the undamaged condition of the structure, their labels are known. In this regard, the main attempt is to classify the test data points regarding the unknown or current state of the structure. Once the threshold has been determined, the novelty indices of the training and validation data should fall below the threshold, as shown in Fig. 2.4. On the other hand, if any novelty index of the test sample is over the threshold, HMC-MD or HMC-DAE-MD alarms the occurrence of damage; otherwise, the structure is still undamaged.

### 2.3.2.1 Autoencoders

The autoencoders (AEs) are nonlinear unsupervised classes of ANNs that mainly intend to learn generative models for regenerating or reconstructing unlabeled data points (Charte et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2017; Karhunen et al. 2015; Larochelle et al. 2009). More specifically, the AEs are often trained to encode the inputs into some representations in an effort to reconstruct them. In this regard, an AE consists of an encoder, an encoding layer, and a decoder (Charte et al. 2018). The encoder and decoder are two key parts of an AE. The encoder aims to encode the inputs and maps them into a new feature space. The decoder uses the coded data to reconstruct the inputs. The great advantages of the AEs include extracting useful features continually during the propagation process and particularly filtering the useless features (Liu et al. 2017). However, the key merit of the AEs is to filter out noisy data or sampling data with outliers (Kramer 1992). For this reason, this kind of ANN is an appropriate choice for data normalization (Pathirage et al. 2018; Entezami et al. 2020).

The basic structure of an AE is based on a feedforward neural network. This structure can be designed in shallow or deep configurations. A shallow AE consists of three layers of input, encoding, and output. It is the simplest form of the AE model due to using one hidden layer. In most cases, it is preferable to modeling a DAE by exploiting more than one hidden layer. On the other hand, both shallow and deep AEs can be modeled in undercomplete and overcomplete configurations. More precisely, if the hidden layer(s) of the AE is smaller than the input and output layers in terms of the neuron sizes, this strategy forms an undercomplete network. In contrast, if the hidden layer(s) is larger than the input and output layers, the AE is overcomplete (Charte et al. 2018). For simplicity, Fig. 2.5 shows the different forms of the AEs. Figure 2.5a,b indicates the shallow AEs with one hidden layer (i.e., encoding layer) in the undercomplete and overcomplete configurations. In contrast, the DAEs of these configurations are presented in Fig. 2.5c, d, both of which have (2L + 1) hidden layers including L mapping layers, one encoding layer, and L de-mapping layers.

One of the well-known DAEs is comprised of three hidden layers of mapping, encoding or encoding, and de-mapping as shown in Fig. 2.6. In some literature, this type of DAE is known as auto-associative neural network (Charte et al. 2018; Larochelle et al. 2009). Once the best AE configuration has been selected, it is necessary to optimize or select the neuron sizes of the hidden layers and then train the AE. For the second task, one needs to use the unlabeled training data points (i.e., the augmented displacement samples of the undamaged condition of the structure), which are applied to the input layer of the AE of interest. A neuron located in any hidden layer receives several inputs from the preceding layer. This neuron computes the weighted sum of these inputs and finally utilizes a certain operation called activation function to produce or predict the outputs. Linear, binary, rectified linear unit (ReLU), logistic, hyperbolic tangent (Tanh), and scaled exponential linear units (SELU) are commonly-used activation functions for learning the AEs. Among them, sigmoid functions based on logistic and Tanh are undoubtedly the most popular activations in AEs (Charte et al. 2018). It is worth remarking that the DAEs can take different activation functions at different hidden layers.

Having considered the simplest AE configuration with one hidden layer, the learning process begins by encoding the hidden representation of the inputs (i.e., the vector x = [x1, ..., xp]T ) using the following coding function:

```
h = s_1(W^{(1)}x + b^{(1)})\qquad (2.29)
```

```

```
