## 4.2 Data Augmentation and EOC Investigation

To address the limitation of directly analyzing displacement values for SHM, the HMC and SLS algorithms are applied to augment small displacement data. For the HMC technique, the number of samples for augmentation (N) and the number of chains (C) are 100 and 10, respectively.

Additionally, the number of samples for augmentation via the SLS technique corresponds to 1000. Hence, the small set of 45 displacement samples from the eight sites of the bridge are expanded to two different sets (matrices) with 100 and 1000 augmented displacement data points as shown in Fig. 4.4. From this figure, one can realize that the augmentation of small displacement points can better show the variations in the structure by either the EOCs or damage. Regardless of the augmented samples related to Images #44 and #45, a simple graphical comparison reveals that the HMC with a fewer sample number than the SLS can better indicate the EOCs in the displacement samples, especially their seasonal variability in the one-year monitoring program. Nonetheless, the augmented displacement points of the two last images obtained from both HMC and SLS techniques clearly differ from the other images.

To further investigate the EOC influences, Fig. 4.5 presents a statistical analysis via Box plot of the expanded displacements gained by the HMC and SLS techniques. As Fig. 4.5a appears, the EOCs significantly affect the augmented points gained by the HMC. This conclusion emphasizes the positive effect of data augmentation by this technique for better observation of the rate of EOCs. In contrast, it is seen in Fig. 4.5b that the SLS could not give the output as good as the HMC. This makes sense that the HMC provides more obvious evidence in Box plot compared to the SLS in evaluating the variability conditions. Apart from this issue, it can be discerned that both techniques could properly distinguish the deviations in the augmented displacements close to the damaged area compared to the undamaged regions. From Fig. 4.5, one can conclude that as the severity of damage (i.e., scour in the bridge pier at the location of “P2”) increases, the variability in the augmented data increases as well.

Here, the aforementioned conclusions and discussions may lead to confusion about the necessity of applying rigorous machine learning methods for SHM if the statistical analysis of augmented data can detect abnormal changes in any structure. First, it should be clarified that we knew that the last two images and the location of “P2” are close to the partial collapse. Second, another statistical analysis via the medium absolute deviation (MAD) is conducted to prove the necessity of using the proposed methods or other rigorous machine learning techniques for the SAR-aided SHM. In statistics, the MAD is a reliable metric for evaluating the rate of variability so that a large amount of MAD is indicative of high variability, and vice versa. Figure 4.6 displays the evolution of logarithmic MAD values of the expanded displacement points from the HMC and SLS techniques. Unlike the graphically statistical analysis by Box plot, it is seen in Fig. 4.6 that both techniques could obviously indicate the EOC impacts on the augmented displacement points. As this figure reveals, the emergence of damage increases the amount of MAD. However, there are suspicious large increases in the MAD quantities on some dates (images), when the bridge was undamaged. As these suspicious increases have been underlined in Fig. 4.6, one can discern that their amounts are close to the corresponding MAD values of the two last images (damaged states). Hence, this statistical analysis helps us to realize the importance of applying advanced machine learning method for early damage detection and pre-collapse prediction. On the other hand, one should mention that the numerical statistical analysis via the MAD is superior to the graphical way based on Box plot.

## 4.3 Early Damage Detection by Hybrid Learning Methods

This section presents the results of early damage detection or pre-collapse prediction in the Tadcaster Bridge by using the proposed HMC-DAE-MD, HMC-UTSL-MD, and HMC-DTL-MD methods. As the main outputs of data augmentation via the HMC technique have been presented and discussed in the previous section, one attempts to indicate the outputs of SHM and the effects of various data normalization approaches proposed in this book.

It is important to describe that these methods need training and test datasets. According to the case study, the augmented displacement samples have been extracted from the eight target points, in which case \( p = 8 \). Moreover, the HMC sampling technique generates 1000 displacement points for each image; hence, the total number of augmented samples correspond to 4500. Among them, the first 4100 augmented points (\( n = 4100 \)) regarding the first 41 images, which are related to the undamaged state of the bridge, are employed to produce the training set \( \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{8 \times 4100} \). The remaining 2000 augmented data samples concerning Images 42 and 43 are taken as the validation data or known test data. Here, it is assumed that Images 44 and 45 pertain to the unknown state of the bridge and one attempts to detect their states. Accordingly, the 2000 augmented samples related to these images are labeled as the unknown test points. Totally, the test data is comprised of 4000 samples (\( m = 4000 \)) from the eight target points; that is, \( \mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{8 \times 4000} \).
