For the first scenario, Fig. 3.3 shows the displacement points at the target points “B” (i.e., the undamaged area) and “F” (i.e., the damage area). From Fig. 3.3a, it is seen that the displacements of all cases are roughly in the same range, while Fig. 3.3b indicates some deviation in displacement points regarding the damaged cases, particularly the second and third cases (i.e., DC2 and DC3). However, it is slightly difficult to distinguish the first damaged case (DC1) from the normal condition (NC). On the other hand, as can be observed in Fig. 3.3b, some displacement points of the normal condition in the monitoring phase (i.e., the validation data) have displacement values equal or more than the corresponding values concerning DC1 and DC2, most likely due to the influence of the noise applied to the simulated displacements. This phenomenon may occur in reality such as the effect of temperature fluctuations on the structural properties of a civil structure that causes unwanted changes in the structural responses (Behkamal et al. 2023a, b). From Fig. 3.3, although the third damaged cases can be detected by directly analyzing the displacement points, the above-mentioned discussions prove that this strategy is not always applicable and effective, particularly when the severity of damage and variability conditions are both unknown. On the other hand, the direct analysis of displacement points may be problematic by choosing many target points for SHM, which may be time-consuming and erroneous. Finally, the last limitation is that this strategy is a graphical data assessment. There is no doubt that the use of a more rigorous numerical or analytical approach highly assists civil engineers to make a better and more confident decision on the health of a civil structure.
