
## Chapter 3 Simulating Reality: Numerical Assessments of a Bridge Health Monitoring

**Abstract** In contrast to conventional sensing technologies, the preparation and extraction of displacement data from a large number of SAR images are not trivial. Notably, this process is difficult and time-consuming for long-term monitoring. For this issue, this chapter aims to validate the proposed probabilistic unsupervised learning method via simulated displacement responses of a numerical model of a real-world bridge structure. On this basis, the numerical model of this bridge was developed in MATLAB environment. To simulate remote sensing-based SHM, a few points in the bridge deck are selected as the target areas for extracting structural responses. Three damage scenarios in the vicinity of one of the bridge piers are simulated to assess whether the proposed probabilistic unsupervised learning method can effectively detect such scenarios.

**Keywords** Numerical model · Displacement simulation · Real-world bridge · Probabilistic unsupervised learning · Extreme value theory · Large data

### 3.1 I-40 Bridge: A Box-Girder Bridge

This section conducts a numerical study for validation of some proposed methods. This study is based on a numerical model of a full-scale bridge called the I-40 Bridge, over the Rio Grande River in Albuquerque, New Mexico, US (Farrar et al. 2000), as shown in Fig. 3.1a. The finite element model of this bridge was constructed to simulate different displacement points and investigate some SHM methods. Because the numerical model of the I-40 Bridge has recently been constructed and updated (Rezaiee-Pajand et al. 2021), it is considered for validating the proposed NNS-EVT and HMC-MD methods.

### 3.2 The Numerical Model of the I-40 Bridge

The I-40 Bridge was built from a steel girder and concrete deck with the width of 13.3 m and the thickness of 0.178 m. The deck was connected with two steel plate girders and three steel stringers as depicted in Fig. 3.1b. The stringers and the floor beams undertook the load transformation from the deck to the girders. Additionally, cross-bracing was considered to join the floor beams. Figure 3.1c shows a part of the I-40 Bridge, which was selected to equip with some SHM systems for the numerical and experimental studies such as finite model updating (Rezaiee-Pajand et al. 2021) and model-based damage detection (Daneshvar et al. 2023). Therefore, the numerical model of this part is incorporated here to verify the proposed NNS-EVT and HMC-MD methods.

The part of interest comprised three spans of the total length of 129.5 m including the middle span of the length of 49.7 m and two side spans of the lengths of 39.9 m. The finite element model of this part of the I-40 Bridge was built in MATLAB by utilizing the shell and 3D beam elements (Rao 2011). Figure 3.2 displays the schematic forms and element numbers of the bridge regarding the deck, girder webs and the piers. For the concrete deck and girders, the numerical modeling is based on 144 and 48 shell elements with four nodes in terms of the membrane and rectangular plate elements. More precisely, as Fig. 3.2a indicates, the elements 1–144 are concerned with the deck shell components. Additionally, in Fig. 3.2b and c, the elements of the girder webs in the north and south sides are labeled by 145–168 and 169–192. The 3D beam elements are considered to build the top and bottom piers, which are labeled by the numbers 193–204. Steel and concrete were the major materials for the numerical modeling of the I-40 Bridge. Table 3.1 lists the main mechanical properties of these materials used in the numerical modeling of the bridge. To simulate damage, it is assumed that the stiffness values of Elements 47, 54, 152, 153, and 194 in the vicinity of the second pier are uniformly reduced by 2%, 10%, and 15% leading to total reductions in the structural stiffness equal to 10%, 50%, and 75%, respectively (i.e., the total stiffness reduction 10% is determined by considering 5 × 2%). This damage pattern is a realistic simulation of the detachment between the deck and the second pier causing increases in displacements at those areas. Based on Fig. 3.2, the target point “F” is the location of damage.

### 3.3 Simulating Structural Displacements

To simulate displacements of the normal and damaged conditions, one assumes that different static loads are vertically applied to all nodes of the deck, see Fig. 3.2a, at different times. Based on the simple concept of structural analysis and finite element method, the relationship between the applied load $f$, structural stiffness $K$, and displacement $\delta$ is written in the following equation:

$f_i = K \delta_i$ (3.1)

where $i = 1,...,T$ denotes the number of measurements. In the field of the SAR-aided SHM, this value is equivalent to the number of SAR images. Firstly, one supposes that there are 500 measurements of displacement points so that 70% of samples (i.e. the measurements 1–350) belong to the normal condition and the remaining 30% of displacements are associated with the three damaged cases; that is, 10% for Case 1 with 10% of total stiffness reduction (i.e. the measurements 351–400), 10% for Case 2 with 50% of the total stiffness reduction (i.e. the measurements 401–450), 10% for Case 3 with 75% of the total stiffness reduction (i.e. the measurements 451–500). According to the main idea behind machine learning, 80% of the displacement points related to the measurements 1–350 in the normal condition of the baseline stage is used to generate the training matrix X₁ ∈ ℝ⁹ˣ²⁸⁰. Hence, the remaining displacement, concerning the measurements 281–350, and all displacements of the damaged cases are considered to generate the test matrix Z₁ ∈ ℝ⁹ˣ²²⁰. Since the problem under study is roughly related to a long-term SHM process, a noise level equal to 1% is added to the displacement points to simulate some realistic EOCs in these samples.

For the first scenario, Fig. 3.3 shows the displacement points at the target points “B” (i.e., the undamaged area) and “F” (i.e., the damage area). From Fig. 3.3a, it is seen that the displacements of all cases are roughly in the same range, while Fig. 3.3b indicates some deviation in displacement points regarding the damaged cases, particularly the second and third cases (i.e., DC2 and DC3). However, it is slightly difficult to distinguish the first damaged case (DC1) from the normal condition (NC). On the other hand, as can be observed in Fig. 3.3b, some displacement points of the normal condition in the monitoring phase (i.e., the validation data) have displacement values equal or more than the corresponding values concerning DC1 and DC2, most likely due to the influence of the noise applied to the simulated displacements. This phenomenon may occur in reality such as the effect of temperature fluctuations on the structural properties of a civil structure that causes unwanted changes in the structural responses (Behkamal et al. 2023a, b). From Fig. 3.3, although the third damaged cases can be detected by directly analyzing the displacement points, the above-mentioned discussions prove that this strategy is not always applicable and effective, particularly when the severity of damage and variability conditions are both unknown. On the other hand, the direct analysis of displacement points may be problematic by choosing many target points for SHM, which may be time-consuming and erroneous. Finally, the last limitation is that this strategy is a graphical data assessment. There is no doubt that the use of a more rigorous numerical or analytical approach highly assists civil engineers to make a better and more confident decision on the health of a civil structure.
