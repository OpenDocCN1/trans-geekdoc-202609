- (2) Extraordinary loads applied to the civil structure;
- (3) Aging, material deterioration, erosion, fatigue, failure, etc.;
- (4) Neglecting automated and continuous SHM.

To avoid such catastrophic events, SHM provides a practical and effective technology to protect civil structures against any type of damage and collapse by taking advantages from advanced data-based techniques under the concepts of artificial intelligence and machine learning. In general, a data-based SHM strategy is decomposed into some preliminary steps including: (i) sensing and data acquisition systems, (ii) data transformation and storage, and (iii) initial data analysis. Subsequently, it is necessary to apply feature extraction tools for extracting damage-sensitive features (i.e., those are only dependent on the structural properties and damage information and not variability sources, noises, and outliers) and develop advanced computational/statistical models in the view of the concept of machine learning for decision-making or feature discrimination. This step sets out to firstly determine novelty indices or damage indicators of the training and test data points and subsequently differentiate the obtained indications of the damaged condition from the undamaged one through an alarming threshold.

In general, a data-based SHM method is implemented in the training/baseline and monitoring/inspection phases (Entezami et al. 2020d; Entezami et al. 2021). The main objective is to learn an automated computational model via training data in the first phase and then assess the current structural condition in the second phase via test data and the learned model. According to the concept of machine learning, these steps can be carried out by supervised learning and unsupervised learning techniques. In the context of SHM, supervised learning refers to the technique that requires fully labeled data from both the normal/undamaged and damaged conditions. In other words, one needs to prepare damage-sensitive features of both conditions in order to learn a classifier (i.e., the computational model in supervised learning) for classifying new features (test samples) and predicting their labels. On the contrary, unsupervised learning depends only on the features of the normal conditions; hence, one attempts to evaluate the status of the current state of the structure using new features (test data) by a novelty (anomaly) detector (i.e., the computational model in unsupervised learning). An important note is that the preparation of fully labeled data in the problem of health monitoring of civil structures is not only difficult, time-consuming, and labor-intensive, but also illogical. This is because there is no justification to impose intentional damages on complex and vital civil structures in order to prepare limited and controlled damaged data (e.g., the SHM problems of the Z24 and S101 Bridges Maeck and De Roeck 2003; Döhler et al. 2014). To cope with this limitation, in some case, one can construct the numerical model of the real structure and simulate various damage patterns to provide damaged data (Figueiredo et al. 2019; Torzioni et al. 2022; Daneshvar et al. 2023a; Entezami et al. 2020c). However, the major obstacle to perform this approach pertains to the inevitable differences between the numerical and real modes due to idealization, simple assumptions, wrong modeling, and noisy data. In this case, finite element model updating is mandatory (Ereiz et al. 2022; Rezaiee-Pajand et al. 2021, 2020), which may impose additional challenges. Therefore, one can realize that unsupervised learning is a better solution to SHM of civil structures.

Sensing technology is a broad field in SHM that contains various kinds of sensors and data acquisition systems from contact sensors (e.g., strain gages, accelerometers, optical fibers, etc.) (Wang et al. 2014b; a) to non-contact-based sensors (Feng and Feng 2018). Although the use of non-contact sensing technology via common and accessible devices (e.g., commercial cameras, video devices, smartphones, etc.) is prevalent in academic and industrial SHM activities (Sony et al. 2019; Dong and Catbas 2021), some limitations do not allow civil engineers and researchers to achieve reasonable outcomes. One of them is that this type of sensing technology cannot provide a simple routine for monitoring of large-scale structures. On the other hand, pre-collapse investigation of some vital structural systems such as bridges and dams constructed in large-scale areas may be problematic. This limitation becomes worse in continuous long-term SHM programs. Apart from these limitations, the great obstacle to easily apply non-contact sensors for SHM applications is the devoid of rich information about initial undamaged states of many constructed civil structures. Even though, the optimal sensing technology has brought an important opportunity to detect surface damages (e.g., concrete crack Kim et al. 2019; Kang et al. 2020; Dung and Anh 2019) and spalling (Cao et al. 2021; Dawood et al. 2017), steel corrosion (Yao et al. 2019), fatigue cracks (Dung et al. 2019; Kong and Li 2018; Xu et al. 2019), etc.), some important damage patterns are not simply visible. In order to cope with these limitations, synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images acquired from remote sensing from space and satellites has brought a new technology for health assessment of civil structural systems. The great benefits of the SAR-aided SHM include but are not limited to:

- Evaluating large-scale structural systems in wide regions without any concern about sensor placement, deployment, and installation
- Accessing initial information of undamaged states of civil structures
- Observing wide zones around civil structures
- Capturing SAR images in the presence of clouds and tough weather
- In general, a SAR-aided SHM strategy is divided into three main parts:
- Selecting the types of satellites such as Sentinel-1, COSMO-SkyMed, TerraSAR-X, downloading SAR products based on different formats in terms of single look complex (SLC), ground range detected (GRD), Raw images, bands, polarizations, orders (i.e., descending or ascending), etc.
- Applying interferometric methods such as InSAR, DInSAR, SqueeSAR, etc. to extracting displacement data points
- Analyzing displacement data for SHM.¹

## 1.2 Literature Review: Existing Knowledge and Existing Gaps

In relation to the SAR-aided SHM applications, one can refer to the research conducted by Selvakumaran et al. (2018) who assessed the partial collapse in a masonry bridge caused by the scour of one of the bridge piers by utilizing 45 SAR images acquired from TerraSAR-X. They directly analyzed the displacements from these images and predicted the occurrence of the partial collapse by finding anomalies in the displacement distribution. Milillo et al. (2019) carried out a research on evaluating the structural conditions of the Morandi Bridge before its partial collapse by applying relatively several SAR images from various satellites. They only observed displacements in the proximity of the damaged region and investigated the feasibility of alarming the partial collapse. Giordano et al. (2022) performed a long-term damage assessment strategy for evaluating possible damage in a historic iron bridge by using SAR images acquired by COSMO-SkyMed between 2011 and 2019. They emphasized the serious influences of the EOCs on displacement data of civil structures gained by SAR images. For this issue, the classical PCA method was applied to remove possible EOCs in the measured displacement data points and the control charts were considered for anomaly detection. They finally concluded that the removal of the EOCs from displacement data enabled them to detect the settlement of the bridge piers up to 2 mm. Biondi et al. (2020) proposed a vibration-based early damage warning method in terms of modal analysis by using SAR images without displacement sample extraction. In particular, they examined the possibility of their methodology on the Morandi Bridge. Farneti et al. (2022) conducted a long-term monitoring process on the Albiano-Magra Bridge in Italy, which was globally collapsed on April 2020, between 2015 and 2020 using a relatively large set of SAR images from COSMO-SkyMed. For this issue, they extracted two-dimensional displacement samples obtained from 121 SAR images in the ascending orbit from January 2013 to April 2020 and 64 SAR images in the descending orbit between March 2015 to March 2020. Their major engineering goal was to quantify uncertainties in the two-dimensional displacement measurements gained by the InSAR techniques.
