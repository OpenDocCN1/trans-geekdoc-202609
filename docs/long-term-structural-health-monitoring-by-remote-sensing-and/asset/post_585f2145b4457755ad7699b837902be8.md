## 2.3 Tackling Small Data Challenges: Proposed Hybrid Learning Methods

Apart from the HMC-MD technique, the other proposed hybrid learning methods entail three key steps of the data augmentation by a Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm (i.e., HMC or SLS), (ii) data normalization by ANN-based approaches, and (iii) feature discrimination by the MD and EMD. Once the small set of displacement samples has been augmented, the expanded data is decomposed into training and test subsets. The subsets can be either directly applied to a novelty detector (e.g., MD) or fed into a data normalization tool (e.g., DAE) or algorithm (e.g., UTSL, DTL, and ODTL) for removing any source of variability. Regarding the HMC-MD technique, no data normalization is considered in cases that the level of EOCs is low (Sarmadi 2021).

### 2.3.1 Data Augmentation by Markov Chain Monte Carlo

In statistics, Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods are comprised of a class of probabilistic algorithms for systematic random sampling from a probability distribution. In contrast to Monte Carlo sampling techniques that are capable of drawing independent samples from the distribution of interest, Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods can draw samples where the next sample is dependent on the existing sample, called a Markov chain. Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms, as their names indicate, consists of two parts of Monte Carlo and Markov Chain. In probability theory, Monte Carlo is an approach to randomly sampling a probability distribution and approximating a desired quantity. On this basis, Monte Carlo techniques typically suppose that it is possible to efficiently draw samples from a target distribution, in which case, from the samples that are drawn, one can estimate the sum or integral quantity as the mean or variance of the drawn samples. On the other hand, Markov Chain is a systematic stochastic procedure for generating a sequence of random samples, where the current sample is probabilistically dependent on the value of the previous sample. In case of combining these two methodologies (i.e., Markov Chain and Monte Carlo), it allows us to draw random samples for high-dimensional distributions that hold the probabilistic dependence between samples by constructing a Markov Chain that comprises the Monte Carlo sample. For this aim, it suffices to build a Markov chain that contains the desired distribution as its equilibrium distribution and then gain a sample of that distribution by recording states from the chain (Van Ravenzwaaij et al. 2018). The successful performance of data augmentation in SHM has been studied by Entezami et al. (2022) regarding damage localization and threshold estimation with a small number of anomaly indices.

### 2.3.1.1 Hamiltonian Monte Carlo Sampling

The HMC sampling is a Markov Chain Monte Carlo technique for obtaining a sequence of random samples from a target probability distribution when the direct sampling process is intractable (Neal 2011). This method corresponds to an instance of the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm with a Hamiltonian dynamics evolution that can be simulated by using a time-reversible and volume-preserving numerical integrator to provide a move to a new sample. Unlike the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, the HMC method decreases the correlation between successive sampled states by performing moves to far states that can provide a high probability of acceptance owing to having approximate energy conserving characteristics of the simulated Hamiltonian dynamic. Simply speaking, the reduced correlation makes sense that fewer Markov chain samples are required to approximate integrals with respect to the target probability distribution.

Assume that \( f(\mathbf{x}) \) is the target distribution that is supposed to be sampled by using a chain of samples \( \{\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, ...\} \). Accordingly, the Hamilton’s equations are written in the following forms:

```
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial w_i} \quad (2.22)
\frac{\mathrm{d}w_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i} \quad (2.23)
```

where \( x_i \) and \( w_i \) denote the \( i \)th element of the parameter and momentum vector, respectively. Moreover, \( H \) refers to the Hamiltonian function. Suppose that \( \mathbf{M} \) is a symmetric and positive definite (mass) matrix. On this basis, the Hamiltonian is expressed as follows:

```
H(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w}) = U(\mathbf{x}) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{w} \quad (2.24)
```

where

```
U(\mathbf{x}) = - \ln f(\mathbf{x}) \quad (2.25)
```

From Eq. (2.25), one can understand that the sampling method under the algorithm of HMC relies on the logarithmic function related to the target distribution. Moreover, this sampler exploits the leap-frog technique (Neal 2011), which is an approximate solution to the motion of non-interacting classical particles, to determine the parameter and momentum components at any time. For this purpose, the sampling algorithm needs a positive integer for the number of leap-frog steps \( L \) and a positive number for the step size \( \Delta t \). Subsequently, it draws a random Gaussian momentum \( \mathbf{w}(0) \) from a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and covariance equal to the mass matrix \( \mathbf{M} \). In the next step, the particle runs under Hamiltonian dynamics for time \(L\Delta t\). It should be noted that this procedure is carried out by the leap-frog technique by numerically solving the Hamilton's equations. In this regard, the parameter and momentum vectors after time \(\Delta t\) are given by:

\(\mathbf{w}\left(t + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\right) = \mathbf{w}(t) - \frac{\Delta t}{2} \nabla U(\mathbf{x})|_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}(t)}\) (2.26)

\(\mathbf{x}(t + \Delta t) = \mathbf{x}(t) - \Delta t \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{w}\left(t + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\) (2.27)

\(\mathbf{w}(t + \Delta t) = \mathbf{w}\left(t + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\right) - \frac{\Delta t}{2} \nabla U(\mathbf{x})|_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}(t+\Delta t)}\) (2.28)

The above-mentioned equations should be applied to \(\mathbf{x}(0)\) and \(\mathbf{w}(0)\) \(L\) times to determine \(\mathbf{x}(L\Delta t)\) and \(\mathbf{w}(L\Delta t)\). Let \(\mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times 1}\) denotes a set of displacement points of \(p\) target points from a SAR image. Note that each \(\mathbf{d}\) is equivalent to \(\mathbf{x}\). On this basis, the underlying goal of the HMC sampler is to forecast the vector \(\mathbf{d}\) under \(C\) chains and \(N\) sample numbers. This means that the HMC technique makes attempt to generate \(N\) points of the vector \(\mathbf{d}\) from a target distribution (i.e., Gaussian distribution in this research) in an effort to make a set of augmented displacement points in a new matrix \(\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times N}\), under \(C\) chains in an iterative algorithm. In other words, for the \(j\)th chain and \(k\)th sample, where \(j = 1, ..., C\) and \(k = 1, ..., N-1\), one can derive an augmented matrix \(\mathbf{D}_{k+1}^{(j)}\). Using a hypothesis based on the Gelman-Rubin statistic (Gelman and Rubin 1992), if the statistic converges on the hypothesis criterion, one can fix the simulated parameter at the \((k + 1)\)th iteration; unless, one should choose the simulated parameter of the previous at the \(k\)th iteration. After determining all \(C\) augmented matrices, their average is used as the final set of the augmented displacement points. Eventually, the matrix of the augmented displacement points (**D**) is divided into the training set (matrix) \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n}\) and test set (matrix) \(\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times m}\), where \(n < N\) and \(m < N\).

### 2.3.1.2 Slice Sampling

The SLS is the other algorithm of Markov chain Monte Carlo for generating random data points on the basis of their previous states. The fundamental principle of the SLS lies in the idea that a random variable can be sampled uniformly from the area under the graph of a uniform probability distribution and then only observe the horizontal coordinates of the samples. For this purpose, one needs to construct a Markov chain that can meet the uniform distribution of interest via uniformly sampling from the vertical interval represented by the density at the current sample and from the union of intervals that comprises the horizontal slice.
