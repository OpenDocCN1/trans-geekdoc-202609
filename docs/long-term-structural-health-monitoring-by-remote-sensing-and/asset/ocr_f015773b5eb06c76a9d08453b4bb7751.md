# 2.2.1 Extreme Value Theory and Its Applications

In statistics, the EVT offers a well-known and useful probabilistic technique for studying on unusual (rare) events and samples rather than usual ones. In other words, this technique intends to analyze and model extreme data samples in terms of maximum and minimum samples and rare events (Coles et al. 2001; Kotz and Nadarajah 2000; Castillo 1988). In contrast to the central limit theory (CLT), which concentrates on the mean of randomly sampling data or the center of probability distributions, the fundamental principle of the EVT lies in the extreme samples and the tails of probability distributions. This makes sense that the most relevant statistics for modeling the right and left tails of a probability distribution include the maximum and minimum samples. The great advantage of the EVT is to only focus on a few assumptions about the distribution of data rather than the modeling of the whole data distribution.

To model the extreme values, there are generally two techniques of block maxima (BM) under Fisher-Tippet theorem and peak-over-threshold (POT) under Balkema-de Haan-Pickands theorem (Résillat et al. 2018; Sarmadi and Yuen 2022; Sarmadi et al. 2023a). The first technique is based on dividing randomly sampling data into some blocks (segments) and choose the maximum or minimum sample of each block as the extreme value (Sarmadi and Yuen 2022; Sarmadi and Karamodin 2020). The second technique sets a threshold for selecting extreme (only maximum) samples (Sarmadi et al. 2023a, 2022). On this basis, the maximum data points over the threshold of interest are chosen as the main extreme values for the extreme value modeling. This means that both techniques require hyperparameters (i.e., the number of block b and the threshold level) that should be determined properly. Once the extreme values have been selected, one should consider an EVT distribution for modeling. Different applications of the extreme value theory to SHM estimation can be found in (Sarmadi and Yuen 2022; Sarmadi et al. 2023a) for anomaly detection and (Sarmadi and Karamodin 2020; Sarmadi et al. 2022; Sarmadi and Yuen 2021; Daneshvar and Sarmadi 2022; Entezami and Shariatmadar 2018) for threshold estimation.

Because this research intends to take advantage of the EVT for feature discrimination, the term “extreme” refers to the maximum value. This is due to the fact that the occurrence of any abnormal change in a civil structure (damage) increases the deviation of an anomaly detector. In the context of SHM, the process of anomaly detection is based on finding maximum novelty indices. Hence, all extreme value models are based on the maximum values. Given the independent random data points (or variables) X1, ..., Xn, a general extreme value distribution sets out to model the statistical behavior of the maximum data in the following form:

```
$$M_n = \max(X_1, ..., X_n)$$
```
