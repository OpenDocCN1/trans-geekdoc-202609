On the other hand, the major reasons for a global collapse can be summarized as:

+   (1) Extremely strong natural disasters such as earthquake, typhoon, flood, etc.;
+   (2) Extraordinary loads applied to the civil structure;
+   (3) Aging, material deterioration, erosion, fatigue, failure, etc.;
+   (4) Neglecting automated and continues SHM.

To avoid such catastrophic events, SHM provides a practical and effective technology to protect civil structures against any type of damage and collapse by taking advantages from advanced data-based techniques under the concepts of artificial intelligence and machine learning. In general, a data-based SHM strategy is decomposed into some preliminary steps including: (i) sensing and data acquisition systems, (ii) data transformation and storage, and (iii) initial data analysis. Subsequently, it is necessary to apply feature extraction tools for extracting damage-sensitive features (i.e., those are only dependent on the structural properties and damage information and not variability sources, noises, and outliers) and develop advanced computational/statistical models in the view of the concept of machine learning for decision-making or feature discrimination. This step sets out to firstly determine novelty indices or damage indicators of the training and test data points and subsequently differentiate the obtained indications of the damaged condition from the undamaged one through an alarming threshold.

In general, a data-based SHM method is implemented in the training/baseline and monitoring/inspection phases (Ente zami et al. 2020d; Ente zami et al. 2021). The main objective is to learn an automated computational model via training data in the first phase and then assess the current structural condition in the second phase via test data and the learned model. According to the concept of machine learning, these steps can be carried out by supervised learning and unsupervised learning techniques. In the context of SHM, supervised learning refers to the technique that requires fully labeled data from both the normal/undamaged and damaged conditions. In other words, one needs to prepare damage-sensitive features of both conditions in order to learn a classifier (i.e., the computational model in supervised learning) for classifying new features (test samples) and predicting their labels. On the contrary, unsupervised learning depends only on the features of the normal conditions; hence, one attempts to evaluate the status of the current state of the structure using new features (test data) by a novelty (anomaly) detector (i.e., the computational model in unsupervised learning).
