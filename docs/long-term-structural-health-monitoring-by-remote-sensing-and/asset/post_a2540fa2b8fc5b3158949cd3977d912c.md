Moser and Moaveni (2011) studied the effects of temperature on modal data of a steel footbridge in order to analyze the structural behavior under temperature fluctuations in steel frame, concrete deck, and the footbridge piers. Their study demonstrated that ambient temperature could change structural responses (natural frequencies) by 4–8%. Zhou and Sun (2019) evaluated the influences of both environmental and operational variability caused by wind loading, structural temperature (i.e., the effect of ambient temperature on structural elements), humidity, sea levels, and traffic on modal frequencies of a long-span sea-crossing bridge. Their investigations indicated that structural temperature and traffic loads were the main factors for significant changes in the vibration responses of the bridge so that the influence of temperature on yearly scale was greater than the traffic. In contrast, the traffic load was dominant on the weekly and daily scales. Zonno et al. (2019) assessed the structural behavior of adobe buildings under long and short-term impacts of temperature and humidity. They concluded that the modal frequencies of the building in the long-term monitoring varied up to 8% due to the environmental variations, especially relative and absolute humidity. In short-term monitoring, the rates of variability was about 2.6–3.8%. Jang and Smyth (Jang and Smyth 2020) performed a numerical study on the effect of temperature distribution on structural responses of a full-scale finite element model of a cable-stayed bridge and modeled two approaches for this study by considering thermal prestress imposed at an element level and temperature-dependent Young's modulus. Their research demonstrated that the thermal prestress at the structural elements was the key reason for significantly variability in the natural frequencies of the bridge. In a similar manner, Teng et al. (2021) analyzed the mechanism of the influence of temperature on the frequency of a steel arch bridge in terms of internal force, geometric size, elastic modulus, and boundary condition. On the daily scale, the temperature distribution on the steel arch and girder behaved differently, while the frequencies of both elements decreased by increasing temperature on the yearly scale. Sarmadi (2021) conducted a comparative study on the effect of various EOC conditions on different structural responses. In that study, he utilized two types of features including time-series-based features obtained from time series modeling and dynamic features from operational modal analysis. He also suggested an effective approach to predict the level of EOCs by using the median absolute deviation. The results of that study indicated that EOCs had more impacts on the dynamic features rather time-series features. Maes et al. (2022) studied the influences of different environmental variability on the structural responses of a retrofitted steel bridge. They observed sudden increases in the natural frequencies of this steel bridge when the temperature reached around 0 °C. Moreover, it was observed that temperature was not the main factor for the environmental variability, in which case this conclusion emphasizes the importance of measuring further and other EOC conditions.

All the aforementioned studies confirm the negative point of the EOCs that these conditions can lead to alterations in any structure similar to damage. Under such circumstances, false positive (Type I) and false negative (Type II) are two major errors that are directly relevant to the economic and human losses, respectively (Giglioni et al. 2021). In the context of SHM, false positive makes sense that the structure is undamaged but the SHM method incorrectly alarms the occurrence of damage. By contrast, false negative means that the structure suffered from damage but the method of interest does not alarm the occurrence of damage. Therefore, since the EOCs are inevitable in real-world SHM projects, it is indispensable to discard any variability from features.

Removal of the effects of EOCs in SHM is often carried out by data normalization techniques (Farrar and Worden 2013: Chapter 12; Wang et al. 2022). Generally, there are two kinds of data normalization techniques called input–output and output-only frameworks. The first approach is based on entire information about the structural response/damage-sensitive features and variability data (e.g., temperature). Indeed, this technique is dependent on the regression problem. In this regard, Hu et al. (2016) compared different regression models such as multiple linear regression, polynomial regression model, and principal component regression by using modal data as the main damage-sensitive feature (i.e., output) and temperature data (i.e., input). Dervilis et al. (2015) proposed a robust regression model as a least trimmed square estimator to remove the influence of temperature-induced variations in an input–output framework, where the input and output were the temperature data and modal frequencies, respectively. Ferreras-Alcover et al. (2015) utilized a weighted least squares regression model to eliminate daily-averaged pavement temperature variations from vibration responses (strain data) of a bridge. Buckley et al. (2021) proposed a dynamic harmonic regression time-series model to detect damage in a bridge by considering the temperature as the environmental data (input) in the regression model. From these references, one can realize that the implementation of an input–output data normalization technique depends on the measurement of environmental and/or operational data and the consideration of a regression model for defining the relationship between structural responses and variability data. Nonetheless, the major drawback of the input–output technique returns its dependency on measuring the variability data. In most cases, environmental data, particularly temperature, is the main information. However, as Maes et al. (2022) indicated, the output data (i.e., structural responses or damage-sensitive features) can be influenced by other variability conditions, which differ from those are measured. Thus, the development of an output-only data normalization method can ignore this limitation.

Totally, the output-only data normalization depends on the structural responses (measured data) and damage-sensitive features without any information about the variability data. In other works, an output-only approach aims to find the variability pattern in the data/features and develop a more robust and rigorous strategy than the input–output framework for removing the EOCs. In most cases, this strategy can be implemented by statistical and artificial neural networks (ANNs) algorithms. Principal component analysis (PCA) and its advanced versions (Comanducci et al. 2016; Yan et al. 2005; Soo Lon Wah et al. 2019; Deraemaeker and Worden 2018; Reynders et al. 2014), nearest neighbor searching (Sarmadi and Karamodin 2020; Sarmadi et al. 2021a, 2022, 2023a; Sarmadi and Yuen 2022), and some linear approaches based on singular value decomposition (SVD) and Gaussian mixture model (GMM) (Figueiredo and Cross 2013; Figueiredo et al. 2011) are some well-known statistical output-only data normalization techniques. The ANN-based data normalization lies in learning an unsupervised ANN by using the structural responses or damage-sensitive features and computing the residuals between inputs applied to the network (i.e., the same structural responses or damage-sensitive features) and the network outputs (predictions). This algorithm can be carried out by shallow neural networks (Figueiredo et al. 2011; Chang et al. 2018; Sarmadi 2021) or deep neural networks (e.g., autoencoders) (Wang and Cha 2021; Shang et al. 2021; Silva et al. 2021). The other approaches are based on advanced machine learning methods. In this regard, Daneshvar et al. (2023b) proposed a novel locally unsupervised learning method through a discriminative reconstruction-based dictionary learning model for removing the environmental effects from modal data. Sarmadi et al. (2023b) proposed a novel hybrid feature weighting-selection algorithm in conjunction with a new nearest neighbor searching called natural nearest neighbor selection to implement an unsupervised (output-only) data normalization on modal data of full-scale bridge structures. Entezami et al. (2023a) put forward the idea of unsupervised meta-learning for eliminating the effects of the EOCs from dynamic data of concrete and steel bridges with missing data. In another study on advanced machine learning, Entezami et al. (2023c) proposed an innovative unsupervised multi-task learning method for unsupervised data normalization. All of these techniques can be classified as non-parametric and parametric algorithms. Although the first algorithm does not need any unknown components that should be estimated before the model development, parametric methods can yield more reliable outputs regarding the removal of the variability conditions by estimating their hyperparameters (Sarmadi 2021).

The other challenge in machine learning-aided SHM is related to the learning strategy. Depending upon the type of processing and acquiring measured data, the learning process can be carried out in offline or online manners. The central idea behind the offline learning is to handle and analyze an approximately large set of measurements at once. Indeed, this strategy brings a systematic approach to process numerous data points, which are gathered over a period of time. One of the advantages of this approach is the possibility of capturing all potential EOCs in long-term monitoring. In this regard, one supposes that adequate training data is available to develop a plausible computational/statistical model in terms of machine learning. By contrast, online learning is the process of being able to almost instantaneously analyze data, update the initial model, and the learning strategy in an online manner by receiving new data (Hoi et al. 2021).
