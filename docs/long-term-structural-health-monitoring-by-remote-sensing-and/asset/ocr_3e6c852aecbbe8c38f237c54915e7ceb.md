All the aforementioned studies confirm the negative point of the EOCs that these conditions can lead to alterations in any structure similar to damage. Under such circumstances, false positive (Type I) and false negative (Type II) are two major errors that are directly relevant to the economic and human losses, respectively (Giglioni et al. 2021). In the context of SHM, false positive makes sense that the structure is undamaged but the SHM method incorrectly alarms the occurrence of damage. By contrast, false negative means that the structure suffered from damage but the method of interest does not alarm the occurrence of damage. Therefore, since the EOCs are inevitable in real-world SHM projects, it is indispensable to discard any variability from features.

Removal of the effects of EOCs in SHM is often carried out by data normalization techniques (Farrar and Worden 2013: Chapter 12; Wang et al. 2022). Generally, there are two kinds of data normalization techniques called input–output and output-only frameworks. The first approach is based on entire information about the structural response/damage-sensitive features and variability data (e.g., temperature). Indeed, this technique is dependent on the regression problem. In this regard, Hu et al. (2016) compared different regression models such as multiple linear regression, polynomial regression model, and principal component regression by using modal data as the main damage-sensitive feature (i.e., output) and temperature data (i.e., input). Dervilis et al. (2015) proposed a robust regression model as a least trimmed square estimator to remove the influence of temperature-induced variations in an input–output framework, where the input and output were the temperature data and modal frequencies, respectively. Ferreras-Alcover et al. (2015) utilized a weighted least squares regression model to eliminate daily-averaged pavement temperature variations from vibration responses (stain data) of a bridge. Buckley et al. (2021) proposed a dynamic harmonic regression time-series model to detect damage in a bridge by considering the temperature as the environmental data (input) in the regression model. From these references, one can realize that the implementation of an input–output data normalization technique depends on the measurement of environmental and/or operational data and the consideration of a regression model for defining the relationship between structural responses and variability data. Nonetheless, the major drawback of the input–output technique returns its dependency on measuring the variability data. In most cases, environmental data, particularly temperature, is the main information. However, as Meas et al. (2022) indicated, the output data (i.e., structural responses or damage-sensitive features) can be influenced by other variability conditions, which differ from those are measured. Thus, the development of an output-only data normalization method can ignore this limitation.

Totally, the output-only data normalization depends on the structural responses (measured data) and damage-sensitive features without any information about the variability data. In other works, an output-only approach aims to find the variability pattern in the data/features and develop a more robust and rigorous strategy than the input–output framework for removing the EOCs.
