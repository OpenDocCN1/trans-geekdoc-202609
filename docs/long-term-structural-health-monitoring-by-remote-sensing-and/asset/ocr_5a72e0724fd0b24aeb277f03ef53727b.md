This is because there is no justification to impose intentional damages on complex and vital civil structures in order to prepare limited and controlled damaged data (e.g., the SHM problems of the Z24 and S101 Bridges Maeck and De Roeck 2003; Döhler et al. 2014). To cope with this limitation, in some case, one can construct the numerical model of the real structure and simulate various damage patterns to provide damaged data (Figueiredo et al. 2019; Torzoni et al. 2022; Daneshvar et al. 2023a; Entezami et al. 2020c). However, the major obstacle to perform this approach pertains to the inevitable differences between the numerical and real modes due to idealization, simple assumptions, wrong modeling, and noisy data. In this case, finite element model updating is mandatory (Ereiz et al. 2022; Rezaiee-Pajand et al. 2021, 2020), which may impose additional challenges. Therefore, one can realize that unsupervised learning is a better solution to SHM of civil structures.

Sensing technology is a broad field in SHM that contains various kinds of sensors and data acquisition systems from contact sensors (e.g., stain gages, accelerometers, optical fibers, etc.) (Wang et al. 2014b; a) to non-contact-based sensors (Feng and Feng 2018). Although the use of non-contact sensing technology via common and accessible devices (e.g., commercial cameras, video devices, smartphones, etc.) is prevalent in academic and industrial SHM activities (Sony et al. 2019; Dong and Catbas 2021), some limitations do not allow civil engineers and researchers to achieve reasonable outcomes. One of them is that this type of sensing technology cannot provide a simple routine for monitoring of large-scale structures. On the other hand, pre-collapse investigation of some vital structural systems such as bridges and dams constructed in large-scale areas may be problematic. This limitation become worse in continuous long-term SHM programs. Apart from these limitations, the great obstacle to easily apply non-contact sensors for SHM applications is the devoid of rich information about initial undamaged states of many constructed civil structures. Even though, the optimal sensing technology has brought an important opportunity to detect surface damages (e.g., concrete crack Kim et al. 2019; Kang et al. 2020; Dung and Anh 2019) and spalling (Cao et al. 2021; Dawood et al. 2017), steel corrosion (Yao et al. 2019), fatigue cracks (Dung et al. 2019; Kong and Li 2018; Xu et al. 2019), etc.), some important damage patterns are not simply visible. In order to cope with these limitations, synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images acquired from remote sensing from space and satellites has brought a new technology for health assessment of civil structural systems. The great benefits of the SAR-aided SHM include but are not limited to:

+   * Evaluating large-scale structural systems in wide regions without any concern about sensor placement, deployment, and installation
+   * Accessing initial information of undamaged states of civil structures
+   * Observing wide zones around civil structures
+   * Capturing SAR images in the presence of clouds and tough weather
