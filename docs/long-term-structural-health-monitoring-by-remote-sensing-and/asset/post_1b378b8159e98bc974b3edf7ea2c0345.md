## Chapter 1

### Pioneering Remote Sensing in Structural Health Monitoring

**Abstract** Recently, long-term structural health monitoring (SHM) of civil structures by using the technology of remote sensing has received increasing attention by civil engineers. This is because such technology can facilitate SHM by introducing useful products such as synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images acquired from some satellites suitable for monitoring of large-scale civil structures in wide areas. In contrast to conventional contact-based and next-generation vision-based sensors, a long-term monitoring program via spaceborne remote sensing cannot provide high-dimensional structural responses. On this basis, it is feasible to conduct the program with a few SAR images. Using such products, one can extract structural responses in terms of displacements at different areas of a civil structure and monitor the responses for detecting any abnormal change. Because the long-term monitoring process is based on analyzing structural displacement responses, the main focus is on machine learning. For this process, environmental and operational changes seriously affect the performance of data-driven machine learning-aided techniques. Due to the importance of SHM in every society, this chapter intends to explain the main parts of remote sensing-based health monitoring of civil structures through SAR images and machine learning.

**Keywords** Structural health monitoring · Remote sensing · Synthetic aperture radar image · Environmental and operational change · Machine learning

### 1.1 Introduction

Civil structures such as high-rise buildings, bridges, dams, offshore oil platforms, hospitals, nuclear power plants, railways and subways systems are of paramount importance to every society due to their significant influences on human social life, transportation networks, medicine services, economics, etc. Most of the constructed facilities were designed by different codes and guidelines and were constructed by various construction techniques, which may be outdated. Some of them were built several years or decades ago, in which case aging and material deterioration are two major factors for their undesirable separability. On the other hand, in reality, any civil structure may encounter unpredictable excitation sources such as earthquake, strong wind, hurricane, typhoon, flood, etc. with different magnitudes, or a sequence/combination of these sources. Under such circumstances, structural damage can emerge leading to adverse changes in the structural performance and serviceability, and dramatically partial and global collapses (Li et al. 2016; Mesquita et al. 2016; Webb et al. 2015; Modares and Waksmanski 2013; Deng et al. 2016; Chen 2017; Sassu et al. 2017; Alencar et al. 2019).

In civil engineering, the practical process of evaluation of integrity and safety of civil structures is called structural health monitoring (SHM). This methodology is often based on equipping civil structures with various sensors, measuring useful data (i.e., vibration responses such as acceleration time histories or new responses such as images and videos), analyzing measured data, and implementing an SHM method (Gharehbaghi et al. 2022; Rizzo and Enshaeian 2021; Sun et al. 2020). The main tasks of this methodology include but are not limited to early alarming of damage occurrence or declaring a safe condition, localization of damaged areas after ensuring the occurrence of damage, recognition of damage type, and estimation of damage severity (Heravi et al. 2022; Esfehani et al. 2023; Entezami et al. 2023b; Sarmadi et al. 2023c). After these tasks, the damaged areas should be repaired or replaced to enhance the structural performance and serviceability.

A damaged structure can experience different damage patterns with various severity levels. Generally, these levels are classified as minor, moderate, and major cases. Regardless of minor damage, a damaged structure may sustain partial or global collapses under moderate and major damage patterns (Deng et al. 2016; Chen 2017; Abdelwahed 2019). Collapse in a civil structure is a phenomenon that all or parts of the structure may be destroyed by serious damages caused by natural hazards or fatigue loads so that the structure no longer operates and services normally. Human deaths and injuries as well as huge economic losses are two catastrophic consequences of the collapse phenomenon. Some tragedies caused by collapses occurred in Minnesota I-35W Bridge (Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) with 13 deaths in 2007 (Astaneh-Asl 2008), Morandi Bridge (Genova, Italy) with 43 deaths in 2018 (Rania et al. 2019), Champlain Towers South Condominium (Miami, USA) with 98 deaths in 2021 (Kong and Smyl 2022), and Abadan Metropol Building (Abadan, Iran) with 31 deaths in 2022.

Collapses in civil structures can occur in global and partial conditions. During a global collapse, the entire structure demolishes that causes much more tragic deaths and injuries along with considerable economic losses due to rebuilding the destroyed structure. In contrast, a partial collapse leads to a lower level of a collapse by most likely fewer casualties than the global collapse. In this case, a small part or some parts of a structure fall down; nonetheless, the structure may keep up its stability somehow. Under such circumstances, there may be chance to conserve the entire stability of the structure by repairing or replacing the partially collapsed area.
