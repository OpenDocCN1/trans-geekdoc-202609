## Chapter 4
From Theory to Reality: Advanced SHM Methods to the Tadcaster Bridge

### Abstract
Experimental validation of SHM methods is a critical process in evaluating the accuracy and reliability of such methods for damage assessment, especially in long-term monitoring programs. In this chapter, real-world applications of the proposed unsupervised learning methods (i.e., HMC-DAE-MD, HMC-UTSL-MD, HMC-DTL-MD, HMC-ODTL-EMD and SLS-ODTL-EMD) developed for coping with the limitation of small data are investigated by using a small set of displacement responses of a masonry bridge called the Tadcaster Bridge located in Tadcaster, UK. Because this bridge experienced a partial collapse, it is an appropriate case study for verifying the proposed methods. This chapter also indicates how a data augmentation process can help to better observe the EOCs in augmented displacement responses.

### Keywords
Pre-collapse detection • Masonry bridge • Small data • Data augmentation • Machine learning • Unsupervised learning

### 4.1 The Tadcaster Bridge: A Masonry Bridge

The real-world application of the other proposed methods is based on the valuable small displacement dataset of the Tadcaster Bridge, Tadcaster, UK (Selvakumaran et al. 2018). For some important reasons, we believe that this dataset provides the best condition for validating the proposed methods. The major motives for considering this structure are: (i) it brings a realistic condition of a partial collapse event in a real structure, (ii) the sample displacement data comes from a continuous long-term monitoring plan, (iii) similar to the installation of contact-based sensors, appropriate target points were incorporated and distributed to extract the displacement points.

This structure is a famous masonry bridge with seven piers belonging to the date around 1700 (i.e., built between 1698 and 1699 based on documentary evidence). The bridge crosses the “River Wharfe” in the town Tadcaster, North Yorkshire, UK and connects the Tadcaster to the town York. The transportation characteristic of this bridge is based on carrying a single lane of vehicles at each way and a pedestrian corridor on each side. The main dimensions of the Tadcaster Bridge include the length of roughly 100 m and the width of 10 m. The present structure of the Tadcaster Bridge consists of joining two structures in different dates. This scenario was considered to develop the width the elderly bridge. Floods are the main events that endanger the safety and integrity of the Tadcaster Bridge.

Due to the historic form of this bridge and the devoid of installing any SHM system for intelligent monitoring, visual inspections via divers were conducted to evaluate the scour in the bridge piers and observe the movement of the river bed. Nevertheless, the Tadcaster Bridge suffered from a partial collapse on 29 December 2015 after a flood caused by Storm Eva. Figure 4.1a, b displays actual pictures of the collapsed zone of this bridge. After a major reconstruction and widening, the Tadcaster Bridge reopened to traffic on 3 February 2017. Figure 4.1c displays the retrofitted structure of this bridge. Recently, Selvakumaran et al. (2018) studied on this structure by taking advantage of remote sensing and considering a few SAR images for continuous long-term SHM. The main objective of their research was to extract displacement samples from these images and directly analyze them. As the occurrence of partial collapse in a structure is often a rarity, the Tadcaster Bridge is a proper case study for further research on SHM by advanced machine learning methods, which is considered in this research.

Before the partial collapse, 45 SAR images from TerraSAR-X between 9 March 2014 to 26 November 2015 were captured to extract a limited number of displacements at eight target areas for a roughly one-year monitoring program. The last image belonged to 26 November 2015, approximately one month before the partial collapse. Therefore, the extracted displacement samples and the outputs of the machine learning method in this book can significantly help authorities and bridge’s owners to avoid catastrophic and economic losses caused by any collapse. Applying the SAR images, the DInSAR methodology and Small Baseline Subset (SBAS) technique were applied to extract 45 displacement samples at eight points labeled as “P1”, “P2”, “P3”, “P4”, “P5”, “P6”, “P7”, and “P8”. Figure 4.2 illustrates the locations of these target points in an image of the Tadcaster Bridge. It should be clarified that the point “P2” is the partial collapse location (Selvakumaran et al. 2018). In this case, the other points refer to the undamaged regions of the bridge. Moreover, Fig. 4.3 displays the extracted displacements of the eight points.
