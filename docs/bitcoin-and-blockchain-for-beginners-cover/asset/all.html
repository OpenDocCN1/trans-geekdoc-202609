Introduction

As Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies gain popularity, more individuals are interested in these new technologies. The bitcoin market is vibrant, dynamic, and brimming with opportunities. It can also be highly perplexing for newbies.

Much of the accessible cryptocurrency information presupposes a pretty sophisticated level of knowledge with cryptography, computer programming, financial markets, and mathematics in general. This book has a unique approach. Rather than dwelling on the theoretical, we'll get down to business: What exactly is Bitcoin? How do I put it to use? Why would I want to do that?

On the one hand, Bitcoin is difficult to understand. Some bizarre arithmetic stuff happens under the hood. Using Bitcoin, on the other hand, is relatively straightforward! To be able to drive, you do not need to comprehend everything about how an automobile works. Similarly, you don't have to be a computer genius to purchase, sell, mine, or spend Bitcoin.

This book will not provide you with a thorough grasp of public-key cryptography or hashing techniques. Instead, you'll have a solid foundation in both the practical application of Bitcoin and the technology that underpins it. We will go over the fundamentals of Bitcoin, including what it is, how it works, why people use it, and how to utilize it. We will also look into how to make money using Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies and the burgeoning markets in this field.

CHAPTER 1

 What Exactly Is Bitcoin?

Bitcoin is classified as a "digital currency" in broad terms. In many aspects, Bitcoin functions similarly to previous forms of money, except that there is no actual money, and all transactions take place exclusively through the Internet. Various types of digital currencies are available, but Bitcoin is now the most well-known, extensively utilized, and valued.

As we get started, it will be helpful to consider Bitcoin in two ways: as a currency and as a technology. La

ter in this book, we will look at the groundbreaking technology underpinning Bitcoin. But for the time being, let's focus on Bitcoin as money.

 

Bitcoin as a Payment Method

When we consider Bitcoin as a currency or a digital asset, there are two primary questions that most people have immediately away:

  •                 How is Bitcoin's value determined?
  •                 How is it genuinely put to use?

How Is the Bitcoin Price Determined?

To address the first question, consider paper money, such as US dollars. There isn't much of a physical difference between a $100 bill and a piece of paper when it comes down to it. A one-hundred-dollar bill is perceived as more than a piece of paper because citizens have agreed to accept that particular piece of paper as having a set value that may be traded for goods and services. There are many processes behind the scenes that lead to a society reaching this conclusion, but in a nutshell, any money has value because enough people agree that it does.

The same idea applies to Bitcoin. The value of Bitcoin is determined by the number of people willing to accept it as payment, a group rising virtually every day. Obviously, if no one were ready to accept Bitcoin as a form of currency, it would be a bad thing to have, nobody would want it, and it would be worthless. However, looking at the price of Bitcoin, we can see that people use it and that it has worth. The price of Bitcoin, like the price of other commodities, is determined by the interplay between supply and demand at any particular time.

As the degree of demand rises, so does the price of Bitcoin, and vice versa.

In the following chapters, we will look at some of the reasons why an increasing number of individuals are accepting Bitcoin as a legitimate, if not preferable, form of payment. But, before we get there, let's take a look at how Bitcoin is used. What is the appearance of a Bitcoin transaction?

What is the Function of Bitcoin?

Bitcoin can be used in the same way that any traditional currency, such as US dollars or Euros, can. You can use Bitcoin to send and receive payments, make purchases, or even save money in a "savings account."

Bitcoin can also be viewed as an investment, in the same way that a stock can be.

Have you ever used a credit card to make an online purchase or transferred money from one account to another? In practice, the process of utilizing Bitcoin for transactions seems extremely similar. We can make purchases by moving a specific quantity of Bitcoin from one account to another, from buyer to seller.

Bitcoin can currently be used to purchase many items, but not all. To make a purchase using Bitcoin, you must first locate a vendor who is ready to take it as payment. Subway, Microsoft, OkCupid, Whole Foods, Etsy, and Overstock.com are just a few of the well-known companies that accept Bitcoin.

New vendors from a variety of industries are constantly adding Bitcoin payment options. As more people across the world begin to join in the Bitcoin economy, retailers are responding to the growing demand from customers to accept Bitcoin.

What Makes Bitcoin Unique?

One of the greatest hurdles to entry for Bitcoin is that many individuals are uncomfortable with the concept of a non-physical currency. When we think of traditional currencies (sometimes known as "fiat"), such as the US Dollar or the Euro, we frequently think of paper money. Many of us are accustomed to thinking of money as cash—a real item that we carry around with us.

In practice, though, most people do not carry sacks of cash with them.

The majority of our money is kept in banks. Why is this the case?

Paper money has been used for a long time, although it is not very practical. It is easily misplaced, stolen, or damaged. Furthermore, there is nothing that binds a specific piece of paper money to any one person.

If you have your entire life savings buried under your mattress and you are robbed, there is nothing in the money that can be used to identify you as the owner. Unless the robber is apprehended by the authorities, the money is likely to be lost forever. Because of this vulnerability, we rely on banks to keep the majority of our money for us.

        

Banks provide a measure of security in exchange for fees and a degree of control over the funds in our bank accounts.Historically, banks were practically the only choice for consumers to keep their currency in a secure, centralized location that was backed up by a guarantee. But, are banks truly the best solution to the problem of money storage?

Banks can restrict, limit, and manage our access to our own money based on a variety of variables. For example, we may be prohibited from withdrawing more than a specific amount at any given time. To transfer money between our accounts, we may be charged a large transaction fee. Because everything is housed in one centralized database, if a bank is hacked, millions of accounts may be affected. Despite the numerous drawbacks, most of us end up putting our money in banks since there is no other option. We have no choice but to put our trust in these institutions or risk putting money under the mattress.

Bitcoin is a viable option. Bitcoin, unlike traditional money, is decentralized. Transactions are conducted peer-to-peer, eliminating the need for a "middleman" such as a bank, government agency, or other third-party entity. As a result, Bitcoin and many other cryptocurrencies are frequently referred to as "trustless," implying that we can conduct transactions without putting our trust in either the person with whom we are trading or a financial institution.

If you're unfamiliar with Bitcoin, you might be wondering how it works. To shed some light on the inner workings of this space, let's take a step back and look at Bitcoin as a technology for a moment.

CHAPTER 2

Blockchain Technology Overview

When you first start exploring the world of cryptocurrencies, one of the first terms you'll come across is "blockchain." Understanding the basics of blockchain technology is crucial for understanding Bitcoin, cryptocurrencies in general, and the future of decentralized computing. So, what exactly is blockchain and why is it so important?

The blockchain is the framework, or "protocol," on which Bitcoin and many other cryptocurrencies and developing technologies operate. A blockchain is essentially a public ledger that is distributed across numerous computers all over the world. Every Bitcoin transaction is recorded and added to the blockchain as a new "block," and it is linked to the previous transaction with a particular timestamp, much like a link in a lengthy "chain."

The blockchain is controlled by a decentralized, peer-to-peer network, which means that it is constantly saved and updated across many different computers all over the world. Anyone can run the program that turns their computer into a "node" in this massive network, contributing to the ongoing record of

transactions. Because of this distributed nature, a plethora of distinct versions of the blockchain exist all over the world, making it nearly impossible for anyone to modify transaction information after it has been recorded.

        

By Design, Security

To help visualize the blockchain more clearly, consider Fort Knox. Fort Knox is famous for housing the United States' gold bullion depository. Fort Knox has a boatload of gold stored deep within its walls. Fort Knox utilizes armed guards, bomb-proof vaults, and a slew of additional onsite security procedures to keep this gold safe. If gold must be moved, we can envisage armored vehicles and soldiers armed with machine guns keeping watch as the gold is moved. It would be incredibly difficult for a criminal to break into Fort Knox and steal the gold, not just because of the fortified facility, but also because gold is a heavy physical substance that would be difficult to transfer.

When it comes to asset protection, banks (and many other institutions) have typically followed a similar paradigm, storing everything in centralized places that rely on layers of security mechanisms. However, in today's world, the great bulk of financial information exists digitally, in the form of data. We believe that banks have the cash to back up the numbers in our bank accounts, yet for the vast majority of people, those figures are merely a record of worth, rather than a tangible cash sum locked in a vault.

         

Rather of physical vaults, our financial assets exist mostly as financial data saved in a bank's server's "digital vault." Banks seek to transform these servers into digital Fort Knoxes, but in practice, this centralized architecture does not transition well into the new world of digital transactions. Whereas breaking into Fort Knox and stealing gold would need explosives, special equipment, escape vehicles, and Ocean's 11-style elegance, hackers are able to enter into bank servers and steal financial information on a pretty frequent basis using simply computers. Credit card fraud, identity theft, and data breaches are all genuine risks that financial institutions face on a daily basis.

Banks continue to install new levels of security around their "digital vaults," yet hackers continue to breach them. Fort Knox is wonderful for holding physical amounts of gold, but when we try to apply it to data in the digital domain, it falls apart. If we take a step back and look at the problem, we can begin to ask if there is a better model for storing digital information and conducting digital transactions than continuing to try to lock down one central server. This is where blockchain technology comes into play.

Each block in the blockchain is linked to the preceding block and is publicly recorded across many different nodes across the world. Rather than entering into a single central server and stealing or modifying data, a hacker would have to change the information not only on one block, but on the entire blockchain, simultaneously, across the majority of the machines that hold it all around the world.

Technically, this would necessitate so huge processing power that it would be effectively impossible to achieve under current conditions. Because there is no centralized data storage, the blockchain system is secure by design. If someone attempts to enter a fake transaction, such as sending themself Bitcoins that do not exist, the many computers that maintain the blockchain will notice that the math does not add up. If the math does not add up, the transaction is ruled illegitimate and rejected, and it is not published to the blockchain record.

Beyond Bitcoin, Blockchain Technology

Blockchain technology is frequently linked with Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies, but it offers far-reaching potential for a wide range of applications, including smart contracts, crowdfunding, governance, intellectual property, healthcare, file storage, the Internet of Things, and much more. Many sectors are only now beginning to investigate the potential of distributed ledgers for resource and transaction management. As a result, investors interested in investment prospects in emerging areas should look into blockchain technology alone.

CHAPTER 3

 Wallets, Storage, and Transactions for Bitcoin

Before you can obtain or spend Bitcoin, you must first have a location to store it. Bitcoins are kept at a digital Bitcoin address, which is referred to as a "wallet." There are various sorts of Bitcoin wallets, and we'll go through some of the more popular ones as well as how to make your own. Let's take a closer look at how Bitcoin wallets function before we get into how to choose and construct your wallet.

The phrase "cryptocurrency" refers to cryptography, which is the science of secure communication. Modern encryption is mainly reliant on complicated mathematical methods that are decrypted by machines. People frequently encrypt their e-mails when sending communications containing sensitive information so that only the intended recipient can decipher them. To a third party, encrypted e-mails normally appear as total nonsense, and they will be unable to understand the contents unless they have the specific password or key to decrypt the message.

While most people equate modern encryption with digital data, cryptography has been around for decades. Originally used primarily by militaries, older kinds of encryption included utilizing secret codes to transport messages through hostile areas. The "Caesar Cypher,"

reportedly created by Julius Caesar to communicate with his generals, is one of the most renowned examples of Classical encryption. The Caesar Cypher is a simple substitution cypher in which certain letters in the original message are substituted with letters a specific number down from the original, resulting in a sort of "word scramble" that could only be understood if the number of the shift was known and the process reversed to decipher the message.

      

 

Encryption is now, of course, a considerably more sophisticated mathematical operation. We all use encryption on a daily basis, whether we realize it or not, whether it's an ATM pin number to access your bank account or the simple practice of using a password to open your phone. Many people utilize more elaborate systems with numerous layers of heavy-duty encryption when communicating sensitive information.

Bitcoin wallets are protected with asymmetric encryption, often known as "public key cryptography." This may seem familiar if you've used email encryption software like PGP.Don't worry if you don't! Again, some strange arithmetic happens in the background, but utilizing most wallets is pretty simple.

       

All basic Bitcoin wallets contain two essential components: a public key and a private key. These two keys are produced by an algorithm and typically appear as long strings of random characters and digits.

 

The General Public Key

The address for your Bitcoin wallet is the public key. This is a one-of-a-kind digital address, similar to your "email address for Bitcoin."

Your public key is visible to everyone. People will send Bitcoin to you using this address, which is your public key. When you make a transaction, your public key address is also recorded on the blockchain, as Bitcoins either move into your wallet from somewhere else or you send Bitcoins from your wallet to another wallet's address (i.e. someone else's public key).

Your public key may be directly or indirectly linked to your identity depending on how your Bitcoin wallet is configured. In reality, unless you are very deliberate about implementing advanced security steps to guarantee your anonymity, you should presume that someone could link your public key address to your identity.

The Secret Key

If your public key is analogous to your "email address" in Bitcoin, your private key is analogous to your password. Your private key is only visible to you, and it allows you to view the contents of your wallet and transmit Bitcoins to others.

When you send Bitcoin to someone else, the transaction is validated by the combination of their public key and your private key. Your private key tells the system, "Hey, I am the true owner of this wallet," while your public key tells it, "This other wallet is where the Bitcoins should go."

Your private key is incredibly valuable! Anyone who obtains your private key, such as a hacker, will be able to control your funds. If this occurs, there is little redress, which is why it is critical to keep your private key secure. You can't just "reset" your private key if you misplace it, unlike an email password, so be cautious. If you misplace your private key, there is no way to recover it, and you will be unable to access your bitcoins!

There are numerous choices for generating a Bitcoin wallet, just as there are numerous email services. We'll look at some of the most popular methods for creating a Bitcoin wallet, as well as the benefits and drawbacks of each.

CHAPTER 4

 Making Your Own Bitcoin Wallet

Before we get into the various types of Bitcoin wallets, it's crucial to know that Bitcoin transactions are irreversible! If you're just starting started with Bitcoin, you should make sure you understand how your wallet works before making any large transactions.

Some wallets are more sophisticated, but they provide greater security and anonymity. Others are a little simpler, although they rely more on a third party, such as a Bitcoin exchange. The Bitcoin community has many diverse viewpoints on best practices for wallets, and this field evolves frequently as new options enter the market. Some users generate a fresh wallet address for each transaction and disperse their Bitcoins across many wallets. Some people maintain all of their Bitcoins in a single wallet and utilize it indefinitely.

You can examine some of the numerous options for building a wallet at https://bitcoin.org/en/choose-your-wallet, including mobile, desktop, hardware, and internet wallets, at https://bitcoin.org/en/choose-your-wallet.

Whatever wallet you end up choosing, keep in mind that Bitcoin is actual money that can be lost or stolen. We'll go over the fundamentals here, but if you're going to be dealing with substantial numbers, it's a good idea to learn about some more advanced security methods to protect your digital assets.

         

Wallets on the Internet

One of the most common types of Bitcoin wallets is the online wallet. Several online services, such as Bitcoin exchanges, allow you to save your money online in one of their wallets. These wallets are simple to make, but anything that keeps your funds online is deemed less safe. In fact, numerous of these services have already been hacked or had severe security breaches.

Anything that is connected to the Internet is obviously more vulnerable to hacking or infection than anything that is not connected to the Internet.

Bitcoin wallets are no different. Many people prefer to retain the majority of their Bitcoin offline while also keeping a small amount of cash in an online wallet for quick and easy online transactions.

Wallets for Desktop and Mobile

There are numerous alternatives for creating Bitcoin wallets that can be stored on your computer and/or mobile phone. To some extent, these can provide more security than third-party online storage.

However, as previously stated, every device that is connected to the Internet is potentially vulnerable to malware. You can still be vulnerable to cyber-attacks unless you have a computer that is never connected to the Internet (this is known as "air-gapped").

Many wallets have additional levels of encryption, password protection, and other security features that can significantly decrease the dangers. You will also be able to make backups and add more layers of encryption, which is a smart idea. However, if you are dealing with a large amount of Bitcoin, the safest approach to store it is offline.

"Cold Storage"

Keeping your Bitcoin wallet offline and in a secure location is widely regarded as the most secure method of storing Bitcoin. It is typical practice for consumers to keep the majority of their Bitcoin offline, often known as "cold storage," and only retain a small amount online for routine transactions. The disadvantage of generating and using a cold storage wallet is that it requires some technical expertise.

BitKey, Armory, and other services assist in the creation of secure offline wallets.

Wallets Made of Hardware

Hardware wallets are a popular security and efficiency solution. These are tangible devices, comparable in form to a USB drive or keychain, designed solely for the purpose of storing Bitcoin. In fact, several hardware wallets link to a computer via USB to perform transactions. Even when linked to a hacked computer, strong hardware wallets are impervious to the malware and viruses that can harm software wallets. Security, ease of use, and recovery options varies between models;therefore, it is prudent to study reviews and compare the features of various devices.

Selecting the Best Wallet

We've discussed some of the most prevalent techniques of wallet construction, but there are others. Varying people place different values on security, complexity, and other aspects. Choosing a safe wallet option is highly recommended, especially for those expecting to make a significant investment in Bitcoin, even though it may necessitate some more labor to set it up properly. Reading reviews, viewing explanation videos, and asking questions on Bitcoin forums like bitcointalk.org are all effective ways to stay informed, ask questions, and select the best wallet for your needs.

How Do I Obtain Bitcoin?

You can truly obtain some Bitcoin after you have a place to invest your money! There are several ways to obtain Bitcoin, but the most frequent include:

  •                 By exchanging fiat currency for Bitcoin via an exchange Obtaining Bitcoin from someone who already owns Bitcoin mining
  •                 We'll look at all of these ways, but first, let's take a step back and consider a more fundamental question: What is the origin of Bitcoins?
  •                 Where Do Bitcoins Originate?

Who "manufactures" bitcoins?

This is a significant question, and it links to another one you may have: how is the value of bitcoin determined?

To answer each of these problems, it is useful to first examine the origins of standard fiat currency. Governments and institutions, in general, control the creation of paper money. The amount of money printed has an effect on the value of that currency in the global financial market. The economics of how this all works can be quite sophisticated, but in a nutshell, the more money a government creates, the less value that currency has in general. In economic terms, this principle is known as "scarcity," which means that the less there is of something and the more people who want it, the more valuable that product is. This is true for commodities, fiat money, and Bitcoin.

There are several examples throughout history of governments printing massive amounts of paper money to fund short-term expenses (nearly invariably tied to war), and the value of that currency dropped as a result. The most well-known example is the hyperinflation that happened in Germany's Weimer Republic following World War I. Because paper money had such a low value, people famously wallpapered their homes with it.

One key difference between Bitcoin and fiat currency is that there is a hard limit on the number of Bitcoins that can be created. There will never be more than 21 million Bitcoins. The final Bitcoin is expected to come around the year 2140. This inherent scarcity is an important factor in determining Bitcoin's value.

However, we have not fully addressed the topic of where Bitcoins originate. The short answer is that Bitcoin miners "mine" them.

Mining for Bitcoin

If Bitcoins are obtained by mining, it may be tempting to assume that one should abandon all other pursuits and become a Bitcoin miner. Mining is one of the first things that newbies to the Bitcoin space generally hear about. At first appearance, this appears to be a primrose route to "free money." It may appear like mining is as simple as launching an application on your PC and seeing the Bitcoins pour in! Unfortunately, mining, like most things involving "free money," is not as straightforward as it appears.

We previously discussed the blockchain and how transactions are stored over a distributed network of computers. Each transaction is confirmed and uploaded to the blockchain, but how does this happen?

This is where miners enter the picture. Bitcoin miners utilize specialized software to solve complicated mathematical problems in order to verify transactions, maintain the blockchain, and add blocks. A "node" (a specific mining station) can distinguish between valid and invalid transactions by comparing a new transaction to the public ledger of previous transactions (the blockchain).

If someone tries to spend Bitcoins that don't exist, the system will say, "Hey, hold for a second, this doesn't line up with the history on the blockchain..." and the transaction will be refused. Miners are in charge of the intensive computer work required to review all new Bitcoin transactions, verify them, and add them to the blockchain.

            

Miners are compensated with a set quantity of new Bitcoin in return for solving blocks, thereby increasing the global volume of available Bitcoins in circulation. This incentive encourages more people to mine, resulting in a more secure system due to greater dispersion.

In the early days of Bitcoin, mining could be done on almost any old computer, and the reward for "discovering" a block was substantial, despite the fact that the overall value of Bitcoin was extremely low and there were not that many Bitcoins in circulation. The conditions evolved over time as more people began to use Bitcoin and become miners.

A link between the number of miners and the level of difficulty required in solving the challenge, or "mining," each block is built into the Bitcoin protocol. In principle, additional miners entering the scene should result in more blocks being created at a faster rate.

The Bitcoin protocol is designed in such a way that when more blocks are issued, the difficulty of solving the complicated math problems necessary to effectively "mine" a block increase. Making it more difficult to mine a block reduces the rate of block generation. This link between the number of Bitcoins in circulation and the difficulty of mining new ones keeps the ecosystem constant over time.

Today, the level of difficulty necessary to mine a block is so resource-intensive, both in computer power and electricity, that it necessitates the use of specialized equipment. In most circumstances, the expense of a mining operation substantially outweighs the potential earnings from mining Bitcoin over a long period of time. Many miners currently work in "pools," or collectives, where participants pool resources and split rewards. Joining a mining pool is one approach to boost your chances of recouping your mining equipment expenditures and possibly making a profit. It is still feasible to earn Bitcoin through mining today, but it is far from simple or free to get started.

Purchasing Bitcoin

While there is certainly a possibility in mining, it is far from the most straightforward way to obtain Bitcoin. Rather of trying to mine fresh Bitcoin, it is considerably faster and easier to obtain existing Bitcoin from somewhere else.

You have various alternatives for getting Bitcoin in this manner. Whatever path you choose, you will need to create a Bitcoin wallet to receive and hold your funds.

Exchanges for Bitcoin

In many countries, using fiat currency to purchase Bitcoin is the most convenient option. Bitcoin exchanges function basically the same as any other currency exchange, in that you use one currency to purchase another. At the time of writing, Coinbase (coinbase.com) is one of the most popular Bitcoin exchanges in the United States. Coinbase is an online platform that allows you to build a Bitcoin wallet, connect your bank account, and purchase or sell Bitcoin using a relatively simple user interface.

Transaction fees are common on Bitcoin exchanges, and Coinbase is no exception. While most Bitcoin exchanges are nominally "free," there will be percentage fees associated with most or all transactions. It is also usual to encounter delays when dealing via exchanges, which can range from slightly annoying to crippling. Learning the process and factoring in fees and wait times will make your transactions operate much more smoothly.

Most exchanges that allow you to buy Bitcoin using fiat currency will ask you to link a bank account and provide some personal information. This enables quick and easy exchange of fiat currency for Bitcoin. Once again, it will be worthwhile to conduct some research and choose an exchange that can exhibit some endurance and has a decent reputation.

Even yet, storing all or most of your Bitcoin in an online exchange is generally not a good idea. For many years, Mt. Gox was the world's dominant bitcoin exchange. At the height of its popularity, Mt. Gox handled almost 70% of all Bitcoin transactions in the world.

Then, in 2014, a huge security breach occurred, resulting in the loss or theft of around 850,000 Bitcoins under fairly strange circumstances. Exchanges are beneficial for transferring funds between Bitcoin and fiat currencies, trading, and actively engaging with Bitcoin, but the Mt. Gox debacle demonstrates why it is not a good idea to retain all of your assets in an online exchange.

ATMs for Bitcoin

Bitcoin ATMs are another option to buy Bitcoin that is growing increasingly popular. These machines are appearing everywhere, from malls to airports to city centres. They resemble typical ATMs, but there are some key differences. Bitcoin ATMs, for the most part, are not linked to any banks.

                     

They are only connected to the Bitcoin network's world via the Internet. Many Bitcoin ATMs support bi-directional exchange, which means you may either insert cash to have it converted to Bitcoin and transferred to a public key address, or have Bitcoins from your own account turned into cash and dispensed by the machine. Some companies only handle one-way transfers.

ATMs can provide a more anonymous option to purchase Bitcoin without linking your bank account to a platform such as Coinbase. However, depending on the machine, there may be significant transaction fees and limits on how much you can deposit or withdraw. You can find Bitcoin ATMs in your area by using the Coin ATM Radar website, which can be found at https://coinatmradar.com/.

Obtaining Bitcoin from Others

One of the most prevalent ways to get started with Bitcoin since its inception has been to find someone ready to gift or sell you some. Given that Bitcoin is a digital asset, it should come as no surprise that the majority of the Bitcoin community exists online. Forums like bitcointalk.org and Reddit's r/bitcoin are excellent locations to interact with other Bitcoin users. Some Bitcoin aficionados are willing to offer a modest sum to a novice in order to assist them in setting up their first wallet. After all, the more individuals who use Bitcoin, the bigger the demand, and hence the higher the value, at least in principle. Through that lens, it makes long-term financial sense to assist new users in getting started, even if it involves spending some of your own money at first.

There are also a number of internet tools for locating local Bitcoin exchanges, where users meet together offline, in person, to trade Bitcoin. This can be an excellent method to save transaction costs, meet other Bitcoin aficionados in your neighborhood, and potentially boost transaction anonymity. As with any scenario involving meeting someone "from the Internet," employ caution whenever meeting up to exchange Bitcoin in person.

CHAPTER 6

 Bitcoin's History

We can argue about the specific timing when Bitcoin hit the mainstream. Generally speaking, despite previous rises in Bitcoin's value, most would say that 2016 - 2017 saw a major increase in public awareness and acceptance of both Bitcoin and blockchain technology. As a potential investor, it is beneficial to review Bitcoin's history to acquire a better understanding of how it has evolved. Studying the events and behaviors that have shaped the current market might assist us in shaping our picture of what the future may hold and making informed judgments.

One of the most intriguing anecdotal characteristics of Bitcoin is the mystery surrounding its creation. Bitcoin originally surfaced in 2008, when a document describing the concept was distributed to a cryptography email list. The article, titled "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System," was written by a person known as Satoshi Nakamoto.

A few months later, in January 2009, Satoshi Nakamoto built Bitcoin and made the code "open-source" (meaning anyone can look at it). Nakamoto mined the first Bitcoin block, known as the "genesis block," which launched the bitcoin blockchain.

Despite the fact that a few people have claimed to be Nakamoto or to know his/her/their genuine identity, none of these claims have ever been proven.

Satoshi Nakamoto's identity is still unknown to this day. Nobody knows whether Satoshi is a single individual, a group of people, a hidden society, or an alias for a nation-state actor. What is known is that he/she/they mined approximately a million Bitcoins in the early days of the blockchain.

Of course, at the time, Bitcoin had a small user base and little monetary value.

The trade of 10,000BTC for the delivery of two pizzas was one of the most well-known early Bitcoin transactions. Of course, 10,000BTC is now worth more than $20,000,000! At the time, Bitcoin was primarily seen as a subcultural novelty with which a tiny group of people were experimenting.

Many people have heard about the dramatic increase in the value of Bitcoin in recent years and worry if it is too late for them to profit by investing in Bitcoin. Bitcoin Investing

There is a lot of advice circulating around on how to make money with Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies, especially since Bitcoin has grown in popularity. In truth, there is no straightforward, guaranteed way to invest $100 and get $10,000 back. Bitcoin trading, like any other market, is a game of risk and reward, with no certainties. Because Bitcoin is a digital currency, it is possible to lose "actual money." Of course, you can make real money as well, but keep in mind that this is a high-risk business.

The bitcoin market is particularly volatile when compared to regular markets. Prices can change dramatically in a single day, or even in a single hour. It is fairly uncommon for readings to fluctuate by 20% or more in a 24-hour period. The peaks and collapses in the cryptocurrency arena may surely get your adrenaline racing, especially for traders coming from traditional markets.

Many people have made the mistake of hearing about Bitcoin (or another cryptocurrency) for the first time, generally as a result of some hype, and instantly purchasing in when the price is high, believing they will get in before it rises even further.

Then they see the currency's value collapse. They sell everything in a frenzy to cut their losses before it sinks any more, only to see it level off or skyrocket again a few days (or hours) later. Even seasoned traders cannot guarantee that this will not occur, but there are several things you can do to increase your chances of success.

        

The most important thing you can do as a cryptocurrency investor is to conduct your own research. Take the time to analyze the trends and arm yourself with appropriate materials. There has been a significant increase of self-proclaimed Bitcoin experts, some of whom are more reputable than others, particularly as Bitcoin has entered the mainstream. Explore freely, but keep in mind that anything promising "quick wealth" should be approached with caution.

Spending time looking at charts is a wonderful approach to become acquainted with the Bitcoin exchange scene. Almost every Bitcoin exchange has charts that show historical patterns and provide real-time updates on value, volume, and other statistics. "The Quick Nickel or the Slow Dime?"

You may have heard the idiom "the fast nickel or the sluggish dime" in regard to investment. This essentially means generating a small profit in the short term or a larger profit in the long run. When it comes to trading Bitcoin, many people apply both of these tactics.

Some investors choose to acquire Bitcoin only once, ideally when the price is low, and then hold on to it, expecting that the price would rise over time and eventually pay off handsomely. This obviously worked out quite well for early adopters who kept their first Bitcoin deposits.

Some predict that Bitcoin's price will skyrocket again in the future. These individuals will continue to hang onto their Bitcoin despite price swings, hedging their chances on long-term gain. This is the "slow dime" strategy. (In online cryptocurrency groups, purchasing a coin and holding on to it is commonly referred to as/spelled "hodling").

Others keep a close eye on the market and regularly transfer funds back and forth between Bitcoin, fiat, and other cryptocurrencies, taking advantage of slight price movements in a manner similar to Wall Street-style day trading. These speculators seek to buy when prices are low and sell when they rise in order to profit quickly (the "fast nickel"). The gains are often less when employing this method, but enough successful trades can quickly add up.

While trading between fiat and Bitcoin is one way for people to make (or lose) money, cryptocurrency trades frequently include swapping one cryptocurrency for another rather than trading with cash.In this context, Bitcoin functions as a "gateway" cryptocurrency.

 Common Bitcoin Myths, Mistakes, and Misconceptions

If you've been following along, you should be fairly sure that you understand the fundamentals of Bitcoin and blockchain technology at this point. You have the tools in your toolbox to create a Bitcoin wallet, purchase Bitcoin, and conduct transactions. That being said, in order to actually get started, there are a few common misconceptions regarding Bitcoin that should be addressed in order to avoid falling into traps, making mistakes, or being duped.

        

Is Bitcoin an anonymous currency?

The fact that Bitcoin is "anonymous" is frequently mentioned in writings about it. This has caused a great deal of confusion, but the quick answer is: No, Bitcoin is not anonymous! Bitcoin transactions are known to be recorded on the blockchain as transfers from one public Bitcoin address to another.

There are techniques to raise the level of privacy around transactions, although anonymity is not always accomplished by default when taken into account in context.

For example, when you buy Bitcoin with fiat currency via a bank-linked exchange like Coinbase, your funds are put into a specific Bitcoin wallet address. There is a clear link between your bank account and your Bitcoin address in this instance.

Because every Bitcoin transaction is permanently kept on the publicly available blockchain, anyone may monitor the activities of a specific wallet's public address. In theory, anonymity can be achieved by avoiding any action that could link a wallet's public address to the wallet's owner. In practice, however, when converting Bitcoin to currency, information about a user's identity is frequently made public, or at the very least accessible to third parties. It is better to proceed with the idea that Bitcoin is secure by default, but not anonymous.

Is Bitcoin taxed?

Many people, frequently under the mistaken belief that Bitcoin is "anonymous" or "untraceable," believe that Bitcoin is not taxed. This is incorrect, at least in the majority of countries.

In the United States, the IRS regards Bitcoin and other types of digital currency as an asset, similar to property, rather than a currency. This means that when dealing in digital currency, you will be exposed to capital gain or loss.

Furthermore, whether you are an individual or a business, your income is taxed if you are paid in Bitcoin. According to the IRS, taxpayers must report Bitcoin transactions based on their monetary value in US dollars.

An in-depth examination of tax law in regard to Bitcoin is beyond the scope of this book, but serious Bitcoin investors should do their homework to avoid problems with the IRS. The IRS Virtual Currency Guideline is available online at https://www.irs.gov/uac/newsroom/irs-virtual-currency-guidance.

Is Bitcoin a tool used by criminals?

While popular media such as TV dramas and movies have clearly contributed to the mainstreaming of Bitcoin, it is nearly always portrayed as being used for unlawful transactions in some form of criminal setting. It has also been argued that terrorists are the primary users of Bitcoin! While unlawful items and services are sold online, particularly on the "dark net," the great majority of Bitcoin users are not involved in criminal activities.

Bitcoin is not a suitable money for performing illicit operations, owing in large part to the public record of transactions and, once again, the fact that Bitcoin is not anonymous by default. To date, the famed Winkelvoss Twins, creators of the successful Gemini Bitcoin exchange, have said that Bitcoin is solely used by "dumb criminals."

The association of Bitcoin with nefarious operations stems in large part from the aforementioned episode involving the prominent Mt. Gox exchange.

The murky circumstances surrounding the loss of a large amount of Bitcoins from Mt. Gox prompted numerous headlines that blamed Bitcoin itself, rather than the Mt. Gox exchange's bad management.

Similarly, when the FBI shut down the famed "Silk Road" website, which was mostly used for selling illegal narcotics and other products on the dark web, in 2013, many people confused this episode with Bitcoin itself being "shut down."

Bitcoin can be difficult to grasp, and it is clear to see how much early media coverage of Bitcoin failed to distinguish between Bitcoin and numerous platforms created on top of it, such as Silk Road and Mt. Gox. Today, public perception is rapidly shifting as Bitcoin gains traction around the world, but there are still many people who believe Bitcoin is intrinsically illegitimate or unlawful.

Is Bitcoin a Rigged Ponzi Scheme?

The term "Ponzi Scheme" has become synonymous with various cryptocurrencies. There are other "alt-coins" that could be Ponzi Schemes, but Bitcoin is not one of them.

Ponzi schemes operate by promising enormous profits in order to lure investors to commit money into a notion that is never fully realized. Because Bitcoin is decentralized, there is no CEO or "head of Bitcoin" to defraud investors. Furthermore, Bitcoin has never made any promises about market value. Anyone who is familiar with Bitcoin will tell you that it is extremely volatile, and you never know whether it will rise or fall on any given day.

Finally, the defining feature of a Ponzi Scheme is that it provides no genuine value. On an almost daily basis, we witness new examples of real-world uses of Bitcoin and blockchain technology across a wide range of industries, backed by a widespread consensus within the tech community that this is a breakthrough technology that is not going away anytime soon.
