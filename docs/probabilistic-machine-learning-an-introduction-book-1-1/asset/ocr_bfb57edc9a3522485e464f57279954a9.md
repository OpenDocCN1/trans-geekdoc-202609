where I(D ∈ h) is non zero iff all the data points lie in the support of h. This crucial equation embodies what Tenenbaum calls the size principle, which means the model favors the simplest (smallest) hypothesis consistent with the data. This is more commonly known as Occam’s razor.

To see how it works, let D = {16}. Then p(D|h_two) = 1/6, since there are only 6 powers of two less than 100, but p(D|h_even) = 1/50, since there are 50 even numbers. So the likelihood that h = h_two is higher than if h = h_even. After 4 examples, the likelihood of h_two is (1/6)^4 = 7.7 × 10^−4, whereas the likelihood of h_even is (1/50)^4 = 1.6 × 10^−7. This is a likelihood ratio of almost 5000:1 in favor of h_two. This quantifies our earlier intuition that D = {16,8,2,64} would be a very suspicious coincidence if generated by h_even.

# 2.3.1.2 Prior

In the Bayesian approach, we must specify a prior over unknowns, p(h), as well as the likelihood, p(D|h). To see why this is useful, suppose D = {16,8,2,64}. Given this data, the concept h′ = “powers of two except 32” is more likely than h = “powers of two”, since h′ does not need to explain the coincidence that 32 is missing from the set of examples. However, the hypothesis h′ = “powers of two except 32” seems “conceptually unnatural”. We can capture such intuition by assigning low prior probability to unnatural concepts.
