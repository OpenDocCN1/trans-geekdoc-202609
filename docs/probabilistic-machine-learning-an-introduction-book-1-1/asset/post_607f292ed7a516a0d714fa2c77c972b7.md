
where $\mathrm{IW}$ is the inverse Wishart distribution. Unfortunately, $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ appear together in a non-factorized way in the likelihood in Eq. (7.117) (see the first exponent term), so the factored prior in Eq. (7.118) is not conjugate to the likelihood.

The above prior is sometimes called **conditionally conjugate**, since both conditionals, $p(\boldsymbol{\mu}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})$ and $p(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu})$, are individually conjugate. To create a fully conjugate prior, we need to use a prior where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ are dependent on each other. We will use a joint distribution of the form $p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=p(\boldsymbol{\mu}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma})p(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})$. Looking at the form of the likelihood equation, Eq. (7.117), we see that a natural conjugate prior has the form of a **Normal-inverse-Wishart** or **NIW** distribution, defined as follows:

$$\operatorname{NIW}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\tilde{\mathbf m},\tilde{\kappa},\tilde{\nu},\tilde{\mathbf S}) \triangleq \mathcal N\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}\mid\tilde{\mathbf m},\frac{1}{\tilde{\kappa}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\times\operatorname{IW}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\tilde{\mathbf S},\tilde{\nu})$$

$$=\frac{1}{Z_{\mathrm{NIW}}}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{\kappa}}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\tilde{\mathbf m})^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\tilde{\mathbf m})\right)$$

$$\times |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-\frac{\tilde{\nu}+D+1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf S})\right)$$

where the normalization constant is given by

$$Z_{\mathrm{NIW}} \triangleq 2^{\tilde{\nu}D/2}\Gamma_D(\tilde{\nu}/2)(2\pi/\tilde{\kappa})^{D/2}|\tilde{\mathbf S}|^{-\tilde{\nu}/2}$$

The parameters of the NIW can be interpreted as follows: $\tilde{\mathbf m}$ is our prior mean for $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, and $\tilde{\kappa}$ is how strongly we believe this prior; $\tilde{\mathbf S}$ is (proportional to) our prior mean for $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, and $\tilde{\nu}$ is how strongly we believe this prior.

Using the language of directed graphical models, we see that $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ become dependent when conditioned on $\mathcal D$ due to explaining away. See Fig. 7.15(a).

Note that our uncertainty in the mean is proportional to the covariance. In particular, if we believe that the variance is large, then our uncertainty in $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ must be large too. This makes sense intuitively, since if the data has large spread, it will be hard to pin down its mean.

## Posterior

To derive the posterior, let us define the sum-of-squares matrix

$$\mathbf S_0 \triangleq \sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf y_n\mathbf y_n^\top=\mathbf Y^\top\mathbf Y$$

so we can rewrite the scatter matrix as follows:

$$\mathbf S_{\mathbf y}=\mathbf S_0-\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf y_n\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf y_n\right)^\top=\mathbf S_0-N\bar{\mathbf y}\bar{\mathbf y}^{\top}$$

Now we can multiply the likelihood and the prior to give

$$p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mid\mathcal D)\propto |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-N/2}\exp\left(-\frac{N}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\bar{\mathbf y})^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\bar{\mathbf y})\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf S_{\mathbf y})\right)$$

$$\times |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-\frac{\tilde{\nu}+D+1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{\kappa}}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\tilde{\mathbf m})^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\tilde{\mathbf m})\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf S})\right)$$

$$=|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-\frac{N+\tilde{\nu}+D+2}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf M)\right)$$

where

$$\mathbf{M} \triangleq N(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\overline{\mathbf{y}})(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\overline{\mathbf{y}})^{\top}+\tilde{\kappa}\, (\boldsymbol{\mu}-\tilde{\mathbf{m}})(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\tilde{\mathbf{m}})^{\top}+\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{y}}+\tilde{\mathbf{S}} \tag{7.127}$$

$$= (\tilde{\kappa}+N)\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}+N\overline{\mathbf{y}})^{\top}-(\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}+N\overline{\mathbf{y}})\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}+\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}^{\top}+\mathbf{S}_{0}+\tilde{\mathbf{S}} \tag{7.128}$$

We can simplify the $\mathbf{M}$ matrix using a trick called completing the square (Sec. B.2.1.6). Applying this to the above, we have

$$(\tilde{\kappa}+N)\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}+N\overline{\mathbf{y}})^{\top}-(\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}+N\overline{\mathbf{y}})\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top} \tag{7.129}$$

$$= (\tilde{\kappa}+N)\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\frac{\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}+N\overline{\mathbf{y}}}{\tilde{\kappa}+N}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\frac{\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}+N\overline{\mathbf{y}}}{\tilde{\kappa}+N}\right)^{\top} \tag{7.130}$$

$$-\frac{(\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}+N\overline{\mathbf{y}})(\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}+N\overline{\mathbf{y}})^{\top}}{\tilde{\kappa}+N} \tag{7.131}$$

$$=\tilde{\kappa}\, (\boldsymbol{\mu}-\tilde{\mathbf{m}})(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\tilde{\mathbf{m}})^{\top}-\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}^{\top} \tag{7.132}$$

Hence we can rewrite the posterior as follows:

$$p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D})\propto |\mathbf{\Sigma}|^{-(\hat{\nu}+D+1)/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left[\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\left(\hat{\kappa}\, (\boldsymbol{\mu}-\hat{\mathbf{m}})(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\hat{\mathbf{m}})^{\top}+\hat{\mathbf{S}}\right)\right]\right) \tag{7.133}$$

$$=\mathrm{NIW}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma}\,|\,\hat{\mathbf{m}},\hat{\kappa},\hat{\nu},\hat{\mathbf{S}}) \tag{7.134}$$

where

$$\hat{\mathbf{m}}=\frac{\tilde{\kappa}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}+N\overline{\mathbf{y}}}{\hat{\kappa}}=\frac{\tilde{\kappa}}{\tilde{\kappa}+N}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}+\frac{N}{\tilde{\kappa}+N}\overline{\mathbf{y}} \tag{7.135}$$

$$\hat{\kappa}=\tilde{\kappa}+N \tag{7.136}$$

$$\hat{\nu}=\tilde{\nu}+N \tag{7.137}$$

$$\hat{\mathbf{S}}=\tilde{\mathbf{S}}+\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{\tilde{\kappa}\,N}{\tilde{\kappa}+N}(\overline{\mathbf{y}}-\tilde{\mathbf{m}})(\overline{\mathbf{y}}-\tilde{\mathbf{m}})^{\top} \tag{7.138}$$

This result is actually quite intuitive: the posterior mean $\hat{\mathbf{m}}$ is a convex combination of the prior mean and the MLE; the posterior scatter matrix $\hat{\mathbf{S}}$ is the prior scatter matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}$ plus the empirical scatter matrix $\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{y}}$ plus an extra term due to the uncertainty in the mean (which creates its own virtual scatter matrix); and the posterior confidence factors $\hat{\kappa}$ and $\hat{\nu}$ are both incremented by the size of the data we condition on.

### Posterior marginals

We have computed the joint posterior

$$p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}|\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathcal{D})p(\mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}\,|\,\hat{\mathbf{m}},\frac{1}{\hat{\kappa}}\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathrm{IW}(\mathbf{\Sigma}\,|\,\hat{\mathbf{S}},\hat{\nu}) \tag{7.140}$$

We now discuss how to compute the posterior marginals, $p(\mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D})$ and $p(\boldsymbol{\mu}|\mathcal{D})$.

It is easy to see that the posterior marginal for $\Sigma$ is

$$p(\mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D}) d\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathrm{IW}(\mathbf{\Sigma}|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}, \hat{\nu}) \tag{7.141}$$

For the mean, one can show that

$$p(\boldsymbol{\mu}|\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D}) d\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathcal{T}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu} \mid \widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}, \frac{\widehat{\mathbf{S}}}{\hat{\kappa}\hat{\nu}'}, \hat{\nu}'\right) \tag{7.142}$$

where $\hat{\nu}' \triangleq \hat{\nu} - D + 1$. Intuitively this result follows because $p(\boldsymbol{\mu}|\mathcal{D})$ is an infinite mixture of Gaussians, where each mixture component has a value of $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ drawn from the IW distribution; by mixing these altogether, we induce a Student distribution, which has heavier tails than a single Gaussian.

### Posterior predictive

Following Sec. 7.2.2.4, we now discuss how to predict future data by integrating out the parameters. If $\mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma})$, where $(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D}) \sim \mathrm{NIW}(\widehat{\mathbf{m}}, \hat{\kappa}, \hat{\nu}, \widehat{\mathbf{S}})$, then one can show that the posterior predictive distribution, for a single observation vector, is as follows:

$$p(\mathbf{y}|\mathcal{D}) = \int \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma}) \mathrm{NIW}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma} | \widehat{\mathbf{m}}, \hat{\kappa}, \hat{\nu}, \widehat{\mathbf{S}}) d\boldsymbol{\mu} d\mathbf{\Sigma} \tag{7.143}$$

$$= \mathcal{T}\left(\mathbf{y} \mid \widehat{\mathbf{m}}, \frac{\widehat{\mathbf{S}} (\hat{\kappa}+1)}{\hat{\kappa}\hat{\nu}'}, \hat{\nu}'\right) \tag{7.144}$$

where $\hat{\nu}' = \hat{\nu} - D + 1$.

## 7.2.5 Beyond conjugate priors

We have seen various examples of conjugate priors, all of which have come from the exponential family (see Sec. 12.2). These priors have the advantage of being easy to interpret (in terms of sufficient statistics from a virtual prior dataset), and easy to compute with. However, for most models, there is no prior in the exponential family that is conjugate to the likelihood. Furthermore, even where there is a conjugate prior, the assumption of conjugacy may be too limiting. Therefore in the sections below, we briefly discuss various other kinds of priors.

## 7.3 Noninformative priors

When we have little or no domain specific knowledge, it is desirable to use an **uninformative**, **noninformative** or **objective** prior, to "let the data speak for itself". Unfortunately, there is no unique way to define such priors, and they all encode some kind of knowledge. It is therefore better to use the term **diffuse prior**, **minimally informative prior** or **default prior**.

As an example of the difficulty of being uninformative, consider a Bernoulli parameter $\theta \in [0,1]$. One might think that the most uninformative prior would be the uniform distribution, Beta(1,1). However, this prior is not uniform on the logits $\eta = \log(\theta/(1-\theta))$,
