
## E.2. Fisher information matrix (FIM)

**Lemma 1.** The expected value of the score function is zero, i.e.,

$$ E_{p(x|\theta)} [\nabla \log p(x|\theta)] = 0 \quad (E.6) $$

We will prove this lemma in the scalar case. First, note that since $\int p(x|\theta)dx = 1$, we have

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \int p(x|\theta)dx = 0 \quad (E.7) $$

Combining this with the identity

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} p(x|\theta) = \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log p(x|\theta) \right] p(x|\theta) \quad (E.8) $$

we have

$$ 0 = \int \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} p(x|\theta)dx = \int \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log p(x|\theta) \right] p(x|\theta)dx = E [s(\theta)] \quad (E.9) $$

Now we return to the proof of our main theorem. For simplicity, we will focus on the scalar case, following the presentation of [Ric95, p263].

**Proof.** Taking derivatives of Eq. (E.9), we have

$$ 0 = \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \int \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log p(x|\theta) \right] p(x|\theta)dx \quad (E.10) $$

$$ = \int \left[ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} \log p(x|\theta) \right] p(x|\theta)dx + \int \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log p(x|\theta) \right] \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} p(x|\theta) \right] dx \quad (E.11) $$

$$ = \int \left[ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} \log p(x|\theta) \right] p(x|\theta)dx + \int \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log p(x|\theta) \right]^2 p(x|\theta)dx \quad (E.12) $$

and hence

$$ - E_{x\sim\theta} \left[ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} \log p(x|\theta) \right] = E_{x\sim\theta} \left[ \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log p(x|\theta) \right)^2 \right] \quad (E.13) $$

as claimed. $\square$

Now consider the Hessian of the NLL given $N$ iid samples $\mathcal{D} = \{x_n : n = 1 : N\}$:

$$ H_{ij} \triangleq - \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j} \log p(\mathcal{D}|\theta) = - \sum_{n=1}^N \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j} \log p(x_n|\theta) \quad (E.14) $$

From the above theorem, we have

$$ E_{p(\mathcal{D}|\theta)} [H(\mathcal{D})|_\theta] = N F(\theta) \quad (E.15) $$

We will use this result later in this chapter.

### E.2.3 Examples

In this section, we give some simple examples of how to compute the FIM.

#### E.2.3.1 FIM for the Bernoulli

Suppose $x \sim \text{Ber}(\theta)$. The log likelihood for a single sample is

$$ l(\theta|x) = x \log \theta + (1 - x) \log(1 - \theta) \quad (E.16) $$

The score function is just the gradient of the log-likelihood:

$$ s(\theta|x) \triangleq \frac{d}{d\theta}l(\theta|x) = \frac{x}{\theta} - \frac{1 - x}{1 - \theta} \quad (E.17) $$

The Fisher information is the expected value of the gradient of the negative score function:

$$ F(\theta) = E_{x\sim\theta}[-s'(\theta|x)] = \frac{\theta}{\theta^2} + \frac{1 - \theta}{(1 - \theta)^2} = \frac{1}{\theta(1 - \theta)} \quad (E.18) $$

#### E.2.3.2 FIM for the Gaussian

Consider a univariate Gaussian $p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathcal{N}(x|\mu, v)$. We have

$$ \ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \log p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\frac{1}{2v}(x - \mu)^2 - \frac{1}{2}\log(v) - \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi) \quad (E.19) $$

The partial derivatives are given by

$$ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} = (x - \mu)v^{-1}, \quad \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \mu^2} = -v^{-1} \quad (E.20) $$

$$ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial v} = \frac{1}{2}v^{-2}(x - \mu)^2 - \frac{1}{2}v^{-1}, \quad \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial v^2} = -v^{-3}(x - \mu)^2 + \frac{1}{2}v^{-2} \quad (E.21) $$

$$ \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \mu \partial v} = -v^{-2}(x - \mu) \quad (E.22) $$

and hence

$$ \mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \begin{pmatrix} E[v^{-1}] & E[v^{-2}(x - \mu)] \\ E[v^{-2}(x - \mu)] & E[v^{-3}(x - \mu)^2 - \frac{1}{2}v^{-2}] \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{v} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2v^2} \end{pmatrix} \quad (E.23) $$

### E.2.4 Connection between FIM and KL divergence

The Fisher information can be viewed as an approximation to the KL divergence between two similar distributions, as we now show.

Let $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})$ and $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}'}(\mathbf{x})$ be two distributions, where $\boldsymbol{\theta}' = \boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\delta}$. We can measure how close the second distribution is to the first in terms of their predictive distribution (as opposed to comparing $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta}'$ in parameter space) as follows:

$$ \mathbb{KL}(p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \| p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}'}) = E_{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) - \log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}'}(\mathbf{x})] \quad (E.24) $$

Let us approximate this with a second order Taylor series expansion:

$$ \mathbb{KL}\left(p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}'}\right) \approx -\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\top}E\left[\nabla\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right] - \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\top}E\left[\nabla^{2}\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right]\boldsymbol{\delta} \quad (E.25) $$

Since the expected NLL is zero (from Eq. (E.6)), the first term vanishes, so we have

$$ \mathbb{KL}\left(p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}'}\right) \approx \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\top}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{\delta} \quad (E.26) $$

where **F** is the FIM

$$ \mathbf{F} = -E\left[\nabla^{2}\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right] = E\left[\left(\nabla\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)\left(\nabla\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)^{\top}\right] \quad (E.27) $$

## E.3 Sampling distributions

In frequentist statistics, uncertainty is not represented by the posterior distribution of a random variable, but instead by the **sampling distribution** of an **estimator**. Let us now define these terms.

As explained in the section on decision theory in Sec. 8.1, an estimator is a decision procedure that specifies what action to take given some observed data. In the context of parameter estimation, where the action space is to return a parameter vector, we will denote this by \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = \pi(\mathcal{D})\). For example, \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) could be the maximum likelihood estimate, the MAP estimate, or the method of moments estimate.

The sampling distribution of an estimator is the distribution of results we would see if we applied the estimator multiple times to different datasets sampled from some distribution; in the context of parameter estimation, it is the distribution of \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), viewed as a random variable that depends on the random sample \(\mathcal{D}\). In more detail, imagine sampling \(S\) different data sets, each of size \(N\), from some true model \(p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})\) to generate

$$ \hat{\mathcal{D}}^{(s)} = \left\{ \mathbf{x}_{n} \sim p(\mathbf{x}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}) : n = 1 : N \right\} \quad (E.28) $$

We denote this by \(\mathcal{D}^{(s)} \sim \theta^{\star}\) for brevity. Now apply the estimator to each \(\mathcal{D}^{(s)}\) to get a set of estimates, \(\left\{\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathcal{D}^{(s)})\right\}\). As we let \(S \rightarrow \infty\), the distribution induced by this set is the sampling distribution of the estimator. More precisely, we have

$$ p\left(\pi(\hat{\mathcal{D}})=\boldsymbol{\theta}|\hat{\mathcal{D}} \sim \boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\right) \approx \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\delta(\boldsymbol{\theta}=\pi(\mathcal{D}^{(s)})) \quad (E.29) $$

In some cases, we can compute this analytically (assuming we know \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\)), as we discuss in Sec. E.3.1, although typically we need to approximate it by Monte Carlo. In both cases, we have to deal with the issue that \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\) is unknown, as we will discuss.

### E.3.1 Exact sampling distribution of the MLE

In some cases, it is possible to analytically derive the sampling distribution of an estimator. We give an example below.

#### E.3.1.1 Example: Univariate Gaussian

The MLE for the mean of a univariate Gaussian given a sample $\\mathcal{D}$ of size $N$ is given by $\hat{\mu}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})=\bar{x}$, where $\bar{x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_n$ is the empirical mean. If we assume the data is generated from $x_n\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$, then the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ can be written in the following form:

$$ p(\bar{x}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})=m\mid\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2))=\mathcal{T}\left(m\mid\mu,\frac{s^2(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})}{N},N-1\right)\quad (E.30) $$

where $s(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})$ is the sample standard deviation defined in Eq. (7.96), which we repeat here for convenience:

$$ s^2(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\triangleq\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(x_n-\bar{x}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\right)^2\quad (E.31) $$

For a proof of this result, see e.g., [Ric95, p542] or [CB02, p554]. Note that these books write the result as follows:

$$ \frac{\mu-\bar{x}}{\sqrt{s^2/N}}\mid\bar{x}\sim t_{N-1}\quad (E.32) $$

However, in this notation, it is unclear which terms are random variables (function of the unknown hypothetical dataset $\tilde{\mathcal{D}}$, namely $\bar{x}$ and $s$), and which are known constants (namely $N$ and $\mu$). Eq. (E.30) makes this clearer (although we don’t explicitly include $N$ on the RHS of the conditioning bar).

#### E.3.1.2 Connection with the posterior distribution

In many simple cases, the sampling distribution is identical to the posterior distribution obtained using uninformative priors, as discussed in [BT73]. For example, from Sec. 7.2.3.4, we know that the posterior $p(\mu\mid\mathcal{D})$ using a uniform prior is given by

$$ p(\mu\mid\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{T}\left(\mu\mid\bar{x}(\mathcal{D}),\frac{s^2(\mathcal{D})}{N},N-1\right)\quad (E.33) $$

By exploiting the fact that $\mathcal{T}(\mu\mid\bar{x},\tau^2,\nu)$ can be rewritten as $\mathcal{T}(\bar{x}\mid\mu,\tau^2,\nu)$, we see that the posterior is equivalent to the sampling distribution in Eq. (E.30). Intuitively, the reason the result is the same is that the posterior only depends on the likelihood, since the prior is uninformative. Nevertheless, semantically the sampling distribution and the posterior are quite different.

#### E.3.1.3 Example: Linear regression

The sampling distribution for the MLE for linear regression is derived in many books on frequentist statistics. The result is usually written as follows:

$$ \frac{w_d-\hat{w}_d}{s_d}\sim t_{N-D}\quad (E.34) $$
