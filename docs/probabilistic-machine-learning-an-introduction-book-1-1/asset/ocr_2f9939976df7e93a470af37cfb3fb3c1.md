where $\hat{w}_d$ is the OLS estimate, and $s_d = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2_{\text{unb}} 2 C_{dd}}{N-D}}$ is the standard error of the estimated parameter, where $\mathbf{C} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}$. This is equivalent to the marginal posterior under an uninformative prior given in Eq. (11.154), which we repeat here:

$p(w_d|\mathcal{D}) = \mathcal{T}\left(w_d|\hat{w}_d, \frac{s_d^2}{N-D}, N-D\right)$  (E.35)

# E.3.2 Large sample approximation

When the sample size becomes large, the sampling distribution of the MLE for certain models becomes Gaussian. More formally, we have the following result:

**Theorem E.3.1.** *If the parameters are identifiable, then*

$p(\pi(\tilde{D}) = \hat{\theta}|\tilde{D} \sim \theta^*) \to \mathcal{N}(\hat{\theta}|\theta^*, (N\mathbf{F}(\theta^*))^{-1})$  (E.36)

*where* $\mathbf{F}(\theta^*)$ *is the Fisher information matrix, defined in Eq. (E.2).*

This is known as the **asymptotic normality** of the sampling distribution. Intuitively, the reason for this is that the variance of the MLE is (inversely) related to the amount of curvature of the log likelihood surface at its peak, which is measured by the Fisher information.

We now prove the above theorem for the scalar case, following [Ric95, p264].

*Proof.* Define the log likelihood as

$l(\theta) = \sum_{n=1}^N \log p(x_n|\theta)$  (E.37)

By the law of large numbers, we have

$\frac{1}{N} l(\theta) \to \mathbb{E}[\log p(X|\theta)] = \int p(x|\theta^*) \log p(x|\theta) dx$  (E.38)

To maximize the expected log likelihood, let us first compute its derivative:

$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \int p(x|\theta^*) \log p(x|\theta) dx = \int p(x|\theta^*) \frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} p(x|\theta)}{p(x|\theta)} dx$  (E.39)

At $\theta = \theta^*$, the gradient vanishes:

$\int \left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} p(x|\theta)\right]_{\theta=\theta^*} dx = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \left[\int p(x|\theta) dx\right]_{\theta=\theta^*} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} 1 = 0$  (E.40)

The MLE $\hat{\theta}$ is, by definition, a point that maximizes the log likelihood, so the gradient of the log likelihood at $\hat{\theta}$ is therefore also 0. If there is only one such stationary point (so the MLE is unique), then we can conclude that $\hat{\theta} \to \theta^*$, which shows that the MLE is a consistent estimator (Sec. E.5.2).
