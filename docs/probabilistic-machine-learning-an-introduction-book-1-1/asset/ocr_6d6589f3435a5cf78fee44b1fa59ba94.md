It is easy to see that the posterior marginal for Σ is

$p(\mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D}) d\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathrm{IW}(\mathbf{\Sigma}|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}, \hat{\nu})$  (7.141)

For the mean, one can show that

$p(\boldsymbol{\mu}|\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D}) d\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathcal{T}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu} \mid \widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}, \frac{\widehat{\mathbf{S}}}{\hat{\kappa}\hat{\nu}'}, \hat{\nu}'\right)$  (7.142)

where $\hat{\nu}' \triangleq \hat{\nu} - D + 1$. Intuitively this result follows because $p(\boldsymbol{\mu}|\mathcal{D})$ is an infinite mixture of Gaussians, where each mixture component has a value of $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ drawn from the IW distribution; by mixing these altogether, we induce a Student distribution, which has heavier tails than a single Gaussian.

# Posterior predictive

Following Sec. 7.2.2.4, we now discuss how to predict future data by integrating out the parameters. If $\mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma})$, where $(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma}|\mathcal{D}) \sim \mathrm{NIW}(\widehat{\mathbf{m}}, \hat{\kappa}, \hat{\nu}, \widehat{\mathbf{S}})$, then one can show that the posterior predictive distribution, for a single observation vector, is as follows:

$p(\mathbf{y}|\mathcal{D}) = \int \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma}) \mathrm{NIW}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma} | \widehat{\mathbf{m}}, \hat{\kappa}, \hat{\nu}, \widehat{\mathbf{S}}) d\boldsymbol{\mu} d\mathbf{\Sigma}$  (7.143)

$= \mathcal{T}\left(\mathbf{y} \mid \widehat{\mathbf{m}}, \frac{\widehat{\mathbf{S}} (\hat{\kappa}+1)}{\hat{\kappa}\hat{\nu}'}, \hat{\nu}'\right)$  (7.144)

where $\hat{\nu}' = \hat{\nu} - D + 1$.

# 7.2.5 Beyond conjugate priors

We have seen various examples of conjugate priors, all of which have come from the exponential family (see Sec. 12.2). These priors have the advantage of being easy to interpret (in terms of sufficient statistics from a virtual prior dataset), and easy to compute with. However, for most models, there is no prior in the exponential family that is conjugate to the likelihood. Furthermore, even where there is a conjugate prior, the assumption of conjugacy may be too limiting. Therefore in the sections below, we briefly discuss various other kinds of priors.

# 7.3 Noninformative priors

When we have little or no domain specific knowledge, it is desirable to use an **uninformative**, **noninformative** or **objective** priors, to "let the data speak for itself". Unfortunately, there is no unique way to define such priors, and they all encode some kind of knowledge. It is therefore better to use the term **diffuse prior**, **minimally informative prior** or **default prior**.
