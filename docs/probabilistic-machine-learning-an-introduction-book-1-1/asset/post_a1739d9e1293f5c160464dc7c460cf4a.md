## 3.5.2 Mahalanobis distance

In this section, we attempt to gain some insights into the geometric shape of the Gaussian pdf in multiple dimensions. To do this, we will consider the shape of the level sets of constant (log) probability.

The log probability at a specific point \(\mathbf{x}\) is given by

\[
\log p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})+\mathrm{const} \tag{3.85}
\]

The dependence on \(\mathbf{x}\) can be expressed in terms of the **Mahalanobis distance** \(\Delta\) between \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\), whose square is defined as follows:

\[
\Delta^2 \triangleq (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) \tag{3.86}
\]

Thus contours of constant (log) probability are equivalent to contours of constant Mahalanobis distance.

To gain insight into the contours of constant Mahalanobis distance, we exploit the fact that \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), and \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\), are both positive definite matrices (by assumption). Consider the following eigendecomposition (Appendix C.4) of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\):

\[
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_d\mathbf{u}_d\mathbf{u}_d^\mathsf{T} \tag{3.87}
\]

We can similarly write

\[
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{1}{\lambda_d}\mathbf{u}_d\mathbf{u}_d^\mathsf{T} \tag{3.88}
\]

Let us define \(z_d\triangleq\mathbf{u}_d^\mathsf{T}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\), so \(\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\). Then we can rewrite the Mahalanobis distance as follows:

\[
(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\left(\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{1}{\lambda_d}\mathbf{u}_d\mathbf{u}_d^\mathsf{T}\right)(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) \tag{3.89}
\]

\[
=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{1}{\lambda_d}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{u}_d\mathbf{u}_d^\mathsf{T}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{z_d^2}{\lambda_d} \tag{3.90}
\]

As we discuss in Sec. C.4.4, this means we can interpret the Mahalanobis distance as Euclidean distance in a new coordinate frame \(\mathbf{z}\) in which we rotate \(\mathbf{x}\) by \(\mathbf{U}\) and scale by \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\).

For example, in 2d, let us consider the set of points \((z_1,z_2)\) that satisfy this equation:

\[
\frac{z_1^2}{\lambda_1}+\frac{z_2^2}{\lambda_2}=r \tag{3.91}
\]

Since these points have the same Mahalanobis distance, they correspond to points of equal probability. Hence we see that the contours of equal probability density of a 2d Gaussian lie along ellipses. This is illustrated in Fig. C.6. The eigenvectors determine the orientation of the ellipse, and the eigenvalues determine how elongated it is.

We can also define the Mahalanobis distance in terms of a linear mapping \(\mathbf{L}\) such that \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}=\mathbf{L}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{L}\), where \(\mathbf{L}\) may map from \(\mathbb{R}^D\) to \(\mathbb{R}^d\) where \(d\leq D\). Then we have

\[
(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{L}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=(\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\delta})^\mathsf{T}(\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\delta})=\|\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_2^2 \tag{3.92}
\]

where \(\boldsymbol{\delta}=\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\). This can be thought of as measuring Euclidean distance in a lower dimensional space.

## 3.5.3 Marginals and conditionals of an MVN

Suppose we have two discrete random variables, \(Y_1\) and \(Y_2\). We can represent their joint distribution as a 2d table, all of whose entries sum to one. For example, consider the following example with two binary variables:

| \(p(Y_1,Y_2)\) | \(Y_2=0\) | \(Y_2=1\) |
|---|---|---|
| \(Y_1=0\) | 0.2 | 0.3 |
| \(Y_1=1\) | 0.3 | 0.2 |

Given a joint distribution, we define the marginal distribution as follows:

\[
p(Y_2=y_2)=\sum_{y_1} p(Y_1=y_1,Y_2=y_2) \tag{3.93}
\]

We define the conditional distribution as follows:

\[
p(Y_1=y_1|Y_2=y_2)=\frac{p(Y_1=y_1,Y_2=y_2)}{p(Y_2=y_2)} \tag{3.94}
\]

We now discuss how to extend these results to jointly Gaussian random variables, where we replace sums with integrals. We will partition our vector of random variables \(\mathbf{x}\) into two parts, \(\mathbf{x}_1\) and \(\mathbf{x}_2\), where \(\mathbf{x}_2\) are the variables of interest, and \(\mathbf{x}_1\) are called nuisance variables. The marginal distribution for \(\mathbf{x}_2\) can be computed by integrating out \(\mathbf{x}_1\) as follows:

\[
p(\mathbf{x}_2)=\int \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\,d\mathbf{x}_1=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_2|\boldsymbol{\mu}_2,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}) \tag{3.95}
\]

where we have defined the following partition of the (moment) parameters:

\[
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1\\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_2\end{pmatrix},\quad
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11} & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21} & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\end{pmatrix} \tag{3.96}
\]

Now suppose we observe the value of \(\mathbf{x}_2\), and we want to predict the value of \(\mathbf{x}_1\) conditional on this observation. That is, we want to transform the prior distribution \(p(\mathbf{x}_1)\), which represents our beliefs about the values of \(\mathbf{x}_1\) before we have seen any observations, into the posterior distribution \(p(\mathbf{x}_1|\mathbf{x}_2)\), which represents our beliefs about the values of \(\mathbf{x}_1\) after (posterior to) seeing \(\mathbf{x}_2\). Exercise 3.8 asks you to derive the following important result:

\[
p(\mathbf{x}_1|\mathbf{x}_2)=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x}_1\,\middle|\, \boldsymbol{\mu}_1+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2-\boldsymbol{\mu}_2),\; \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\right) \tag{3.97}
\]

Note that the posterior mean is a linear function of \(\mathbf{x}_2\), but the posterior covariance is independent of \(\mathbf{x}_2\). See Exercise 3.5 for a simple example.

## 3.5.4 Example: Imputing missing values

As an example application of the above results, suppose we observe some parts (dimensions) of \(\mathbf{x}\), with the remaining parts being missing or unobserved. We can exploit the correlation amongst the dimensions (encoded by the covariance matrix) to infer the missing entries; this is called missing value imputation.

![](img/ea9d4e848ba303d6c19c90b8606f3bf6_95_0.png)

Figure 3.13: Illustration of data imputation using an MVN. (a) Visualization of the data matrix. Blank entries are missing (not observed). Red are positive, green are negative. Area of the square is proportional to the value. (This is known as a Hinton diagram, named after Geoff Hinton, a famous ML researcher.) (b) True data matrix (hidden). (c) Mean of the posterior predictive distribution, based on partially observed data in that row, using the true model parameters. Generated by gaussImputationDemo.m.

Fig. 3.13 shows a simple example. We sampled \(N\) vectors from a \(D=10\)-dimensional Gaussian, and then deliberately “hid” 50% of the data in each sample (row). We then inferred the missing entries given the observed entries and the true model parameters. More precisely, for each row \(n\) of the data matrix, we compute \(p(\mathbf{x}_{n,h}|\mathbf{x}_{n,v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\), where \(v\) are the indices of the visible entries in that row, \(h\) are the remaining indices of the hidden entries, and \(\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\). From this, we compute the marginal distribution of each missing variable \(i\in h\), \(p(x_{n,i}|\mathbf{x}_{n,v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\). From the marginal, we compute the posterior mean, \(\bar{x}_{n,i}=\mathbb{E}[x_{n,i}|\mathbf{x}_{n,v},\boldsymbol{\theta}]\).

The posterior mean represents our “best guess” about the true value of that entry, in the sense that it minimizes our expected squared error, as explained in Chapter 8. We can use \(\mathrm{Var}[x_{n,i}|\mathbf{x}_{n,v},\boldsymbol{\theta}]\) as a measure of confidence in this guess, although this is not shown. Alternatively, we could draw multiple posterior samples from \(p(\mathbf{x}_{n,h}|\mathbf{x}_{n,v},\boldsymbol{\theta})\); this is called multiple imputation, and provides a more robust estimate to downstream algorithms that consume the “filled in” data.

## 3.6 Linear Gaussian systems

In Sec. 3.5.3, we conditioned on noise-free observations to infer the posterior over the hidden parts of a Gaussian random vector. In this section, we extend this approach to handle noisy observations.

Let \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^D\) be an unknown vector of values, and \(\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^K\) be some noisy measurement of \(\mathbf{z}\). We assume these variables are related by the following joint distribution:

\[
p(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_z,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z) \tag{3.98}
\]

\[
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_y) \tag{3.99}
\]

where \(\mathbf{W}\) is a matrix of size \(K\times D\). This is an example of a linear Gaussian system.
