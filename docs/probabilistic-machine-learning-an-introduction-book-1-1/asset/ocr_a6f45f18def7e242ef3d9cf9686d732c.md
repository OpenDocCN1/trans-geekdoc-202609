The log probability at a specific point **y** is given by

$$\log p(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma})=-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})+\mathrm{const} \tag{3.85}$$

The dependence on **x** can be expressed in terms of the **Mahalanobis distance** $\Delta$ between **x** and $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, whose square is defined as follows:

$$\Delta^2 \triangleq (\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) \tag{3.86}$$

Thus contours of constant (log) probability are equivalent to contours of constant Mahalanobis distance.

To gain insight into the contours of constant Mahalanobis distance, we exploit the fact that $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, and $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}$, are both positive definite matrices (by assumption). Consider the following eigendecomposition (Appendix **C.4**) of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$:

$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\lambda_d\mathbf{u}_d\mathbf{u}_d^\mathsf{T} \tag{3.87}$$

We can similarly write

$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{1}{\lambda_d}\mathbf{u}_d\mathbf{u}_d^\mathsf{T} \tag{3.88}$$

Let us define $z_d\triangleq\mathbf{u}_d^\mathsf{T}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})$, so $\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})$. Then we can rewrite the Mahalanobis distance as follows:

$$(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\left(\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{1}{\lambda_d}\mathbf{u}_d\mathbf{u}_d^\mathsf{T}\right)(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) \tag{3.89}$$

$$=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{1}{\lambda_d}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{u}_d\mathbf{u}_d^\mathsf{T}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{z_d^2}{\lambda_d} \tag{3.90}$$

As we discuss in Sec. **C.4.4**, this means we can interpret the Mahalanobis distance as Euclidean distance in a new coordinate frame **z** in which we rotate **y** by **U** and scale by **Λ**.

For example, in 2d, let us consider the set of points $(z_1,z_2)$ that satisfy this equation:

$$\frac{z_1^2}{\lambda_1}+\frac{z_2^2}{\lambda_2}=r \tag{3.91}$$

Since these points have the same Mahalanobis distance, they correspond to points of equal probability. Hence we see that the contours of equal probability density of a 2d Gaussian lie along ellipses. This is illustrated in Fig. **C.6**. The eigenvectors determine the orientation of the ellipse, and the eigenvalues determine how elongated it is.

We can also define the Mahalanobis distance in terms of a linear mapping **L** such that $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}=\mathbf{L}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{L}$, where **L** may map from $\mathbb{R}^D$ to $\mathbb{R}^d$ where $d\leq D$. Then we have

$$(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{L}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=(\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\delta})^\mathsf{T}(\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\delta})=\|\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_2^2 \tag{3.92}$$

where $\boldsymbol{\delta}=\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu}$. This can be thought of as measuring Euclidean distance in a lower dimensional space.
