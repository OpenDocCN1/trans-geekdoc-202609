# ## 4.3 Relationship between crop nutrient levels and yield prediction

The traditional yield prediction models of crops usually adopt the data fitting method using environmental factors and crop growth factors (Gonzalez-Sanchez et al. 2014; Khaki and Wang 2019; Yao et al. 2020). Environmental factors include soil type, light and CO2. Crop growth factors include the crop growth and development rate. In recent years, remote sensing technology combined with spectroscopy has been used to predict crop yields in practical environments. For example, Latwal et al. (2019) used agrometeorological regression models, empirical models based on remote sensing indices, and semi-physical models to estimate crop yields for rapeseed and mustard at the district level. Kang et al. (2021) analyzed six vegetation indices based on multi-spectral images of UAVs and used them to develop regression models for predicting rice yield and protein content. Panda et al. (2010) used a BP neural network (BPNN) modeling method to verify the effectiveness of four spectral vegetation indices (NDVI, green vegetation index (GVI), soil adjusted vegetation index (SAVI) and vertical vegetation index (PVI))in corn crop yield prediction.

Most of these studies are primarily based on the numerical regression of existing factors for yield fitting. The main factors include the N and K absorption content of rice at the current stage, as N and K play significant roles in crop health and development (Vitosuek et al. 2009; Hou et al. 2019). At the same time, the environmental factors and cost require a prudent application of N and K (Chlingaryan et al. 2018). Therefore, it is challenging to establish a dynamic model of the topdressing amount and yield variation with reference to the nutrient level in order to achieve optimal N and K management.
