## 2.2 Image collection and preprocessing

Rice canopy images were collected in 2021 and 2022 using a camera with six 1/2.9-inch CMOS image sensors installed on a UAV (P4 Multispectral, DJI Technology, Co., Ltd., China). The UAV flights were performed at an altitude of 10 m above the plots at a speed of 1 m/s under sunny and windless conditions in the morning of local time (8:00-11:30). Flights were conducted at the tillering stage (TS), early-panicle initiation stage (EPIS) and the intermediate stage (INS) of the rice growth period. The original images were spliced with orthophoto maps by Dajiang Zhitu software (v3.4, DJI Technology Co., Ltd., China). Six images, each 26372*15284 pixels in size, were collected at each stage.

Due to the inclination of the stitched original images, the Hough line detection method (Ballard 1981) was used for slant correction. To avoid the color differences caused by different illumination conditions, the gray world method (Kwok et al. 2011) was used to correct the coloration of the original images. To obtain the image of each plot, a mask was created for each plot and the image of each plot was cut from the original images. To increase the sample size, each plot image was divided into 12 square plot images, each 512*512 pixels, so 216 square plot images were obtained from each N and K treatment in each rice growth stage. We obtained 10368 original sub-images and randomly divided the rice sample dataset for 2021 into 80% for training the models and 20% for testing the models. In order to further test the generalization ability of the models, on a different farmland with the same soil properties and fertilization treatments, we collected rice canopy images in the three growth periods of TS, INS and EPIS in 2022 according to the same operational methods of 2021. The new dataset for 2022 was used to test the model trained on the dataset for 2021.

To improve the robustness and generalization ability of the model, the number of training images was extended to 500 images in each treatment by data enhancement methods of left and right mirror, image 90° rotation and adding Gaussian noise. Since our model was trained on time-series images, noise was randomly added to the images, and other data enhancement operations were performed on all images in the training set. Finally, a total of 24000 training samples were obtained. Figure 3 shows examples of the time-series images of HHZ rice in 16 different N and K dosage treatments at three different rice growth stages. These examples show that rice with different nutritional levels have limited visual differences and high similarity at the same growth stage (Fig. 3). Among them, N2K0, N2K1, N2K2, and N2K3 are not clearly different at TS, but there are differences in the whole growth process of rice from TS to EPIS.

## 2.3 Rice physiological indicator measurements

After the images for the different rice growth stages were collected by the UAV, rice plants were sampled from each plot. The plant height was measured and tiller number was counted manually. Then the samples were separated into green leaf blades, dead leaf blades and culms plus leaf sheaths, and the samples were oven-dried at 80°C for more than 72 hours before the dry weights of the constituent organs were determined. The leaf area index (LAI) was measured with a leaf area meter (LI-3000A, LICOR, Lincoln, NE, USA), and the specific leaf weight (SLA) was calculated as the ratio of the green leaf area and its dry weight. The N and K concentrations in the plant tissues were determined using the Kjeldahl method after wet digestion (Goyal et al. 2022), and N and K accumulations were expressed as the product of the concentration and its dry matter weight.

## 2.4 Rice nutrient diagnosis model

Rice growth is a dynamic process, and the N and K phenotypes of rice plants in different growth stages are different. To accurately diagnose the nutrient level of rice in real-time, a nutrient diagnostic model CNN-LSTM based on the deep learning method was proposed to extract the temporal and spatial features of the rice canopy images. The LSTM involved is a special recursive neural network (RNN) that can learn the short-term and long-term dependencies between time steps in sequential data (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997). The input of LSTM is composed of a cell state C t-1, a hidden state ht-1 and input vector xt, and the output is composed of a cell state Ct and a hidden state ht, which are mainly controlled by the input gate (i), forgetting gate (f), candidate gate (g) and output gate (o). A sample unit diagram of the LSTM is shown in Fig. 4.

Currently, the typical CNN identification models include AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al. 2012), VGG16 (Simonyan and Zisserman 2015), GoogleNet (Szegedy et al. 2015), DenseNet (Huang et al. 2017), inception v3 (Szegedy et al. 2015), and ResNet101 (He et al. 2016). In this study, these six different CNN models were used for feature extraction. All of these networks applied the pretrained models on the Imagenet public dataset (Weiss et al. 2016) to speed up the training. Finally, the best model (ResNet101) was used as the basic model for extracting the features in this study. Then the SENet channel attention mechanism was added to the ResNet101 model to enhance the extraction of key features. The essence of SENet is to use the relevant feature maps to learn the weight distribution, and then the trained weights are applied to the original feature maps for weighted summation, thereby further improving the ability of the network to obtain local effective information.

The network framework of ResNet101-SE-LSTM is shown in Fig. 4. Firstly, the neural network model ResNet101 was combined with the Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) module to extract features from the time serial images. In order to avoid the loss of useful features, the model skipped the last fully connected layer, and then directly transformed the features into feature vectors through the reshape layer to regenerate a feature matrix suitable for the LSTM inputs. Each cell input for LSTM (size: m*n) is a group of feature vectors of the rice canopy images containing each time step, where m is the total number of images at each time step, including TS, INS and EPIS, and n is the feature vectors extracted by the neural network. In Fig. 4, i is the time step of the input model framework, which equals 2 or 3. In fact, due to the flexibility of this framework, the time step can be adjusted according to the actual needs and model output feedback. Secondly, through the end-to-end trainable class architecture, the LSTM model uses the back-propagation algorithm to iterate repeatedly, and it uses the cross entropy as the loss function for model training. The hyperparameters of the LSTM are shown in Table 3. Finally, the hidden layer ht of the last LSTM cell unit encodes the key information of the sequential data. We converted the generated output vector through a full connection layer, and its length was converted into the number of classes. Then the softmax layer was used to classify the rice nutrient level and its neuron number is the same as the number of classes.

## 2.5 Model training

All models were run on a PC with a CPU which has an AMD Ryzen 5 3600 6-Core Processor 3.60GHz. The computer was equipped with 3 GeForce GTX 1080Ti. Its operating system was Linux 16.04 and its host memory was 32.00 GB.

During the training process, the weights of the pretrained model were fine-tuned, the model was trained with 16 sets of small-batch sample data on three GPUs simultaneously by multi-threading, and stochastic gradient descent was selected as the optimization strategy.
