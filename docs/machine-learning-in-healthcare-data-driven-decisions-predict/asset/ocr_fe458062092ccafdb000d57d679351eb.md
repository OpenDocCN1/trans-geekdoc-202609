Electronic health records or EHRs were a primary way of retaining, altering, and retrieving clinical information in digital format when the wave of digitization started in the late twentieth century. The digital revolution promised efficiency, precision, and more effective care coordination. Pervasive digitalization in hospitals and clinics ensued as a result of government and global health agency pressures to embrace the use of EHRs, e.g., the US Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act.

With increasing volume and complexity of digital data, new challenges emerged, foremost among them being safe data exchange, privacy, and interoperability. Hospitals, diagnostic laboratories, insurance companies, and research centers all must communicate data with each other smoothly because healthcare systems in the world now are so integrated. But seamless interoperability is being hindered by proprietary systems, decentralized data ownership, and the lack of a common standard. These risks are also heightened by the sensitivity of health information: diagnoses, genetic information, treatment history, and insurance status are all contained in individual medical records and must rigorously meet standards like HIPAA, GDPR, and national health data protection legislation.

Different privacy-preserving methods have been researched over time in a bid to safeguard against such privacy concerns. Data anonymization, masking or removal of personally identifying information, has been the most common approach used. As a minimum layer of defense, anonymization has frequently been found to be reversible by data linkage attacks, particularly when data sets have been merged with other publicly available information. Pseudonymization is another alternative, where IDs are mapped to pseudonyms; this approach also becomes susceptible to reidentification attacks when the pseudonymized data are released or it is needed to track longitudinal information.

Homomorphic encryption was suggested to enable calculations over encrypted data without decrypting the data. Although of theoretical interest, its heavy computing cost and latency render it unsuitable for utilization in real-time, high-throughput healthcare applications. Differential privacy was created because it offers both utility and privacy, especially in medical studies and research at the population level, by adding statistical noise into query results. However, it is sometimes accused of providing less useful information, particularly when applied to extremely small datasets, which are prevalent in specialized therapy settings.
