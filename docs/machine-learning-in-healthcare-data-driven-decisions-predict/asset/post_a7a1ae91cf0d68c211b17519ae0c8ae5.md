## 3.2 Multi-objective Optimization via Pareto Front Approximation

The Pareto front P is defined as

P = \{J(\theta) \in R^m : \nexists \theta' \text{ with } J(\theta') \succ J(\theta)\} (3)

where $J(\theta') \succ J(\theta)$ means $J(\theta')$ dominates $J(\theta)$.

To approximate $P$, the system integrates an evolutionary multi-objective algorithm (e.g., NSGA-II) that evolves a population of policy parameters $\{\theta_i\}_{i=1}^N$ based on dominance and diversity criteria.

### 3.2.1 Fitness Function

The fitness for individual $i$ with policy $\pi_{\theta_i}$ is calculated using dominance rank $r_i$ and crowding distance $d_i$

\(\text{Fitness}(\theta_i) = r_i + \frac{1}{d_i + \epsilon}\) (4)

where $\epsilon$ is a small constant to avoid division by zero.

## 3.3 Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

PPO is employed as the policy optimization engine within the evolutionary framework. PPO improves policy stability by limiting the update magnitude via a clipped surrogate objective.

The PPO objective for a single objective $i$ is

\(L^{(i)}(\theta) = E_t [ \min ( r_t^{(i)}(\theta) A^{\hat{(i)}}_t, \text{clip}( r_t^{(i)}(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon ) A^{\hat{(i)}}_t ) ]\) (5)

where
\(r_t^{(i)}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)}\) is the probability ratio,
\(A^{\hat{(i)}}_t\) is the advantage estimate for objective $i$,
\(\epsilon\) is the clipping parameter.

For multi-objective PPO, the overall objective is a weighted combination

\(L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^m w_i L^{(i)}(\theta)\) (6)

where weights $w = (w_1, w_2, ..., w_m)$ are adaptively updated to explore different regions of the Pareto front.

## 3.4 Adaptive Weighting Scheme

To balance exploration and exploitation across objectives, weights $w_i$ are dynamically adjusted based on reward gradients

\( w_i^{t+1} = w_i^t + \eta \frac{\partial J^{(i)}(\theta)}{\partial \theta} \) (7)

where \(\eta\) is a learning rate. After update, weights are normalized

\( w \longleftarrow \frac{w}{\|w\|_1} \) (8)

This enables the algorithm to emphasize under-optimized objectives and improve overall Pareto coverage.

## 3.5 Policy Evaluation and Update

We use generalized advantage estimation (GAE) to compute \(\hat{A^i}_t^{(i)}\)

\(\hat{A^i}_t^{(i)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}^{(i)}\) (9)

where

\(\delta_t^{(i)} = r_t^{(i)} + \gamma V^{(i)}(s_{t+1}) - V^{(i)}(s_t)\) (10)

\(\lambda \in [0, 1]\) is the GAE parameter controlling bias-variance tradeoff and \(V^{(i)}\) is the value function for objective \(i\).

The parameter update for each policy \(\theta_i\) can be expressed as

\(\theta_i^{k+1} = \theta_i^k + \alpha \nabla_\theta L\left(\theta_i^k\right)\) (11)

where \(\alpha\) is the learning rate and

\(\nabla_\theta L\left(\theta_i^k\right) = \sum_{t=0}^T \sum_{j=1}^m w_j \nabla_\theta \min \left( r_t^{(j)}\left(\theta_i^k\right) \hat{A^{-(j)}}_t , clip \left( r_t^{(j)}\left(\theta_i^k\right), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon \right) \hat{A^{-(j)}}_t \right)\) (12)

The evolutionary update is

\(\theta_i^{k+1} = \text{Evolve}\left( \left\{ \theta_i^k \right\}_{i=1}^N, \text{Fitness}\left( \theta_i^k \right) \right)\) (13)

### 3.5.1 Convergence and Complexity

The combination of PPO and evolutionary multi-objective optimization aims to leverage the sample efficiency and stability of PPO with the global search ability of evolutionary algorithms. While PPO guarantees monotonic improvement under certain assumptions, the multi-objective setting requires careful balancing of objectives and exploration to avoid local optima. The computational complexity per iteration is dominated by trajectory sampling O(N · T), where N is population size and T trajectory length, and by the PPO gradient update O(N · |θ|). Evolutionary operations add O(NlogN) complexity due to sorting in NSGA-II.

This comprehensive system methodology offers a mathematically sound framework for resolving complicated, conflicting healthcare objectives by integrating PPO with multi-objective evolutionary optimization. A dynamic, scalable, and efficient solution for real-time healthcare decision intelligence is ensured by the application of vector-valued rewards, adaptive weighting, Pareto front approximation, and policy gradient approaches.

## 4 Experimental Results

The proposed Reinforced Multi-Objective Optimization Framework for Healthcare Decision Intelligence (RMO-HDI) was experimentally evaluated utilizing Python 3.10 with TensorFlow 2.13, Stable-Baselines3, and NSGA-II (via DEAP) frameworks in a controlled simulation environment. A 16-core Intel Xeon CPU, 128 GB of RAM, and two NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs running Ubuntu 22.04 LTS were used to run the simulations, allowing for parallelized training and real-time inference evaluation. PhysioNet’s eICU Collaborative Research Database and MIMIC-IV (version 2.2) were included in the dataset, which comprised more than 35,000 patient records, more than 200 clinical variables, and multi-temporal modalities such as lab results, vital signs, and medication history.

Four significant evaluation metrics are false positive rate (FPR), diagnostic accuracy (DA), treatment recommendation accuracy (TRA), and convergence time (CT) for optimal policy stabilization that were utilized to thoroughly assess the efficacy of the proposed RMO-HDI framework. They are selected to test whether such a system can generate recommendations fast, accurately, and specifically to a patient. Four cutting-edge comparison techniques were applied in order to provide a solid baseline: (i) Standard expert systems are represented by the rule-based clinical pathway engine (RB-CPE); (ii) ensemble-based learning is reflected by the static multi-class XGBoost (MC-XGB); (iii) sequence-aware deep learning, without adaptive learning, is represented by the attention-based LSTM with fixed weights (ALSTM-FW); and (iv) a deep multimodal neural architecture that integrates temporal and spatial patterns is the multi-head CNN-RNN hybrid (MHCNN-RNN). A clear performance landscape was presented by this multi-model comparison, emphasizing the significant gains in RMO-HDI's training efficiency, diagnostic reliability, recommendation accuracy, and error minimization.

In particular, its accuracy in making treatment recommendations was 92.3%, while those of RB-CPE, MC-XGB, ALSTM-FW, and MHCNN-RNN were 70.4%, 75.6%, and 80.1%, respectively. ## 5 Conclusion

In order to dynamically customize patient-specific diagnostic and treatment paths, this study introduces RMO-HDI, which successfully combines DRL with Pareto-based optimization. Experiments conducted on extensive benchmark datasets show notable gains in treatment suggestion accuracy, diagnostic precision, and false positive reduction over conventional rule-based and static machine learning techniques, along with faster convergence. These results demonstrate that RMO-HDI addresses major shortcomings of current systems and facilitates more intelligent, flexible, and real-time clinical decision-making. This paradigm has the potential to revolutionize clinical practice and enhance patient outcomes in increasingly complicated medical contexts by enhancing personalized healthcare workflows.
