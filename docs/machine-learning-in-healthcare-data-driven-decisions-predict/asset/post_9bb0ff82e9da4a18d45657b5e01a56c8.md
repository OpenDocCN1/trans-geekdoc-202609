## 8.2 Data Quality and Standardization Issues

Reliable healthcare data is the most useful need, because it can be used trustfully. When data is poorly collected, meaningful analyses cannot be performed, clinicians doubt useful knowledge from data, and actual treatment choices might compromise a patient's health. Some data problems in healthcare result from missing parts of patients' records, wrong data entry, various types of coding, and information that is either too old or not recorded [54]. Data collected in healthcare can be from EHRs, medical images, lab results, records collected by patients, and important hospital systems. Because different sources have their own structures, formats, and rules, it is difficult to integrate them. The standardization of data is tied to how accurate it is. The main problem is that the standards for data organization and ways to change or exchange information are not identical in healthcare. Many organizations and countries differ in their use of HL7, FHIR, and SNOMED CT, despite their existence [55]. As a result of this variation, the same medical concept can be recorded in several ways, preventing data from being collected and analyzed correctly and consistently. The situation becomes even more challenging when standard terms and codes are not used for diagnoses or laboratory tests [27]. Mistakes such as errors in typing, having multiple entries for things, and forgetting some needed information may occur with manual data entry. Since gathering live data is often tricky in many areas, history-taking often does not accurately reflect what happens currently in the hospital. Many times, tasks related to cleaning and validating data are not automated and still require too much work from data analysts, causing estimation delays. It is not easy to standardize maps for both space and time. Any differences in when information is recorded or entered may disrupt long-term research. Integration of data from various care places, locations, or nations where health practices are different results in spatial heterogeneity. To make these improvements, enterprises must use global standards, organize training for their staff, install appropriate verification systems, and monitor all information processes regularly [25].

## 8.3 Resistance to Change and Skills Gap

Data is often underused because people seem to stand in the way. Internally, doctors and nurses usually resist changes when given new information systems or devices. It is possible for clinicians, administration, and leaders to resist change because they expect it to be labor-intensive, negatively affect usual routines, or not be valuable [53]. In some cases, individuals may strictly avoid using data systems, follow only a few guidelines, or add data to their work but not use it to make real decisions.

Typically, people resist data analytics because they simply do not understand the results or doubt them. Those primarily trained to provide healthcare may not be skilled enough in data analysis to assess the findings. As a result, many of them do not feel comfortable using data to make changes in their work. Besides, you may not want to refer users to AI-recommended products due to the risk of being held accountable or liable for them. A lack of certain skills is apparent in nurses, doctors, managers, and IT workers alike. Around the world, there is not enough knowledge among data scientists, clinical informaticists, and health IT specialists for working with domain-specific information to transform raw data into useful intelligence [54]. Many programs for training and education in health data analytics are not designed with healthcare settings in mind [28]. Thus, healthcare companies have difficulty creating teams that can collectively use complex information. Because technology is progressing rapidly, we must learn new skills all the time. Data analysts and other members of the team should stay informed about any new technologies, changes in the regulations, and improve their knowledge of best data management practices. If employees are not given opportunities for continuous learning and development by their organization, the existing skills gap will make it more difficult to work with data. Managing the changes worked out and boosting knowledge require well-designed strategies. Those efforts should be supported by involving stakeholders on time, providing the advantages of data usage, creating available courses and instructional material, and designing a workplace that values change. In order for these changes to succeed, it is up to leaders to guide them and provide the tools and resources necessary for training workers.

## 8.4 Managing Uncertainty in Data Interpretation

Data interpretation in healthcare is inherently complex due to the probabilistic nature of medical knowledge, variability in patient populations, and limitations in data completeness. Managing uncertainty is a fundamental challenge when leveraging healthcare data for decision-making. Uncertainty arises from multiple sources, including measurement errors, incomplete data capture, biases in data collection, and limitations in analytic models [55].

Healthcare data often contains noise and outliers that can skew results, if not appropriately handled. For example, vital signs recorded during emergency situations may be atypical and misrepresent the patient's baseline status. Similarly, missing data due to skipped tests or lost records introduces uncertainty that complicates analysis. Statistical methods to handle missing data, such as imputation, can mitigate but not eliminate these challenges. We should also pay close attention to model uncertainty. Historical data is often used in developing models, but they may not be reliable when treating patient groups with other health issues. This applies mainly in situations where the situation develops rapidly such as during a disease outbreak or the launch of a new type of therapy [31]. Clinicians should be able to use an analytic system with confidence, which requires that the model's process, testing, and drawbacks are explained. Clinicians may find it challenging to make sense of the results provided by many data models. Statistics needed for confidence intervals, risk scores, and likelihood ratios is not commonly understood by most people using the tool. If data is misinterpreted, there is a risk of people either using it too much or not using it enough, each situation having negative effects. The issue of ethics is connected to situations of uncertainty. Using information or data that is uncertain can put one's belief in justice, and a patient's rights and risk assessment at risk. Contingency calls for using clinical experience, patient wishes, and understanding the environment and data in combination. Healthcare providers should create interpretative tools that can display explanations, express how uncertain a result is, and are simple for doctors to use. Clinicians should also receive training in handling and managing uncertainty. For data-driven decisions to be more secure and trustworthy, the efforts of data scientists, clinicians, and ethicists should be brought together (Figure 6).
