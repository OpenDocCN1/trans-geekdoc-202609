The feature extraction interpretation of these medical images in the early stages of computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) was used greatly to extract and characterize areas of interest, while traditional methods tended primarily to rest on handcrafted methods such as edge detection, texture analysis (e.g., gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) and local binary patterns (LBPs)), morphological operators, and histogram-based features. Although revolutionary, these methods suffered from the limitation of relying on domain-specific knowledge, sensitivity to noise, and bad generalization across different imaging conditions and disease types.

With the increasing popularity of machine learning in the 1990s and early 2000s, supervised classification models such as random forests, k-nearest neighbors (k-NNs), and support vector machines (SVMs) began employing these manually designed features to diagnose diseases. Feature engineering was still the major driver of success for these approaches. This limitation led to the transition to deep learning methods, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which offered the ability to learn features from raw image data directly in a data-driven and hierarchical manner. CNN models that learn discriminative features automatically from large datasets like AlexNet, VGGNet, GoogLeNet, and ResNet far outperformed traditional methods.

In medical imaging tasks, early CNN models, though incredibly effective, had limitations. Class imbalance, noise corruption, and small inter-class differences are some of the challenges that accompany medical data, which is inherently complex and often high-dimensional. Due to variations in feature distribution and clinical relevance, generic CNNs trained on natural image datasets (such as ImageNet) do not necessarily translate well to the medical domain. Furthermore, because medical anomalies vary in size, shape, and texture, single-scale convolutional filters might not adequately capture their multi-scale nature. While larger disorders require coarse-level context, small lesions may require fine-grained details. Moreover, standard CNN architectures equally process all regions of the image, which can lead to the inclusion of noisy features or irrelevant ones and cause a decrease in classification accuracy.

Over the past few years, various advanced feature extraction techniques have been released to solve these problems. Due to its ability to extract spatial patterns at various resolutions, multi-scale feature extraction has gained popularity. In response to scale variation, architectures like dilated convolutions, inception blocks, and feature pyramid networks (FPN) have been proposed. Deep learning itself has been revolutionized by the attention mechanism, enabling models to pay preferential attention to the most informative parts of an image. Approaches that draw attention to diagnostically salient areas while disregarding noise and redundant information include transformer-based self-attention modules, spatial attention, and channel attention (e.g., squeeze-and-excitation networks). However, there is no information about how multi-scale learning and attention mechanisms could be integrated into one framework for medical diagnosis.
