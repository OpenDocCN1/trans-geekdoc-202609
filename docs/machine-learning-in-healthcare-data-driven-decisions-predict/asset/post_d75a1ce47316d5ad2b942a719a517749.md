where

$\mu_t, \sigma_t = \text{\textit{BiLSTM}}_\theta(X^t)$

Decoder,

$X'^t = \text{GRU}_\phi(z_t)$

Loss function,

$L_{\text{TVAE}} = E[||X^t - X'^t||^2] + \beta \cdot D_{KL}(q(z|X^t) \| N(0, I))$

## Step 5: Clinical Text Representation

Each clinical document $d_i \in X^u$ is embedded using BioClinicalBERT.

Tokenization,

$\text{input} = [\text{CLS}] + \text{tokens}(d_i) + [\text{SEP}]$

Embedding,

$e_i = \text{BERT}_{[\text{CLS}]}(d_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$

Salient concept detection,

$S_i = \{ c \in d_i \mid c \in \text{UMLS concepts} \}$

Final document vector,

$v_i^{\text{text}} = \text{AttentionPool}(e_i, S_i)$

## Step 6: Multimodal Fusion

Three modalities are combined:

- $H_G$: Graph embeddings
- $Z_t$: Temporal embeddings
- $V_u$: Text embeddings

### Gated Fusion

$F_{fused} = \sigma(W_g[H_G||Z_t||V_u] + b_g)$

where $W_g \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 3d}$, $\sigma$ is sigmoid gate.

## Step 7: Reinforcement Learning-Based Policy Optimization

Let the state $s_t$ represent the patient vector at time $t$. The RL agent chooses an action $a_t$ (e.g., medication class or procedure) based on policy $\pi(a_t|s_t)$.

### Reward Function

$r_t = \lambda_1 \cdot Survival + \lambda_2 \cdot (1 - Readmission) - \lambda_3 \cdot Treatment Cost$ (19)

### Q-Learning Objective

$Q^*(s_t, a_t) = r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a')$ (20)

We use deep Q-network (DQN) with a shared encoder over fused features and optimize

$L_{DQN} = E[(Q(s_t, a_t) - y_t)^2], y_t = r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q'(s_{t+1}, a')$ (21)

BERT-based clinical NLP allows contextual understanding of unstructured notes; variational encoding of temporal signals models disease progression; graph-based relational reasoning captures patient similarities and connections; and RL dynamically adjusts treatment policies. The system preserves contextual awareness by gated multimodal fusion, and DQN-based policy optimization guarantees patient-specific, outcome-aware choices. Trust and interpretability in clinical situations are ensured by the architecture's support for explainability through SHAP and attention visualizations.

## 4 Experimental Results

To benchmark the PATHTRACK-RL framework's predictive capacity, treatment recommendation capacity, and overall impact on patient outcomes, an extensive experimental series was performed. Each experiment was executed under the conditions listed below within a controlled, high-performance computing environment: 256 GB RAM, an NVIDIA A100 Tensor Core GPU (40 GB), an Intel Xeon Gold 6338 CPU (32 cores), and Ubuntu 22.04 LTS. PyTorch 2.0, HuggingFace Transformers 4.35, DGL 1.1 for graph operations, and scikit-learn for evaluating baseline metrics were utilized in the implementation. The MIMIC-IV dataset was handled with PostgreSQL, and high-throughput in-memory data processing was enabled through Apache Arrow.

The eICU Collaborative Research Database and MIMIC-IV v2.2 were the two benchmark datasets utilized to evaluate the proposed PATHTRACK-RL system. A filtered cohort of 47,518 ICU patient admissions was selected from MIMIC-IV, according to the quality of the clinical records, laboratory results, and diagnostic codes. The eICU dataset supplemented 31,762 additional records in 200 hospitals. Preprocessing both datasets included using consistent temporal granularity (hourly bins), SNOMED/ICD-10 standard coding through mapping, and note segmentation, with a pretrained BioClinicalBERT tokenizer.

We developed several strong baselines in order to provide comparable performance: For tabular EHR features, employed XGBoost (gradient boosted decision trees); feedforward neural networks (FNNs) with dropout regularization and ReLU activations; models that only employ transformers, e.g., standard encoder stacks, which are learned from concatenated multimodal embeddings; GAT-only variant, excluding the policy and VAE layers; variation that only employs VAE, with no reinforcement layers or graph structure; policy learner based on REINFORCE, as a non-deep RL baseline. To provide balanced representation for both positive and negative outcome classes (e.g., ICU readmission, death, and sepsis onset), each model was trained and validated in a fivefold stratified cross-validation environment. Early stopping was used based on validation AUC with a 10-epoch patience duration.

Three primary aspects of the assessment were accuracy of early risk prediction, precision of recommendations for treatment, and clinical utility outcomes. We utilized AUROC, F1-score, accuracy, precision, and recall for classification tasks (e.g., 24-h sepsis prediction). We utilized NDCG (normalized discounted cumulative gain) and top-K precision (K = 1, 3, 5) for recommendation tasks. We quantified readmission reduction, mortality prediction accuracy, and length-of-stay minimization by replicating care pathway optimizations under simulation using retrospective data to estimate real-world effectiveness.

From Figure 2, PATHTRACK-RL surpassed the top baseline (Transformer-only, 88.2% accuracy) by attaining 94.1% accuracy, 92.6% recall, and 93.3% F1-score on early ICU risk prediction (Figure 3). In Figure 4, compared to 84.7% and 0.891 for GAT-only, treatment recommendation tasks had a Top-3 precision of 91.8% and NDCG@5 of 0.944. Longitudinal simulations also recorded a 7.4% improvement in discharge outcome scores based on SAPS-II risk scale and an average 11.2% reduction in the ICU readmission rate. Omitting reinforcement-based treatment optimization reduced the quality of recommendations by over 7.1% in Top-3 precision, whereas removing the GAT layer reduced accuracy by 4.9% based on ablation experiments. Including the stacked VAE was also essential, as it enhanced multimodal encoding efficiency by 3.4% according to reconstruction loss and latent clustering purity.

PATHTRACK-RL possesses the best balance between accuracy and recall, attaining the highest accuracy (94.1%) and F1-score (93.3%).
