Features with weak predictive power in this database, for example, sugar or specific gravity, can be excluded irrespective of their clinical importance in some cases. Highly weighted attributes, for example, albumin, hemoglobin, SC, blood urea, and red blood cell count, have larger coefficients, which confirms their clinical importance in the detection of early CKD. The Lasso-based filtering appears to keep only those features that give a high signal to the learning algorithm and hence shrink the feature space from 35 to 40 to a more manageable and informative 15–20 features.

Apart from speeding up the training and inference process, this dimensionality reduction also improves model interpretability, something of paramount importance in the context of healthcare applications. A model where a small number of known biomarkers are used for prediction is beneficial to clinicians. Furthermore, the model will not overfit when the noisy and redundant variables are removed, something important when working with small and imbalanced datasets like CKD. Finally, the critical HRAB classifier is then supplied with the selected features such that the ensemble is working on a statistically valid and clinically relevant set of the original data.

# ## 3.3 Base Learner Setup

After preprocessing and feature selection operations, the resultant dataset, normalized, balanced, and dimensionally compressed to around 15–20 meaningful features are provided as input to the base learners of the HRAB algorithm. The HRAB algorithm is specifically designed to use the relative strengths of light, efficient, and regularized learners in order to achieve high generalization ability, computational lightness, and robustness against overfitting. Unlike the traditional ensemble models, which would most likely utilize homogeneous learners (e.g., an ensemble of decision trees), HRAB maximally leverages heterogeneous base learners, each of which possesses inherent regularization and noise tolerance properties. Such heterogeneity introduces diversity among the learners, which is crucial in reducing the correlation of the individual errors and improving the overall generalization of the ensemble.

From Table 1, regularized decision trees are used as the base class of learners. They are shallow trees with a maximal depth (fixed typically at 3 or 4) and minimal samples per leaf (such as 10 or more). These limits are such that they keep the trees from overfitting noise, which is an issue with small, unbalanced clinical datasets like CKD. The simplicity of the tree ensures quick execution and interpretability without being unable to model important, nonlinear relations in the data. Penalized logistic regression (L2 regularization) is the second learner type. The classifier is trained with respect to loss function under penalty on squared magnitude of coefficients, which prevents model variance and overfitting. In CKD, where lab values are strongly correlated (e.g., SC and blood urea), L2 regularization prevents feature importance from runaway oscillation and enforces stability on weights.
