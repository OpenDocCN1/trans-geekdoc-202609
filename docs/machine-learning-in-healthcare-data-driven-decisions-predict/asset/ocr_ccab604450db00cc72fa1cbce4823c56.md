Hence, the width and depth of comprehensive evaluation also call for metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and recall. There are some aggregate measures that show the model’s performance scores on different classification thresholds, such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). It is most used for imbalanced datasets that characterize most of healthcare; for example, detecting rare diseases, as AUPRC is considered more informative than AUROC.

Clinical validation, more than statistical measures, is an important phase to ascertain how well an ML model works in a real-world healthcare environment, while being exposed to a diverse patient population and clinical operational flows. It usually involves prospective studies comparing the efficiency of the AI system to a current human expert in clinical practice. Regulatory agencies such as the FDA are developing frameworks for the validation and approval of medical AI devices, with an emphasis on good evidence regarding safety and effectiveness, which will often include the types of data that they require clinical trials to demonstrate efficacy and safety in the target population. Such dynamic characteristics of health data and clinical practice necessitate continuous monitoring and recalibration of deployed ML models. This is basically ensuring performance does not degrade over time because of shifts in data distribution due to changes in treatment protocols. Establishment of robust post-market surveillance systems is critical in identifying potential issues and ensuring the dependable deployment of AI systems [17].

Model interpretability is an emerging area of research and an important one for the acceptance of ML in healthcare. This is understood as important for clinicians to see why an AI would recommend or predict something and adopt it in the decision-making process while holding the machine accountable, as well as in building up the trust of their patients. Although such black-box systems promise high accuracy, they often fail to accommodate the demands of a clinical workflow, which needs transparency and justification. Intrinsic interpretability and post hoc interpretability are the two broad categories under which interpretability methods can be classified: intrinsic interpretability encompasses approaches that use inherently understandable models such as trees or linear models; post hoc interpretability relates to the application of techniques designed to explain the predictions of complex models. A post hoc method includes feature importance studies, partial dependence plots, surrogate models, and techniques such as SHAP and LIME. These techniques help to shed light on which of the input features most possibly influence a patient’s prediction by the model. Providing interpretable explanations will initiate a critical appraisal by clinicians of the output from the model, as such, identify the errors or biases that are likely, as well as build confidence in the AI system as a reliable tool rather than a mysterious oracle. The degree of interpretability needed will depend on the type of clinical application: generally, high-stakes decisions such as diagnosis and treatment planning will require more transparency than administrative automation tasks.
