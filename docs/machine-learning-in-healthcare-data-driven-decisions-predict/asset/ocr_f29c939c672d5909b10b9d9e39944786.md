Recently we faced the Corona virus outbreak, which was an infectious disease that threatened mankind. According to the up-to-date available data till July 2022, World Health Organization (WHO) has confirmed that 562 million people were infected and 6.3 million people lost their lives due to the disease. It just spread from human to human in contact or in air (small distance); for tracking, we ha to backtrack the persons' past contact history with others. All these studies are just like studying a complex network. These things follow a pattern that can be analyzed in deep to tackle the problem.

There are several other virus outbreaks that happened in the world, for example, influenzas A(H1N1) pandemic from 2009 to 2010, Ebola virus disease epidemic in west African from 2013 to 2016, and the zika virus epidemic from 2015 to 2016; all these diseases spread human to human through spreaders in numerous countries across different continents. The investigation of the occurrence of these rising disease epidemics has been driven by an increasing research curiosity. Concurrently, there has been a growing need for trustworthy open data from authoritative sources. The available databases are restricted in terms of the subject matter, since they only cover a few infectious illnesses or are focused on a particular demographic, nation, or area. In addition, there are additional datasets that rely on unofficial data, which may include inaccurate facts or deliberate misinformation from fraudulent reports, and are not accessible to the public, thus impeding their reuse and usage.

Leung et al. conducted a study to Cambodia in 2012 and they found that social interactions inside families tend to occur between individuals of different age groups, highlighting intergenerational relationships [13]. The research targeted places with high levels of interaction, such as homes and schools, and emphasized differences in mixing patterns between rural and urban inhabitants. These findings provide useful information for predicting the spread of infectious diseases.

In an investigation of viral contact relationships, using network analysis is a valuable method for uncovering the specific structures of individual infectious contact networks. During the investigation in Korea of the 2015 Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) epidemic, Yang and Jung [14] examined how illnesses spread among people via contact networks. They focused on identifying the unique characteristics and structural advantages of a small number of super-spreaders, who had a significant role in aiding the continuous transmission of the disease.

The WHO has compiled a comprehensive dataset on infectious disease outbreaks. This dataset includes data on 70 different infectious illnesses and 2,227 public health events. It covers data from January 1996 to March 2022 and spans across 233 countries and territories. The data was sourced from the Illness Epidemic News and the Corona-virus Dashboard. Munguía et al. examined the dataset's potential usefulness for the research community [15]. They also exemplified by analyzing the spatial distribution of disease outbreaks, which uncovers concentrated areas or “hot spots” in Asia, Africa, and America. This analysis offers policymakers valuable insights for identifying regions with a greater probability of experiencing disease outbreaks.
