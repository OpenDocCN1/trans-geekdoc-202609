
## 3 System Methodology

### 3.1 Dataset Description

Two publicly available, large clinical datasets, eICU Collaborative Research Database and MIMIC-IV (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care, Version 4.0), were utilized for the development and evaluation of the proposed system. Detailed de-identified health information associated with over 53,423 adult intensive care unit admissions were recorded between 2008 and 2019 at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center from MIMIC-IV. Demographics, vital signs, prescription medications, laboratory tests, diagnostic codes (ICD-9/10), and clinical procedures are some of the structured records that are part of the collection. It also contains unstructured data such as nurse notes and discharge summaries. A focused cohort of 38,610 unique patients with comprehensive hospital stay information, continuous monitoring records, and a minimum of one documented diagnostic result and therapy recommendation were selected.

The model's generalizability was externally validated based on the eICU database, which holds multicenter intensive care unit data from 208 United States hospitals between 2014 and 2015. This dataset's similar structured data formats to MIMIC-IV and over 200,000 ICU admissions make cross-dataset alignment easier. About 41,770 admissions of the eICU cohort were filtered based on the presence of physician notes, ICU LOS (LOS ≥ 24 h), and completeness of data. These datasets together provided over 10 million rows of time-series measurements, 1.8 million clinical notes, and 5,200 unique diagnostic and procedural codes, which formed the basis for treatment sequence modeling and patient-level prediction tasks.

### 3.2 Preprocessing

Data harmonization was conducted first to ensure compatibility among datasets. Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) crosswalks were employed to normalize structured fields, including diagnosis and procedure codes, to ICD-10 CM and CPT coding systems. To align dates and times among hospital systems, timestamp alignment was performed. K-nearest neighbor imputation (KNNI) for lab tests and forward-filling for time-series vital signs were implemented to achieve missing value imputation; k = 5 was selected experimentally based on validation performance. Target-based encoding, which calculates the frequency of every category and tags low-frequency tokens (<0.5%) under the “OTHER” category, was utilized to summarize infrequent categorical values in fields like drug names and disease descriptions.

With the cTAKES pipeline, clinical NLP preprocessing was conducted on all text data, such as nursing notes and discharge summaries. This entailed negation identification, UMLS concept identification, sentence splitting, and part-of-speech tagging. A stopword list, specific to the domain, was utilized to remove stopwords, and the ScispaCy model tuned for biomedical text was utilized to apply lemmatization. Finally, to ensure temporal consistency, time-series values were resampled uniformly, with linear interpolation at hourly sampling. Interquartile range (IQR) filtering was employed to identify outlier lab values and vital signs, and a reduced z-score (±3 standard deviations) was applied to correct them. For gradient-based optimization during training of the model, the entire dataset was standardized via z-score normalization, separately for every variable.

### 3.3 Feature Extraction

A relational graph was constructed for the structured data, with nodes denoting patients, diagnoses, treatments, and clinical events, and edges denoting semantic or temporal relations (e.g., “treated-with” and “diagnosed-with”). This graph structure was subsequently pre-trained with GATs, allowing the model to learn context-aware embeddings that take both the global graph structure and local neighborhood into account. A 128-dimensional one-hot encoded vector was used to initialize each node embedding, which was then updated by multiple-head attention layers (with 8 heads of size 64).

A temporal VAE (TVAE) represented time-series physiological data, such as blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and heart rate. Each signal was compressed into a 64-dimensional latent space, and latent temporal patterns were placed in irregular sequences. Fourier transform features were also reconstructed to represent periodicity and signal change as power spectral density components. BioClinicalBERT, a pre-training language model of MIMIC-III notes and PubMed abstracts, was used to produce text embeddings of unstructured clinical notes. A self-attention pooling layer pooled every document, following tokenization into 512-word segments and embedding into 768-dimensional space. Salient phrases like “acute respiratory failure” and “suspected sepsis” were stored as semantic tags and appended to the note vector as binary features using UMLS mapping.

A cross-modal fusion layer was added to control interactions between different data modalities. The output of the TVAE (for temporal signals), BERT embeddings (for textual comprehension), and the GAT-derived embeddings (for relation structure) were combined in this layer. To construct a single patient image, a controlled fusion mechanism subsequently weighted each modality according to learned confidence levels. Each patient’s individual temporal, relational, and contextual properties were mapped to the 512-dimensional multimodal feature vector that was created. To be used in downstream tasks like death prediction, therapy recommendation, and risk of ICU readmission, this high-dimensional patient vector was employed. In this case, that attention weight visualization, feature attribution via SHAP, and node rank importance in the GAT graph guaranteed interpretability so that each prediction that could actually be explained was significant.

### 3.4 Proposed Algorithm

The proposed system is a multimodal, reinforcement-driven clinical decision support system that employs deep learning models to synthesize temporal signals, unstructured clinical narrative, and structured EHR. BioClinicalBERT for natural language understanding, TVAE for sequential modeling, and GAT for relational reasoning are the basic building blocks of the architecture. Utilizing latent states specific to every patient, an RL module learns to optimize the selection of treatment policy. Every component is integrated in an end-to-end trainable pipeline wherein cross-modal fusion is performed using a gated attention mechanism.

### Step 1: Data Acquisition and Cohort Selection

Two large-scale de-identified ICU datasets – MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD – were used. The inclusion criteria are as follows:

- LOS ≥ 24 h
- Complete records of vitals, labs, medication, and diagnosis
- At least one discharge summary and ICU outcome

### Step 3: Graph-Based Feature Encoding

A heterogeneous graph \(G = (V, E)\) is constructed,
\(V = \{v_p, v_d, v_m, v_o\}\): patients, diagnoses, medications, and outcomes
\(E = \{(v_i, v_j, r)\}\): edges with relations \(r \in \{\text{diagnosed\_with, treated\_with, has\_outcome}\}\)

### Graph Attention Network (GAT)

Each node embedding is computed as

\(h'_i = \sigma \left( \sum_{j \in N(i)} a_{ij} W h_j \right)\)

where attention coefficient

\(a_{ij} = \frac{\exp(\text{LeakyReLU}(a^T[Wh_i||Wh_j]))}{\sum_{k \in N(i)} \exp(\text{LeakyReLU}(a^T[Wh_i||Wh_k]))}\)

Final graph-based feature vector,

\(H_G = [h'_1, h'_2, \dots, h'_N] \in R^{N \times d}\)
