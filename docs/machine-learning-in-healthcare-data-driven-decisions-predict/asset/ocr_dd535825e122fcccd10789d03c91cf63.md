One can never be certain of generalizability. Because of differences in population demographics, practices of medicine, and data collection protocols, models trained on the data of one site usually do not work on other external datasets. Legitimacy and scalability of ML-based systems are compromised owing to the paucity of robustness.

Interpretability of the model is another big concern. The most precise models, especially DL models, are “black boxes” because clinicians do not have a clue about how they reach their conclusions. Transparency and credibility are paramount in the line of work where choices have the potential to deeply transform an individual’s life. The demand for explainable AI in medicine is also emphasized by regulatory frameworks like the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which even establishes the right to explanation in automatic decision-making. Moreover, interpretability constrains healthcare professionals from accessing and utilizing such systems in real-world scenarios. Sparsity and data quality are genuine concerns.

Whereas unbalanced datasets, where a few diseases or outcomes are underrepresented, are likely to lead to biased predictions with disproportionate effects on minority groups, missing data, noise, and annotation error can decrease model performance. The ethical, legal, and societal implications offer the greatest challenge over these technical challenges. Ownership of the data, informed consent, and privacy are concerns raised by the use of patient data. Further, even more harm than benefit may be created by applying predictive models without adequate validation because they may result in overdiagnosis, unwarranted therapies, or missed diagnoses. These points highlight the necessity of human-in-the-loop systems, constant model monitoring, and effective validation frameworks.

The dynamic nature of healthcare environments introduces another level of complexity. New diseases, altered treatment protocols, and shifting clinical practice guidelines can all make models already trained, out of date. This phenomenon, sometimes termed “model drift” or “concept drift,” necessitates adaptive learning systems, with the capability to update model parameters real time or very close to real time. The majority of today’s existing models are static and do not react to such changes without complete retraining, which is complicated for most healthcare centers and is resource-intensive.

The ML-based health systems are still quite strong despite the following serious drawbacks. It is being driven by various drivers, including the rising disease burden of chronic diseases, aging population, growing healthcare expenditure, and desire for personalized therapy. Clinicians, academics, and politicians are all increasingly in agreement in their perception that evidence-based interventions have the potential to revolutionize healthcare provision through the provision of proactive rather than reactive interventions, provided they are well constructed and carried out in an ethical manner.
