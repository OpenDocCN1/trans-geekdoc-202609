The use of deep learning has brought about a dramatic shift in the field of cervical cancer classification. From interpretable and multimodal AI models to handcrafted hybrid systems, and from standard CNNs to transformer-based and graph-based approaches, the field is constantly working to come up with more accurate, scalable, and clinically applicable solutions. The literature consulted shows that despite significant progress, models that are able to extract contextual and morphological features in a complete way, process imbalanced data, and provide interpretability for application in clinics are still urgently needed. To close these gaps and improve the state-of-the-art for cervical cytology image analysis, the HCFEN method was proposed.

# 3 System Methodology

# 3.1 Dataset Description

Herlev dataset, being one of the most widely used and freely available cytological imaging datasets for cervical cancer research, is employed to design and evaluate the proposed system for cervical cancer classification. This dataset was collected from the Herlev University Hospital in Denmark and is officially known as the Herlev Pap smear dataset or “Pap smear benchmark dataset.” It consists of 917 Pap smear images of individual cells that have been carefully annotated by pathologists and cytotechnologists with confirmed diagnoses.

Seven diagnostic categories, which cover all stages of cervical cell abnormalities from normal to malignant, consist of the 917 images. The Bethesda standardized system for cervical cytology reporting is utilized in this hierarchical system. The data set consists of four categories of abnormal cells, which include carcinoma in situ (CIS) and low- and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (LSIL and HSIL), as well as three categories of normal cells.

Each image is a 768 × 576 grayscale microscopic image obtained at 20x or 40x magnification and stored in TIFF format in order to preserve quality. Manual annotation of the cytoplasmic and nucleic boundaries by the dataset is useful for segmentation and feature extraction purposes. Furthermore, to validate high labeling accuracy, every sample was independently classified and verified by two experienced cytotechnologists prior to establishing a consensus diagnosis by a senior pathologist.

The data is divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) subsets by stratified sampling to preserve the class proportion in each subset to ensure reproducibility and generalizability. The model has to be trained with class-weighted loss functions or oversampling/augmentation methods because of the minor imbalance in the dataset, especially between the normal and strongly abnormal classes.
