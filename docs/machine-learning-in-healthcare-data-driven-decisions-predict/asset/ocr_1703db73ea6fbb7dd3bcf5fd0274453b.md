# 2.2 Stress Recognition Using Wearable Sensors and Mobile Phones

Over the last few years, the combination of wearable sensors and mobile devices has received important attention for inobtrusive stress detection and long-term health monitoring. Various studies have investigated physiological signals such as HR, HRV, EDA, skin temperature, and respiration rate, which are captured through wearable devices such as smartwatches, chest straps, and wristbands. The authors showed the viability of integrating accelerometer and EDA wristband data to identify stress incidents with high accuracy under real-world use. Smartphones, in contrast, add behavioral and environmental data – apps used, call/SMS history, ambient noise level, location – and these can reveal aspects of the user’s day-to-day environment and life. They created a multimodal predictive model based on smartphone sensing and physiological signals to forecast stress levels using ML algorithms. Current methods increasingly utilize deep learning-based models such as CNNs and LSTMs, which have the ability to learn intricate temporal and spatial features automatically from multimodal data. There are, however, challenges in the areas of generalizability across individuals, privacy of data, and performance in actual-world settings. It is indicated in the current literature that the integration of psychological and behavioral characteristics – acquired passively through wearables and smartphones – has the potential to greatly improve the accuracy and usability of stress detection systems.

# 2.3 SELF-CARE: Selective Fusion with Context-Aware Low-Power Edge Computing for Stress Detection

Recent developments in wearable sensor technology and machine learning have greatly improved stress detection methods. The older statistical and rule-based techniques have been widely replaced by ML and deep learning (DL) methods, which are more able to capture the variability and complexity of physiological information. For example, a study presented an interpretable deep learning model that incorporated LSTM networks with deep generative ensembles (DGE) to identify stress from wearable sensor signals with better precision and recall compared to conventional methods.

Supervised learning methods, including SVM, RF, and GBM, are still used for stress classification purposes. Nonetheless, the use of deep learning models such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and LSTMs has proven to provide better performance in identifying intricate patterns from raw sensor data. One of the examples is the STREDWES approach, which transforms physiological signals into Gramian angular fields and applies CNNs for stress detection, which outperforms state-of-the-art models.
