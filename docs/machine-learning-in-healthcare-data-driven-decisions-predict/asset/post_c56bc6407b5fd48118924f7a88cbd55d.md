## 5 Analytical Methods for Evidence-Based Decision Making

Healthcare is taking a more data-driven and analytics-based approach, as it now relies on analytics to extract useful insights from a huge amount of data. In healthcare, using analysis methods is key to transforming data into evidence used for making decisions that are best for patients. This part explains important analytical procedures – descriptive, predictive, and prescriptive analytics – and discusses how machine learning, artificial intelligence, and decision-support systems improve healthcare by transforming it.

### 5.1 Descriptive, Predictive, and Prescriptive Analytics

Descriptive analytics forms the first step in analyzing data in the healthcare field. Historical data is used to look for regularities as well as any irregularities. Examples are the number of readmissions at hospitals, how long patients remain there, who they are, and the rate when a certain disease is contracted over time [33, 34]. To help understand the past events, descriptive analytics depends on dashboards, histograms, and summary statistics. To monitor the quality and compliance of care and assess performance, descriptive analytics is important [35]. It relies on statistics, machine learning, and old data to help predict what will happen next. Such models are also used in hospitals to predict how a disease will develop, how often the same patient is admitted to the hospital, and how many people will visit the emergency department. Many predictive models rely on logistic regression, time-series models, decision trees, and neural networks. Thanks to models, doctors are able to predict sepsis in patients at the hospital and start care well before the patient even shows signs of it [36]. In addition, prescriptive analytics uses the predicted outcomes to suggest ideas for the best possible steps to take. It uses decision science, along with predictions, to guide you toward the most desired results. Thanks to prescriptive analytics, doctors may select the best medical plan for each patient, figure out who to place in ICU beds, and at which dose and personalize the patient's medications. Commonly used for prescriptive decision-making are models, simulation tools, and reinforcement learning [37]. The three kinds of analytics are related to one another. In descriptive analytics, we focus on the present, predictive analytics helps with the future, and prescriptive analytics influences choices and decisions we make. With the help of this approach, healthcare professionals can start offering prevention-based care.

### 5.2 Machine Learning and AI Techniques in Healthcare

By applying these two technologies, the field of healthcare analysis has undergone a major shift. By using ML, it becomes easy to spot patterns in large data, which allows models to improve each time they are updated with new data. Common use of supervised learning implies that random forests, support vector machines and gradient boosters are often tasked with diagnosing illnesses or predicting a person's risk of death [38]. Some approaches in unsupervised learning, such as clustering, help group patients or recognize additional disease groups, as well as highlight how care is used. In fact, ML helps divide diabetic patients into groups that matter to clinicians, versus those based solely on age and gender. In ML, deep learning uses multilayer neural networks to deal with information in several dimensions. The field is most useful for cases like radiology, pathology, and electrocardiography. With CNNs, detecting cancerous lesions in radiography images is now as accurate as what radiologists do [39].

AI, through NLP, is able to find insights in statements written by doctors, discharge reports, or pathology records. Adverse drug events, assessing who is eligible for clinical trials, and improving EHRs can all be achieved with NLP models. Reinforcement learning uses AI algorithms to find the best actions by being part of an environment. This field is studied in healthcare to support personal evolution of therapies, adjust settings in intensive care, and maintain chronic conditions [40]. Still, when ML and AI are used, it is important to be cautious, as they may display bias, behave in unpredictable ways and their results may only apply to specific situations. It is still difficult to explain, verify fairness in AI, and test clinical AI for use in a variety of populations.

### 5.3 Risk Stratification and Outcome Prediction Models

Risk stratification involves grouping patients who have a higher risk of experiencing problems related to their health. Practicing medicine in this way ensures that health needs of those who are at highest risk are met as efficiently as possible. Many times logistic regression, survival analysis, and ML-based scores are used for risk estimates. The CHADS2 model is used to check for stroke risk in individuals living with atrial fibrillation. Another use is to estimate the chances of hospital readmission within 30 days, find patients who could experience a heart failure episode, and guess the probability of surgical complications. Predicting the outcome of a condition depends on information such as clinical markers, societal factors, lifestyle habits, comorbid illnesses, and demographics. Clinicians can administer proactive care by using integration of models into their dashboard tools. A real-time algorithm for predicting sepsis may be used in a hospital to send a message to the care team once early symptoms are spotted. Accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC-ROC are a few methods used to assess how well a prediction model performs. Probability estimations must be in agreement with what happens in the real world. Because overfitting, data drift, and testing on a small set often happen, people should always test and retrain the machine learning models [41].

### 5.4 Decision Support Systems and Clinical Dashboards

DSSs assist doctors and hospital staff by providing information and tools for making decisions. They use data collected from different places, run various analysis algorithms, and share the results in a straightforward way for users. CDSS is a kind of DSS that is part of EHRs. For example, they may send alerts if a patient is taking multiple medicines that work against one another, give doctor-chosen guidelines for specific cases, and remind patients when it is time for some preventive care. CDSS have helped raise safety when drugs are used, provide accurate diagnoses, and increase compliance with set clinical practices [42]. Real-time dashboards make it easier for caregivers to make important decisions. In some cases, an ICU dashboard will show patient vitals, test findings, and calculated risks, all at once, which helps nurses and doctors in taking quick actions. Within a hospital, administrative dashboards collect statistics related to the number of beds filled, average length of stay for patients, and their levels of satisfaction. Deploying predictive analytics on dashboards helps with taking steps ahead of any problems. In this case, leaders can accommodate more staff and supplies to the emergency room before the rise in visits occurs. For dashboards to be helpful to all users, they should be able to accommodate the needs of clinicians as well as executives. Still, to prevent too much information, a lot of alerts, and slow work, DSS and dashboards need to be designed with care. Ensuring development and use of artificial intelligence requires focusing on how people will interact, considering the engagement of stakeholders (Figure 4).

## 6 Applications of Data-Driven Approaches in Healthcare Delivery

By digitizing healthcare, methods that involve analyzing data have been incorporated into daily clinic and hospital routines. All the data available from various electronic and medical sources can now be used as a useful tool to improve management, delivery, and quality of healthcare. Now that intelligent algorithms are being used in healthcare, we are seeing improvements in patient care, healthcare resources, and more.

### 6.1 Enhancing Diagnostic Accuracy and Treatment Planning

In healthcare, using data-based methods often increases the accuracy of diagnosis. Using the usual methods to diagnose a condition can be influenced by a doctor's opinion and, at times, fail to be accurate. Using AI algorithms and evidence-based models with clinical information, doctors, and nurses can now detect hidden trends that the human eye may miss, making it less likely there will be errors during diagnoses. There have been promising outcomes using ML techniques after training on extensive medical records in radiology, pathology, and dermatology. For instance, using CNNs, it is now common to distinguish malignant tumors and benign tumors as well as expert radiologists do [43]. Furthermore, some DSSs look at a patient's history, labs, and clinical guidance to recommend the best care plan. They allow for the same practices to be used by health professionals and increase consistency in medical care.
