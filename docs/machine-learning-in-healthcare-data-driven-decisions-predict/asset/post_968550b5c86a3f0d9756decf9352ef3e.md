
## 2 Foundations of Evidence-Based Data Utilization in Healthcare

### 2.1 Definitions and Key Concepts

Understanding certain concepts is necessary for establishing evidence-based use of data in healthcare. Using evidence-based practice (EBP) means experts apply and evaluate the most reliable evidence available to craft individual medical decisions and healthcare strategies [6]. By using clinical expertise, patient values, and the latest research, it forms a team of three to direct medical care. Here, the primary use of strong and reliable data is important for healthcare decisions about patients, programs, and the organization. In healthcare, data can be organized into categories like clinical data, administrative data, patient-reported results, and evidence collected from the health of a population [7]. Clinical data focuses on facts obtained from interactions with patients, for example, disease conditions, lab tests, various medical images, and everything related to their treatment. Both types of data include operational and billing information that help us see how healthcare services are used, their costs, and how resources are managed. Health information reported by patients through wearables or their personal accounts provides more details that improve the understanding of their health. Studies in the real world are furthered by gathering information from large databases and tracking changes over a long period and in many people. Data quality is essential for proper use of data. Quality data is expected to be accurate, complete, prompt, and consistent. Without them, any findings or advice could be questionable and might result in incorrect decisions [8]. Because of this, data governance frameworks must put standards in place for collecting, saving, using, and protecting data to ensure it is trustworthy. Data analytics plays a major role as the practice of analyzing data to recognize patterns, draw conclusions, and create plans for action. In the field of healthcare, analytics cover descriptive, predictive, and prescriptive methods. Descriptive analytics is concerned with reviewing information from the past, predictive analytics is interested in predicting what may happen, and prescriptive analytics proposes steps to improve the future [9]. Merging analytics into CDSS has greatly enhanced the use of data insights at the time of patient care. In conclusion, effective use of data is only possible with strong interoperability of systems. Interoperability means that several different systems, devices, and applications can use data with each other. It is widely acknowledged that when data systems cannot communicate, it prevents the full use of data and affects its accuracy [10].

### 2.2 Evolution of Data Utilization in Healthcare Practice

Improvements in healthcare data use are based on new technologies, better approaches, and cultural changes. In the beginning, healthcare information was managed by hand-written charts, making it tough to obtain and examine complete history. When standards were not the same and information was spread out, doctors had trouble providing the same level of care each time. At the end of the twentieth century, the introduction of electronic health records (EHRs) made it much easier to handle, store, and share medical records. In the early days, EHRs were mainly for recording data, which left most information unused because it was not analyzed. With the help of protocols such as ICD and lab data standards, secondary uses of EHR data, such as quality measurement and research, became more practical over time [11]. Improved capabilities for HIE networks allowed patient data to be more widely shared, so doctors and other health professionals had access to better patient records. Integrating HL7 and FHIR made it easier to share data, and thus prepared the foundation for integrated analytics in care programs. Healthcare data now benefit greatly from new processing and utilization technologies, such as big data, AI, and machine learning. Through artificial intelligence, algorithms can look at both organized and unorganized data from several sources and spot complex relationships that are too hard for people to notice. For example, we now use predictive models to identify patients at risk of deterioration, allocate resources efficiently, and adapt the way we treat each patient [12]. Furthermore, data coming from wearable devices, mobile health apps, and home monitoring systems makes it possible to obtain data all the time. This increases the scope of health records and supplies a more complete view of patient well-being at home. The adoption of data from daily life is influencing healthcare, so doctors focus more on preventing diseases and managing chronic cases. Beginning with the digitization of data and continuing to data integration and standardization, advanced analytics, and ultimately using real-time and patient-centered data in decision-making, there is a phased track followed in data utilization. ### 2.3 Principles of Evidence-Based Medicine and Decision-Making

EBM is the process of combining proven medical evidence, what healthcare professionals know, and what patients want to create treatment decisions. Evidence-based data utilization is built on EBM principles, which determine the best ways to interpret and use data in healthcare [13]. Firstly, we should generate valid evidence and then examine it closely. We should ensure that data is taken, examined, and interpreted using reliable methods to help avoid bias. This means that patients should first know that systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials are the most trustworthy, then observational studies, and finally expert opinion. When using data, this rule says data sources and methods should be open and properly verified by colleagues. It also focuses on how clinicians use their judgment in light of available evidence. Despite having strong data and evidence, a trained doctor must look at each patient cautiously to come to a conclusion. To use it well, a healthcare professional should be able to apply evidence to suit the specific requirements of each patient. Decisions should take into account what is important to the patient. It is understood that letting patients help choose their care and treatment takes into account their goals and experiences. People need to receive these data-driven insights in a way that helps them understand and decide for themselves.

The use of evidence-based recommendations has improved with the design of CDSS. They analyze patient information with clinical guidelines and models to support quick choices by doctors. CDSS need to help rather than replace what doctors decide, and their recommendations have to be constantly updated with recent evidence [14]. The basis of evidence-based data use is constant re-evaluation and improvements. With the rapidly changing nature of healthcare, continuous data acquisition and analysis are required for monitoring, noticing issues, and optimizing ways of working. This idea is present in healthcare settings where diagnosis and treatment methods are constantly changing due to feedback loops created by learning health systems [15]. Evidence-based data use in healthcare is based on a well-defined framework focused on the importance of good data, solid analysis, and uniting evidence with medical knowledge and patient concerns. Advances in technology and culture have gradually allowed healthcare organizations to use data in new ways. It is important to know and practice evidence-based medicine to make the most of healthcare data.

## 3 Sources and Types of Healthcare Data

Healthcare organizations depend on various kinds of data to support decisions, organize their work, and manage the health of the population. To make the most of data-driven methods, it is essential to know what these datasets involve and how they develop. In this section, we talk about the major sources of healthcare data, such as clinical, administrative, patient-generated, public health, and new data streams.

### 3.1 Clinical Data from Electronic Health Records (EHRs)

Information gathered from EHRs forms the main foundation of healthcare informatics in modern medical settings. Information saved in EHRs includes personal details, symptoms, healthcare plans, test results, drug charts, and pictures from scans. The data is arranged and predefined in such a way that different software can use and analyze it. The HITECH Act and similar policies strongly contributed to the development of EHR systems over the past 20 years [16]. EHR systems hold clinically useful information that can be used in CDSS, models for predicting outcomes, and programs for quality assessments. In spite of their usefulness, EHRs often include imperfections, including coding inconsistencies, missing data, and data entry errors, which need to be resolved [17]. With the help of NLP, health centers are now able to get useful information from EHRs such as clinical narratives and unstructured data from physician notes and radiology reports. The use of both structured and unstructured data makes it easier to identify and manage risks in patients [18].

### 3.2 Administrative and Operational Data

Administrative and operational data include information collected from the business and operational tasks in healthcare groups. The data involve details about billing systems, claims, planning staff schedules, keeping track of supplies, and using facilities. Although numerous people think of clinical information as primary, administrative data is crucial for looking at finances, resources, and compliance. Patient data, as a resource, offer a wide range of information that includes details from their care in different places. They are commonly utilized in health services research, cost analysis, and to check healthcare differences [19].
