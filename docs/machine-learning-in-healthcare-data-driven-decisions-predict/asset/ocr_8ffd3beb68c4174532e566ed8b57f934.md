The penalty score is computed as

\( P_i = \varepsilon_i + \alpha \cdot C_i \)

where \(\alpha\) is a tunable regularization coefficient that controls how much the model penalizes complexity over error. This formulation ensures that base learners that are both accurate and simple receive preference in the final ensemble, while overly complex or unstable classifiers are downweighted, even if their raw error is low.

Once penalty scores are computed for all learners in the current boosting round, the ensemble weight (\(\beta_i\)) for each learner is inversely proportional to its penalty score

\( \beta_i = \frac{1}{P_i + \delta} \)

Here, \(\delta\) is a small constant added to avoid division by zero. This scheme means that even a model with a slightly higher error but much lower complexity may contribute more to the final prediction ensemble than a complex learner with marginally better accuracy.

A more balanced and understandable ensemble is produced by HRAB's regularization-aware weighting method. Applying this new penalty mechanism consistently enhanced generalization measures in internal tests on the CKD dataset (approximately 500 cases and 20 features after preprocessing). For instance, in cases with noisy or overlapping class boundaries, HRAB performed better than even gradient boosting methods such as regular AdaBoost (accuracy ~94.6%) with accuracy 98.1%, precision 97.8%, and \(F_1\)-score 97.9%.

Additionally, the group intentionally selects weakly biased but consistent models in preference to highly erratic ones that could overfit to the training distribution, based on the bias-variance tradeoff principle. In the medical context, in which incomplete and unbalanced data is a common constraint, this proves very beneficial. HRAB imposes simplicity and enhances the robustness of predictions on unseen patient data by incorporating structural penalties directly into the ensemble learning cycle. This is an essential requirement for deployment in real clinical decision support systems.
