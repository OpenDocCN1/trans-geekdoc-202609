# 1 Introduction

Mental disorders, particularly depression and anxiety, have emerged as significant public health problems around the world. More than 300 million people worldwide are projected to have depression by the WHO [1], while anxiety disorders afflict an equally large number of people. The conditions extend beyond personal distress, causing immense economic and social costs to healthcare systems, workplaces, and communities. Mental disorders contribute more disability-adjusted life years, more suicides, and reduced quality of life among the patients. Indirect costs such as productivity loss, absence, and lower efficiency at the workplace also add to the added world burden of such diseases [2].

Mental illnesses such as depression and anxiety are also very difficult to diagnose because measurement of symptoms is subjective in nature [3]. Compared to physical disease that can often be diagnosed through objective laboratory testing or imaging, mental health evaluation is based largely on clinical interview, self-report questionnaire, and behavioral observation. These traditional diagnostic techniques, while unavoidable, have some limitations in and of themselves. They are subject to reporting bias, social desirability, and differences in symptom expression by culture. Moreover, symptoms of depression and anxiety tend to overlap with one another and with other medical or mental illnesses, complicating differential diagnosis [4]. The episodic course of the disorders and users' underreporting due to stigma further hinder early and correct detection. Beyond clinical complexity, mental health data itself is complex in nature, rendering it difficult to tap through conventional statistical methods.

Mental health data sets are usually high-dimensional, asymmetric class distributed, and consist of mixtures of structured (e.g., demographic data and survey responses) and unstructured data types [5]. Furthermore, confidentiality of patient information and the sensitive nature of mental health data limit access to large, diverse data sets, contributing to the challenge of analyzing large samples and generalizing.
