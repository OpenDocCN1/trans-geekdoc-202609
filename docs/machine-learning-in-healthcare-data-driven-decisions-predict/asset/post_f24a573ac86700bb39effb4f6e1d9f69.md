
The landscape of ML and DL techniques for healthcare predictive analytics was reviewed in an orderly fashion in early studies, showcasing its increased use in diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment planning [1]. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), and autoencoders have been contrasted with methods like decision trees, Support Vector Machines (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and ensemble learning in various clinical fields. The majority of the studies document issues with model interpretability and generalizability, even with impressive performance on laboratory tests. Similarly, even though the supervised learning models perform exceptionally well with accuracy, their ranking on labeled and structured data restricts them in scalability on actual healthcare settings, based on a particular review on the predictive healthcare role of ML [2].

There have been different attempts to develop big data and ML-based disease prediction models. There was a model that was uploaded to use large datasets in predicting chronic diseases that was very promising when it used ensemble classifiers, but was plagued by feature sparsity and class imbalance [3]. This was supplemented by others who combined ML with m-Health principles, highlighting real-time analysis in patient engagement and remote monitoring and the importance of integration and data protection issues [4]. Real-time prediction systems were created to determine the onset of conditions like diabetes and cardiovascular diseases in a bid to pinpoint early onset [5]. Two critical elements of clinical adoption, interpretability and generalizability across populations, are frequently overlooked in these articles.

A detailed review of big data analytics in medicine detailed a number of architectures for applying ML pipelines to filter, store, and analyze vast amounts of clinical data [6]. Although these architectures improve scalability and computational efficiency, they tend to compromise clinical interpretability and patient-specific adjustability of ML models. Based on real-world electronic health record data, another study introduced an effective implementation of predictive analytics in a bariatric surgery scenario [7]. Although the ML models utilized here had promise for risk classification, with up to 85% accuracy, their clinical utility was diminished by the observations of data standardization and explainability flaws.

The role of ML in population health management has been explored to identify how vital it is in determining high-risk patients and allocating resources as efficiently as possible [8]. Such models are great at predicting at the population level but tend to fall short of the specificity required for individualized treatment. Using random forest and neural networks, a heart health predictive data analysis technique, more specific to cardiovascular risk, was extremely accurate [9]. However, because feature extraction varied by EHR platforms, it could not be scaled. The need for the interpretation of contextual data and the shortcoming of statistical models in isolation require working with noisy or missing clinical records. This has also been recognized in research into brain ML for clinical predictive analytics [10].

The capability of neural networks to reveal hidden patterns in imaging and genomic information has been demonstrated by various DL-based studies [11]. Although highly precise, these methods are frequently computationally costly, data-hungry, and black box in nature. Other research have explored how big data analytics and ML are transforming precision medicine through enabling personalization of diagnosis and treatment plans [12]. The majority of studies are still far from addressing model repeatability, bias minimization, and regulatory compliance adequately. Studies on healthcare analytics were characterized by theoretical underpinnings and applied practice in different domains of diseases in a systematic review [13]. They emphasized the requirement for strong unsupervised and semi-supervised approaches that can utilize unlabeled clinical data, along with highlighting the dominance of supervised ML approaches.

Research has also attempted to use AI and ML in deriving predictive healthcare insights from big data [14], and it has been shown that current models tend to have limited clinical feedback loops, are not scalable, and do not address missing data concerns. Although ML model integration into clinical processes is still in its infancy, another study on healthcare management highlighted the need for process improvement using analytics [15]. Numerous studies have investigated the use of ML in detecting diseases earlier through data-driven approaches; though it makes accurate predictions, real-time implementation is complicated [16].

Furthermore, multimodal affective computing has been reported to enhance emotional intelligence in medical AI systems [17], which is important in patient engagement and mental wellbeing. Both the speed of algorithmic innovation and nonalignment of research and bedside deployment were emphasized in a review of predictive analytics ML and DL technologies [18]. Likewise, research examined deployment patterns and real-world difficulties in ML-based healthcare, i.e., clinician pushback, data silos, and model drift [19]. Healthcare ML books and anthologies offered more comprehensive insights through the combination of case studies and applied model frameworks [20].

These texts highlight that adoption continues to be restricted by usability, infrastructure, and ethical concerns in spite of technical capabilities improving. Other studies [21] thought about the use of AI and big data analysis in m-Health platforms, including mobile devices for ongoing health tracking and issues of privacy, connectivity, and battery limitation. With a demand for interpretable models and transparent pipelines, there was a systematic review of applications of ML and DL, highlighting how these technologies were revolutionizing diagnostics and clinical decision-making [22]. Although the problems it raised for heterogeneity in image acquisition and annotation, digital pathology studies with ML platforms showed potential in automated disease detection [23]. The tardy shift to clinical-grade from research prototype was also mentioned in survey reports on big data analytics for healthcare, which aggregated the trends and technologies utilized [24].

The literature under study, in general, shows a wide range of approaches, tools, and frameworks established over the years to incorporate data analytics and ML into healthcare. In disease prediction, risk assessment, personal therapy, and management of healthcare resources, it is clear that ML and DL have shown promise. Numerous studies uniformly point out the following shared weaknesses: poor clinical adoption strategies, poor scalabilities in institutions, multimodal data integration difficulties, and lack of interpretability. Despite the usually outstanding performance within research settings, it is still difficult to transfer these observations into practical, reasonable healthcare applications. Furthermore, the critical requirements of explainability and trust in sensitive clinical settings are not yet adequately met by most systems.

The rationale supporting the proposed system rests on these said shortcomings. A next-generation architecture that not only harmoniously combines multimodal data but also provides interpretability, robustness, and responsiveness to various clinical settings is obviously in need. A technically valid but therapeutically effective, comprehensible, and individualized and scalable decision-making guidance-giving system would constitute a significant contribution to the literature. Based on the strengths of the current literature and filling its gaps, the suggested method is intended to adequately address such limitations.

## 3 System Methodology

### 3.1 Dataset Description

Using electronic health data (EHR) from the MIMIC-III (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III) database with chest X-ray image information from the NIH ChestX-ray14 dataset, the proposed approach utilizes a combined dataset containing both structured and unstructured health data. More than 58,000 hospital stays of 38,597 adults (16 years and older) and 7,870 neonates during 2001 through 2012 are represented in the MIMIC-III database. Clinical data like vital signs, prescription medications, lab results, chart notes, and demographics are all incorporated. Labeled with a maximum of 14 disease categories, including cardiomegaly, emphysema, pneumonia, and infiltration, the NIH ChestX-ray14 dataset consists of 112,120 frontal-view X-ray images from 30,805 unique patients. Natural Language Processing (NLP) is employed to assign each image a name, where its resolution is 1,024 × 1,024 and is applied in radiology reports. The ChestXray14 high-dimensional pixel data and the MIMIC-III structured tabular data together offer a multimodal dataset that enables visual, diagnostic, as well as temporal trends for advanced predictive analytics.

### 3.2 Data Preprocessing

Two modes of preprocessing are employed: image data and clinical tabular data. For MIMIC-III, a hybrid imputation approach is employed to deal with missing variables.
