
where \( f_{LGBM} \) is the trained model that outputs class probabilities through an ensemble of decision trees. The probability vector \(\hat{p}\) is transformed using a learnable linear transformation to create a dense embedding vector, which is then used to integrate the ML output with DL components:

\( Z_{ml} = \sigma(W_{ml}\hat{p} + b_{ml}) \in \mathbb{R}^k \) (13)

where \( W_{ml} \in \mathbb{R}^{k*C} \) is the learnable weight matrix, \( b_{ml} \in \mathbb{R}^k \) is the bias term, and \( \sigma \) is the activation function (ReLU or tanh). In the hybrid feature fusion layer, the resultant vector \( Z_{ml} \in \mathbb{R}^k \) becomes the conventional ML-derived latent representation, prepared for concatenation.

### 3.3.4 Hybrid Feature Fusion Layer

In hybrid feature fusion, the embeddings are computed across three heterogeneous pathways, \( Z_{img} \) from image data processing through CNN, \( Z_{clin} \) from clinical relation modeling through GNN, and \( z_{ml} \) from vanilla ML pipeline and these are fused together into a holistic composite vector, represented as \( z_{fused} \). The combined representation employs complementary data modalities and combines spatial, relational, and hand-designed features in a shared latent space. The subsequent vector is then input to a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with dropout regularization and batch normalization. Dropout avoids overfitting by randomly removing neurons during training, and batch normalization stabilizes and accelerates training by normalizing layer inputs. This hybridization technique of merging various forms of features allows the network to integrate various forms of features in a seamless manner to generate stronger and more precise predictions for risk stratification and outcome prediction of function.

\( Z_{\text{fused}} = [Z_{\text{img}} || Z_{\text{clin}} || Z_{\text{ml}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 + d_2 + d_3} \quad (17) \)

Next, a MLP with dropout and batch normalization is applied to this fused vector:

where \( W_1, W_2 \) are the weight matrices, \( b_1, b_2 \) are the biases, \( BN_1, BN_2 \) are the batch normalization functions, and \( p \) is the dropout probability. During the multitask learning phase, final predictions are made using this MLP output \( h_2 \)

### 3.3.5 Multitask Learning

The proposed architecture features a two-task learning process between two task-specific output layers which have the capability of optimizing two clinically useful goals concurrently. Risk stratification is associated with the first output layer that specifies low, medium, or high risk levels for the patients through a softmax activation. This is able to enable the model to produce class probabilities so class probabilities may be outputted by the model for more understandable risk estimations. The second output layer for mRS score prediction is handled as an ordinal regression task to honor the ordered nature of the mRS. Methods like CORAL (cumulative ordinal regression) or deep ordinal regression are used to learn from this ordinal output in an efficient way. The ultimate training goal is guided by an aggregate loss function, expressed as follows:

\( \mathcal{L} = \alpha . \mathcal{L}_{\text{risk}} + \beta . \mathcal{L}_{\text{outcome}} \quad (21) \)

Here, \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) are hyperparameters that can be tuned to adjust the two-task contribution. The model is capable of leveraging shared representations and task-specific learning at the same time by optimizing the two tasks jointly, and accordingly the better generalization and prediction performance will be achieved for risk classification and functional outcome prediction [27].

### 3.3.6 Model Integration and Output

At the last phase of integration, learned representations of three different model pieces \( z_{\text{img}} \) (embedding image-derived), \( z_{\text{clin}} \) (graph-based embedding clinical), and \( z_{\text{ML}} \) (regular ML-derived embedding) are fused together to construct a unified single vector reflecting a patient's total health profile. The aggregated feature vector holds information about spatial patterns of images, inter-patient clinical connections, and structured domain-knowledge aware features. The joint representation is fed into a multilayer feedforward neural network, with the objective to carry out dual-task learning. The first task is risk stratification, posed as a multiclass classification task to predict low, medium, or high risk of bad outcomes. The second task is prediction of functional outcome, represented as an ordinal regression task to target the mRS, an ordered scale from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death). This unified framework allows the model to learn both common patterns across tasks as well as task-specific features, which improves its predictive performance and generalizability in clinical applications.

## 4 Results and Findings

Performance of the hybrid model is strictly tested on stratified fivefold cross-validation so that there is an even class distribution of outcomes for training and validation splits. In the task of risk stratification, performance is measured with AUC-ROC, a threshold-independent assessment of the capacity of the model to discriminate between classes, and $F_1$-score, which optimizes precision and recall to express classification accuracy in imbalanced scenarios. For predicting mRS score, mean absolute error (MAE) measures the average size of prediction errors, indicating precision of the model in functional outcome estimation. QWK also assesses the concordance between predicted and real mRS scores considering ordinal scale and penalizing greater discrepancies more. Overall, these measures supply a complete valuation of the model’s discriminability, predictive performance, and clinician usability.

The suggested hybrid model and four other methods are among the five models whose quantitative performance comparison is shown in Table 1. It uses four primary metrics to assess each model:

- **AUC-ROC (risk stratification)**

It assesses how well the model can differentiate across classifications such as low and high risk. The true positive rate (TPR) versus false positive rate (FPR) is given by

\( \text{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN} \quad (22) \)

The ROC curve’s integral, or AUC, is usually calculated mathematically (e.g., by applying the trapezoidal rule).

- **$F_1$-score (risk stratification):** It strikes a balance between classification accuracy and recall (used here for risk categorization). It is computed as follows:

\( F_1\text{-score} = 2 * \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} \quad (23) \)

where the precision and recall is given as follows:

\( \text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}, \text{Recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}} \quad (24) \)

- Mean absolute error (mRS prediction): It calculates the average absolute error between the mRS scores that were anticipated and those that were obtained:

\( \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i| \quad (25) \)

where \( y_i \) is the actual mRS score, \( \hat{y}_i \) is the predicted mRS score, and \( n \) is the number of samples.

- QWK (mRS prediction): It compares the ground truth to a model or two raters’ agreement for ordinal classifications such as mRS scores:

\( k = 1 - \frac{\sum_{ij} w_{ij} O_{ij}}{\sum_{ij} w_{ij} E_{ij}} \quad (26) \)

where \( O_{i,j} \) is the observed agreement matrix, \( E_{i,j} \) is the expected agreement matrix, and \( w_{i,j} \) is the quadratic weight and it is specified as follows:

\( w_{i,j} = \frac{(i - j)^2}{(k - 1)^2} \quad (27) \)

As an example, mRS scores from 0 to 6 \(\rightarrow k = 7\), where \( k \) is the number of categories. The QWK scale goes from -1 (total disagreement) to 1 (perfect agreement), where 0 denotes agreement at the chance level.
