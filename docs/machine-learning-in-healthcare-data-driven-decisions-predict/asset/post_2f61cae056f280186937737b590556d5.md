The consideration of several physiological parameters not only enhances the reliability and correctness of stress prediction models but also makes it easier to design personalized stress management systems. Such systems may learn to adjust according to individual baselines and give dynamic thresholds for alerting and intervention.

In brief, the chapter presents a holistic method of stress detection and prediction with the help of ML, accompanied by an extensive range of physiological, behavioral, and biometric measures. ## 2 Literature Review

### 2.1 Continuous Stress Detection Using a Wrist Device: In Laboratory and Real Life

Stress is a widespread health issue that has important physical and mental health consequences. Conventional stress measurement techniques – self-reports and clinical interviews – are subjective and not well-suited for real-time or long-term monitoring. Advances in wearable technology and the miniaturization of physiological sensors have made objective, continuous, and noninvasive stress monitoring possible, which has opened the door for ML-based techniques. The quality and diversity of input data directly affect the performance of stress detection models.

Physiological Signals: Heart rate (HR), heart rate variability (HRV), electrodermal activity (EDA/GSR), body temperature, blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), and respiration rate are some of the most frequently used.

Behavioral and Contextual Information: Sleep, limb and eye movement, and activity rate supply more context to stress prediction. Wearable devices: The inclusion of wearable sensors (e.g., Empatica E4, Fitbit, and Oura Ring) has facilitated continuous real-time data capture both in controlled and free-living settings. Feature engineering, such as time-domain and frequency-domain feature extraction, is crucial in enhancing model performance. Sophisticated models increasingly make use of both domains to have a complete overview of the stress response.

### 2.2 Stress Recognition Using Wearable Sensors and Mobile Phones

Over the last few years, the combination of wearable sensors and mobile devices has received important attention for unobtrusive stress detection and long-term health monitoring. Various studies have investigated physiological signals such as HR, HRV, EDA, skin temperature, and respiration rate, which are captured through wearable devices such as smartwatches, chest straps, and wristbands. The authors showed the viability of integrating accelerometer and EDA wristband data to identify stress incidents with high accuracy under real-world use. Smartphones, in contrast, add behavioral and environmental data – apps used, call/SMS history, ambient noise level, location – and these can reveal aspects of the user's day-to-day environment and life. They created a multimodal predictive model based on smartphone sensing and physiological signals to forecast stress levels using ML algorithms. Current methods increasingly utilize deep learning-based models such as CNNs and LSTMs, which have the ability to learn intricate temporal and spatial features automatically from multimodal data. There are, however, challenges in the areas of generalizability across individuals, privacy of data, and performance in actual-world settings. It is indicated in the current literature that the integration of psychological and behavioral characteristics – acquired passively through wearables and smartphones – has the potential to greatly improve the accuracy and usability of stress detection systems.

### 2.3 SELF-CARE: Selective Fusion with Context-Aware Low-Power Edge Computing for Stress Detection

Recent developments in wearable sensor technology and machine learning have greatly improved stress detection methods. The older statistical and rule-based techniques have been widely replaced by ML and deep learning (DL) methods, which are more able to capture the variability and complexity of physiological information. For example, a study presented an interpretable deep learning model that incorporated LSTM networks with deep generative ensembles (DGE) to identify stress from wearable sensor signals with better precision and recall compared to conventional methods.

Supervised learning methods, including SVM, RF, and GBM, are still used for stress classification purposes. Nonetheless, the use of deep learning models such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and LSTMs has proven to provide better performance in identifying intricate patterns from raw sensor data. One of the examples is the STREDWES approach, which transforms physiological signals into Gramian angular fields and applies CNNs for stress detection, which outperforms state-of-the-art models.

Hybrid approaches mixing more than one algorithm have also appeared, trying to build on each building block's advantages. For instance, the SELF-CARE system utilizes context-aware selective sensor fusion together with low-power edge computing for boosting stress detection accuracy without compromising on energy efficiency. Also, federated learning methods have been introduced to deal with privacy issues in stress detection, supporting decentralized model training on the wearable devices without exposing raw data. These advances reflect an important trend toward advanced, precise, and privacy-conscientious stress detection systems made possible by the interplay of wearable technology and advanced ML algorithms.

### 2.4 Monitoring and Detection of Psychological Stress with the Help of Machine Learning (ML)

This has come a long way due to the widespread use of wearable sensors, mobile devices, and the demand for real-time, noninvasive mental health monitoring. From 2022 to 2025, many studies have shown the feasibility of using ML algorithms in the reliable prediction of stress levels from multimodal physiological and behavioral data.

Vos et al. [9] performed a systematic review examining the generalizability of ML-stress models based on wearable data. They emphasized that while high accuracy is attained with ML methods such as RF, SVM, and k-nearest neighbors (KNN), most models have poor generalization as a result of inconsistencies in the datasets, label variability, and limited sample sizes. The authors stressed the importance of standardized benchmarking and reliable cross-validation methods to enhance deployment in real-world environments.

In a broad scoping review, Razavi et al. [10] surveyed more than 90 studies that used ML and DL in the detection of stress and mental disorders. They indicated that deep neural networks, particularly LSTM and CNN, are superior to conventional ML methods when ample time-series data is present. Their work also highlighted the value of data preprocessing operations (e.g., feature scaling, removal of artifacts) and multimodal input (heart rate, skin conductance, respiration, behavioral indicators) for improving the performance and interpretability of the model.

### 2.5 Physiological Signal-Based Approaches

Physiological signals like HR, HRV, EDA, respiration rate, and electroencephalography (EEG) have been extensively explored with regards to stress correlation. The authors investigated the detection of stress with HRV and EDA data acquired from wearable devices in a simulated laboratory environment [5,6]. They used various classifiers, such as SVM, RF, and logistic regression. The RF classifier performed better than the others, with 90% accuracy. Signal preprocessing – more so noise removal and normalization – was stressed by the study as crucial to enhancing the reliability of the classifiers. This study also concentrated on real-time stress detection from raw ECG signals. They introduced a deep CNN model that automatically extracted features from time series without human engineering. Their model had a classification accuracy of 93.2% and also showed excellent generalizability across various datasets (WESAD and SWELL-KW). Interestingly, CNN was superior to conventional models because it could extract refined and hierarchical features from ECG data, including variability in QRS complex and ST-segment changes, which are associated with sympathetic activation.

### 2.6 Behavioral Signal Integration-Based Approaches

Whereas moment-to-moment information comes from physiological signals, behavioral indicators such as sleep patterns, movement rhythm, eye movement, and snoring provide longitudinal information regarding stress trends. The researchers designed a multimodal stress detection system incorporating physiological signals (HR, EDA) together with behavioral patterns (sleep duration, movement abnormalities, and activity level). Through GBM, their system registered an _F_<sub>1</sub>-score of 91.8%. The research proved that including behavioral data helps minimize false positives by providing context – e.g., distinguishing between an increase in HR during exercise vs. emotional stress [7]. The study utilized actigraphy data and smartphone log sensor data to assess daily behavior patterns. Using XGBoost on ambient noise, screen time, and physical activity features, they attained 88% accuracy. In their work, behavioral drift within days can be an early warning sign of burnout or chronic stress.

### 2.7 Deep Learning and Hybrid Architectures

Deep learning structures have unlocked new potential in the modeling of nonlinear and high-dimensional stress signals. They are capable of learning short-term fluctuations as well as long-term dependencies within multimodal streams of data. The study introduced a hybrid CNN-LSTM model that is trained on combined EEG, EDA, and accelerometer data from the AMIGOS dataset. CNN layers were employed to learn spatial features, and LSTM learned temporal dependencies [8]. The model had 94.6% accuracy and 93.8% recall. Their result indicated that hierarchical combination of signals improves the stability of predictions, particularly with noisy or missing data.

Authors presented a multi-head attention-based transformer network to detect stress from physiological time-series data. Their approach enhanced explainability by determining which components of the signal (e.g., HR spikes or EDA troughs) were primarily responsible for the stress classification outcome.
