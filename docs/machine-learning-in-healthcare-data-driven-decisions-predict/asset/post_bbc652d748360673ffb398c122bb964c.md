## Machine Learning Applications in Mental Health: Ensemble-Based Predictive Modeling for Depression and Anxiety detection

Abstract: Psychological diseases such as depression and anxiety are prevalent globally, and thus there is a need for advanced detection techniques to ensure maximum clinical interventions and patient care. Traditional machine learning techniques, such as random forest, support vector machines, and logistic regression, although efficient, are at the cost of interpretability, computational performance, and predictability in handling advanced mental health data. In this research work, triple neuro-probabilistic ensemble (TNPE) is proposed to be a breakthrough ensemble model appropriate for depression and anxiety detection. TNPE leverages three foundation learners with complementing strengths: extreme learning machine for speed and generalization overall on small-to-medium-sized datasets; probabilistic neural network for probabilistic decision-making in a clinical setting; and CatBoost, specifically crafted for dealing with categorical and mixed-type data. The models are blended via an optimal soft voting ensemble mechanism driven by adaptive weight optimization with particle swarm optimization. The model was trained on a multisource clinical record, mental health surveys, and social media text dataset preprocessed and balanced with methods such as ADASYN. Feature selection was carried out using the Boruta algorithm. TNPE was tested with 10-fold cross-validation and performed better than standard classifiers like k-nearest neighbors, decision tree, Naive Bayes, multilayer perceptron, gradient boosting machine, and AdaBoost. TNPE performed better on critical metrics: ROC-AUC (0.963), F1-score (0.927), and accuracy (92.6%). Interpretability enhanced by LIME and SHAP, TNPE became a clinical acceptable decision-making system for diagnosing mental health.

Keywords: depression and anxiety detection, ensemble learning, extreme learning machine (ELM), probabilistic neural network (PNN), mental health prediction

## 1 Introduction

Mental disorders, particularly depression and anxiety, have emerged as significant public health problems around the world. More than 300 million people worldwide are projected to have depression by the WHO [1], while anxiety disorders afflict an equally large number of people. The conditions extend beyond personal distress, causing immense economic and social costs to healthcare systems, workplaces, and communities. Mental disorders contribute more disability-adjusted life years, more suicides, and reduced quality of life among the patients. Indirect costs such as productivity loss, absence, and lower efficiency at the workplace also add to the added world burden of such diseases [2].

Mental illnesses such as depression and anxiety are also very difficult to diagnose because measurement of symptoms is subjective in nature [3]. Compared to physical disease that can often be diagnosed through objective laboratory testing or imaging, mental health evaluation is based largely on clinical interview, self-report questionnaire, and behavioral observation. These traditional diagnostic techniques, while unavoidable, have some limitations in and of themselves. They are subject to reporting bias, social desirability, and differences in symptom expression by culture. Moreover, symptoms of depression and anxiety tend to overlap with one another and with other medical or mental illnesses, complicating differential diagnosis [4]. The episodic course of the disorders and users' underreporting due to stigma further hinder early and correct detection. Beyond clinical complexity, mental health data itself is complex in nature, rendering it difficult to tap through conventional statistical methods.

Mental health data sets are usually high-dimensional, asymmetric class distributed, and consist of mixtures of structured (e.g., demographic data and survey responses) and unstructured data types [5]. (e.g., social media entries and clinical notes). Furthermore, confidentiality of patient information and the sensitive nature of mental health data limit access to large, diverse data sets, contributing to the challenge of analyzing large samples and generalizing. Despite these challenges, enhanced access to electronic health records, mobile health applications, and internet-based behavioral data have provided opportunities for computational models to facilitate mental health assessment and prediction. The past few years have been opened up for improving mental healthcare due to the arrival of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) techniques [6].

These technologies hold the promise of identifying hidden patterns, trends, and associations in advanced, multimodal data sets often overlooked in conventional analyses. ML techniques have been applied with significant success in a wide range of fields of mental health research including the prediction of suicide risk, the identification of post-traumatic stress disorder [7], the detection of substance use disorders, and the screening of depression and anxiety from textual or behavioral data. These models can assist clinicians in decision-making, enhance early detection methods, and provide scalable techniques for population-level mental health screening.

Furthermore, confidentiality concerns patients and the sensitive character of mental health data limit access to large, diverse data sets, contributing to the challenge of having to analyze large samples and make generalization. Despite these challenges, greater access to electronic health records, mobile health applications, and online behavioral data have provided avenues for computational models to facilitate assessment and prediction of mental health. The last few years have provided new windows of opportunity to improve mental health healthcare, courtesy of the arrival of AI and ML approaches. Random forest (RF) [8], support vector machine (SVM) [9], logistic regression (LR) [10], and deep boosters like XGBoost [11] and LightGBM [12] are few of the most commonly applied ML algorithms for predicting mental health. These models have exhibited decent performance in diagnosing mental health disorders when trained on both clinical and non-clinical data.

However, several challenges exist in the proper utilization of these models in mental health informatics. Numerous current models fail to address class imbalance, a typical problem in medical datasets where positive instances of mental disorder may be fewer than healthy controls. In addition, interpretability of sophisticated ML models continues to be an issue of major concern, especially in medicine, where it is essential to have transparent and interpretable decision-support tools in order to make their responsible usage possible and gain confidence in AI-driven recommendations. More importantly, most current research in this area have been dedicated to applying single classifiers or standard ensemble methods like RFs.

Although these approaches have some benefits in predictive performance and stability, they might not be as flexible as required to deal with the heterogeneous nature of mental health data, which tends to have a mix of categorical, numerical, and textual features. More scalable, flexible, and interpretable ML architecture is yet to be developed. The objectives of this study are as follows:

- To create, deploy, and test a novel ensemble-based prediction model framework to detect depression and anxiety by integrating heterogeneous ML algorithms with experience in dealing with complex and heterogeneous mental health data.
- To analyze the proposed ensemble framework against existing benchmark models using various prediction performance metrics so that accuracy, robustness, and reliability could be ensured during mental health classification tasks.
