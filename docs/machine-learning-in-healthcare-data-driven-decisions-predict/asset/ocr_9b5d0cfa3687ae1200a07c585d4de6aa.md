# 10 Synthesis of Use Case Outcomes

In CDSS, ML has improved early disease detection, treatment recommendations, and patient monitoring. Ensemble learning has effectively reduced adverse drug interactions, while reinforcement learning has facilitated personalized therapy planning. Anomaly detection models have supported ICU monitoring with real-time alerts, thereby minimizing response latency in critical scenarios (Table 1).

The practical application of ML models in healthcare has led to promising outcomes across various domains. For disease diagnosis, models such as deep neural networks and random forests have demonstrated superior accuracy levels of 95% and 91%, respectively, particularly in tasks like medical imaging and cancer classification. Logistic regression and decision trees, though less complex, offer higher interpretability and are better suited for diseases such as diabetes and heart conditions due to their logical decision boundaries and low training requirements (see Table 2).

Explainable AI methods, such as SHAP and LIME, provide critical transparency to model predictions, enhancing user trust and clinical interpretability. SHAP has emerged as a reliable framework for drug response modeling, while LIME has proven useful for local interpretability in structured EHR data (Table 3).

The results highlight that the application of ML in healthcare can significantly enhance accuracy, efficiency, and decision-making transparency. However, model selection remains context-dependent. High-performing models, such as deep neural networks, are data-intensive and often lack interpretability, which raises concerns in sensitive use cases. Consequently, hybrid approaches that combine interpretability (e.g., decision trees) with high-performance models may provide a balanced solution.

Clinical deployment also requires consideration of training time, data availability, and regulatory standards. The integration of ML into CDSS has revealed key challenges, such as data heterogeneity, temporal data gaps, and reward function design, underscoring the need for robust data engineering and model validation [30].

Furthermore, explainability is no longer optional. As black-box models become integrated into clinical workflows, the demand for explainable AI has grown. Approaches such as SHAP and counterfactuals not only enhance trust in models but also assist clinicians in validating automated decisions. Overall, while ML holds the promise of transforming healthcare, its success depends on responsible deployment, continuous evaluation, and a human-in-the-loop paradigm.
