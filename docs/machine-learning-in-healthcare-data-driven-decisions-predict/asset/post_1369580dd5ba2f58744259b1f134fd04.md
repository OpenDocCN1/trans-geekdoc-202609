Pap smear image variability hinders classification. Contextual information, including background artifacts or clustering patterns, is as relevant as morphological characteristics such as cell size, shape, texture, and nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio. Fractional coefficients and machine learning classifiers were used in a research study to screen for cervical cancer; these tools produced more accurate results with balanced datasets, but they failed to generalize to larger and more heterogeneous samples [4]. The use of whole slide images (WSIs) and liquid-based cytology was a significant innovation that supported mass learning and noise elimination, as illustrated by models that prioritize AI-aided screening across entire specimens [5].

Ensemble learning techniques are now more commonly used as a solution for dealing with variability in features at varying magnifications and imaging conditions. Through the utilization of numerous deep models, one ensemble technique showed higher robustness and classification performance on cytology images [6]. Similarly, CervixFuzzyFusion improved cervical cell image classification sensitivity and explainability through the combination of CNNs and fuzzy logic [7]. The study demonstrated the increased ability of AI for routine screening using application of precision AI-based models to classify cervical cytology grades with high accuracy [8].

Feature extraction is still a fundamental aspect. By employing a pyramid structure to preserve high-resolution details as well as learning representations at various levels of abstraction, exemplar pyramid deep feature extraction models have been promising [9]. In the meanwhile, graph convolutional networks are suggested to better encode the spatial interaction of cells [10]. These methodologies highlight the significance of not just being aware of the properties of a single cell but also cell contact and topological relationship between cells.

Multimodal systems are utilized to boost classification. There are multimodal models that incorporate imaging, pathology, and clinical data. An example is the enhanced multimodal fusion deep learning neural network used to diagnose lung cancer and provide results that can be used in the detection of cervical cancer [11]. In order to improve feature extraction and avoid background noise, some studies have focused on cell nuclei and cytoplasm separation and consequently improved the use of segmentation-based methods [12].

For the sake of learning from unbalanced and noisy data, there have been some techniques, such as CervixNet and its variants, which have applied attention mechanisms and hierarchical architectures [13]. Reviews also spoke on the significance of trust and explainability in computer-aided diagnosis, particularly in high-stakes applications like cancer detection [14]. More contemporary approaches like single-cell segmentation have also been fruitful in increasing the accuracy of classification, particularly when used in liquid-based cytology [15].

Light models and transfer learning are growing in popularity due to their capacity to offer state-of-the-art accuracy with low processing power. To speed up training and deliver higher accuracy, numerous models have used pre-trained models like VGG, ResNet, and EfficientNet and have fine-tuned them on cervical cytology datasets [16]. Aside from these light models, explainable AI (xAI) techniques are being applied to produce interpretable diagnosis outputs, raising the confidence level of physicians [17].

Recently, reinforcement learning architectures and multimodal data fusion have been established to individualize categorization models and therapy choices. These sophisticated systems dynamically learn from cytological input and patient data [18]. Transformer models have also been modified for cervical cancer classification in order to counteract dataset imbalance, using token-based representations and taming techniques to enhance generalization on minority classes [19]. Dimensionality reduction with preservation of discriminatory ability has also been demonstrated to be effective when deep feature selection techniques employ optimization techniques like principal component analysis and gray wolf optimization [20].

A massive hierarchical benchmark dataset for cervical cytology allowing systematic evaluation of classification algorithms has recently been introduced [21]. Additionally, research using cytology with pathology and colposcopy photographs suggests that deep learning methods can be used with a variety of diagnostic modalities [22]. Higher accuracy and robustness have also been facilitated by patch-based fusion models, advanced segmentation methods, and progressive learning-based approaches. Among them is CerMixer, which supports enhanced feature learning in cytology images by applying multi-scale depthwise convolution and patching [23].

Particularly in datasets that have overlapping cells and staining artifacts as issues, segmentation is the central theme in other studies as a prerequisite to precise categorization [24]. It is suggested that causal-learning-based CNN models such as CausalCervixNet should incorporate an interpretable layer within the decision process of the network in order to enable more precise diagnoses using cause-and-effect reasoning [25]. A systematic review on AI for cervical cancer diagnosis in future work suggests more diverse datasets, open model architectures, and coworking with clinicians [26].

Numerous problems continue to exist in spite of these developments. Many models are learned and evaluated against little or simulated sets that lack the diversity found in the real world. Others fail to generalize across varying cytology preparation techniques and imaging platforms, or they overfit with limited sample sizes. Furthermore, models tend to produce inferior classification results by failing to properly account for local morphological information as well as global context. Instituted by such limitations, the need for a unified model capable of learning hierarchical features, expressing cross-scale and cross-domain correlations, and coping with class imbalance is evident. To meet such limitations, this study introduces the HCFEN with the objective of integrating some of the advantages recognized in earlier studies, class-aware optimization, multimodal learning, hierarchical representations, and attention mechanisms into one framework for sound cervical cancer classification.

The use of deep learning has brought about a dramatic shift in the field of cervical cancer classification. From interpretable and multimodal AI models to handcrafted hybrid systems, and from standard CNNs to transformer-based and graph-based approaches, the field is constantly working to come up with more accurate, scalable, and clinically applicable solutions. The literature consulted shows that despite significant progress, models that are able to extract contextual and morphological features in a complete way, process imbalanced data, and provide interpretability for application in clinics are still urgently needed. To close these gaps and improve the state-of-the-art for cervical cytology image analysis, the HCFEN method was proposed.

## 3 System Methodology

### 3.1 Dataset Description

Herlev dataset, being one of the most widely used and freely available cytological imaging datasets for cervical cancer research, is employed to design and evaluate the proposed system for cervical cancer classification. This dataset was collected from the Herlev University Hospital in Denmark and is officially known as the Herlev Pap smear dataset or “Pap smear benchmark dataset.” It consists of 917 Pap smear images of individual cells that have been carefully annotated by pathologists and cytotechnologists with confirmed diagnoses.

Seven diagnostic categories, which cover all stages of cervical cell abnormalities from normal to malignant, consist of the 917 images. The Bethesda standardized system for cervical cytology reporting is utilized in this hierarchical system. The data set consists of four categories of abnormal cells, which include carcinoma in situ (CIS) and low- and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (LSIL and HSIL), as well as three categories of normal cells.

Each image is a 768 × 576 grayscale microscopic image obtained at 20x or 40x magnification and stored in TIFF format in order to preserve quality. Manual annotation of the cytoplasmic and nucleic boundaries by the dataset is useful for segmentation and feature extraction purposes. Furthermore, to validate high labeling accuracy, every sample was independently classified and verified by two experienced cytotechnologists prior to establishing a consensus diagnosis by a senior pathologist.

The data is divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) subsets by stratified sampling to preserve the class proportion in each subset to ensure reproducibility and generalizability. The model has to be trained with class-weighted loss functions or oversampling/augmentation methods because of the minor imbalance in the dataset, especially between the normal and strongly abnormal classes.
