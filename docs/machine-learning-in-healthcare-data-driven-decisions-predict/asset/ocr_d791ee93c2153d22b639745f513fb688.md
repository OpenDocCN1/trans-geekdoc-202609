# 1 Introduction

Data analytics and machine learning (ML) in the healthcare sector have brought out tremendous transformation during the last few decades. Previously, the doctors’ experience dominated in clinical judgment and manual recording, and simple statistical approaches were used. Early in the twentieth century, population-level data were applied in epidemiological research to establish the correlation between risk factors and clinical outcomes, on which evidence-based medicine was founded. Patient information in large amounts can be stored and retrieved now due to advances made during the latter part of the twentieth century in digital health records. These systems were primarily transactional in scope and centered on storage rather than analytics. The health industry started shifting toward intelligent systems that would provide real-time, data-driven insights as a result of the rise in computing capabilities, internet connectivity, and digitalization of health data from unstructured radiological images and doctor notes to structured electronic health records (EHRs) and lab reports.

Early healthcare data analytics approaches were in favor of standard statistical techniques such as linear regression, logistic regression, Cox proportional hazard models, and time-series analysis. Although the use and comprehension of these models were simple, they made robust assumptions about variable interactions and data distribution. Classical approaches couldn’t identify nonlinear relationships and variable interactions as healthcare data grew more intricate and high-dimensional. Because of this constraint, ML methods were adopted and gained prominence in the 2000s. Disease prediction, stratification of patients, and response to therapy analysis, all utilized supervised learning algorithms like decision trees, support vector machines (SVMs), random forests, and ensemble learning, while phenotyping, patient subgroup identification, and anomaly detection were done using unsupervised learning approaches like k-means clustering and hierarchical clustering.

But the actual paradigm shift occurred with deep learning (DL), a branch of ML based on the structure and function of the human brain. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), autoencoders, and transformer are a few of the DL models that have achieved unprecedented success in unstructured data processing, including medical images, signals (such as ECG and EEG), and free-text clinical notes. RNNs and LSTMs have been used to model patient trajectories in the time dimension, whereas in certain radiological diagnostics, CNNs have outperformed humans. Initially intended for NLP applications, transformer-based models are increasingly being reused for use in the clinical setting for tasks like EHR-based predictive models, question-answering in the clinical setting, and generation of medical reports.

The ubiquity of ML in clinical settings is still being held back by a range of grand challenges, in spite of these developments. Foremost of these is the problem of heterogeneous data.
