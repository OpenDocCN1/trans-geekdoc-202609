# # 5.1 Descriptive, Predictive, and Prescriptive Analytics

Descriptive analytics forms the first step in analyzing data in the healthcare field. Historical data is used to look for regularities as well as any irregularities. Examples are the number of readmissions at hospitals, how long patients remain there, who they are, and the rate when a certain disease is contracted over time [33, 34]. To help understand the past events, descriptive analytics depends on dashboards, histograms, and summary statistics. To monitor the quality and compliance of care and assess performance, descriptive analytics is important [35]. It relies on statistics, machine learning, and old data to help predict what will happen next. Such models are also used in hospitals to predict how a disease will develop, how often the same patient is admitted to the hospital, and how many people will visit the emergency department. Many predictive models rely on logistic regression, time-series models, decision trees, and neural networks. Thanks to models, doctors are able to predict sepsis in patients at the hospital and start care well before the patient even shows signs of it [36]. In addition, prescriptive analytics uses the predicted outcomes to suggest ideas for the best possible steps to take. It uses decision science, along with predictions, to guide you toward the most desired results. Thanks to prescriptive analytics, doctors may select the best medical plan for each patient, figure out who to place in ICU beds, and at which dose and personalize the patient's medications. Commonly used for prescriptive decision-making are models, simulation tools, and reinforcement learning [37]. The three kinds of analytics are related to one another. In descriptive analytics, we focus on the present, predictive analytics helps with the future, and prescriptive analytics influences choices and decisions we make. With the help of this approach, healthcare professionals can start offering prevention-based care.

# # 5.2 Machine Learning and AI Techniques in Healthcare

By applying these two technologies, the field of healthcare analysis has undergone a major shift. By using ML, it becomes easy to spot patterns in large data, which allows models to improve each time they are updated with new data. Common use of supervised learning implies that random forests, support vector machines and gradient boosters are often tasked with diagnosing illnesses or predicting a person's risk of death [38]. Some approaches in unsupervised learning, such as clustering, help group patients or recognize additional disease groups, as well as highlight how care is used. In fact, ML helps divide diabetic patients into groups that matter to clinicians, versus those based solely on age and gender. In ML, deep learning uses multilayer neural networks to deal with information in several dimensions. The field is most useful for cases like radiology, pathology, and electrocardiography. With CNNs, detecting cancerous lesions in radiography images is now as accurate as what radiologists do [39].
