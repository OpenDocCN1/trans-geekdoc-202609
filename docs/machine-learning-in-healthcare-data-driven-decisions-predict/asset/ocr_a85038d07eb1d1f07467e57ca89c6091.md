Clinicians would be trained in learning how to interact with and understand the AI systems’ output, knowing the abilities and limitations of these systems and possible sources for error. Clinicians have a changing position in this new age of healthcare AI. There are aspects of patient care, including building rapport, communicating effectively, making complex decisions in uncertain situations, and, of course, providing compassionate care, which will always be human functions, which is why AI can never fully replace the clinician. Clinicians will have to evolve to more or less act as supervisors and interpreters of AI systems, who use AI insights to illuminate their clinical judgement rather than simply following the algorithms pronouncements. They would have to acquire fresh skills in data literacy, an understanding of AI concepts, as well as the application of making critical evaluations about the performance and limitations of such AI tools. This indicates that the future care delivery mode will be primarily through a host–guest relationship wherein both man and AI are involved in the healthcare process for different intended purposes. Their challenge is to develop an appropriate training and education model for healthcare professionals to effectively steer them into this changing landscape.

# # 10 Implementation and Deployment Considerations

The road to implementing and deploying ML models in clinical practice is highly complicated, thus subjecting such models to some technical, organizational, and logistical challenges. One major challenge is integrating ML models into existing healthcare IT infrastructures. Mostly, the healthcare systems have been developed over time based on patches of legacy applications, their own proprietary software applications, and segregated databases, all of which were not designed with the intention of AI integration. The technological fragmentation acts as an impediment to seamless data sharing and real-time inference of models. Interoperability can easily become a challenge, therefore requiring standard data formats (e.g., HL7 FHIR), APIs that have been developed to a high standard, and middleware solutions to facilitate communication between AI tools and EHR systems. Simultaneously, there is a need for secure, reliable, and high-throughput data pipelines to ensure that, at least with respect to real-time applications, the models have access to their required clinical data while respecting levels of patient privacy. This includes encryption, anonymization, and strong access control in an appropriate way. Another critical technical consideration refers to infrastructure requirements for deploying ML models in real-time clinical environments. Hospitals must invest in scalable platforms, on-premises or cloud-based, with low-latency inference, high availability, and fault tolerance. All of this must be achieved while adhering to strict regulatory requirements related to data security, privacy, and ethical use of patient information, such as HIPAA in the USA or GDPR in Europe.
