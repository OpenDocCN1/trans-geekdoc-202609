# 7.2 Informed Consent and Patient Autonomy

When using health data, informed consent is vital to both ethics and the law because it helps preserve a patient's autonomy. It means providing details about how patients' information will be managed and helping them decide whether to participate in the study. Informed consent process has to be followed during patients' initial visits to a doctor or before entering a research study. On the other hand, with digital advancements in the health industry, the process involves more factors because of wider access to and secondary purposes of collected data. The same data used for treatment in hospitals can be utilized for research, improving quality or used in other ways, making some people wonder if it was properly consented for these other uses from the beginning. Using dynamic consent, patients are now able to select how and when their data should be shared online. It helps patients to participate more and maintain regular communication with those who collect and use their personal information. Even so, this makes it harder to execute because people might lack computer skills, different systems may fail to connect. and there is more paperwork for administrations. The process of getting someone's consent ought to be done honestly, allowing complete choice, and clarifying everything. Every individual's information should be adjusted for their level of understanding and background culture. Extra steps must exist to ensure that people having cognitive difficulties or are poor have the proper safeguards in place for making informed decisions [49]. The rules about informed consent vary from one location to another. As an illustration, under GDPR, permission should be “voluntary, distinct, clear and precise,” while HIPAA requires authorization under certain conditions in the United States. Still, simply meeting the law is not enough to be ethical. Organizations in healthcare should ensure their practices center on the rights, dignity, and empowerment of their patients. Using online tools for consent and decision-making can make the consent experience more significant. Interactive charts, understandable information, and different languages are used to improve a patient's knowledge and understanding of the options. Still, conflicts between right and wrong arise when people's freedom is compared with outcomes made possible by analyzing big data, like community health and preventative measures [50, 51].
