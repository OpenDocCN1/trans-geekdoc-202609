Faults by human beings, mental fatigue, variable levels of competence, and the complex nature and diversity in medical information sometimes lead to failures in the diagnosis of diseases or latencies in diagnosis. ML models are actually paradigm-shifting adjuncts to human judgment. Enabling the analysis of extensive and intricate datasets, such as old medical records, genetic information, and imaging, these patterns and correlations arise from an almost imperceptible scale to the human observer. Thus, ML not only increases the detection accuracy but also enhances the speed of detection, indicating early signs in the history of disease progression, and statistically supports clinical decision-making. Medical image analysis has been the most fruitful breeding ground for ML. In deep learning, promise can be seen primarily in convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which have emerged as the best-performing architecture when it comes to interpreting highly complex radiological imaging modalities like X-ray, MRI, CT, or mammogram. Tumors, fractures, hemorrhages, lesions, and many other problems are detected with a very high precision using these models. In many clinical situations, an ML system acts as a “second reader,” drawing radiologists’ attention to suspicious areas of an image and thereby increasing diagnostic speed and reducing oversight [2]. Some highly effective deep learning systems are being trained to tell apart tumors that are benign from malignant in order to help with early cancer detection, tumor grading, and personalizing treatment planning. ML goes beyond diagnostics to risk prediction and risk stratification. Predictive algorithms assess the probability that an individual will develop a certain disease based on many factors [3]. These factors may include genetic predispositions, lifestyle factors (such as smoking habits, diet, and exercise), environmental exposure, demographic information, and electronic health records. The aggregation and analysis of these disparate datasets would enable ML to generate personalized risk scores for diseases like diabetes, hypertension, various cardiovascular conditions, and some types of cancer. Risk stratification allows healthcare to be proactive in identifying high-risk individuals without having to first see clinical signs. Interventions, lifestyle changes, and early screenings may be targeted to such high-risk individuals even before symptoms appear [4].
