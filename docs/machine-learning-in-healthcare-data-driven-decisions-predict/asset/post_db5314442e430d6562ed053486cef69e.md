
## 1 Introduction

From a clinically nonsignificant disease to one of the most urgently required chronic diseases of known medicine to the present time, chronic kidney disease (CKD) has been a persistent global health threat spanning decades. Renal disease pathophysiology is as far back as civilizations that existed many years ago where physicians observed renal features but could not use science skills to assess renal function. With the development of physiology and pathology in the twentieth and nineteenth centuries, nephrotic and nephritic syndromes were categorized and chronic renal failure was identified as an irreversible and progressive disease. Serum creatinine (SC) and urea, valuable indicators of declining renal function, could be quantified by clinicians following the introduction of biochemical assays in the mid-twentieth century [1].

There arose a new emphasis on the role of early treatment and screening after realizing that CKD is an asymptomatic, silent disease, particularly in the initial stages. Various healthcare systems globally have implemented various methods of early diagnosis because of increasing cases of CKD, most notably in those with comorbidities like diabetes and hypertension. Nephrologists formerly utilized imaging scans, baseline urine and blood, and intermittent kidney biopsies as means of diagnosis. Among the most commonly used markers are SC, albuminuria, blood urea nitrogen, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Although these methods have a sound foundation for the detection of renal impairment, their drawbacks include unpredictable interpretation, delayed onset of symptoms, and poor sensitivity at the commencement of the disease process [2].

In addition, these methods are constrained by the availability of trained personnel and laboratory facilities, particularly in developing nations, and are static in nature, capturing renal function as an instantaneous snapshot rather than predictive data. New medical diagnostic avenues have been explored in recent years with the advent of data-driven approaches, particularly with the development of machine learning (ML). High-dimensional, intricate healthcare issues can be addressed by employing ML techniques, wherein computers are trained from experience and predict outcomes without being explicitly coded. ML has been utilized in the case of CKD to assist in therapeutic decision-making, risk stratification, and disease onset prediction [3].

Clinical information has been employed for training predictive models such as support vector machines (SVM), decision trees, random forests (RFs), logistic regression, k-nearest neighbors, and ensemble methods such as GBMs. These models are likely to operate on structured data such as patient history, laboratory results, and demographic information and attempt to map input features to diagnosis categories such as the presence or extent of CKD. While promising, there are several drawbacks to the ML systems employed for CKD diagnosis. To start with, generalizability suffers due to the fact that the majority of models are trained on small datasets and, in some cases, an individual organization or community [4].

Issues such as class imbalance, in which there are significantly more or significantly fewer cases of CKD than non-CKD, can lead to biased models that fail to properly identify cases of minority classes. Overfitting remains an intrinsic problem for standard ML models, especially where highly correlated or redundant features are not subject to sufficient control of regularization or feature dimensionality reduction. In addition, much of the prior work simply addresses model accuracy without regard for model interpretability, a regard important to ensure to facilitate adoption within clinical settings in which physicians must understand the reasoning behind algorithmic prediction. Some algorithms are “black boxes” and do not provide the transparency that is needed in order to convince clinicians. Additionally, preprocessing pipeline, such as missing value handling, outlier detection, and feature selection, is largely underappreciated even though it has a significant impact on model performance [5].

Lack of standardization in performance metric measurement and reporting is another pressing problem with current practice. It is difficult to compare across model results because most published research uses varying classification criteria and thresholds. Another reason the laboratory success does not translate into clinical application is that models which perform well using training sets usually fall apart seriously when exposed to external or in vivo datasets. Placing ML systems in practice in the healthcare industry is also hindered by ethical and data privacy considerations. Because of patient data sensitivity, poor anonymization or security protocols can hinder proper large-scale collaborations necessary for creating robust models.

Finally, the problems with most ML systems of today are that they are passive and don’t have adaptive mechanisms to learn from and adapt to new patient data in real time. These constraints underscore the urgency for novel approaches to tackle the serious problems of robustness, interpretability, fairness, and clinical validity as opposed to attempting to optimize current data to achieve the best possible performance using small gains. There are several reasons that compel the requirement for the construction of an effective ML-based diagnosis model for CKD. Because CKD stealthily advances, it is typically diagnosed late, when treatments are not as successful and due to medical costs.

Smart algorithms capable of early diagnosis will assist with early medical treatment and lifestyle adjustments to retard the course of the disease and enhance outcomes for the patients. Secondly, in situations of resource limitations where specialization in nephrology would not be easily available, a reliable predictive model can act as a tool of second opinion for general practitioners and nonspecialist doctors. Third, technologically, accuracy and explainability, those at times characterized as compromise in traditional ML methods, can be achieved through the inclusion of feature selection techniques, regularization, and ensemble methods within a unified, systematic model. In addition, dynamic risk assessment and tailored treatment planning are facilitated by the capacity of a well-crafted ML system to learn from constantly evolving electronic health records. Also, a supportive environment for the application of innovative diagnosis solutions is being developed by rising usage of ML tools by the clinical and regulatory groups.

Formal healthcare practice is increasingly looking to include AI/ML-based devices as bodies such as FDA and EMA are beginning to release guidelines about their testing and certification. Collaborations between clinics and academia are also increasing, providing more heterogeneous and richer datasets for improved model training and validation. Lastly, it is also possible to develop and deploy sophisticated models in academia or even in mid-size clinics due to increased computational power and the existence of open-source ML platforms. It can be viewed from all these developments and constraints that even now there is a huge gap between the complete potential of ML in medicine and traditional diagnosis. It will require innovative thinking, sound methodology, and care for both computational power and clinical utility to bridge this chasm. This is the premise and inspiration behind the present work, which is to come up with a novel ML approach specifically designed for early diagnosis and accurate classification of CKD going beyond present limitations without being incompatible with the requirements of real clinical practice.

## 2 Related Study

Due to its wide global prevalence and soundless progression, medical data science has drawn tremendous interest toward preempting CKD at its early stage. As measures to enhance clinical decision-making and prevent burden on healthcare systems, researchers have employed ML algorithms increasingly to preempt CKD at its early stage in the last decade. Silveira et al. [6] explored diverse applications of diverse ML techniques, such as logistic regression, RF, and SVM, to small and imbalanced datasets for the diagnosis of CKD. It brought forth the reality that data imbalance renders the situation difficult and demonstrated the ensemble methods to be strongly resilient in working on the same, the accuracy was 98.5%.

Zhao et al. [7] used clinical information and ML methods to construct a CKD prediction model. The 99.8% accurate study used gradient boosting and other approaches to infer that boosting techniques at high levels are capable of enhancing CKD predictions' accuracy. Rubini et al. [8] utilized a range of classifiers in their comparison of the prediction of early-stage CKD. SVM was 96.5% accurate and had better performance relative to other models in the study, which presents its potential for early detection scenarios. Saif et al.
