The deployment of biased AI systems in health can have serious ethical and social consequences since it contributes to existing health inequities in access and quality of care. A comprehensive and multifaceted strategy will be required for addressing bias, including diverse data collection, algorithmic fairness techniques, and continuous assessments of how the model performs under real-world conditions.

Almost every healthcare dataset has issues regarding missing values since some records may be incomplete, appointments are often missed, and the clinical workflow may vary. Appropriate treatment of missing data is essential. The naive way of ignoring any record with missing data will lead to highly biased models and loss of valuable information. There are various imputation methods, relatively simple ones like mean imputation or median imputation, and slightly more sophisticated model-based approaches, but the choice of method is important as it profoundly affects the model performance and validity. Moreover, in healthcare, temporal dependencies are another characteristic in this view, as conditions and treatment vary with time. Time-series data from EHRs or wearable devices must be analyzed through specialized ML techniques capable of modeling sequential patterns and dependencies, recurrent neural networks, or hidden Markov models. The challenges of health and disease being in constant flux raise the issues of building ML systems that learn and predict time-variant changes, as compared to simple static models. Another problem is the explainability of ML models, especially in the case of deep learning models that may be computationally complex. In medicine, the practitioners must understand “why” a model is making a certain prediction or recommendation to be able to trust and make adequate use of the AI-based system. Black-box models are usually not trusted, despite being accurate, in clinical practice, where accountability and the explanation of the decision-making basis matter. Therefore, the interpretation of healthcare ML models, via SHAP (SHapley additive exPlnations) and LIME (local interpretable model-agnostic explanations), has gained traction to provide insights into modeling for clinicians [16].

# 7 Ensuring Trust and Reliability: Model Evaluation and Interpretation

The successful deployment of ML models in healthcare does not only rely on technical performance. A critical aspect of model use in healthcare involves trusting and interpreting by the clinical user. Measurement of an ML model in the healthcare domain would still require more metrics beyond simple accuracy, given that some event is assumed to be clinical in case of false positives and false negatives. For example, in the case of a diagnostic task, a false negative (missing to detect the disease present) is usually far worse than false positives (wrongly identifying the disease that is absent).
