## DigiCure: A Patient-Centric Framework for Digital Transformation in Healthcare

Abstract: Traditional healthcare systems tend to suffer from issues like disjointed information, late diagnosis, and suboptimal patient activation impact overall therapeutic outcomes and impede timely interventions. The study introduces DigiCure, a simulation-based digitalization framework to optimize the provision of healthcare through the use of technology, as a solution to these issues. The model uses telemedicine, the Internet of medical things (IoMT), artificial intelligence (AI), and electronic health records to design a networked, patient-centered platform. The simulated work used the MIMIC-III dataset, including the diagnostic records, clinical measurements, and patients’ demographics. The simulated IoMT data streams were used to model real-time monitoring conditions, and AI models were trained from the dataset for supporting risk assessment and prediction diagnosis. In addition to that, a prototype for a mobile app was also built to emphasize features like teleconsultation, tracking medications, and booking appointments. From the outcomes of the simulation, performance evaluation reveals that the proposed model, a hybrid architecture combining random forest and Bi-LSTM, outperforms all traditional classifiers across multiple metrics: it achieves accuracy of 92.5%, precision of 91.8%, recall of 91.2%, F1-score of 91.5%, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 94.2%. These results establish a clear margin over CNN-LSTM (accuracy: 90.3%, AUC: 91.7%) and conventional machine learning models such as support vector machine (accuracy: 87.1%) and logistic regression (accuracy: 85.2%). The study holds great promise for live deployment, with the future holding clinical trials and implementation into live hospital systems.

Keywords: digital health, patient-centric care, Internet of medical things (IoMT), artificial intelligence (AI), telemedicine, electronic health records (EHR)

### 1 Introduction

During the past decades, where healthcare and information technologies have originated has fundamentally transformed, changing how medical care is delivered, overseen, and obtained. The concept of computerized health records gained traction once the application of computerized systems for keeping track of records replaced traditional hand recording toward the latter half of the twentieth century [1]. Electronic health record (EHR) systems were initially utilized in a limited way because of technological and infrastructural constraints, but they gained momentum in the early 2000s after government efforts to finance healthcare IT. Its application in hospitals and clinics has been encouraged by nations such as the United States by programs such as the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act of 2009. These technologies have been facilitated by better documentation, ease of retrieval, and coordination among providers [2, 3].

Beyond EHRs, digital health now encompasses wearable devices for health monitoring, telemedicine, mobile health (mHealth), artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and the Internet of medical things (IoMT). Telemedicine has evolved as a necessity tool for remote consultations, especially in disadvantaged and rural communities [4, 5]. Wearable technology permitted real-time monitoring of vital signs, while mHealth applications enabled patients to monitor and control their own health using smartphones and tablets. AI and ML technologies are being utilized for individualized treatment planning, predictive modeling, and diagnosis. IoMT facilitated real-time data acquisition and transfer for anticipatory delivery of healthcare with Internet connectivity of sensors and medical devices [6].

Computerized healthcare systems have been constructed with a variety of methodologies. These range from stand-alone computer programs for particular purposes, like billing or patient scheduling, to full-hospital information systems with clinical, financial, and administrative operations. Since it provides remote data storage and computational power required for big data, cloud computing has also played an important factor in scalability and accessibility of digital healthcare [7, 8]. In addition, Health Level Seven (HL7) standards and application program interfaces have made it simpler for healthcare systems to communicate, while ensuring security and ease in data exchange.

Some of the supervised learning models employed in AI-based applications for disease classification prediction and patient outcomes are decision trees, support vector machines (SVMs), and neural networks [9]. Patient segmentation and anomaly have been made possible by the use of unsupervised learning approaches. Reinforcement learning has been used experimentally to optimize treatment. The quality of the healthcare data used, the interpretability of the models, and how well they integrate into current clinical workflows all contribute to how useful these systems are, even as they become more advanced [10].

Despite all these advancements, there have been numerous challenges and pitfalls to the shift to fully digital healthcare systems. Interoperability is also a critical concern because the majority of health organizations use disparate systems that are not communicating effectively, and this leads to redundancy and data silos. Patient privacy issues and data security have also been monolithic concerns, particularly with the rising reports of cyberattacks on healthcare infrastructure. In addition to that, small and medium-sized healthcare setups are also constrained by resources as installation, maintenance, and training, summing up the initial capital expenditure, are system components.

Without supplemental resources, patients and professionals working in the system generally lack requisite digital competence and digital platform for realizing benefits of advanced medical solutions. Validity and reliability of AI models have been questionable for a long time, particularly when based on low or biased data. Clinical resistance to the adoption of new technology is also hindered by a desire to continue being consistent with previous practice or distrust of machine-based decision-making. Furthermore, most current infrastructure was designed with technology in mind before patients. Patient empowerment and patient outcomes have thus been harmed by irrational solutions. Even in most instances, excessive dependence on electronic technology has added to the administrative burden of healthcare professionals, leading to burnout and reduced direct time with patients.

A stronger, patient-centered digital healthcare system that is not just technically sound but also adaptive, secure, and scalable is required in the light of such challenges. The abovementioned principal shortcomings and weaknesses in prevalent digital health solutions are the driving force behind the given methodology. Systems capable of integrating heterogeneous systems, facilitating data interoperability, improving the precision of diagnosis, and offering ongoing, personalized care through real-time monitoring are more in demand. The need for digital transformation in the healthcare sector was also augmented by the COVID-19 pandemic.

Digital prescriptions, home monitoring devices, and remote consultations were a necessity due to lockdowns and social distancing. In addition to reaffirming the importance of digital technology in enabling continuity of care during crises, this hastened adoption of telehealth. It did reveal, though, structural vulnerabilities as well, including absence of infrastructure in some areas and incapacity of the current digital infrastructure to handle excess traffic. The system proposed is designed to close the gap between historic care and future-proof digital health. The system must simulate a real healthcare setting where AI, IoMT, and cloud technologies collaborate to enhance outcomes through the use of publicly available facts.

The objective is to render the system usable, accessible, and flexible across a variety of therapeutic settings rather than focusing on technical complexity.

The main objectives are:

- 1. To implement a simulation-based digital healthcare framework that integrates EHR, AI, IoMT, and telemedicine to improve healthcare accessibility and quality.
- 2. To design an AI-driven diagnostic model using the MIMIC-III dataset for accurate prediction of critical health conditions and patient risk stratification.
