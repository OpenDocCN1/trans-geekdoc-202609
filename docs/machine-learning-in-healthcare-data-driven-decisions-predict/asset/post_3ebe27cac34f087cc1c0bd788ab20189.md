## On Mental Health Monitoring Using Commercial Wearable Devices and Machine Intelligence

Abstract: Mental health problems such as anxiety, stress, and depression are increasingly prevalent nowadays due to fast-paced lifestyles, work pressure, social isolation, and other factors. Technological advancements in wearable devices, including smart-watches, smart bands, and smart rings, offer a promising approach to continuous physiological monitoring. Since the cohesion between mental/emotional health and physiological parameters is already an established fact through various researches, wearable devices should necessarily enable the early detection of mental health conditions as well. Our research studies the feasibility of commercially available smart-watches for emotion detection. Commercial wearable devices do not consider mental/emotional health as their primary concern in their design goal. To study its feasibility in such devices, in our experiment, we use the LifeSnaps dataset, a real-life multimodal dataset that captures physiological and psychological data related to 71 users from various demographic locations. First, we examine several physiological features such as heart rate, oxygen levels, calories burned, and sleep patterns (including light sleep, deep sleep, etc.) in the time domain, as well as some psychological surveys such as PANAS and STAI and emotional state survey collected using SEMA3 mobile app, and try to establish their cohesion using several machine learning models. We achieve a maximum accuracy of 78.75% using random forest. Furthermore, to improve accuracy, we extract additional signal-level features using fast Fourier transformation and apply recursive feature elimination to identify the most relevant features through our proposed methods: (I) selective feature-based approach and (II) holistic feature-based approach. Next, with these enhancements, the machine learning model achieves the highest accuracy of 81.01% using XGBoost. Additionally, we explore two neural network models (bidirectional long short-term memory and FNN (feedforward neural network)) with random oversampling and achieve 97% accuracy with FNN.

Keywords: mental health, depression, smart devices, smartwatch, emotions, physiological data, psychological data, cognitive science, big data

## 1 Introduction

In the twenty-first century, causes like social rat race, fast-paced lifestyle, increased work pressure in corporate sectors, digital overexposure, etc. are affecting mental health significantly, resulting in rising cases of anxiety, stress, and depression [1]. Conventional therapy and medical treatments remain essential for addressing mental health problems no doubt, but timely detection of such conditions is still a challenge. However, with advancements in wearable smart devices, a new method emerges to monitor health and offers hope for early detection of mental health conditions and symptoms. Wearable smart devices, such as smartwatches, smart bands, and smart rings, all come equipped with a variety of sensors. Core features of these devices include activity tracking, heart rate monitoring, blood pressure monitoring, oxygen level measurement, and GPS tracking.

These wearable devices collect physiological parameters closely related to mental health, as it is already an established fact that emotional and physiological states influence bodily functions. Various body signals, such as heart rate variability, skin conductance, sleep patterns, and brain activity, provide valuable insights into an individual's mental well-being. Understanding these physiological parameters enables the detection of stress, anxiety, depression, and other mental health issues. A crucial aspect of understanding an individual's well-being is detecting their emotional state. A continuously negative emotional state over an extended period indicates early signs of mental health issues [2]. Previous researchers [4–11, 33, 34] used various nonneural and neural network machine learning models to classify emotions. We will discuss this briefly in the literature survey part.

Several professional roles, such as doctors, pilots, and loco pilots, require optimal mental stability during work hours because a single poor decision can lead to life-threatening consequences. Therefore, early detection of an individual's unstable emotional state becomes vital. Numerous research studies focus on this domain, with researchers using various smart devices to predict emotional states. A detailed discussion of existing research in this category appears in Section 2. However, many limitations exist in earlier research. Researchers often develop custom devices, such as headbands, helmets, or heavy medical equipment, to collect physiological data. However, these custom devices tend to be expensive and are typically tested in controlled lab environments rather than real-life conditions.

Hence, we investigate whether it is feasible to detect human emotions using commercially available smartwatches. To study this, we explore the LifeSnaps [3] dataset. LifeSnaps data were collected over four months in real-life situations across various geographical locations. The device used in the LifeSnaps dataset is a Fitbit smartwatch, which is a commercially available product. The LifeSnaps dataset contains various physiological features such as heart rate, oxygen level, calories burned, filtered demographic VO2max, resting heart rate, and step count, among others. We established in our previous study [33] that the physiological data collected using commercially available devices, such as smartwatches, strongly correlate with user-reported mental states and the same can be employed for mental state detection in both nonneural machine learning and neural networks approaches. All the features explored in the said study were in time domain. In this chapter, we would like to explore if the addition of other features could improve the performance. We primarily focus on frequency domain analysis and utilize fast Fourier transformation (FFT) to derive additional features to verify if it improves the machine learning classification performance. We propose two feature selection approaches: (1) selective feature-based approach and (2) holistic feature-based approach to select the best set of features from a mixed pool of features (existing time domain features from the LifeSnaps and derived frequency domain features), which can enhance emotion classification performance with limited feature set. Using these two approaches, we observed that, among nonneural machine learning methods, SVM improves its accuracy by 7.24% and the highest accuracy achieved by XGBoost, which is 81.01%. However, neural network models surpass the nonneural ML models significantly in both cases.

The rest of the chapter is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the literature survey; Section 3 discusses the methodology we applied in our experiment; Section 4 covers the time-domain and frequency-domain experimental section; and Section 5 concludes the article with a brief mention of the future scope of researches.

## 2 Literature Survey

Detecting human emotions through various physiological and contextual data has been one of the most fascinating fields in which several researchers have engaged over the last few decades. In recent years, extensive research has focused on detecting human emotions using sensor-based customized devices [4-6], smartphones [7-13], and smartwatches [14-19].

Yu et al. [4] proposed a wearable system to recognize the emotions and motions of individuals while they are wearing it. The wearable device comprises three parts: (a) a hardware system, (b) a software system, and (c) a 3D display platform. The authors mapped the physical, emotional, and environmental contexts of the user in virtual space, utilizing twin technology. They conducted their experimental study using 10 volunteers with an average age of 22.3 years. Similarly, some research works detected emotion from keystrokes. Qi et al. [5] used touch panels to identify emotions. In their experiment, they employed 24 volunteers (12 males and 12 females) for evaluation. Kwon et al. [6] developed a glass-type wearable device that detects emotions using local facial images, EDA (electrodermal activity), and PPG (photoplethysmography). They classified four emotions (fear, happiness, depression, and calm) with an accuracy of 76.09%. The researchers in [10] detected emotions using a smartphone.
