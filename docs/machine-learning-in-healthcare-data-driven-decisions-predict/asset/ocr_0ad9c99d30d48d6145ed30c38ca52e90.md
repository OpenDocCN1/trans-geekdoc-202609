# ## 3.1 Dataset Description

Two real-world publicly available datasets, the MIMIC-III (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care) v1.4 and a commercial hospital EHR dataset obtained over 3 years from an academic tertiary hospital, were used to test the patient-oriented treatment recommendation model proposed. They are well-suited for training and testing AI-based decision support systems since together they cover a wide range of clinical diseases. De-identified patient records for more than 53,423 individual hospital stays of 38,597 adult patients who were admitted to critical care units at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2001 and 2012 are included in the MIMIC-III dataset.

A subset cohort of 24,112 patients was used in this study based on the availability of longitudinal clinical data, record completeness, and occurrence of treatment outcomes. In total, 4,217,306 time-stamped events, including vital signs, test results, prescription medications, procedures, discharge summaries, and diagnoses, are included within this subgroup. A formal dataset with 110 clinical features, 45 laboratory test and vital sign values, 32 drug entries, 18 procedure-related indicators, and 15 demographic or administrative features was created from these events.

In addition, 12,304 patient records from an Indian multispecialty hospital were contributed by a proprietary EHR dataset. With 92 features per patient including structured fields such as diagnosis codes, age, gender, medications prescribed, and unstructured ones such as doctor notes and discharge summaries, the dataset covers several departments like internal medicine, cardiology, and endocrinology. By allowing for the coverage of the acute and chronic care pathways, the combined dataset supports the creation of a treatment recommendation model that is valid and generalizable.

# ## 3.2 Preprocessing

An end-to-end preparation pipeline consisting of multiple steps that addressed the typical issues of real-world clinical datasets like missing values, temporal inconsistencies, variable units, and unstructured text was utilized to prepare raw healthcare data for modeling. To get all the quantitative characteristics (e.g., heart rate, glucose level, and blood pressure) onto a comparable scale, data standardization was done initially. Normal features were normalized using Z-score normalization, whereas skewed features such as the creatinine and lactate levels were normalized using min-max normalization.
