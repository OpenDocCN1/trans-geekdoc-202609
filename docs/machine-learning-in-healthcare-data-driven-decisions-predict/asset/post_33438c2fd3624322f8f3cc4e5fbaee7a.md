
### 2.9 Temporal Graph Neural Network for Epidemic Forecasting

Precise prediction of infectious diseases is vital for successful epidemic management and public health policy planning. GNNs have been proposed in recent times as a promising method for epidemic forecasting. Yet, current GNN-based models have two critical shortcomings: (1) they generally widen the receptive field through depth augmentation in the network, which does not really grasp the semantics of long-range connections between geographically remote but epidemiologically connected areas and (2) they tend to work at a single scale, ignoring the subtle, multiscale patterns that are typical of epidemic spread. To address such challenges, a new multiscale spatiotemporal GNN (MSGNN) has been proposed to introduce the multiscale aspect to epidemic modeling. MSGNN incorporates a new graph learning module that seeks to identify long-range dependencies by learning from trans-regional epidemic signals to form a rich multiscale graph structure. We then build upon this by designing a sophisticated graph convolution module that captures both scale-shared and scale-specific epidemic patterns to facilitate more accurate and detailed multiscale epidemic modeling. The MSGNN is tested on new COVID-19 case prediction in the United States. Experimental results show that our model far exceeds current state-of-the-art approaches in accuracy and stability. Moreover, analysis and visualizations show that MSGNN yields interpretable predictions with valuable insights into epidemic dynamics [16].

The ever-present threat of re-emerging and newly emerging infectious disease hazards makes epidemic forecasting extremely crucial and therefore timely. The rapid pace of development of predictive models from the classic time-series models like ARIMA and Holt's exponential smoothing to more advanced AI-based models like DL ensembles, hybrid models, and GNNs is highlighted in this review. Each strategy possesses a unique set of strengths for addressing central challenges of data heterogeneity, non-stationarity, and the complex spatiotemporal dynamics of disease spread. Of special interest are recent developments in deep transfer learning, multiscale GNNs and epidemic-guided hybrid models that have made significant advances in prediction accuracy and interpretability. Besides, the integration of human optimization techniques and uncertainty estimation enhances the robustness and usability of these models for real-world public health settings. With the advancing technology, the synergy between ML, domain knowledge, and high-quality data will be instrumental in the development of future-proof, publicly understandable, and actionable forecasting systems. These advances not only facilitate Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG-3) by strengthening health systems but also lay the foundation for improved epidemic preparedness, response, and policy-making globally.

## 3 Methodology

In response to the growing complexity and pace of public health threats, integration of next-generation computational technologies is now essential for real-time disease surveillance and intervention. This framework outlines a next-generation, AI-based health surveillance system that leverages an extensive range of data sources and current state ML techniques to yield real-time actionable knowledge [17]. Fundamentally, the system consists of four synergistic components: the data acquisition and fusion layer, which integrates a number of heterogeneous data sources; the spatiotemporal disease modeling [18] module, which identifies the complex disease transmission dynamics in space and time; the adaptive online learning engine, which maintains model currency against changing conditions; and the detection and early warning system [19], which extracts predictive information into alerts and visual dashboards to facilitate quicker public health decision-making. ### Data Acquisition and Fusion Layer

The data acquisition and fusion layer is the central component for integrating a myriad of heterogeneous and real-time data sources to enhance predictive analytics and decision-making for healthcare monitoring systems. This layer gathers structured and unstructured data streams including electronic health records (EHRs) of hospitals, search engine search query trends (e.g., Google Trends), social media (e.g., Twitter and Reddit), environment and mobility data (e.g., weather, traffic volume, public transportation usage, and GPS mobility traces), official health notifications from organizations like the WHO and CDC, and wearable/ IoT sensor data if available. To accommodate the variability and complexity of such sources, a data lake architecture is used to allow scalable, schema-flexible storage and retrieval. Moreover, Bayesian data fusion methods are applied to resolve inconsistencies and uncertainties between data modalities in order to generate a coherent, probabilistically consistent representation of the integrated information. This facilitates stronger downstream analytics and model performance, especially in public health surveillance and early warning systems. Let the input be

$D = \{D_1, D_2, \dots, D_N\}$ (1)

where \(D_i\) denotes the set of data sources that correspond to different modalities. For example, \(D_1\) represents the EHRs, \(D_2\) denotes the social media, \(D_3\) is the search engine queries, \(D_4\) is the mobility and environmental data, \(D_5\) is the official health notifications, and \(D_6\) represents wearables/IoT sensor data. Let \(X_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_i}\) represent the observed data vector from source \(D_i\) and \(z \in \mathbb{R}^k\) be the latent fused representation integrating all modalities. The Bayesian data fusion framework aims to infer the posterior distribution over the latent representation z given all observed modalities:

\(P(z|x_1, x_2, \dots x_N) \propto P(z) \prod_{i=1}^{N} P(x_i|z)\) (2)

where \(P(z)\) denotes the latent fused state and \(P(x_i|z)\) is the observed data from source i conditioned on z. This model allows handling of missing modalities. The example likelihood models for structured data (EHRs) are as follows:

\(P(x_1|z) = N(W_1z + b_1, \Sigma_1)\) (3)

For categorical or text data

\(P(x_2|z) = \text{Categorical (softmax}(W_2z + b_2))\) (4)

For time series data

\(P(x_i^{(t)}|z, x_i^{(<t)}) = N\left(f_\theta(z, x_i^{(<t)}), \sigma^2 I\right)\) (5)

The inference via variational Bayesian inference to approximate the posterior:

\(q_\mathcal{O}(z|x_1 \dots x_N) \approx P(z|x_1, \dots x_N)\) (6)

To optimize the evidence lower bound (ELBO)

\(\mathcal{L}(\theta, \mathcal{O}) = E_{q_\mathcal{O}}(z|.)[\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P_\theta(x_i|z] - KL(q_\mathcal{O}(z)||P(z))\) (7)

The fusion output for downstream tasks, the latent vector z can then be passed to downstream tasks such as

- forecasting models
- classification tasks
- anomaly detection

### Spatiotemporal Disease Modeling Module

The spatiotemporal disease modeling module aims to shed light on the complex spatial and temporal dynamics of disease spread between geographical locations. It applies a GNN to represent spatial relations in which nodes are used to record individual geographic positions like districts or cities and edges are used to record true relations like human movements and transport networks. In order to effectively model temporal change, the GNN is coupled with a TCN [20] or a transformer so that the system can learn patterns of disease progression between fine-grained time steps (hourly or per day). The dual architecture allows the learning of dynamic spatiotemporal correlations that allow the model to make predictions about how diseases spread through space and time correctly. This dual strategy allows proactive public health actions by revealing burgeoning hotspots and predicting outbreaks to come. Let the disease spread network be represented by a graph

$G = (V, E, A)$

In eq. (1) \( V = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\} \) is the set of nodes each representing a geographic unit, \( E \) is the set of edges representing mobility or interaction, and \( A \in R^{N \times N} \) is the adjacency matrix, where \( a_{ij} \) reflects the strength of connection. Each node \( v_i \) is associated with feature vector at time \( t \). For example, number of cases, population density, and hospital load.

The GNN or graph attention network to capture spatial dependencies. A common GCN update rule at layer \( l \)

$H^{(l+1)} = \sigma(AH^{(l)} W^{(l)})$

where \( A \) is the normalized adjacency matrix with self-loops, \( H^{(l)} \in R^{N * d_t} \) are the node features at layer \( l \) and \( W^{(l)} \) are the learnable weight matrix and \( \sigma \) is the nonlinear function. The output is given by

$H_{\text{spatial}} = \text{GNN}(X^{(t)}, A)$

A TCN applies to 1D convolutions over the temporal axis per node is given by

$H_{\text{temporal}} = \text{TCN}(H_{\text{spatial}}^{1:T})$

Each node's time series is processed as

$h_i^{(t)} = f_{\text{TCN}}(h_i^{(t-k+1)}, \dots, h_i^{(t)})$

In eq. (12) \( k \) is the receptive field and \( f_{\text{TCN}} \) is a stack of dilated casual convolutions.
