Light models and transfer learning are growing in popularity due to their capacity to offer state-of-the-art accuracy with low processing power. To speed up training and deliver higher accuracy, numerous models have used pre-trained models like VGG, ResNet, and EfficientNet and have fine-tuned them on cervical cytology datasets [16]. Aside from these light models, explainable AI (xAI) techniques are being applied to produce interpretable diagnosis outputs, raising the confidence level of physicians [17].
Recently, reinforcement learning architectures and multimodal data fusion have been established to individualize categorization models and therapy choices. These sophisticated systems dynamically learn from cytological input and patient data [18]. Transformer models have also been modified for cervical cancer classification in order to counteract dataset imbalance, using token-based representations and taming techniques to enhance generalization on minority classes [19]. Dimensionality reduction with preservation of discriminatory ability has also been demonstrated to be effective when deep feature selection techniques employ optimization techniques like principal component analysis and gray wolf optimization [20].

A massive hierarchical benchmark dataset for cervical cytology allowing systematic evaluation of classification algorithms has recently been introduced [21]. Additionally, research using cytology with pathology and colposcopy photographs suggests that deep learning methods can be used with a variety of diagnostic modalities [22]. Higher accuracy and robustness have also been facilitated by patch-based fusion models, advanced segmentation methods, and progressive learning-based approaches. Among them is CerMixer, which supports enhanced feature learning in cytology images by applying multi-scale depthwise convolution and patching [23].

Particularly in datasets that have overlapping cells and staining artifacts as issues, segmentation is the central theme in other studies as a prerequisite to precise categorization [24]. It is suggested that causal-learning-based CNN models such as CausalCervixNet should incorporate an interpretable layer within the decision process of the network in order to enable more precise diagnoses using cause-and-effect reasoning [25]. A systematic review on AI for cervical cancer diagnosis in future work suggests more diverse datasets, open model architectures, and coworking with clinicians [26].

Numerous problems continue to exist in spite of these developments. Many models are learned and evaluated against little or simulated sets that lack the diversity found in the real world. Others fail to generalize across varying cytology preparation techniques and imaging platforms, or they overfit with limited sample sizes. Furthermore, models tend to produce inferior classification results by failing to properly account for local morphological information as well as global context. Instituted by such limitations, the need for a unified model capable of learning hierarchical features, expressing cross-scale and cross-domain correlations, and coping with class imbalance is evident. To meet such limitations, this study introduces the HCFEN with the objective of integrating some of the advantages recognized in earlier studies, class-aware optimization, multimodal learning, hierarchical representations, and attention mechanisms into one framework for sound cervical cancer classification.
