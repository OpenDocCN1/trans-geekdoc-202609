Jayaprabha et al. [10] gave a comprehensive overview of some ML methods and their application to CKD prediction in the early days. While precision statistics were not reported, the survey highlighted the importance of feature engineering and data preprocessing to model performance. Sawhney et al. [11] compared performances of a number of artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms in early CKD prediction. GBM performed better than the other models tried, with the maximum accuracy of 99.0%. James et al. [12] discussed the needs of CKD early detection and prevention. The research explained how early detection would prevent disease progression and further improve patient outcomes, although none was directed toward a special single ML algorithm.

Habiba et al. [13] investigated the combination of feature selection techniques and ML models employed to forecast CKD. With a 98.3% accuracy rate using the RF classifier, the research concluded that adding feature selection enhanced model performance. Tangri et al. [14] used clinical features to construct a model to predict CKD progression to renal failure. Rather than level comparison with ML-based models, the model, constructed with classical statistical methods, had a c-statistic of 0.91 and exhibited excellent predictive ability. Schena et al. [15] evaluated the application of AI algorithms for predicting the development and course of CKD. Without firm performance metrics, the study demonstrated the ability of AI to detect high-risk patients.

George et al. [16] looked at the need for screening and early detection of CKD, especially in low- and middle-income countries with populations at risk for diabetes. The article did not address specific ML methods or results but did emphasize predictive models in poor-resource areas. Debal et al. [17] highlighted CKD prediction using multiple ML algorithms such as decision trees and SVM. The SVM classifier's ability to correctly classify CKD detection tasks came in at a rate of 98.3%. Shih et al. [18] utilized health screening data to develop a risk prediction model for the early detection of CKD. Based on logistic regression, the model was reasonably good with an AUC of 0.88.

Chien et al. [19] employed a cohort analysis to develop a prediction model of the probability of incident CKD. The c-statistic value of 0.85 from traditional statistical analysis reflects a good degree of predicted accuracy. Islam et al. [20] addressed prediction of CKD with ML methods and the comparison of performance of a number of classifiers. The reliability of the RF classifier for the prediction issues in CKD was confirmed through the accuracy of 98.0% of the study. Xiao et al. [21] compared and developed ML algorithms to predict the trajectory of CKD. Following algorithm testing, the study concluded that the XGBoost classifier performed best in modeling the trajectory of CKD with the best accuracy of 99.2%.

Similar to the case of connected work, the problem of CKD prediction has been addressed by a wide range of models from more advanced ensemble methods such as RF and gradient boosting to more traditional algorithms such as SVMs, decision trees, and logistic regression. With models such as XGBoost and gradient boosting achieving up to 99.8% accuracy, most experiments yielded promising results, with accuracy rates often exceeding 97%. These results are clear enough to demonstrate how predictive models using ML can learn to recognize risk markers and underlying patterns within structured clinical data.

There are concerns over whether the models generalize to broader, more varied populations since the majority of them were trained using very small or imbalanced datasets [6, 13]. Although boosting methods and ensemble classifiers such as RF have the tendency to overcome this issue, they are still highly dependent on pretreatment of data and tuning, which varies significantly across implementations. It is challenging to evaluate the diagnostic reliability of these studies [8, 11, 17] with high accuracy but without exhaustive analysis with clinically relevant metrics like precision, recall, F1-score, or ROC-area under the curve (AUC).

Apart from this, although precise, some models were black-box and noninterpretable in nature, a prerequisite for their clinical uptake and acceptance [15]. Apart from this, to prevent redundancy and enhance model explainability, not many studies employed domain knowledge or feature selection techniques explicitly [13, 20]. The non-availability of standard benchmarking datasets and evaluation protocols, preventing direct model comparison, was another common shortfall in some reports. While published research to date shows the possibility of ML to diagnose CKD in its early stages, shortcomings caused by the biased dataset, lack of interpretability, and over-reliance on hyperparameter tuning indicate the urgent need for a more credible, generalizable, and interpretable method. Emboldened by these limitations, this work introduces a novel ML model named HRAB, and the model is designed to balance clinical usability, interpretability, and performance. The emphasis lies on incorporating strict feature selection and regularization techniques to prevent overfitting and to render the model interpretable by physicians. Overall, though previous studies have set a good foundation, the above shortcomings in previous studies justify the construction of a better and context-aware ML framework for predicting early CKD.

## 3 Proposed Hybrid Regularized Adaptive Boosting (HRAB) Algorithm

By combining the robustness of adaptive ensemble learning with aspects of regularization and interpretability, a new HRAB algorithm is suggested in Figure 1 for the improvement of the prediction of CKD. The six most significant steps of the whole methodology include data preprocessing, feature selection, configuration of base learner, addition of regularization, adaptive boosting ensemble creation, and model evaluation and explanation.

### 3.1 Data Preprocessing

The HRAB model is a trained and cross-validated CKD dataset of the UCI Machine Learning Repository. This dataset consists of 400 cases and 26 variables with 1 class label to indicate the presence or absence of CKD and 25 features (numerical and categorical). For ensuring the reliability of the ML model, the dataset is preprocessed well because there is a high percentage of missing values generated by the clinical nature of the dataset. Preprocessing starts with missing value treatment, where missing values occur in more than 20% of the dataset. Specifically, there are some missing values in variables such as SC, albumin, red blood cells, blood pressure (BP), and blood glucose random (BGR).

Mean of the same attribute is used to replace the missing value for continuous numerical variables such as age, BP, blood glucose, SC, and blood urea. For instance, if the mean SC of the dataset mean is 1.48 mg/dL, it is used as the imputation value for missing values for this attribute. The mode (most common category) is used to impute categorical variables like RBC (red blood cells), PC (packed cell), HTN (hypertension), DM (diabetes mellitus), and appet (appetite). For example, all such missing values for this attribute are replaced by “normal” whenever “normal” is the mode in RBC. In a manner that causes no statistical bias, the algorithm helps to ensure the integrity and distribution of original data.

Coding of categorical attributes to make them machine readable follows missing value imputation. There are roughly 12 categorical attributes like RBC (Red Blood Cells), PC (Packed Cell), PCC (Pus Cell Count), BA (Bacteria), HTN (Hypertension), DM (Diabetes Mellitus), CAD (Coronary Artery Disease), appet (Appetite), PE (Pedal Edema), and ANE (Anemia) in the CKD dataset. On the basis of the number of categories, one-hot encoding along with label encoding is employed collectively to convert them. Label encoding is applied for binary categorical variables like HTN (“yes”/“no”) or appet (“good”/“poor”), with “yes” being mapped as 1 and “no” as 0. One-hot encoding is employed to get separate columns for each category of multiclass variables like RBC (“normal” and “abnormal”), adding the dimensionality without making the model incompatible. Based on the number of unique categories present in each categorical feature, the dataset stretches from 26 features to about 35–40 features after encoding.

The numerical features of the dataset, including age, BP, BGR, BU, SC, and SOD, are subsequently scaled using Min–Max scaling. This type of transformation normalizes all numeric features to a value between 0 and 1 in a bid to avoid any individual feature overpowering the learning process. BP is between 50 and 180 mmHg and SC between 0.4 and 15 mg/dL. Without normalization, features with larger magnitudes would overwhelm the gradient updates in training and warp the results. Finally, class imbalance is an important problem for the CKD dataset.

There are 150 (37.5%) non-CKD cases out of 400 cases and 250 (62.5%) CKD-positive patients. Default classifiers will also bias toward the majority class due to this imbalance.
