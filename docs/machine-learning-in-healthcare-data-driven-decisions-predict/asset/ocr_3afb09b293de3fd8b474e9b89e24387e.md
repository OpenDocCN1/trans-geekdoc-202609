# # 1 Introduction

From a clinically nonsignificant disease to one of the most urgently required chronic diseases of known medicine to the present time, chronic kidney disease (CKD) has been a persistent global health threat spanning decades. Renal disease pathophysiology is as far back as civilizations that existed many years ago where physicians observed renal features but could not use science skills to assess renal function. With the development of physiology and pathology in the twentieth and nineteenth centuries, nephrotic and nephritic syndromes were categorized and chronic renal failure was identified as an irreversible and progressive disease. Serum creatinine (SC) and urea, valuable indicators of declining renal function, could be quantified by clinicians following the introduction of biochemical assays in the mid-twentieth century [1].

There arose a new emphasis on the role of early treatment and screening after realizing that CKD is an asymptomatic, silent disease, particularly in the initial stages. Various healthcare systems globally have implemented various methods of early diagnosis because of increasing cases of CKD, most notably in those with comorbidities like diabetes and hypertension. Nephrologists formerly utilized imaging scans, baseline urine and blood, and intermittent kidney biopsies as means of diagnosis. Among the most commonly used markers are SC, albuminuria, blood urea nitrogen, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Although these methods have a sound foundation for the detection of renal impairment, their drawbacks include unpredictable interpretation, delayed onset of symptoms, and poor sensitivity at the commencement of the disease process [2].

In addition, these methods are constrained by the availability of trained personnel and laboratory facilities, particularly in developing nations, and are static in nature, capturing renal function as an instantaneous snapshot rather than predictive data. New medical diagnostic avenues have been explored in recent years with the advent of data-driven approaches, particularly with the development of machine learning (ML). High-dimensional, intricate healthcare issues can be addressed by employing ML techniques, wherein computers are trained from experience and predict outcomes without being explicitly coded. ML has been utilized in the case of CKD to assist in therapeutic decision-making, risk stratification, and disease onset prediction [3].

Clinical information has been employed for training predictive models such as support vector machines (SVM), decision trees, random forests (RFs), logistic regression, k-nearest neighbors, and ensemble methods such as GBMs. These models are likely to operate on structured data such as patient history, laboratory results, and demographic information and attempt to map input features to diagnosis categories such as the presence or extent of CKD. While promising, there are several drawbacks to the ML systems employed for CKD diagnosis. To start with, generalizability suffers due to the fact that the majority of models are trained on small datasets and, in some cases, an individual organization or community [4].
