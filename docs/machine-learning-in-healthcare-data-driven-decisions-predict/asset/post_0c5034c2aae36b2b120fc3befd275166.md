
Against this background, designing a sophisticated therapy recommendation framework is motivated by bridging the clinical usability and technology capability gaps. Beyond proposing accurate and tailored treatment options, the system should operate within the realities of real-world healthcare environments, including data heterogeneity, legal mandates, and variability in levels of digital maturity of institutions. The aim is to create a model that is compliant with patient-centered principles of care and employs state-of-the-art AI methods to aid clinicians in delivering higher-quality, evidence-based, and tailored treatment plans.

The two main objectives for your patient-centric treatment recommendation:

- 1. To develop a patient-centric, AI-powered treatment recommendation system that leverages multimodal healthcare data including EHRs, diagnostic reports, and real-time monitoring to generate personalized, evidence-based treatment plans tailored to individual patient profiles.
- 2. To enhance clinical decision-making by ensuring high accuracy, precision, and interpretability of recommendations through the integration of deep learning models (such as LSTM and knowledge graphs), while maintaining interoperability with existing healthcare systems and compliance with medical data governance standards.

## 2 Related Study

A paradigm shift in patient-centered care delivery has been introduced by advances in digital health technologies. Digital change has become a lead driver of the aspirations of healthcare systems toward higher accessibility, personalization, and efficiency. The consequences of this change have been the subject of much research, from user experience and system integration to clinical outcomes and infrastructure modifications. As discussed in [1], digital health technology has increasingly been used to address systemic inefficiencies and inequities in healthcare access, especially in the developing world. Beyond improving the quality of care, these technologies have allowed institutions and governments to react quickly to patient needs. In [2], more far-reaching strategies for infusing patient-centeredness into digital systems have been envisioned, promoting scalable solutions that improve patient autonomy and continuity of care.

As explained in [3], EHR adoption has been shown to improve patient outcomes and clinical decision-making. A change in healthcare power and an increase in health literacy are witnessed in the spread of the “e-patient,” who participates in their healthcare choices using digital media, as explained in [4]. Furthermore, Mulukuntla and Venkata [5] looked at how digital transformation might enhance clinical coordination, operational transparency, and patient safety. Binsar et al. [6] studied leadership-driven digital uptake in mid-sized health organizations and identified infrastructure gaps, cultural adaptation, and institutional preparedness as areas of primary concern. In [7], it was observed that developing digital health literacy was essential to optimal use of digital applications, particularly among individuals with limited experience in technology. For the sake of enhancing responsiveness and inclusion in health systems, a co-creation solution where stakeholders participated in developing digital care pathways was suggested in [8].

At the same time, it was claimed in [9] that dynamic assessment models were needed to evaluate digital health interventions from a patient-centered perspective. Digital solutions have also been proven to improve clinician–patient relationships via the elimination of administrative burden and development of improved communication, according to [10]. Zhang et al. [11] described the shift from volume-based toward value-based models of digital care, providing frameworks prioritizing patient-centered and outcome-focused delivery of care. Forward-looking studies like [12] cited recent technological innovations that will increasingly democratize and personalize care. While Levander et al. [13] showed how patient-expectation-driven digital change can better align healthcare priorities with value-based expectations, Seaone et al. [14] showed how human-centered design principles had been proven to take center stage in developing fair healthcare solutions.

Cross-cultural evaluations in [15] demonstrated how, combined with contextual understanding, technological interventions augment patient experiences globally. An assessment of AI use for monitoring and disease care was published in [16], demonstrating how intelligent systems can predict health changes and provide individualized interventions as per need. Although EHRs are widespread, functional gaps of congruence between EHRs and actual patient-centered care were uncovered in [17], thus context-aware design and usability are still lacking. Organizational wisdom and adaptive leadership for digital change were tackled in [18], and ground-level activity in preventive personal health record design was described in [19].

Machine learning models can possibly identify and react to patients’ emotional cues, improving the quality of interaction as well as mental health monitoring, grounded on recent multimodal affective computing advancements [20]. On a larger scale, a literature review of coordinated care programs across Europe in [21] showed how the digital health revolution may enable person-centered, coordinated care. It is clear from reading the literature that has been researched so far that digital transformation is not just a technical innovation, but a total redesign of healthcare around the patient. More patient empowerment, integrated data, real-time monitoring, customization enabled by AI, and transition towards value-based care are some of the major themes.

Notwithstanding such developments, there are still formidable challenges to overcome before interoperability, digital literacy, and universal access to care can be achieved. Enriched by these findings, the current investigation seeks to develop and test a model for operationalizing patient-centered notions through joined-up, clever, and inclusive digital health technologies, with the aim of low-resource settings or underprivileged groups.

## 3 System Methodology

### 3.1 Dataset Description

Two real-world publicly available datasets, the MIMIC-III (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care) v1.4 and a commercial hospital EHR dataset obtained over 3 years from an academic tertiary hospital, were used to test the patient-oriented treatment recommendation model proposed. They are well-suited for training and testing AI-based decision support systems since together they cover a wide range of clinical diseases. De-identified patient records for more than 53,423 individual hospital stays of 38,597 adult patients who were admitted to critical care units at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2001 and 2012 are included in the MIMIC-III dataset.

A subset cohort of 24,112 patients was used in this study based on the availability of longitudinal clinical data, record completeness, and occurrence of treatment outcomes. In total, 4,217,306 time-stamped events, including vital signs, test results, prescription medications, procedures, discharge summaries, and diagnoses, are included within this subgroup. A formal dataset with 110 clinical features, 45 laboratory test and vital sign values, 32 drug entries, 18 procedure-related indicators, and 15 demographic or administrative features was created from these events.

In addition, 12,304 patient records from an Indian multispecialty hospital were contributed by a proprietary EHR dataset. With 92 features per patient including structured fields such as diagnosis codes, age, gender, medications prescribed, and unstructured ones such as doctor notes and discharge summaries, the dataset covers several departments like internal medicine, cardiology, and endocrinology. By allowing for the coverage of the acute and chronic care pathways, the combined dataset supports the creation of a treatment recommendation model that is valid and generalizable.

### 3.2 Preprocessing

An end-to-end preparation pipeline consisting of multiple steps that addressed the typical issues of real-world clinical datasets like missing values, temporal inconsistencies, variable units, and unstructured text was utilized to prepare raw healthcare data for modeling. To get all the quantitative characteristics (e.g., heart rate, glucose level, and blood pressure) onto a comparable scale, data standardization was done initially. Normal features were normalized using Z-score normalization, whereas skewed features such as the creatinine and lactate levels were normalized using min-max normalization.
