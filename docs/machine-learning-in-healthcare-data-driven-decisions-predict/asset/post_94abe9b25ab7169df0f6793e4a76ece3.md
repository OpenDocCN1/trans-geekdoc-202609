The LR model, serving as the baseline, yields the lowest scores across all metrics, with an AUC-ROC of 0.76, F1-score of 0.68, MAE of 0.61, and QWK of 0.62. Its linear nature limits its ability to model complex, nonlinear relationships common in stroke data, particularly when interactions between clinical and imaging features are critical. Despite being interpretable and computationally efficient, LR lacks the capacity to process high-dimensional or multimodal inputs effectively, resulting in poor predictive performance for both risk and functional outcomes.

RF performs moderately well, with an AUC-ROC of 0.81 and F1-score of 0.73 for risk stratification, along with a MAE of 0.55 and QWK of 0.70 for mRS prediction. It improves upon LR by handling nonlinearities and feature interactions more effectively. However, as a purely tabular model, it lacks the ability to leverage unstructured data like imaging, which may limit its accuracy for clinical problems where visual patterns (e.g., lesion location or size) significantly influence outcomes. It also offers only moderate interpretability through FI measures.

The deep CNN model, trained solely on imaging data, achieves relatively good performance in outcome prediction (MAE: 0.50 and QWK: 0.68), suggesting that spatial and visual features in neuroimaging are informative for functional recovery. However, its AUC-ROC (0.78) and F1-score (0.71) for risk stratification are lower, likely due to the absence of contextual clinical data such as age, comorbidities, or stroke severity scores. Without integrating clinical parameters, the CNN's view of the patient condition remains incomplete, and its performance suffers in tasks requiring broader context.

Multimodal ensemble model, a fusion of individual models’ output trained on various data modalities, achieves an AUC-ROC of 0.88 and F1-score of 0.80. It also achieves good functional prediction (MAE: 0.45 and QWK: 0.78). Although it capitalizes on the use of multiple sources of data, having a late fusion approach of aggregating predictions as opposed to features constrains rich cross-modal interaction. This model can possibly neglect detailed cross-modal patterns that the hybrid model would learn. Nevertheless, it performs better than single-modality models and shows the potential utility of multimodal methods.

## 5 Conclusion

A novel hybrid ML methodology is proposed toward overall risk stratification and prediction of function in stroke survivors. The model is an integration of DL, graph reasoning, and conventional ML under single model architecture. The suggested method is feasible to harness the full range of patient data available to us spanning from clinical parameters to imaging modalities without compromising accuracy. The improved performance of the model on a range of evaluation measures is supportive of the hypothesis that a hybrid approach is better suited to accommodate the heterogeneity and complexity entailed in poststroke recovery processes. In contrast to standard models, the hybrid system outlined here had considerably improved AUC-ROC and QWK values, reflecting improved classification of both acute risk classes and longer-term functional outcomes. The incorporation of a GNN enabled the model to learn inter-patient patterns and similarities that are typically neglected by typical models, while the CNN module learned descriptive spatial features from image data strongly predictive of outcome trajectories. Their combination in a common latent space learned through multitask learning provided the model with robustness and generalization. Furthermore, this hybrid model represents a significant milestone in the direction of intelligent, data-informed stroke management through the delivery of precise, understandable, and personalized risk and outcome predictions toward ultimately better decision-making and patient care.

## 6 Future Directions

While the hybrid ML model envisioned in this work shows promising results, a number of future directions of research and development are present. First, broadening the data to cover additional heterogeneous patient populations across various hospitals and geographic areas would enhance generalizability and robustness. Multicenter validation trials need to be conducted to test the model under conditions of real-world clinical practice such as imaging protocol variability, electronic health records, and stroke practice. Second, temporal modeling must be incorporated to better reflect the dynamic progression of stroke recovery. The incorporation of recurrent or transformer modules can potentially predict recovery courses over time, and the model can be made increasingly useful for long-term care planning on a clinical basis. Other modalities of data like genetic biomarkers, blood proteomics, or speech/language testing could provide more relevant patient profiles and enhance predictive accuracy. Third, whereas existing approaches such as SHAP and Grad-CAM offer insight, there could be emphasis on creating domain-specific interpretability modules that follow clinical reasoning steps, to enable trust establishment and clinical adoption. Clinical workflow integration is also a top priority. Developing an intuitive, real-time software interface or decision support tool that can be directly integrated into hospital information systems would enable clinicians to use model predictions at high-stakes decision points, for example, during discharge planning or selecting rehabilitation strategy. Lastly, fairness and model bias ethics need to be stringently tested, especially in underrepresented groups. Ensuring transparency through open auditing procedures and fairness-aware training procedures will be needed as these systems proceed toward application in medicine.
