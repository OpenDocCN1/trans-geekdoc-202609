The arrival of electronic health records (EHRs) that centralized patient information and made it more accessible signaled the formal beginning of the digitalization of healthcare. Due to the fact that they facilitate the structured recording of patient encounters, diagnostics, treatment plans, and outcomes, EHR systems have become an important part of health informatics. At the same time, advances in wearable technology, genetics, medical imaging, and remote monitoring technologies contributed to the sophisticated and diverse landscape of healthcare data. The basis for data-driven clinical support systems, especially those intended to assist in diagnosis and therapy recommendation was established by the worth of diverse data.

Numerous techniques have been put forth over the last 20 years to help doctors with treatment planning and decision-making. Among the first methods were rule-based expert systems, which suggested therapies using if-then reasoning and predetermined clinical guidelines. Although providing structured decision support, these systems were too rigid to consider the complexities of patient-specific factors like drug interactions, comorbidities, and individual therapy responses. Their application was thus limited to well-defined, specific therapeutic scenarios.

Traditional machine learning techniques and statistical models then emerged as alternatives. Structured EHR data was applied to logistic regression, decision trees, support vector machines (SVMs), and random forests in order to predict the trajectory of the disease and offer potential treatments. While these methods enhanced predictive capability and scalability of decision support systems, they remained hampered by dependence on feature engineering and inability to address unstructured data, such as radiology reports, physician’s notes, or patient-entered health information. In addition, temporal trends and longitudinal evolution of a patient’s medical history, both being key to accurate and adaptable treatment planning, were often neglected by earlier models.

By enabling the automatic discovery of complex patterns from high-dimensional data, deep learning has revolutionized predictive modeling in the healthcare sector over the past few years. In uses such as clinical outcome prediction and medical image processing, such models as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks have shown significant promise. Specifically, temporal relationships in temporal healthcare data, such as test results, prescription histories, and clinician visits over time, have been well modeled using LSTM-based systems. Intelligent clinical decision support systems that are capable of offering context-aware and personalized therapy recommendations have been established due to these innovations.
