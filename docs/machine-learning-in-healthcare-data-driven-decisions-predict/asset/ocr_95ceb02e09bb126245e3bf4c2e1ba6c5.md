Related laws legally require safeguarding the privacy of patient information by means of administration, physical measures, and technical means, promising patients access to it, letting them make changes, and giving them the right to limit its use. HIPAA Privacy Rule discusses when, why, and to whom a patient's personal health information might be disclosed, while the Security Rule addresses ePHI. GDPR is seen in the United States as one of the world's most detailed data protection laws. While it does not only apply to healthcare, it greatly affects processing health data. GDPR is guided by ideas such as only keeping and using needed data, setting a clear purpose for using it, and deleting it after use. Individuals are given the rights to get information, take their data with them, and have it deleted. What's important to understand is that honor. Professionals, according to GDPR, are required to pay extra attention to health data since it falls under the special category of personal information. Not only organizational decisions and actions are needed, but also changes in the culture of these places are important. All staff should learn that data protection is a central priority for the company. As part of an effective approach, companies should perform compliance audits, assess the privacy risks, and be ready to notify people when data is breached. As health systems cross different countries, it is vital for rules to be compatible with each other. Since each nation may have its own rules for laws, consent, and enforcement, organizations and collaborative groups often encounter difficulties and disputes. It is important to promote data privacy and freedom by adopting the same rules for data sharing globally. We should also keep in mind that failing to follow the rules can lead to legal consequences, damage a company's reputation, and make the public suspicious. In light of this, sticking to regulations should be considered both necessary and important, supporting the field's overall agenda.

# 7.4 Addressing Bias and Equity in Data Use

Fairness and unbiased use of data are essential aspects in healthcare today. The quality of data that an algorithm or tool is trained on will determine its reliability. If available historical data lack reliable or fair information, these systems may contribute to or worsen differences in healthcare services, results, and availability. Errors caused by bias can take place during collection, labeling, the processing, analysis, or interpretation of data. A lack of members from particular groups in medical studies and data can cause algorithms to do poorly on that group later on. In a similar way, including income, education, and housing in data analysis may be hard, meaning the concerned predictions and actions could be unfair. By levelling the playing field and making data more diverse, appropriate strategies must be adopted.
