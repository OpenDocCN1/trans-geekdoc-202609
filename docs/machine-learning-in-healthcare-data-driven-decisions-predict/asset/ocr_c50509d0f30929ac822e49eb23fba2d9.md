With cloud computing growing in popularity, identity management and access con-trol products were required to secure off-premises health data. Fine-grained role-based or attribute-based data access was achieved through extensive use of the role-based ac-cess control (RBAC) and attribute-based access control (ABAC) models. Although these systems implement fundamental access control, they are usually employed by cloud providers or centralized servers that share a single point of failure and susceptibility to external or internal attack. Also, auditability and transparency in such systems are usu-ally insufficient for real-time monitoring or forensic analysis.

At the same time, machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) models began gaining uses in predictive healthcare, ranging from personalized therapy sug-gestions to disease diagnosis. To generalize well, huge volumes of heterogeneous pa-tient data are needed for such models. However, the privacy limitations inherent in healthcare data curtail its availability and sharing across institutions. In addition to being against norms of privacy, centralizing data for model training subjects systems to increased data breaches, leaks, and legal offenses.

Because of these shortcomings, federated learning (FL) proved to be a promising model. In contrast to standard centralized machine learning, FL sends model updates to a central server only, allowing local model training at edge nodes (e.g., hospitals or diagnostic centers). This method does not reveal raw data and maintains data locality. A number of FL frameworks with clinical focus showed great performance in wear-able sensor data processing, predictive diagnosis, and medical image processing. Fed-erated learning is not without challenges such as how to handle trust among the nodes participating, data poisoning, black-box auditability, and problems with secure model aggregation.

Blockchain technology has also found popularity in the healthcare industry due to its tamper-proof, open, and decentralized data management. Because of their lack of access control and scalability limitations, public blockchains like Ethereum and Bit-coin are not ideal for mission-critical healthcare applications. As a result of this, per-missioned blockchains like Hyperledger Fabric are being used more and more to ar-chitect healthcare systems wherein only authorized parties may verify and access transactions. Blockchain improves transparency and minimizes dependence on cen-tralized middlemen with decentralized access control, real-time logging, and indelible audit trails.

Both blockchain and federated learning have strengths but falter individually when trying to address all aspects of the health data interchange challenge. Although FL provides privacy, it has not been shown to have responsibility among nodes and does have a strong trust management layer. Although blockchain provides prove-nance and data integrity, it is not naturally flexible enough for advanced ML compu-tations or privacy-preserving analysis.
