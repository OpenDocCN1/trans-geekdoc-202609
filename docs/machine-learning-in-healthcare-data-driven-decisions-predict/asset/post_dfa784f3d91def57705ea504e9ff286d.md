
In eq. (1) $n$ denoted the data points, $l$ indicates the loss function, $\hat{y}_i^{(t)}$ is the prediction for instance $i$ at boosting iteration $t$, and $\Omega(f_k)$ is the regularization for each tree $f_k$. The regularization term controls the complexity of the model to avoid overfitting:

$$\Omega(f) = \gamma T + \frac{1}{2} \lambda \sum_{j=1}^T w_j^2$$ (2)

In eq. (2), $T$ = number of leaves in the tree, $w_j$ is the score weight on leaf $j$, and $\gamma$ is the regularization of the number of leaves. At each iteration $t$, a new function $f_t(x)$ is added to minimize the loss:

$$\hat{y}_i^{(t)} = \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} + f_t(x_i)$$ (3)

To effectively optimize, a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss around the current prediction is used:

$$l(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t)}) \approx l(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}) + g_i f_t(x_i) + \frac{1}{2} h_i f_t(x_i)^2$$ (4)

where

$$g_i = \frac{\partial l(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)})}{\partial \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}} = \text{first-order derivative (gradient)}$$

$$h_i = \frac{\partial^2 l(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)})}{\partial (\hat{y}_i^{(t-1)})^2} = \text{second-order derivative}$$

- (ii) Image-Based Data: For image-based cancer detection, CNNs will be used. CNNs are good at extracting hierarchical patterns from images and are thus best suited for applications such as tumor detection, segmentation, and classification in medical imaging (e.g., MRI, CT, and histopathological images). The capacity of CNNs to learn spatial features from raw image data automatically provides the capability for identifying subtle tumor features that would otherwise be undetectable with traditional approaches. Assume the input to be an image:

$$X \in R^{H*W*C}$$ (5)

In eq. (5) $H$, $W$, and $C$ are the height, width, and number of channels in an image. A convolutional layer applies a set of learnable filters $K$. For a filter $K \in R^{k_h * k_w * C}$, the convolution operation at position $(i, j)$ is given as follows:

$$Y_{ij}^{(f)} = \sigma \left( \sum_{m=0}^{k_h-1} \sum_{n=0}^{k_w-1} \sum_{c=0}^{C-1} K_{mn,c}^{(f)} X_{i+m, j+n, c} + b^{(f)} \right)$$ (6)

In eq. (6) \( Y_{ij}^{(f)} \) is the output filter \( f \) at position \( (i,j) \), \( \sigma(.) \) is an activation function, and \( b^{(f)} \) is the bias term for filter \( f \). Pooling reduces the spatial dimensions while retaining important features. For example, max pooling over a window \( P * P \)

- (iii) Patient Longitudinal Data: In order to analyze patient data longitudinally, LSTM networks will be used. Both of these models are suited to sequential data, like patient history over time, where the dependency between previous medical records and outcomes in the future needs to be modeled. LSTMs are especially well-suited to model intricate temporal patterns in patient histories and disease. Finally, a hybrid ensemble strategy that combines the predictions of these heterogeneous models will be investigated to enhance both accuracy and robustness. Through the combination of strengths of XGBoost, CNNs, and LSTMs, the ensemble will provide a more complete prediction that combines multiple data modalities, ultimately increasing the overall performance of the framework in oncological disease prediction. Let each patient's sequential medical history be

$$X = \{ x_1, x_2, x_3, ... x_T \}$$

In eq. (14) \( x_t \in R^d \) is the feature vector at time \( t \). At each time step \( t \), an LSTM cell computes.

- **Forget gate:**

$$f_t = \sigma \left( W_f \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f \right)$$ (15)

- **Input gate:**

$$i_t = \sigma(W_i \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i)$$ (16)

$$\hat{C}_t = tanh(W_c \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c)$$ (17)

- **Cell state update:**

$$C_t = f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \hat{C}_t$$ (18)

- **Output gate:**

$$O_t = \sigma(W_o \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o)$$ (19)

$$h_t = o_t \odot tanh(C_t)$$ (20)

In the above equations, \( W_f, W_i, W_c, \text{ and } W_o \) are the weight matrices, \( b_f, b_i, b_c, \text{ and } b_o \) are the biases, \( \sigma(.) \) is the sigmoid activation, \( tanh(.) \) is the hyperbolic tangent activation, and \( \odot \) is the element-wise multiplication.

The final prediction from LSTM uses the final hidden state \( h_T \) for prediction:

$$\hat{y}_{LSTM} = \phi(W_y \cdot h_T + b_y)$$ (21)

where \( \phi(.) \) is an activation function. Then combine XGBoost, CNN, and LSTM predictions as follows.

- **From CNN (image-based data):**

$$\hat{y}_{CNN} = f_{CNN}(I; \theta_{CNN})$$ (22)

- **From LSTM (longitudinal patient data):**

$$\hat{y}_{LSTM} = f_{LSTM}(X; \theta_{LSTM})$$ (23)

- **From XGBoost (structured tabular data):**

$$\hat{y}_{XGB} = f_{XGB}(Z)$$ (24)

In eqs. (22)–(24) \( I \) is the patient data, \( X \) is the sequential medical records, and \( Z \) is the structured tabular patient data. The ensemble fusion strategy combines these predictions as a weighted average ensemble as follows:

$$\hat{y}_{final} = \alpha \cdot \hat{y}_{CNN} + \beta \cdot \hat{y}_{LSTM} + \gamma \cdot \hat{y}_{XGB}$$ (25)

Subject to

$$\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1$$ (26)

In eq. (26), \( \alpha, \beta, \text{ and } \gamma \) are ensemble weights determined with cross-validation or optimization.

The final ensemble model expression is provided as follows:

$$\hat{y}_{final} = f_{\text{ensemble}}(\hat{y}_{CNN}, \hat{y}_{LSTM}, \hat{y}_{XGB})$$ (27)

This model integrates heterogeneous data sources and learning paradigms to enhance prediction accuracy and robustness in oncological disease prediction.

## 4 Results and Findings

This section discusses the detailed analysis of the BC Wisconsin (diagnostic) dataset with different ML and DL models. A hybrid method that combines XGBoost, CNN, and LSTM outperforms the state-of-the-art classifiers in correctly classifying malignant and benign tumors.

### 4.1 Dataset Description

The Wisconsin BC (diagnosis) dataset contains 569 instances, derived from a digitized image of fine needle samples of breast masses. Each example consists of 30 real properties. The properties are calculated as mean, standard error, and “the average of the three worst maximum characteristics for each characteristic”, resulting in a high-dimensional profile of the morphology of the cell nucleus. The target function is a binary function that indicates whether tumor malignant (M) or benign (b) has 212 malignant and 357 benign records. This data record is suitable for training and observation of the performance of ML algorithms in binary classification problems. With its well-designed structure and precisely defined capabilities, it is an ideal choice for the use of algorithms such as XGBoost and is a research benchmark dataset for the diagnosis of BC.

As shown in table 1, the best overall performance in all the measures used is accomplished by the suggested hybrid approach using XGBoost, CNN, and LSTM. It outperforms traditional approaches and DL approaches with a level of accuracy that is 91%. Its precision level of 89% indicates that it has a very high rate to correctly label positive instances with the minimum false positives. The 88% recall value shows its effectiveness in detecting the majority of the actual positive instances, and the equally well-balanced F1-score of 88% shows its overall power. The interaction between the conventional ML and DL models makes this hybrid system capable of processing different types of data efficiently and learning structured and unstructured pattern features, thereby explaining its high performance.
