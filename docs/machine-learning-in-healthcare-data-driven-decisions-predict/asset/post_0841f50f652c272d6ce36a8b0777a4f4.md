
The penalty score is computed as

\( P_i = \varepsilon_i + \alpha \cdot C_i \)

where \(\alpha\) is a tunable regularization coefficient that controls how much the model penalizes complexity over error. This formulation ensures that base learners that are both accurate and simple receive preference in the final ensemble, while overly complex or unstable classifiers are downweighted, even if their raw error is low.

Once penalty scores are computed for all learners in the current boosting round, the ensemble weight (\(\beta_i\)) for each learner is inversely proportional to its penalty score

\( \beta_i = \frac{1}{P_i + \delta} \)

Here, \(\delta\) is a small constant added to avoid division by zero. This scheme means that even a model with a slightly higher error but much lower complexity may contribute more to the final prediction ensemble than a complex learner with marginally better accuracy.

A more balanced and understandable ensemble is produced by HRAB's regularization-aware weighting method. Applying this new penalty mechanism consistently enhanced generalization measures in internal tests on the CKD dataset (approximately 500 cases and 20 features after preprocessing). For instance, in cases with noisy or overlapping class boundaries, HRAB performed better than even gradient boosting methods such as regular AdaBoost (accuracy ~94.6%) with accuracy 98.1%, precision 97.8%, and \(F_1\)-score 97.9%.

Additionally, the group intentionally selects weakly biased but consistent models in preference to highly erratic ones that could overfit to the training distribution, based on the bias-variance tradeoff principle. In the medical context, in which incomplete and unbalanced data is a common constraint, this proves very beneficial. HRAB imposes simplicity and enhances the robustness of predictions on unseen patient data by incorporating structural penalties directly into the ensemble learning cycle. This is an essential requirement for deployment in real clinical decision support systems.

## 3.5 HRAB: Adaptive Boosting with Regularized Weight Adjustment – Algorithm Steps

### Input:
- Training data \( D = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N \), where \( x_i \in R^d, y_i \in \{-1, +1\} \)
- Number of boosting rounds \( T \)
- Learning rate \( a_t \)
- Regularization coefficient \( \lambda \)
- Complexity function: Complexity(\( h_t \))

### Output:
- Final ensemble classifier \( H(x) = sign\left( \sum_{t=1}^T \beta_t h_t(x) \right) \)

### Step 1: Initialize sample weights

Assign uniform weights to each training instance: \( w_i^{(1)} = \frac{1}{N}, \forall i = 1, 2, ..., N \)

### Step 2: For each boosting round \( t = 1 \) to \( T \), perform the following:

### Step 2.1: Train base learner

- Train a base classifier \( h_t(x) \) using the current weighted dataset \( \left\{ \left( x_i, y_i, w_i^{(t)} \right) \right\} \)

### Step 2.2: Compute weighted classification error

\( Error_t = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i^{(t)} \cdot I\left( h_t(x_i) \neq y_i \right) \)

### Step 2.3: Calculate learner complexity

- Compute a complexity score based on model structure:
  - For decision trees: depth or number of leaf nodes
  - For logistic regression: number of nonzero weights
  - For naive Bayes: variance in class-conditional probabilities

Complexity\( (h_t) \)

### Step 2.4: Compute regularized loss

\( Loss_{HRAB}(t) = a_t \cdot Error_t + \lambda \cdot Complexity(h_t) \)

### Step 2.5: Compute ensemble weight

\( \beta_t = \frac{1}{Loss_{HRAB}(t) + \delta} \)

where \( \delta \) is a small constant to prevent division by zero (e.g., \( \delta = 10^{-6} \)).

### Step 2.6: Update sample weights

- Update instance weights to emphasize incorrectly classified samples and reduce focus on overly complex models:

\( w_i^{(t+1)} = w_i^{(t)} \cdot exp\left( \beta_t \cdot I\left( h_t(x_i) \neq y_i \right) \right) \)

- Normalize weights:

\( w_i^{(t+1)} = \frac{w_i^{(t+1)}}{\sum_{j=1}^N w_j^{(t+1)}} \)

This adaptable formula ensures that a mix of performance and easy measures are utilized to select weak learners instead of just error. The ensemble is prevented from being dominated by excessively complicated classifiers by the penalty-based re-weighting. For medical datasets where generalizability, interpretability, and robustness against noise are essential, especially in CKD prediction, this approach is very useful.

## 4 Experimental Results

A systematic experimental setup was employed to develop and evaluate the proposed HRAB algorithm on a real-world CKD dataset. An Intel® Core™ i7-12700H CPU with a frequency of 2.30 GHz, 16 GB RAM, and Windows 11 Pro (64-bit) was utilized to perform the experiment. The development environment used was Python 3.10, and Scikit-learn, Imbalanced-learn, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), Seaborn, and Matplotlib were some of the required libraries. These technologies enabled successful model creation, evaluation, and visualization.

After preprocessing, the CKD dataset containing 400 patient instances and 25 clinical features was composed of numerical and categorical characteristics such as blood urea, SC, specific gravity, count of red and white blood cells, BP, hemoglobin, and albumin. The ultimate feature space that was utilized as input in the ensemble model was concise, clinically meaningful, and resistant to overfitting. A stratified fivefold cross-validation scheme was followed for both the training and evaluation of the models to ensure a generalization basis.

HRAB's performance was compared against a variety of well-known and state-of-the-art ML classifiers. These were RF, GBM, XGBoost, baseline AdaBoost, and SVM with radial basis kernel. All these models offered a good balance between interpretability and predictive accuracy and had proved successful in earlier CKD studies.

The evaluation criteria were rigorous and consistent with clinical significance.
