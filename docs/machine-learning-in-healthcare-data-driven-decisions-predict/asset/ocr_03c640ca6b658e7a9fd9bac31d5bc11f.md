Using a surrogate model (e.g., Gaussian processes) to approximate the objective function, this probabilistic method religiously chooses the subsequent set of parameters to test, based on acquisition functions such as expected improvement. Bayesian optimization has also demonstrated promise for hyperparameter optimization of ML models such as SVMs, gradient boosting machines, and deep neural networks. While model estimation is costly in cross-validation or ensemble training, it is particularly helpful.

Bayesian optimization is costly computationally for real-time operations and scales poorly for high-dimensional spaces. Meanwhile, sequential decision-making task optimization in the healthcare domain, like adaptive clinical trials, dosing adjustment, and treatment planning, has been investigated using reinforcement learning (RL). To train on best strategies in terms of long-run returns, techniques such as policy gradient techniques and Q-learning have been used to mimic environments. RL techniques tend to need to operate on big simulation environments and suffer from stability problems, particularly if used on noisy and sparse clinical data.

The appearance of AutoML (automated machine learning) systems, which seek to automate end-to-end model choice, feature creation, and hyperparameter setting, has been among the major developments in healthcare ML optimization. Solutions such as Google AutoML, H2O.ai, and AutoKeras use neural architecture search, ensemble algorithms, and optimization frameworks to bring ML to nonexperts. AutoML typically works as a “black box,” providing no transparency into model choice and optimization thought, even as it democratizes AI use.

In medicine, where conclusions must be clinically warranted and translatable in order to provide assurances of trust and regulatory conformity, this lack of transparency is a significant obstacle. Even with the variety and evolution of ML optimization techniques, there are still various obstacles that exist to hinder their complete integration into clinical practice. First of all, a majority of optimization methods ignore real-world constraints such as data imbalance, missing values, or noisy inputs, in the interest of concentrating on accuracy or minimizing loss. Heterogeneous data types such as unstructured clinical notes, categorical diagnoses, and numeric lab values often exist in healthcare datasets and can prove to be challenging to harmonize into a single optimization framework.

Second, theoretically successful but operationally inefficient models are produced because existing approaches are usually tested on highly optimized datasets that do not accurately represent the variability of actual clinical environments. Another significant drawback is the problem of overfitting, which is a serious issue in high-capacity models such as deep neural networks. Optimization methods minimize training error, when well regularized, otherwise generalized error is increased unintentionally. Methods such as dropout, L1/L2 regularization, and early stopping are typically used but need to be appropriately tuned, which becomes an optimization problem in itself.
