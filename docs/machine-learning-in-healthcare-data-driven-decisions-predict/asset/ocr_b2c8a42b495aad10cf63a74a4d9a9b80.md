# 3.2 Feature Extraction and Preprocessing

In this section, we first describe the details of feature extraction in two domains: (I) the time domain and (II) the signal domain. Then, we discuss the preprocessing of the data we consider in our experiment.

**(I) Feature Extraction in Time Domain:** We merge Fitbit, SEMA3, and survey data to analyze time domain features. We only consider the PANAS and STAI surveys when creating the customized dataset. The PANAS survey measures a person's enthusiastic, alert, and active behavior based on positive and negative affects over a specific period. Higher positive affect indicates a person is highly excited, concentrated, and engaged in pleasurable activities, while lower positive affect reflects sadness and lethargy. Negative affect captures a person's dimensions of subjective distress and unpleasant engagement. Lower negative affect signifies calmness and serenity, while higher negative affect encompasses feelings of anger, fear, guilt, and anxiety. Conversely, the STAI provides anxiety scores. This is why we only include these two surveys, despite the availability of many psychological surveys. After successfully merging the datasets, we obtain 7,435 rows and 82 columns.

**(II) Feature Extraction in Signal Domain:** To extract additional features in the signal domain and find hidden patterns in physiological data related to emotions, we use statistical analysis, FFT, peak detection, and power spectral density on heart rate variability. The details of the feature extraction are written below step-by-step:

+   1. **Time Domain**: To extract time domain features, we apply statistical analysis and generate new features: mean heart rate (HRmean), standard deviation (HRstd), minimum and maximum HR, interquartile range (HRIQR), skewness, and kurtosis.
+   
+   2. **Frequency Domain**: To derive the spectral characteristics, we use FFT to analyze frequency components and also use PSD estimated via Welch's method.
+   
+   3. **Linear Features**: This category consists of time and frequency domain features, as well as statistical and signal-based measures, assuming some linear relation within the HRV signal. For example, the power spectrum features, autocorrelation metrics, and several slope- and energy-based measures fall under this feature set. While there is some redundancy with the time- and signal-domain features, these features are grouped based on their use of linear analysis models.
+   
+   4. **Nonlinear Features**: These features capture the intricate, messy, and fractal-like behaviors of the HRV signal, which cannot be reframed using linear models. Popular non-linear measures include sample entropy and approximate entropy, Poincaré plot descriptors (SD1 and SD2), detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA), wavelet entropy, and recurrence quantification metrics. Nonlinear metrics and entropy-based metrics stand out from other linear techniques due to their efficacy in revealing minute shifts in autonomic control and physiological complexity.
