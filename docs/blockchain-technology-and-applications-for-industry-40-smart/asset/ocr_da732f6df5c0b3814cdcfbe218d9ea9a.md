The latest block on the top of the blockchain is usually unstable, but there are more blocks on top of it, it is said to be guaranteed. As each new block is difficult to add (because of PoW), a block within the blockchain is said to be secure after one hour approximately, that is, when six blocks are added after it. This is why cryptocurrency exchanges and other services accept Bitcoin for the so-called blocks confirmation.

The last added block in the blockchain is considered unstable because it is quite possible (but very often) at that time, some miner can find another solution and thus cause a fork in the blockchain. In this situation, the blockchain is split into two competing paths forward. Forks in a BTC blockchain appear regular, and so far, each fork has quickly resolved when an additional block was added to one of these two arms of the blockchain (which was lucky to be first). At the same time, the entire network abandons the block from the other arm (the orphan) than since the winning arm contains more PoW. Hypothetically, if a fork remains unresolved, two competing blockchain histories would co-exist, compromising the whole system.

Theoretically, the Byzantine Problem could occur if several miners or a, very powerful attacker could try to control the BTC community by adding enough power or outperforming the entire power of the network (the called "51% attack" [8]). One could try to impose their version of the blockchain, that is, initiating fork so the last few (still unsolified) blocks in the blockchain are replaced by other blocks (in the new branch of the blockchain). These blocks might contain false transactions that nullify the transactions from the regular blockchain branch (causing double-spending) or corrupt the blockchain in another way. The attacker's version of the blockchain will be propagated through the network and accepted by the rest of the miners' community only if it contains more PoW than the regular version. This could happen only if the attacker wins a computing power sufficient to solve six consecutive blocks in a row (i.e., faster than the miners). The probability of such an event in a time becomes almost 100%, as the attacker's power comes close to 50% of the total mining power [9]. In 2014, six blocks were solved in a row by the same mining pool, which concentrated more than 40% of the total hash power owned by the entire mining community. But then, some of the miners from mining pool voluntarily moved to other pools to prevent unwanted effects.

An attacker might have significant power in a grey situation (somewhere between 30 and 50%), where there may be two or more different versions of a block in the network. Consequently, it would lead to the crash of the BTC. But this will not happen due to the introduced PoW, which significantly reduces the probability of solving six consecutive blocks by the same entity.
