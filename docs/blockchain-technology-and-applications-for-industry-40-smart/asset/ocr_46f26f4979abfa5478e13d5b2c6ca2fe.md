If the goal (for a given level of difficulty) is to compute a hash where, for example, the first 72 bits are zero (or in hexadecimal notation: hash = 00000000 00xxxxxx xxxxxxxx xxxxxxxx xxxxxxxx xxxxxxxx), and assuming a uniform distribution of hash values, the probability of success in one attempt would be $2^{-72} = 1/4,722,000,000,000,000,000$. With such a small probability, the time needed for a specialized mining computer (e.g., with the processing power of 16 Hash/s) to solely find the winning combination of nonce and hash would be more than nine years, by a rough estimate! Still, a new block is being added to the blockchain every ten minutes. How is it achieved?

The answer is in the enormous processing power by the combined effort of the computers of all miners in the world. The precise number of BTC miners is difficult to be determined, as it is always changing, with a growing trend. When they are not mining BTC, many use their computers for mining other cryptocurrencies as well, and a few are mining from time to time, for example, during the intervals when a miner's computer is not used for playing games. Eventually, the exact number of miners is not important, as the processing powers of their computers are different. On the other hand, however, it depends on the miners' readiness to invest in hardware, together with all expenses for electric power, among others.

These days, a greater number of miners are gathered in mining pools, as they are aware that mining alone is not profitable, because a block's verification process would take an unacceptable long time. Mining pools are joints of miners who acquire advantages such as more frequent verifications of blocks. Miners from a pool share the rewards for such verifications proportional to the miners' mining powers contributed to the pool. Apart from that, most BTC mining is done on the Application Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) in large, thermally-regulated data centers and warehouses with low-cost electricity. The owners of these warehouses rent the processing resources to miners.

As the world's mining power is increasingly growing, the is expected from the frequency of block verifications and the acceleration of BTC's issuance, they have not happened yet, due to the applied algorithm known as Difficulty, which adjusts the difficulty for solving the verification problem. The algorithm keeps the average block verification frequency almost constant. A new block is added to the blockchain every ten minutes approximately, within two weeks, 2016 blocks should be added to the blockchain. Every two weeks, the algorithm changes the value of the block header field named Difficulty (Fig. 1.2), according to the statistics of the total mining power and the number of transactions.

The value of the 32-bit Difficulty field corresponds (in a bit more complex) to the difficulty of finding the hash, which is small enough to satisfy the condition for block verification.
