Smart contracts are the second cornerstone of the DApp architecture (see Section 2.2 for details). The smart contract was coined in 1996, long before the appearance of the first blockchain networks, by Nick Szabo [27]. He regards smart contracts as contractual clauses, which are implemented in hardware and in such a way to make a breach of a contract prohibitively expensive. He pointed out vending machines and traditional payment or banking transaction systems as forefathers of modern DApps and smart contracts. Interestingly, from the principles in law, economic theory, and contractual conditions often found in practice, he outlined four basic objectives of the contract design [27]:

+   - Observability – the ability to prove one's or to observe other's performance of the contract

+   - Verifiability – proving to an arbitrator that a contract has been performed or breached

+   - Privity – minimizing the vulnerability to third parties and excluding them from the knowledge and control over the content and performance of the contract (privacy and confidentiality)

+   - Enforceability – minimizing the effort for enforcement, including self-enforcing protocols

Szabo weant many of the findings, which several years later, implemented his ideas in the BTC protocol and network that he is considered one of the candidates for the real BTC inventor. The real inventor remains unknown and is still known only by the pseudonym Satoshi Nakamoto. At some extent, the four mentioned objectives are met in many of the current DLTs and networks that reach beyond the simple value transactions and DApp development (Ethereum, Hedera Hashgraph, and Hyperledger).

Smart contracts are neither smart nor binding in the sense of legal obligations. However, in business collaborations, they are typically used to enforce some type of agreement, so that all participants can be certain of the outcome, without an intermediary's involvement [28]. Such an agreement, coded in the smart contract onchain logic, has no central point of failure, can perform operations, hold value, and unlock it only if specific conditions are met [29, 30].
