These equations are similar to those of a simple harmonic oscillator. We can verify that the general solutions to the equations, in (6.8), are

```
$v_x(t) = v_\perp \cos(\Omega_g t + \phi) \implies x(t) = x_0 + \frac{v_\perp}{\Omega_g} \sin(\Omega_g t + \phi) ,$

$v_y(t) = \mp v_\perp \sin(\Omega_g t + \phi) \implies y(t) = y_0 + \frac{v_\perp}{\Omega_g} \cos(\Omega_g t + \phi) ,$

$v_z(t) = v_{z0} = \text{constant} \implies z(t) = z_0 + v_{z0} t ,$
```

where $\Omega_g = (|q|B_z)/m$, $v_\perp$ is constrained by the initial velocity perpendicular to $B$, and $\pm$ accounts for positive or negative charge, $q$. We can also verify that

$v_\perp^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 , \quad v_{||} = v_z$

and

$[x(t) - x_0]^2 + [y(t) - y_0]^2 = r_g^2$

where $r_g = v_\perp/\Omega_g = (m v_\perp)/(|q|B_z)$, $v_\perp$ is the velocity associated with the circular motion of the charged particles around the magnetic field lines, and $v_{||}$ is the velocity associated with the motion of the charged particle along the magnetic field lines as depicted in Figure 6.8b. Setting $t = 0$ (6.9) and (6.10), we obtain $\phi = -v_y(0)/v_x(0)$. That is, the phase, $\phi$, is used to match the initial velocity. So a charged particle in a uniform magnetic field rotates in a circular path perpendicular to $B$-field lines around the point $(x_0, y_0, z)$. Figure 6.8b illustrates particle motion. The radius of the orbiting circle, $r_g$, is known as a gyroradius. All particles of the same species rotate at the same angular frequency (the angular gyrofrequency), $\Omega_g$. For example, protons and electrons rotate in opposite directions.
