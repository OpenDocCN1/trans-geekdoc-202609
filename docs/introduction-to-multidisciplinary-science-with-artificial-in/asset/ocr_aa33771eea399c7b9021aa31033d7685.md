So we can write a ≡ b (mod M) means where b = a - qM, q is some quotient M; b ∈ {0, ... M-1} is the remainder; e.g., consider the ratio 5/3, the quotient is 1/3 and the remainder is 2. The formal way to write this statement is 5 ≡ 2 (mod3). Notice that modular arithmetic of M is periodic. Notice also that if the gcd(M,M) > 1, then we have factorized M by pure luck. Let us look two more examples. Suppose that the factorization is a solution to the equation a² ≡ 1 (modM), where M is an odd integer and r=2, then
a² - 1 ≡ 0 (modM) ⇔ (a+1)(a-1) = 0 (modM)

Thus the factor of M is either gcd(a+1,M) or gcd(a-1,M). For M = 21 and a = 8, we have
8² = 64 ≡ 1 (mod21) ⇔ 8² - 1² = (8-1) × (8+1) ≡ 1 (mod21)
We cab deduce gcd(9,21) = 3 and gcd(7,21) = 7 as the prime factors of 21.

Let us consider a larger integer, M = 314191, and a random guess a = 127 with gcd(a,m) = gcd(127,314191) = 1. So a does not factorized M. Now we see r such that a² = 127^r ≡ 1 (mod 314191). By classical search, r = 17388. This implies that
127^17388 - 1 = 314191 ⇔ (127^17388/2 - 1) (127^17388/2 + 1) = 314191

We can deduce gcd(127^8694 + 1,314191) = 829 and gcd(127^8694 - 1,314191) = 379 are the two factors of M = 314191. Finding r for large integers, where r occurs as periodic bumps of interval r, is prohibitively expensive (or near impossible) by using classical computers. Shor's algorithm is designed to finding this period by using quantum computer since quantum computers run multiple steps simultaneously due to their ability to store qubits in superposition. Table 4.8 describes the key steps of the Shor's algorithm. It shows how the factorization is turn to the problem of period finding.

Period finding problem. Suppose we have a function f(r) that is periodic with period r. Its inverse Fourier transform ˆf is also periodic with period M/r. That is the key property on which finding the period for Shor's algorithm is based.
