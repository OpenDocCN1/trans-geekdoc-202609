As shown in Figure 6.22, a tokamak stands for “magnetic toroidal chamber” in Russian. Tokamaks were initially conceptualized in the 1950s by Soviet physicists Igor Tamm and Andrei Sakharov. The first working tokamak was attributed to the work of Natan Yavlinsky—that is, he created an interface between a 100 million-degree plasma and a room temperature wall. In a tokamak the magnetic field lines bend into circles so that they have no ends; that is the idea behind tokamaks.

As illustrated in Figure 6.22, tokamaks are torus (donut)-shaped fusion facilities where the charged particles are confined using very strong magnetic fields in a vacuum with the magnetic coils arranged in a closed ring thus avoiding end-losses. They contain powerful electromagnets that generate a very strong magnetic field. This main field component in a tokamak is the field in the toroidal direction (around the torus) which is generated by a set of identical toroidal field coils (arranged in the poloidal plane, i.e. the plane perpendicular to the toroidal direction). Usually 16 to 32 toroidal coils are employed. They have rotational symmetry around the vertical axis (which is here chosen to be the z-axis). Alone, the toroidal field cannot totally confine a plasma because the charged particles drift due to the magnetic field gradients and curvature which cause opposite vertical drifts for the differently charged species—ions drift up and electrons down. Electric fields result from the separation of charges and the loss of plasma. Before we discuss remedying this problem, let’s get a better understanding of these drifts.

(c) Coordinate system, (r,θ,φ), for plasma quantities and tokamak field lines. r is the radius, θ is the poloidal angle, φ is the toroidal angle, x = (R + r cos θ) cos φ, y = (R + r cos θ) sin φ, and z = r sin θ.

Basically, the toroidal magnetic field is curved and its strength is not homogeneous. The vertically projected view of the tokamak can be described as a circular curve in the z-plane with the center of the circle at R = 0 (see Figure 6.23). By integrating the Maxwell equation (Ampère’s law) over the enclosed surface (S), we arrive at

∇ × B_tor = μ0 J ⇒ ∫_S n · (∇ × B_tor) dA = μ0 ∫_S n · J dA

or

2π R B_tor = μ0 I , (6.67)

where n is the unit vector perpendicular to the surface and B = B_tor i_φ , the Stokes theorem has been applied to transform the surface integral into an integral along the curve, B_tor is the toroidal component of the magnetic field (i.e., the component in the direction of the unit vector i_r), and I is the total current flowing through the surface. This current is due to the current in the toroidal field coils that cross the surface S. We assume that I is constant so that B_tor = C / R, where C = μ0 I / (2π) is constant, thus the magnetic field varies as 1/R. The gradient of the toroidal magnetic field is

∇ B_tor = ∇ (C / R) = - C / R² i_r = - B_tor / R i_r ⇒ (B_tor × ∇ B_tor) / B_tor² = 1 / R i_r .

Thus the toroidal-magnetic field inhomogeneity causes the following drifts; i.e.,

$$
\boldsymbol{v}_d = \frac{mv_{\parallel}^2 + 0.5\,mv_{\perp}^2}{qB_{\mathrm{tor}}}\frac{\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{tor}}\times\nabla B_{\mathrm{tor}}}{B_{\mathrm{tor}}^2}
= \frac{mv_{\parallel}^2 + 0.5\,mv_{\perp}^2}{qB_{\mathrm{tor}}R}[\boldsymbol{i}_\phi\times(-\boldsymbol{i}_r)]
= \frac{mv_{\parallel}^2 + 0.5\,mv_{\perp}^2}{qB_{\mathrm{tor}}R}\boldsymbol{i}_z\ .\qquad(6.68)
$$

This drift leads to charge separation. Because the sign of drift depends on the sign of the charge $q$, ions and electrons drift parallel to the symmetry axis, in opposite directions, thus leaving their magnetic field lines and leading to an additional vertical electric field. A second drift appears now in the crossed-field arrangement of vertical electric and toroidal magnetic fields. In other words, an $\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{tor}}$ drift appears perpendicular to both $\boldsymbol{E}_{\mathrm{tor}} = -E_{\mathrm{tor}}\boldsymbol{i}_z$ and $\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{tor}} = B_{\mathrm{tor}}\boldsymbol{i}_\phi$ directions. This drift can be expressed as follows:

$$
\boldsymbol{v}_E = \frac{\boldsymbol{E}_{\mathrm{tor}}\times\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{tor}}}{B_{\mathrm{tor}}^2}
= -\frac{E_{\mathrm{tor}}}{B_{\mathrm{tor}}}(\boldsymbol{i}_z\times\boldsymbol{i}_\phi)
= -\frac{E_{\mathrm{tor}}}{B_{\mathrm{tor}}}\boldsymbol{i}_r\ .\qquad(6.69)
$$

It is directed outward and thus causes the plasma torus to expand radially. So the drifts in (6.68), associated with the gradient of $\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{tor}}$, set up an electric field and this electric field results in $\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{tor}}$ drifts, which drive plasma (electrons and ions) to the chamber wall, destroying the confinement.

Let us now turn our attention to the solution to the toroidal confinement problem. We adopt the idea of preventing the charge separation resulting from gradient-B drifts and thereby avoid the $\boldsymbol{E}_{\mathrm{tor}}\times\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{tor}}$ drift. The gradient-B drifts and resulting charge separation can be canceled out by twisting the toroidal field lines to form helices—that is, the twisting of the field lines in Figure 6.23. The basic idea is that the tokamak device includes a poloidal magnetic field along the toroidal magnetic field. The net magnetic field is $\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{tor}}+\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{pol}}$, where $\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{pol}}=B_{\mathrm{pol}}\boldsymbol{i}_\theta$ is the poloidal magnetic field. Let us verify that we indeed have spiral magnetic field lines by introducing $\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{pol}}$. The equation of motion of a particle generated by a poloidal magnetic field is:

$$
r\frac{d\theta}{dt}=\frac{B_{\mathrm{pol}}}{B_{\mathrm{tor}}}v_\phi
=\frac{B_{\mathrm{pol}}}{B_{\mathrm{tor}}}\frac{B_{\mathrm{tor}}}{B}v_{\parallel}
=\frac{B_{\mathrm{pol}}}{B}v_{\parallel}\qquad(6.70)
$$

and $r=$ constant, where $B^2 = B_{\mathrm{pol}}^2 + B_{\mathrm{tor}}^2$. Now add on to this motion the cross field drift in the $\boldsymbol{i}_z$ direction; i.e.,

$$
r\frac{d\theta}{dt}=\frac{B_{\mathrm{pol}}}{B}v_{\parallel}+v_d\cos\theta\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{dr}{dt}=v_d\sin\theta\ .\qquad(6.71)
$$

By eliminating time from these two equations we arrive at

$$
\frac{dr}{dt}\frac{d\theta}{dt}=\frac{dr}{d\theta}\implies\frac{dr}{r}=\frac{v_d\sin\theta}{(B_{\mathrm{pol}}/B)v_{\parallel}+v_d\cos\theta}d\theta\ .
$$

Let us assume $B_\theta$, $B$, $v_{\parallel}$, $v_d$ are constants. By integrating the above orbit equation, we obtain

$$
\ln r = -\ln\left|\frac{B_{\mathrm{pol}}}{B}v_{\parallel}+v_d\cos\theta\right|+C\ .\qquad(6.72)
$$

where C is the constant of integration. By setting r = r0 when θ = π/2, we arrive at

$$
r = r_0 \left[1 + \frac{B v_d}{B_{\mathrm{pol}} v_{\parallel}} \cos\theta\right]^{-1} \approx r_0 - \Delta_t \cos\theta, \qquad(6.73)
$$

where $\Delta_t = \frac{B v_d}{B_{\mathrm{pol}} v_{\parallel}} r_0$. The approximation here is that $\Delta_t / r_0 \ll 1$. Provided $\Delta_t / r_0$ is small, particles will be confined.

To summarize, the development of a tokamak comes down to a construct of toroids with field lines, which encircle the tori toroidally and poloidally and with a field line density, which is higher on the inside than on the outside (torus effect) so that charged particles (electrons and ions) that form the plasma spiral along magnetic field lines. As a result of trapped particles in magnetic confinement, more nuclear collisions occur, increasing fusion efficiency. The primary difficulty in magnetic confinement construction is reducing plasma leakages to insignificant levels.

Note that, as described in Ikelle (2023b), helium-3 can be fused with deuterium to form helium-4 and an energetic proton that can be used to produce electrical power. The challenge is obtaining helium-3 which is rare on Earth but plentiful in the gas giants, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune and in Earth’s Moon.

## Box 6.2: Some Nuclear Fusion Reactions

As discussed in Ikelle (2023b) and above, whenever the heavier element has a lower potential energy compared to the sum of potential energies of two separate nuclei, the fusion reaction is plausible. According to the experiment, iron with atomic number 26 has the lowest level of potential energy, so it would be the most stable nucleus. This shows that the fusion of lighter elements than iron always generates energy. But the released energy depends on the reaction cross section as well as the energy obtained from every individual reaction:

- ²₁D + ³₁T → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n (14.1 MeV) + energy (17.6 MeV)
- ²₁D + ²₁D ⇌ ³₂He + ¹₀n + energy (3.3 MeV), 50%
- ²₁D + ²₁D ⇌ ³₁T + ¹₁H + energy (4.0 MeV), 50%
- ²₁D + ³₂He → ¹₁H + ⁴₂He + energy (18.4 MeV)
- ³₂He + ³₂He → 2 ¹₁H + ⁴₂He + energy (12.9 MeV)
- ²₁D + ⁶₃Li → 2 ⁴₂He + energy (22.4 MeV)
- ¹₁H + ¹¹₅B → 3 ⁴₂He + energy (8.7 MeV)
