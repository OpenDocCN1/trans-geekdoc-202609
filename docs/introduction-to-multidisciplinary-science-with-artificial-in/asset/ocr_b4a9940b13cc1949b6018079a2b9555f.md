Although we cannot analytically solve (6.5) for the most general nonuniform magnetic fields, it can still be solved for some classes of nonuniform magnetic fields. We here describe the motion of charged particles in nonuniform magnetic fields for the case in which the magnetic field can be expressed as follows (Baumjohann and Treumann, 1997):

$$\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x},t) \approx \boldsymbol{B}_0 + \boldsymbol{B}_1(\boldsymbol{x},t) = \boldsymbol{B}_0 + (\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\,,\qquad(6.35)$$

with

$$\boldsymbol{B}_1(\boldsymbol{x},t) = (\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\,,\qquad(6.36)$$

where $\boldsymbol{B}_0$ is a uniform magnetic field and $|\boldsymbol{B}_1| = |(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{B}|$ is very small compared to $|\boldsymbol{B}_0|$. This approximation means that the magnetic field strength can be expanded in a Taylor expansion for distances $|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_0| < |\boldsymbol{x}_0|$, where $r_0 = |\boldsymbol{x}_0|$ is the gyroradius. We have retained only the first two terms of the Taylor series. We also decompose the particle velocity and position as follows:

$$\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{v}_0 + \delta\boldsymbol{v}\quad\text{and}\quad \boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{x}_0 + \delta\boldsymbol{x}\,,\qquad(6.37)$$

with

$$\boldsymbol{v}_0 = \frac{d\boldsymbol{x}_0}{dt}\quad\text{and}\quad \delta\boldsymbol{v} = \frac{d(\delta\boldsymbol{x})}{dt}\,,\qquad(6.38)$$

and with $|\delta\boldsymbol{v}| \ll |\boldsymbol{v}_0|$ and $|\delta\boldsymbol{x}| \ll |\boldsymbol{x}_0|$. By substituting these decompositions in (6.5) along with the assumption that $\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{0}$, we obtain

$$\begin{aligned}
m\frac{d\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)}{dt} &= q\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\times\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\\
&= q\big[\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}_0 + (\boldsymbol{v}_0+\delta\boldsymbol{v})\times(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{B}\big]\\
&\approx q(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}_0)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{grad}}(\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol{v}_0)
\end{aligned}\qquad(6.39)$$

with

$$\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{grad}}(\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol{v}_0)=\boldsymbol{v}_0\times(\boldsymbol{x}_0\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{B}\,.$$
\qquad(6.40)

All the second-order correction terms have been discarded because we are dealing with small changes.

Now we have to deal with the fact that $\boldsymbol{v}_0$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_0$ are periodic functions as described in the previous subsection by average $\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{grad}}(\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol{v}_0)$ over time and phase. Remember that, for $\boldsymbol{B}=B_z\boldsymbol{i}_z$, we have

$$\boldsymbol{x}_0 = [x_0 + r_0\sin(\Omega_0 t + \phi),\, y_0 \pm r_0\cos(\Omega_0 t + \phi),\, z_0 + v_{z0}t]^T\qquad(6.41)$$
