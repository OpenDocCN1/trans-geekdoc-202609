Let us consider a second example in which a classical apparatus measures the energy of a quantum system. The apparatus will measure the energy as if a classical system were substituted for the quantum system. After the measurement has been performed, the microscopic system survives the measurement, and even if we were to immediately remeasure the same quantity, we would get the same result.

To recap, an observable is any property of a system that can be measured; e.g., spin, polarization, position, momentum, energy, and angular momentum, magnetic moment, and so on. In classical mechanics, all observables are functions of generalized coordinates and momenta. So a knowledge of the physical state implies a knowledge of all possible observables of the system. In quantum mechanics, for every observable there exists a corresponding hermitian operator. In the next section, we will define the rule according to which knowing a physical state at some point t implies knowing the expectation value of every observable.
