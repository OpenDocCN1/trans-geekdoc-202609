We can then disregard all other charges except, of course, those charges and currents that exist somewhere to produce the fields we will assume to exist in the region under consideration. In other words, we consider a model in which the motion of each particle is treated individually and collisions between particles are infrequent. This model is generally valid in a plasmatic atmosphere, very far from the Earth’s surface, because deep-space plasmas are generally collisionless such as galaxies and dark matter halos. More importantly, the dark matter that we will discuss in the next chapter may also obey this model. Also plasma in stars in a galaxy and grains in Saturn’s rings may also obey this model.

What are specific examples of places in space where the collisionless model of charged particles holds? As described in Ikelle (2023a and 2023b) and in Figure 6.7, the ionosphere is a layer of plasma formed by stripping electrons from atomic oxygen and nitrogen by highly energetic ultraviolet and X-ray solar radiation. Again, the process of stripping electrons from atoms is known as ionization. The ionosphere ranges from 70- to about 600-km altitude (from the middle of the mesosphere up to the magnetosphere), and the atmospheric layers below the ionosphere remain mostly neutral. At altitudes of 400 kilometers and above, collisions between particles are rare. This part of the ionosphere is an example of a location in space where the collisionless model of charged particles holds.
