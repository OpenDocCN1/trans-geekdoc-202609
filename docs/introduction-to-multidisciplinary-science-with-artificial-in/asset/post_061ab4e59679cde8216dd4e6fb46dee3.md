$$\tilde{x}_b = x_b + \frac{i}{\omega}\int_0^{x_b} d_b(\eta)d\eta \quad (b=1,2,3), \tag{A.101}$$

where $d_b(x_b)$ is the decay factor. We assume that the decay factor is zero [i.e., $d_b(x_b)=0$] inside the main computational domain and positive [i.e., $d_b(x_b)>0$] in the absorbing region. The typical form of the decay factor is

$$d_b(x_b) = \left(\frac{x_b}{L}\right)^2 d_0 \tag{A.102}$$

with

$$d_0 = \frac{3V_{P,\max}\log(R_c)}{2L}, \tag{A.103}$$

where $L$ is the thickness of the PML layer and $R_c$ is the target theoretical reflection coefficient, generally chosen as 0.001.

By writing the spatial derivatives of the stresses and the particle-velocity equations (A.43) and (A.44), as follows:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{x}_b} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_b}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_b}, \tag{A.104}$$

with

$$\varepsilon_b(x_b,\omega) = 1 + i\frac{d_b(x_b)}{\omega}, \tag{A.105}$$

and by taking the Fourier transform of equations (A.43) and (A.44) with respect to time, we arrive at

$$-i\omega v_a(\mathbf{x},\omega) = \sigma(\mathbf{x})\frac{1}{\varepsilon_b(x_b,\omega)}\frac{\partial\tau_{ab}(\mathbf{x},\omega)}{\partial x_b} \tag{A.106}$$

$$-i\omega\tau_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{x},\omega) = c_{\alpha\beta\eta\zeta}(\mathbf{x})\frac{1}{\varepsilon_\eta(x_\eta,\omega)}\frac{\partial\nu_\zeta(\mathbf{x},\omega)}{\partial x_\eta}. \tag{A.107}$$

Notice that rather than defining a new symbol to express this physical quantity after it has been Fourier-transformed, we have used the same symbol with different arguments, as the context unambiguously indicates the quantity currently under consideration. For example, $v_a(\mathbf{x},\omega)$ is the Fourier transform of $v_a(\mathbf{x},t)$ with respect to time. We will use this convention for the remaining part of this section. Finally, notice that we have not included the source terms here because they are generally zero in the absorbing regions.

To facilitate the algebra of the PML equations in (A.106) and (A.107), we found it convenient to redefine the stress field so that the system of equations in (A.43) and (A.44) and the ones in (A.106) and (A.107) can have the same mathematical structures. In other words, we can rewrite equations (A.106) and (A.107) as follows:

$$-i\omega v_a(\mathbf{x},\omega) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_3}\sigma(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\tilde{\tau}_{ab}(\mathbf{x},\omega)}{\partial x_b} \tag{A.108}$$

$-i\omega\tilde{\tau}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=\frac{\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_3}{\varepsilon_\beta\varepsilon_\eta}c_{\alpha\beta\xi\eta}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial v_\zeta(\mathbf{x},\omega)}{\partial x_\eta}$ , (A.109)

with

$\tilde{\tau}_{ab}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=\frac{\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_3}{\varepsilon_b}\tau_{ab}(\mathbf{x},\omega)$ . (A.110)

Note that the tensor $\tilde{\tau}$ is nonsymmetric (i.e., $\tilde{\tau}_{ab}\neq\tilde{\tau}_{ba}$). We can also notice that $\tilde{\tau}=\tau$ across the boundary between the non-PML region and the PML region, which means that $\tilde{\tau}$ is just a continuation of $\tau$ in the PML region. Therefore, we can replace $\tilde{\tau}$ by $\tau$ in equations (A.108)-(A.109) and arrive at the following PML equations:

$-i\omega v_a(\mathbf{x},\omega)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_3}\sigma(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\tau_{ab}(\mathbf{x},\omega)}{\partial x_b}$ (A.111)

$-i\omega\tau_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=\frac{\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_3}{\varepsilon_\beta\varepsilon_\eta}c_{\alpha\beta\xi\eta}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial v_\zeta(\mathbf{x},\omega)}{\partial x_\eta}$ . (A.112)

Because our finite-difference modeling is carried out in the time domain, we have to transform equations (A.111)-(A.112) back to the time domain by taking their inverse Fourier transforms with respect to time. Due to the frequency dependence of the coefficients $\varepsilon_j=\varepsilon_j(x_j,\omega)$, time-convolution operators will appear in the resulting equations. These time-convolution operators must be eliminated or modified to accommodate for the fact that our finite-difference solution here is recursive in time (i.e., the computation of the wavefield values at a given time depends explicitly on the wavefield values at earlier times), but the standard time-convolution operations are not recursive. They are summations over time. One way to eliminate the time-convolution operators from the PML equations is to factorize equations (A.111)-(A.112) into new equations, with only one factor $\varepsilon_b$ per equation. To illustrate this approach, let us consider the following expression:

$w_{ab}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=\varepsilon_b(x_b,\omega)v_a(\mathbf{x},\omega)=\left[1+i\frac{d_b(x_b)}{\omega}\right]v_a(\mathbf{x},\omega)$ , (A.113)

which is a convolution in the time domain. By multiplying this expression by $-i\omega$, we eliminate the convolution operator; i.e.,

$-i\omega w_{ab}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=-i\omega v_a(\mathbf{x},\omega)+d_b(x_b)v_a(\mathbf{x},\omega)$ , (A.114)

which corresponds to

$\frac{\partial w_{ab}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial v_a(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t}+d_b(x_b)v_a(\mathbf{x},t)$ . (A.115)

This idea was proposed by Oskooi and Johnson (2011) for the PML solutions of Maxwell's equations. To extend this idea to the elastic case, we need to factorize equations (A.111)-(A.112). Whereas the factorization of (A.111) is obvious, that of (A.112) requires more attention because of the presence of the ratio of damping
factors in this equation. Actually, this ratio is a product of two damping factors; i.e.,

$$\frac{\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_3}{\varepsilon_\eta} = \varepsilon'_\eta\varepsilon''_\eta = \begin{cases} \varepsilon_2\varepsilon_3 & \text{if } \eta = 1 \\ \varepsilon_3\varepsilon_1 & \text{if } \eta = 2 \\ \varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2 & \text{if } \eta = 3 \end{cases}$$ (A.116)

with $\varepsilon'_\eta \equiv \{\varepsilon_2, \varepsilon_3, \varepsilon_1\}$ and $\varepsilon''_\eta \equiv \{\varepsilon_3, \varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2\}$. This idea was proposed by Oskooi and Johnson (2011) for the PML solutions of Maxwell's equations. By using equations (A.111)-(A.112), we can factorize equations (A.111) and (A.112) as follows:

$$-i\omega\varepsilon_1 w_{a1}(\mathbf{x},\omega) = \sigma(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial \tau_{ab}(\mathbf{x},\omega)}{\partial x_b} ,$$ (A.117)

$$-i\omega\varepsilon_2 w_{a2}(\mathbf{x},\omega) = -i\omega w_{a1}(\mathbf{x},\omega) ,$$ (A.118)

$$-i\omega\varepsilon_3 v_a(\mathbf{x},\omega) = -i\omega w_{a2}(\mathbf{x},\omega) ,$$ (A.119)

$$-i\omega e_{\xi\eta;1}(\mathbf{x},\omega) = -i\omega\varepsilon'_\eta\frac{\partial v_\zeta(\mathbf{x},\omega)}{\partial x_\eta} ,$$ (A.120)

$$-i\omega e_{\xi\eta;2}(\mathbf{x},\omega) = -i\omega\varepsilon''_\eta e_{\xi\eta;1}(\mathbf{x},\omega) ,$$ (A.121)

$$-i\omega\varepsilon_\beta \tau_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{x},\omega) = c_{\alpha\beta\zeta\eta}(\mathbf{x}) e_{\xi\eta;2}(\mathbf{x},\omega) .$$ (A.122)

By taking the inverse Fourier transform of these equations with respect to time, we can deduce the PML equations in the time domain; i.e.,

$$\frac{\partial w_{a1}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} + d_1(x_1) w_{a1}(\mathbf{x},t) = \sigma(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial \tau_{ab}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_b} ,$$ (A.123)

$$\frac{\partial \tau_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} + d_{\beta}(x_{\beta})\tau_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{x},t) = c_{\alpha\beta\gamma\eta}(\mathbf{x})e_{\gamma\eta;2}(\mathbf{x},t) , \tag{A.128}$$

where d' = [d₂(x₂), d₃(x₃), d₁(x₁)]ᵀ and d'' = [d₃(x₃), d₁(x₁), d₂(x₂)]ᵀ. These equations can be solved by using the finite-difference operators in (A.54) and (A.59), just as we did earlier in the non-PML equations in (A.43) and (A.44).

Let us now look at a numerical example of PML boundary conditions. We start by recalling the absorbing boundaries introduced by Cerjan et al. (1985), which was until recently the preferred method. The reason why we are recalling this solution is that the contrast between this Cerjan's solution and the PML solution provides a good indication of the accuracy and effectiveness of the PML solution. The Cerjan's boundary conditions, also known as the damping boundary conditions, are created by surrounding the numerical model with a strip of grids. The stress and particle-velocity fields are multiplied by the factor

$$G(i) = \exp\left\{ -\left[\frac{\alpha}{iabmax}(iabmax - i)\right]^2\right\} \quad \text{for } 1 \le i \le iabmax , \tag{A.129}$$

where iabmax is the strip width in gridpoints and α is a constant determined by trial and error for the optimal absorbing-boundary conditions. For iabmax = 60 gridpoints, the optimum value of α is 0.3. The results in Figure A.4 are based on these values. The model in this example is 2D with a line source.

Let us now turn to the PML boundary conditions. First of all, we note that in a 2D medium with a line source, the equations (A.123)-(A.128) reduce to

$$\frac{\partial w_1(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} + d_1(x_1)w_1(\mathbf{x},t) = \sigma(\mathbf{x})\left[ \frac{\partial \tau_{11}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_1} + \frac{\partial \tau_{13}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_3} \right] \tag{A.130}$$

$$\frac{\partial w_3(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} + d_1(x_1)w_3(\mathbf{x},t) = \sigma(\mathbf{x})\left[ \frac{\partial \tau_{31}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_1} + \frac{\partial \tau_{33}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_3} \right] \tag{A.131}$$

$$\frac{\partial v_1(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} + d_3(x_3)v_1(\mathbf{x},t) = \frac{\partial w_1(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} \tag{A.132}$$ $$\frac{\partial v_3(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} + d_3(x_3)v_3(\mathbf{x},t) = \frac{\partial w_3(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} \tag{A.133}$$ $$\frac{\partial e_{11}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[ \frac{\partial v_1(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_1} \right] + d_3(x_3)\left[ \frac{\partial v_1(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_1} \right]$$ $$\frac{\partial e_{13}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[ \frac{\partial v_1(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_3} \right] + d_1(x_1)\left[ \frac{\partial v_1(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_3} \right]$$ $$\frac{\partial e_{31}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[ \frac{\partial v_3(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_1} \right] + d_3(x_3)\left[ \frac{\partial v_3(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_1} \right] \tag{A.134}$$
