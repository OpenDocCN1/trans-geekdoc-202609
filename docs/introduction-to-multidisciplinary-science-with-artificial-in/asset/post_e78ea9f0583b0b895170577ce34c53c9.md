Another important EM equation that we described in the previous chapter is the continuity equation. It describes the fact that charge can neither be created nor destroyed. As we impose that the time derivatives are zeros, thus

\[
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0 \qquad (3.91)
\]

The currents described by this equation are called steady currents. A steady current refers to a continuous flow that has been going on forever, without change and without charge filling up anywhere. Thus steady currents produce magnetic fields that are constant in time. We are interested in electrostatics and magnetostatics because, in most part, electromagnetic effects on molecules, including on greenhouse gases, can be described by electrostatics, some well-logging studies for subsurface exploration can be described by magnetostatics, and the geomagnetic field that we will discuss in Ikelle (2024), also changes slowly over time and thus can be described by magnetostatics.

We will start our study of electrostatics by investigating the two fundamental laws governing electrostatic fields: Coulomb’s law and Gauss’s law. Both of these laws are based on experimental studies. Although Coulomb’s law is applicable to finding the electric field due to any charge configuration, it is easier to use Gauss’s law when the charge distribution is symmetrical. Special problems can be solved with much effort using Coulomb’s law, but will be solved with ease by applying Gauss’s law.

## The electrostatic force law and the electric field.

We begin with the electrostatic force between two point charges (see Figure 3.13a). This force controls the separation of electric charges which is associated with electrostatic effects.

![](img/f5b5b8709fc3a81631262f22d3552c5e_362_0.png)

**Figure 3.13.** (a) Geometry of Coulomb interaction between two charges. Coulomb’s law describes how a static electric charge, \(q\), located at a point, \(\mathbf{x}\), relative to the origin, \(O\), experiences an electrostatic force from a static electric charge, \(q_1\), located at \(\mathbf{x}_0\). Like charges repel and unlike charges attract. (b) An illustration of the electric field intensity due to each of the charge distributions \(\rho(\mathbf{x}')\). The electric field due to \(\rho(\mathbf{x}')\) is a superposition of the fields due to the numerous point charges.

Given two such charges, \(q\) and \(q_1\), at positions \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{x}_1\), respectively, the force, \(\mathbf{F}_1\), acting on an electrically charged particle with charge \(q\), located at \(\mathbf{x}\), due to the presence of a charge \(q_1\) located at \(\mathbf{x}_1\) is given by the expression (Coulomb’s law)

\[
\mathbf{F}_1(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q q_1}{|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_1|^3} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_1) \qquad (3.92)
\]

where \(\varepsilon_0\) is the permittivity of free space (vacuum). Note that this formula only makes sense if \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{x}_1\) are in different positions. The force on \(q\) due to \(q_1\) is equal in magnitude but opposite in direction to the force on \(q_1\) due to \(q\) (like charges repel and unlike charges attract). This formula assumes that the electric charges are in a vacuum or in free space. When the electric charges are placed in a dielectric material, we simply have to replace the permittivity of free space, \(\varepsilon_0\), with the permittivity of the dielectric material, \(\varepsilon\). Note also that this force is the same if the particles are stationary or moving.

Without loss of generality, we might as well put the second charge at the origin \(\mathbf{x}_1 = 0\), and the force, \(\mathbf{F}_1\), acting on an electrically charged particle with charge \(q\) located at \(\mathbf{x}\), due to the presence of a charge \(q_1\) located at \(\mathbf{x}_1 = 0\) is given by the expression

\[
\mathbf{F}_1(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q q_1}{|\mathbf{x}|^3}\mathbf{x} \qquad (3.93)
\]

This force is known as Coulomb’s law of electrostatics, and is an experimental fact. It does not depend on the velocity of the particles and is the same if the particles are stationary or moving. Note that the magnitude of this force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance of separation of the two charges, \(1/|\mathbf{x}|^2\), and why? For light and sound intensity, the inverse-square law is a result of the energy being equally distributed on a spherical surface. Since the area of a sphere is \(4\pi|\mathbf{x}|^2\), the sound or light intensity decreases as \(1/|\mathbf{x}|^2\). For electrostatic forces there is nothing that is spread out evenly over a spherical surface. Their dependence on the \(1/|\mathbf{x}|^2\) law is a result of the geometry of space.

If we have \(N\) charges \(q_i\) at positions \(\mathbf{x}_i\), \(i = 1, \ldots, N\), then an additional charge \(q\) at position \(\mathbf{x}\) experiences this force

\[
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{q_i}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_i|^3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_i) \qquad (3.94)
\]

So, to get the total force on charge \(q\) due to all the other charges, we simply add up (superpose) the Coulomb force from each charge \(q_i\). In other words, electrostatic forces obey the principle of superposition. We define the electrostatic intensity field, \(\mathbf{E}\), to be the force on a unit test charge (i.e., \(q = 1\)) at position \(\mathbf{x}\):

\[
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = q\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}) \Longleftrightarrow \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^{N} q_i \frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_i)}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_i|^3} \qquad (3.95)
\]

Thus the electric field, which is a fundamental quantity in electromagnetic theory, as described in the previous section, is just a mathematically convenient way of describing the force on a unit test charge. Note that, in contrast to the previous section, the electric field here is analytically defined and time-independent. Generally, it depends on the position and the charges. That is a result of electrostatic approximation. So far, we have only considered forces and electric fields due to point charges, which are essentially charges occupying a very small physical space, that is, at the microscopic scale. Again, at macroscopic scale we use the concept of charge density introduced in the previous section. Let \(\rho_E(\mathbf{x})\) be the charge density per unit volume. The electric field intensity due to each of the charge distributions \(\rho_E(\mathbf{x})\) may be regarded as the summation of the field contributed by the numerous point charges.

As \(\mathbf{E}\) is a gradient its curl is zero (\(\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = 0\); Faraday’s law), and we thus deduce that Coulomb’s electric field also satisfies Faraday’s law in addition to Gauss’s law (i.e., the two Maxwell’s equations of electrostatics are satisfied). Note that the electrostatic potential falls as \(1/|\mathbf{x}|\) while the electric field intensity decreases as \(1/|\mathbf{x}|^2\). Note also that the electrostatic potential is zero “at infinity.”

From Gauss’s law and electrical potential, we have

\[
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\rho_E(\mathbf{x})}{\varepsilon_0} \iff \nabla^2 \phi_E(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\rho_E(\mathbf{x})}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad (3.104)
\]

which is called Poisson’s equation. It reduces to Laplace’s equation when \(\rho_E = 0\); i.e.,

\[
\nabla^2 \phi_E(\mathbf{x}) = 0 \qquad (3.105)
\]

We described the solution to this equation in Chapter 1.
