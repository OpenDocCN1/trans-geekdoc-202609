Figure 1.11. We consider the area swept by a planet orbiting the Sun in a given time interval. (a) In its orbit around the Sun, the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. It travels faster and faster as it gets closer to the Sun. (b) The triangular area \(dA\) is the area swept as the planet travels an infinitesimal arc \(r\,d\theta\). This area is one-half the product of the radius \(r\) and the arc \(r\,d\theta\).

Therefore, the proof of the second law is equivalent to showing that \(dA/dt\) is constant; which is our task here.

Let us start by introducing a physical quantity known as angular momentum. It measures the quantity of rotation of a rotating body just like linear momentum (simply known as momentum) measures the quantity of motion of a moving body. The angular momentum of a mass \(m_2\) with position vector \(\mathbf{x}\) and velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{x} \times (m_2 \mathbf{v})\), where \(\times\) is the cross product defined in Ikelle (2023b). The derivative of \(\mathbf{L}\) with respect to time is

\[
\frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} \times (m_2 \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{x} \times \left(m_2 \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\right)
= \mathbf{x} \times (m_2 \boldsymbol{\gamma}) = \mathbf{x} \times \mathbf{F} = 0. \tag{1.36}
\]

In these derivations we used the fact that the cross product of two parallel vectors is zero. Because \(\mathbf{F}\) is collinear with \(\mathbf{x}\) (i.e., the force is along the direction of the line joining the centers of the Sun and the orbiting planet), \(d\mathbf{L}/dt\) is zero and therefore \(L = |\mathbf{L}|\) is a constant with respect to time, which means in physics that angular momentum is conserved. The other implication of this result is that the orbiting planet always moves in the plane (two-dimensional space) spanned by its velocity, \(\mathbf{v}\), and the position vector, \(\mathbf{x}\), as described in Figure 1.12.

In this plane, the position vector of the orbiting planet is \(\mathbf{x} = r \mathbf{i}_r\) with \(\mathbf{i}_r = [\cos\theta, \sin\theta]^T\) being the unit vector in the direction of the vector position of the orbiting planet (see Figure 1.12). The vector \(\mathbf{i}_\theta = [-\sin\theta, \cos\theta]^T\) is the unit vector orthogonal to the direction of the vector position of the orbiting planet.

![](img/f5b5b8709fc3a81631262f22d3552c5e_37_0.png)

Figure 1.12. (a) We consider the position vector and velocity vector, \(\mathbf{v}\), in a plane containing an orbiting planet and the Sun. We also introduce polar coordinates: \(\mathbf{i}_r = [\cos\theta, \sin\theta]^T\) is the unit vector in the direction of the vector position of the orbiting planet. The vector \(\mathbf{i}_\theta = [-\sin\theta, \cos\theta]^T\) is the unit vector orthogonal to the direction of the vector position of the orbiting planet. (b) Another significant vector associated with this plane is the angular momentum vector, \(\mathbf{L}\), which is the scaled cross-product of the position vector and the velocity of the orbiting planet. The position vector is orthogonal to the angular momentum vector.

The velocity vector of the orbiting planet, \(\mathbf{v}\), is the time derivative of the vector position and can be written as

\[
\mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \frac{dr}{dt}\mathbf{i}_r + r\frac{d\mathbf{i}_r}{d\theta}\frac{d\theta}{dt}
= \frac{dr}{dt}\mathbf{i}_r + r\frac{d\theta}{dt}\mathbf{i}_\theta. \tag{1.37}
\]

The magnitude of the angular momentum vector of the orbiting planet is

\[
\frac{L}{2m_2} = \frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{x} \times \mathbf{v}| = \frac{1}{2}r^2\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{dA}{dt}. \tag{1.38}
\]

Therefore \(dA/dt\) is constant because \(L\) is constant. We can now deduce that the area swept out by the planet’s orbit between time \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) is

\[
A_{12} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{dA}{dt}\,dt
= \frac{1}{2}\int_{t_1}^{t_2}r^2\frac{d\theta}{dt}\,dt
= \frac{1}{2m_2}L(t_2 - t_1). \tag{1.39}
\]

The area swept out by the same planet between time \(t_3\) and \(t_4\) is

\[
A_{34} = \int_{t_3}^{t_4}\frac{dA}{dt}\,dt
= \frac{1}{2}\int_{t_3}^{t_4}r^2\frac{d\theta}{dt}\,dt
= \frac{1}{2m_2}L(t_4 - t_3). \tag{1.40}
\]

If \(t_2 - t_1 = t_4 - t_3\), then \(A_{12} = A_{34}\) because the magnitude of the angular momentum of the orbiting planet, \(L\), is constant. This conclusion proves Kepler’s second law. Also we can clearly see that Kepler’s second law is really a statement of the conservation of angular momentum. Note that the key assumption made in these derivations is that the Sun is not moving and lies at the origin of our coordinate system.

## Proof of the first law.

Our next task is to prove the first law, which says that the planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun being at one of the focuses. By using the heliocentric diagram with the polar coordinates, in Figure 1.12a, and the definition of the velocity vector of the orbiting planet, in (1.37), we can define the acceleration vector, \(\boldsymbol{\gamma} = d\mathbf{v}/dt\), which is the second time derivative of the vector position, as

\[
\boldsymbol{\gamma} = \left[\frac{d^2 r}{dt^2} - r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2\right]\mathbf{i}_r
+ \left[2\frac{dr}{dt}\frac{d\theta}{dt} + r\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\right]\mathbf{i}_\theta
= -\frac{G m_1}{r^2}\mathbf{i}_r + 0\,\mathbf{i}_\theta. \tag{1.42}
\]

Because the gravity force \(\mathbf{F} = m_2 \boldsymbol{\gamma} = -\frac{G m_2 m_1}{r^2}\mathbf{i}_r\) is collinear with the position vector of the orbiting planet, \(\mathbf{x} = r\mathbf{i}_r\), the \(\theta\)-component of the acceleration vector of the orbiting planet is zero and the radial component of the acceleration vector of the orbiting planet is nonzero; i.e.,

\[
\frac{d^2 r}{dt^2} = r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2 - \frac{G m_1}{r^2}
= \frac{L^2}{m_2^2 r^3} - \frac{G m_1}{r^2}. \tag{1.43}
\]

\[
2\frac{dr}{dt}\frac{d\theta}{dt} + r\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}
= \frac{d}{dt}\left(r^2\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right) = 0
\Longrightarrow \frac{L}{m_2} = r^2\frac{d\theta}{dt}. \tag{1.44}
\]

We can see from equation (1.44) that the \(\theta\)-component of the acceleration vector provides an alternative way of showing that the magnitude of the angular momentum vector of the orbiting planet is constant. Our focus here is on equation (1.43). As we will see later the solution to this equation is an ellipse and therefore useful in the proof of the first Kepler’s law.

By using the following identity

\[
\frac{d}{dt} = \frac{d}{d\theta}\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{L B^2}{m_2}\frac{d}{d\theta}, \tag{1.45}
\]

with \(B = 1/r\), we can rewrite equation (1.43)

\[
\frac{d^2 r}{dt^2} = \frac{L^2}{m_2^2 r^3} - \frac{G m_1}{r^2}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{d^2 B}{d\theta^2} + B = \frac{G m_1 m_2^2}{L^2}. \tag{1.46}
\]

We can verify that the solution of this equation is

\[
r = \frac{1}{B} = \frac{k}{1 + \varepsilon \cos\theta}, \quad \text{or} \quad r = k - \varepsilon x, \tag{1.47}
\]

where

\[
k = \frac{L^2}{G m_1 m_2^2} \quad \text{and} \quad
\varepsilon = \frac{L^2}{G m_1 m_2^2} C, \tag{1.48}
\]

and where \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(C\) is a constant of integration. We can also verify that the solution in (1.47) is an ellipse; i.e.,

\[
\frac{(x + c)^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1. \tag{1.49}
\]

by setting

\[
c = \frac{k\varepsilon}{1 - \varepsilon^2}, \quad a = \frac{k}{1 - \varepsilon^2}, \quad \text{and} \quad b = \frac{k}{\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2}}. \tag{1.50}
\]

This equation and associated parameters are illustrated in Figure 1.10. Semi-major and semi-minor axes are \(a\) and \(b\), respectively.

## Proof of the third law with elliptic orbits.

We have already shown that the time it takes a planet to make one complete orbit around the Sun (one planet year) is related to the radius of circular orbits. We now extend this proof to elliptic orbits. The area traced out in one period of the orbit, which is the time to travel around the entire ellipse, is,

\[
A = \int_0^T \frac{dA}{dt}\,dt = \frac{1}{2m_2}LT
\Rightarrow T = \left(\frac{2m_2}{L}\right)A = \left(\frac{2m_2}{L}\right)\pi a b, \tag{1.51}
\]

where \(A = \pi a b\) is the area of the ellipse bounded by the planetary orbit. We now know that \(b^2 = a^2(1-\varepsilon^2)\) and \(a = k/(1-\varepsilon^2)\). We can deduce the third law;

\[
\frac{T^2}{a^3} = \frac{4\pi^2}{G m_1} = \text{constant}. \tag{1.52}
\]
