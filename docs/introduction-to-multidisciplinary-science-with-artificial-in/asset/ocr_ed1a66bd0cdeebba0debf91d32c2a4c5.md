We have assumed that μ is constant. If the gas is adiabatic, we also know that P = Kργ, where γ = cp/cv is the ratio of specific heats. Thus, we also have

dP = K γ ρ^{γ-1} dρ/dr = γ (P/ρ) dρ/dr . (2.146)

By equating (2.145) and (2.146), we arrive at

(dT/dr)_adiab = (1 - 1/γ) (T/P) dP/dr . (2.147)

This value of dT/dr here is the adiabatic temperature gradient and the value of dT/dr in (2.141) is the star temperature gradient. An alternative form is

(dT/dr)_adiab = (1 - 1/γ) (T/P) dP/dT dr

or

∇_adiab = (d log T / d log P)_adiab = γ/(γ - 1) (T/P) dP/dT . (2.148)

For an ideal gas, we have γ = 5/3, so ∇_adiab = [(5/3) - 1]/(5/3) = 0.4. This value is important in the analysis of energy transport through stars.

# Energy transport by radiation and convection.

The energy that a star radiates from its surface is generally replenished from the star's core which is the heat source. The outward energy flux at every dr requires an effective means of transporting energy through the star. Radiation heat transports via photon motions is often the most important means of energy transport in the stars. However, convection transports heat via bulk motions of large parcels of gas also occur in the stars. As described earlier for the sun, the radiation mechanism takes 50 million years to transport energy through the radiation zone. In contrast, the convection mechanism takes just hours through the convective regions. So by predicting the time heat takes to pass through various regions of the star's interiors, we can determine region of stars over which the energy transport is carried by radiation or convection.
