Let us now consider a solid sphere. A solid sphere is just lots of hollow spheres nested together like layers of an onion. Therefore, the field at an external point is just the same as if all the mass was concentrated at the center (see Figure 1.8). This is true not only for a sphere of uniform density, but for any sphere in which the density depends only on the distance from the center, i.e., any spherically symmetric distribution of matter. This justifies Newton’s treatment of the Sun and planets as point masses when analyzing planetary motion.

Inside the solid sphere (i.e., r ≤ R), the mass m1r contained in the volume Vr with radius r is (see Figure 1.9)

$$m_{1r} = \rho V_r = \rho \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = \rho \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right)\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^3 = m_1 \left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^3\,,\qquad (1.19)$$
