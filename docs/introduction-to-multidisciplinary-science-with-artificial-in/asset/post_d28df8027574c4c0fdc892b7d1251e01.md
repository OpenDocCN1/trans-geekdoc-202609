
example, the majority of the negative charges inside cells, which are proteins, cannot get out—that is, the membrane is not permeable to proteins because they are too big (proteins are large molecules). Cations Na⁺ are more concentrated outside than inside and are driven into the cell by the electrical potential difference across the membrane through Na⁺ channels. Again, the resting membrane potential corresponds to Na⁺ and Cl⁻ more concentrated outside the cell, and K⁺ and organic anions (organic acids and proteins) more concentrated inside. The combination of concentration gradients and a semi-permeable membrane creates an electrical potential.

Membrane potential between the interior and the exterior of a cell is particularly important to neurons, because rapid changes in the membrane resting potential of neurons produce the nervous impulse, which is the basis of neuronal signaling. In muscle cells, changes in the membrane potential cause contraction. In endocrine cells, changes in the membrane potential result in the release of hormones. In non-excitable cells, such as glia, epithelial cells, and adipose cells (and others), the resting membrane potential does not change appreciably over time.

The Nernst equation defines the electrochemical equilibrium between a concentration gradient and an electrical potential (i.e., the total energy inside the cell is equal to the total energy outside), for one particular ion. The electrical potential that exactly balances a concentration gradient is called the Nernst potential (also called the reversal potential). The Nernst equation reveals how the reversal potential of an ion depends on the inside and outside concentrations of this ion. It is a simple algebraic formula to calculate membrane potential as follows (Nernst, 1888 and 1889):

$$
z F V_{\mathrm{in}} + RT \ln[\mathrm{ion}]_{\mathrm{in}} = z F V_{\mathrm{out}} + RT \ln[\mathrm{ion}]_{\mathrm{out}}
$$

or

$$
\Delta V = V_{\mathrm{in}} - V_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{RT}{zF} \ln\frac{[\mathrm{ion}]_{\mathrm{out}}}{[\mathrm{ion}]_{\mathrm{in}}} \approx 27 \frac{1}{z} \ln\frac{[\mathrm{ion}]_{\mathrm{out}}}{[\mathrm{ion}]_{\mathrm{in}}}\ \mathrm{mV}, \tag{5.1}
$$

where \(R\) is the gas constant, \(T\) is the absolute temperature (in Kelvin), \(F\) is Faraday's constant [\(9.649 \times 10^4\) C/mol], and \(z\) is the valence of the ion (\(z = 1\) for cations and \(z = -1\) for anions), \(\mathrm{ion}_{\mathrm{out}}\) and \(\mathrm{ion}_{\mathrm{in}}\) are chemical concentrations of outside (extracellular) and inside (intracellular) of the cell, respectively. We used \(T = 310\) degrees Kelvin \(\approx 37\) degrees Celsius for the approximation in (5.1). Note that, in bioelectromagnetism, the quantity of ions is usually expressed in moles (one mole equals the molecular weight in grams and contains \(6.0225 \times 10^{23}\) molecules, Avogadro's number of molecules) and Faraday's constant converts quantity of moles to quantity of charge for a univalent ion. So \(zF\) actually represents the electrical charge. Table 5.1 shows four ions' potentials. We can see that Na⁺ tends to depolarize the cell (make the inside more positive), Ca²⁺ tends to depolarize the cell (make the inside more positive), K⁺ tends to hyperpolarize the cell (make the inside more negative), and Cl⁻ tends to hyperpolarize the cell (make the inside more negative).

The resting potential is reached when the total membrane current is zero; i.e.,

$$
I_{\mathrm{Na}^+} + I_{\mathrm{K}^+} + I_{\mathrm{Cl}^-} = 0 \tag{5.2}
$$

or

$$
g_{K^+}[\Delta V_{\mathrm{res}} - \Delta V_{K^+}] + g_{Na^+}[\Delta V_{\mathrm{res}} - \Delta V_{Na^+}] + g_{Cl^-}[\Delta V_{\mathrm{res}} - \Delta V_{Cl^-}] = 0, \tag{5.3}
$$

where

$$
g_{Na^+} = \frac{I_{Na^+}}{\Delta V_{\mathrm{res}} - \Delta V_{Na^+}}, \quad g_{K^+} = \frac{I_{K^+}}{\Delta V_{\mathrm{res}} - \Delta V_{K^+}},
$$

and

$$
g_{Cl^-} = \frac{I_{Cl^-}}{\Delta V_{\mathrm{res}} - \Delta V_{Cl^-}}, \tag{5.4}
$$

where \(\Delta V_{\mathrm{res}}\) is membrane voltage [mV], \(\Delta V_{Na^+}\), \(\Delta V_{K^+}\), \(\Delta V_{Cl^-}\) are Nernst voltages [mV] for sodium, potassium, and chlorine, respectively, \(I_{Na^+}\), \(I_{K^+}\), \(I_{Cl^-}\) are the electric currents carried by sodium, potassium, and chlorine (leakage current) per unit area mA/m², respectively, \(g_{Na^+}\), \(g_{K^+}\), \(g_{Cl^-}\) are membrane conductances (1/resistance) per unit area for sodium, potassium, and chlorine, respectively—also referred to as the leakage conductance S/m². We can alternatively express the resting membrane potential in (5.3) as follows:

$$
\Delta V_{\mathrm{res}} = \frac{g_{K^+}\Delta V_{K^+} + g_{Na^+}\Delta V_{Na^+} + g_{Cl^-}\Delta V_{Cl^-}}{g_{K^+} + g_{Na^+} + g_{Cl^-}}. \tag{5.5}
$$

Thus, the membrane's resting potential is dominated by the most permeable ions. In these derivations, we only used the three ions that influence bioelectrical phenomena: K⁺, Na⁺, and Cl⁻. Note that Ca²⁺ also contributes to bioelectricity in a few tissues, including the heart.

## 5.1.5. Electrophysiological Activity

Nowadays, medicine routinely measures electric or magnetic signals generated by (1) the spontaneous activity of living tissues inside the body and (2) electronic devices outside biological tissues that can stimulate electric and/or magnetic responses. These electric and magnetic fields are used for medical diagnostics and therapy. The magnetic signals are included here through the link between electric and magnetic fields discussed in the previous subsection. We here describe some of the classical bioelectromagnetic methods used in clinics. We start by some electromagnetic background on calculation of electrical potential and magnetic fields around an inhomogeneous body like the brain.

The brain radius is about 0.25 m whereas the wavelength of electrochemical signals is about 500 m—that is more than 2000 times the brain radius. Therefore the use of Maxwell's quasi-static equations in Chapter 3 is justified. Also, there are two current densities involved in this problem.

The resulting current arises from the bioelectric activity of nerve and muscle cells due to the conversion of energy from chemical to electric form. This current is nonconservative and we denote it \(J^{(a)}=J^{(a)}(x,t)\), where \(x\) is the point at which the field is evaluated. It is responsible for the electric field, \(E=E(x,t)\), and the second current, \(\sigma E\), where \(\sigma\) is the electric conductivity of the brain, is the result of an applied field in a resistive material, such as the brain. So the total current is [see Chapter 5 of Ikelle (2023a) and Chapter 12 of Ikelle (2020)]

$$
J = J^{(a)} + \sigma E \Rightarrow J = J^{(a)} - \sigma\nabla\phi, \quad E = -\nabla\phi
$$

where \(\phi=\phi(x,t)\) is the scalar potential. We also used the fact that \(E=-\nabla\phi\). Using \(\nabla\cdot J=0\) in Chapter 3, we can deduce that

$$
\nabla\cdot J^{(a)} = \sigma\nabla^2\phi \Rightarrow \phi = -\frac{1}{4\pi\sigma}\int_V \frac{1}{|x|}\nabla\cdot J^{(a)}\,dV = \frac{1}{4\pi\sigma}\int_V J^{(a)}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)dV. \tag{5.7}
$$

The first equation here allows us to compute the electric potential under the assumption that brain conductivity is smooth, but it is not because of the various regions in the brain, as illustrated in Figure 5.1. To account for the regions, we divide the brain into a finite number of homogeneous regions. We denote the boundaries of these regions as \(S_m\). At these boundaries both the electric potential \(\phi\) and the normal component of the current density must be continuous:

$$
\phi^{(-)}(S_m) = \phi^{(+)}(S_m)
$$

and

$$
\sigma_m^{(-)}\nabla\phi^{(-)}(S_m)\cdot n_m = \sigma_m^{(+)}\nabla\phi^{(+)}(S_m)\cdot n_m, \tag{5.8}
$$

where the superscripts \((-)\) and \((+)\) represent the opposite sides of the boundary and \(n_m\) is the normal vector to \(S_m\) directed from the \((-)\) region to the \((+)\) one. If we define \(\psi = 1/|x|\), according to Green's theorem (Smyth, 1968), we have

$$
\int_{S_m}\left[\sigma_m^{(-)}\left(\psi^{(-)}\nabla\phi^{(-)} - \phi^{(-)}\nabla\psi^{(-)}\right) - \sigma_m^{(+)}\left(\psi^{(+)}\nabla\phi^{(+)} - \phi^{(+)}\nabla\psi^{(+)}\right)\right]\cdot n_m\,dS_m = \int_{V_m}\left[\psi\nabla\cdot(\sigma_m\nabla\phi) - \phi\nabla\cdot(\sigma_m\nabla\psi)\right]dV_m. \tag{5.9}
$$

By substituting the boundary conditions in (5.8) and the first equation in (5.7) into (5.9), we obtain (Geselowitz, 1967 and 1970):

$$
\phi = \frac{1}{4\pi\sigma}\int_V J^{(a)}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)dV + \sum_m \int_{S_m}\left(\sigma_m^{(+)} - \sigma_m^{(-)}\right)\phi\,\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\cdot n_m\,dS_m. \tag{5.10}
$$

This equation evaluates the electric potential anywhere within the brain.

The current density throughout a volume conductor produces a magnetic field given by the following relationship (Stratton, 1941; Jackson, 1968):

$$
4\pi H = \int_V J \times \nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)dV = \int_V J^{(a)} \times \nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)dV - \int_V \sigma\nabla\phi \times \nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)dV = \int_V J^{(a)} \times \nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)dV - \sum_m \int_{V_m}\sigma_m\nabla\phi \times \nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)dV = \int_V J^{(a)} \times \nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)dV - \sum_m \int_{V_m}\sigma_m\nabla\times\left[\phi\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\right]dV.
$$

In the last equality here, we use the vector identity \(\nabla\times(\phi a) = \phi\nabla\times a + \nabla\phi\times a\), with \(a = \nabla(1/|x|)\), and the fact \(\nabla\times\nabla\Psi = 0\), where \(\Psi\) is an arbitrary function of \(x\); i.e.,

$$
\nabla\times\left[\phi\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\right] = \phi\nabla\times\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right) + \nabla\phi\times\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)
$$

or

$$
\nabla\times\left[\phi\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\right] = \nabla\phi\times\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right). \tag{5.11}
$$

Finally, applying the divergence theorem (see Appendix A of Ikelle, 2020), we arrive at

$$
4\pi H = \int_V J^{(a)} \times \nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)dV + \sum_m \int_{S_m}\left(\sigma_m^{(+)} - \sigma_m^{(-)}\right)\phi\,\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\times n_m\,dS_m. \tag{5.12}
$$

This equation describes the magnetic field outside a finite volume conductor (brain) containing internal (electric) volume sources \(J^{(a)}\) and inhomogeneities \((\sigma_m^{(+)} - \sigma_m^{(-)})\). Equations (5.10) and (5.12) are the basis of electrophysiological activity and measurements. These equations were first derived by Geselowitz (1970).
