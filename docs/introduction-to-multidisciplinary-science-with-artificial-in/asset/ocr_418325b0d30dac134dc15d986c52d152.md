Figure 1.12. (a) We consider the position vector and velocity vector, v, in a plane containing an orbiting planet and the Sun. We also introduce polar coordinates: i_r = [cos θ, sin θ]^T is the unit vector in the direction of the vector position of the orbiting planet. The vector i_θ = [− sin θ, cos θ]^T is the unit vector orthogonal to the direction of the vector position of the orbiting planet. (b) Another significant vector associated with this plane is the angular momentum vector, L, which is the scaled cross-product of the position vector and the velocity of the orbiting planet. The position vector is orthogonal to the angular momentum vector.

and therefore dA/dt is constant because L is constant. We can now deduce that the area swept out by the planet’s orbit between time t1 and t2 is

A12 = ∫_{t1}^{t2} dA / d t = 1/2 ∫_{t1}^{t2} r^2 d θ / d t d t = 1/(2 m2) L (t2 − t1)   (1.39)

and the area swept out by the same planet between time t3 and t4 is A34 = ∫_{t3}^{t4} dA / d t = 1/2 ∫_{t3}^{t4} r^2 d θ / d t d t = 1/(2 m2) L (t4 − t3).   (1.40)

If t2 − t1 = t4 − t3, then A12 = A34 because the magnitude of the angular momentum of the orbiting planet, L, is constant. This conclusion proves Kepler’s second law. Also we can clearly see that Kepler’s second law is really a statement of the conservation of angular momentum. Note that the key assumption made in these derivations is that the Sun is not moving and lies at the origin of our coordinate system.
