This equation is called the time-independent Schrödinger equation because the time variable $t$ does not enter the equation. The solution to this equation is an eigenfunction. So when eigenfunctions are determined, we obtain the solution of Schrödinger equation as

$$\Psi(\boldsymbol{x}, t) = \psi(\boldsymbol{x}) \exp\left[ -i \frac{U}{\hbar} t \right] . \tag{4.11}$$

# Examples of potential energy.

One of the key components of the Schrödinger equation is potential energy. Let us briefly look at some known examples. We start with the Gamow model of $\alpha$ radioactivity, introduced in 1928. Briefly speaking, Gamow assumed that the $\alpha$ particles (Helium-4) were already bound to the nucleus. The potential they feel is the sum of two terms: The first is due to the averaged nuclear force, and can be described as a Woods-Saxon potential $V_{VW}(r)$. The second one is simply the Coulomb potential $V_C(r)$ due to the electromagnetic interaction between the $\alpha$ particle and the other nucleons:

$$V_{VW}(r) = -V_0 \left[ 1 - \exp\left( \frac{r - R}{a} \right) \right]^{-1} \quad \text{and} \quad V_C(r) = \frac{V_0}{r} , \tag{4.12}$$

where $V_{VW}(r)$ is related to the averaged nuclear force and is known as the Woods-Saxon potential $V_{VW}(r)$ and $V_C(r)$ is simply the Coulomb potential $V_C(r)$ due to the electromagnetic interaction between the $\alpha$ particle and the other nucleons. We can write the net potential felt by the $\alpha$ particle as $V(r) = \kappa V_{VW}(r) + (-\kappa) V_C(r)$ where $\kappa$ can be constant or a function of $r$, e.g. a linear function. As discussed in Ikelle (2023b), alpha emission is a radioactive process involving two nuclei X and Y, which can be described in general as follows:

$$ {}^{A}_{Z}X \rightarrow {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha , \quad \text{or} \quad {}^{A}_{Z}X \rightarrow {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} , \tag{4.13}$$

where ${}^{A}_{Z}X$ is the generic unstable nucleus, ${}^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y$, the helium-4 nucleus being known as an alpha particle. We will use alpha particles as a quantum tunneling example in the next section. The Morse potential can be a better approximation of a potential energy surface when working with diatomic molecules.
