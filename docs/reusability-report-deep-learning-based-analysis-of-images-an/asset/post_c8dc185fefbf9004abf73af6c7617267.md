
## Reusability report: Deep learning-based analysis of images and spectroscopy data with AtomAI

Received: 8 November 2023
Accepted: 19 November 2024
Published online: 2 January 2025
Check for updates

Pragalbh Vashistha, Hitesh Gupta Kattamuri, Nikhil Thawari, Murugaiyan Amirthalingam & Rohit Batra

Machine learning (ML) techniques are gaining traction for materials image processing applications. In this context, Ziatdinov et al. developed AtomAI, a user-friendly and comprehensive Python library designed for a wide range of materials imaging tasks, including image segmentation, denoising, image generation, image-to-spectrum mapping (and vice versa) and subsequent atomistic modelling of image-resolved structures. Given its broad applicability, this report aims to reproduce key aspects of the authors’ original work, extend its capabilities to new materials datasets and enhance certain features to improve model performance. We have not only successfully replicated parts of the original study, but also developed improved ML models for multiple datasets across different image processing tasks. The AtomAI library was found to be easy to use and extensible for custom applications. We believe that AtomAI holds significant potential for the microscopy and spectroscopy communities, and further development—such as semi-automated image segmentation—could broaden its utility and impact.

Deep learning techniques are becoming increasingly popular for materials characterization tasks. Irrespective of the type of output from the characterization tool, whether in the form of images or a spectrum, it can be learned using deep learning techniques to accelerate data acquisition and processing, thereby allowing efficient high-throughput experimentation and knowledge extraction. Two types of learning task are particularly important in this domain, image segmentation and image to spectrum (Im2Spec) or spectrum to image. Image semantic segmentation is a task that involves dividing an image into multiple regions or segments and assigning a label to each segment. The goal is to classify each pixel of the image into specific categories, effectively producing a pixel-wise segmentation map. Image semantic segmentation enables many crucial applications in materials science, such as defect detection, micrograph segmentation, porosity quantification and quantitative grain characterization. Similarly, the Im2Spec task involves mapping structural features in the form of images to the associated spectrum: for example, mapping energy spectrum data to the localized image, or mapping hysteresis loops to the associated topographic images from piezoelectric force microscopy.

Traditional image segmentation methods often require manual intervention or rely on handcrafted features, which can be time consuming, subjective and limited in their ability to handle complex image structures. Deep learning models, specifically convolutional neural networks (CNNs), can overcome these limitations by learning hierarchical features from the images and can effectively capture complex patterns and structures without the need for explicit feature engineering. Thus, several attempts have been made to develop robust autonomous (or semi-autonomous) frameworks that utilize CNNs to efficiently and accurately accomplish the image segmentation task. This allows high-throughput segmentation of thousands of microscopy images and enables development of structure-property relations12. In this report, we use the AtomAI framework to successfully reproduce image segmentation and Im2Spec tasks included in the original work, as well as to train models on a recently published nanoparticle dataset. Due to the flexibility offered by the AtomAI framework, we find it quite simple to achieve model accuracy as good as (or even better than) that reported in the previous works. Furthermore, we benchmark the performance of the trained models against different levels of noise and magnification in the unseen images to estimate the genuine performance of these models in realistic situations. Additionally, we expand the capability of the AtomAI framework to perform the instance segmentation task. Using a dataset of additively manufactured alloys, we demonstrate the advantage of using instance segmentation over semantic segmentation. We also note that we limit this report to the image segmentation and the Im2Spec tasks among the other capabilities offered by AtomAI, as we believe these are the most general tasks, with wide applicability.

We also report the limitations of the original implementation of the AtomAI framework and suggest code updates to improve them. For example, the AtomAI framework does not allow for early stopping (ES), a concept commonly used to avoid overfitting and poor generalizability in deep learning models. We update the AtomAI framework by implementing a modularized version of ES with several available user input options. Similarly, we add a module to allow selection of the optimal classification threshold during model training. We show that the improved version of AtomAI with ES and threshold selection consistently achieves better accuracy for different datasets.

Another limitation of AtomAI is that during data augmentation not all possible image orientations and flips are accounted for, thereby resulting in subpar model training. We also found minor bugs in the code that impact the model training process. For instance, there are repeated errors associated with the use of the library numpy.random.randint— while this library returns random integers between the lower limit (inclusive) and the higher limit (exclusive), the AtomAI code is written with the perspective that the integers equal to the higher limit are included as well. The updated version with these fixes is made available.

## Datasets

We focus on two transmission electron microscope image datasets and one optical microscope image dataset. The transmission electron microscope datasets consist of the Sm-doped BiFeO3 (BFO) bulk system and the Au and CdSe nanoparticle systems4. The former dataset is taken from the original AtomAI publication and contains a single labelled image of dimension 3,000 × 3,000 (~20,000 unit cells). Each pixel in the image is labelled as either sublattice A, sublattice B or the background, thus forming the ground-truth data. The second dataset comprises images of Au and CdSe nanoparticles with resolutions of 4,096 × 4,096 and 1,024 × 1,024 pixels, respectively. The ground-truth values for these images were obtained by manually labelling each pixel in the image as either a particle (representing the nanoparticle class) or background (representing the surroundings or empty space).

The optical microscopy dataset was procured using laser bed powder fusion of various alloys, including Inconel 625 and H13. To generate these images, a cube of dimensions 10 × 10 × 10 mm³ is additively manufactured, and is then cut diagonally along the build direction and observed under an optical microscope. Images of different sizes are obtained and showcase porosities of different types, such as gas induced and lack of fusion, formed during the additive manufacturing process. Additionally, the images might also contain scratch marks produced during the sample-cutting process.

Figure 1a captures the overall workflow adopted in this work for the segmentation task. For the BFO and the Au and CdSe nanoparticle datasets, patches of smaller images were extracted from the original image(s)—5,000 patches of size 256 × 256 pixels for the BFO dataset and 172 patches of size 512 × 512 pixels for the CdSe nanoparticle dataset. The number and size of patches were based on previous works and were kept consistent for comparative analysis. Next, the obtained dataset patch images was divided into training, validation and test sets. A 3:1:1 train–validation–test ratio was used for the BFO dataset, while for the CdSe dataset 129 images were used for training and validation and 43 for testing, in accordance with the original works.
