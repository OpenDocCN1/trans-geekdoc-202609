## 3.3.2 NSGA-II

In general, multi-objective optimization has several objective functions with inequality and equality constraints to optimize. Equation (3.7) defines an optimization problem:

```
$$\min f_m(x) m = 1,2,3,...,M$$
$$s \cdot g_j(x) < 0 \quad j = 1,2,3,...,J$$
$$h_k(x) = 0 \quad k = 1,2,3,...,K$$
$$x_i^l \leq x_i \leq x_i^u \quad i = 1,2,3,...,N \quad (3.7)$$
```

The objective of a multi-objective optimization problem is to identify a set of solutions that simultaneously satisfy two criteria: they must be free of any constraint violations, and they must excel in all their objective values. The ultimate result of this type of optimization is a collection of solutions that represent a compromise between various objectives. These solutions are known as Pareto optimal solutions.

Pareto optimal solutions are defined by their property of nondominance, meaning that it is not possible to improve any aspect of these solutions without worsening at another objective; enhancing one criterion would necessitate a compromise in another. The aggregate of these viable nondominated solutions constitutes the Pareto optimal set.

Within the field of multi-objective optimization, NSGA-II marks a notable progression. This evolutionary algorithm utilizes a swift nondominated sorting technique, offering computational efficiency over older methodologies. NSGA-II incorporates a distinct selection mechanism that creates a mating pool by combining parent and offspring populations. This mechanism prioritizes solutions using measures of performance (fitness) and the variety among solutions (spread). Simulation outcomes across a range of difficult test scenarios reveal that NSGA-II reliably surpasses competitors in two crucial aspects: it secures a wider distribution of solutions and exhibits enhanced convergence to the genuine Pareto optimal front.

NSGA-II retains the core framework of the genetic algorithm while incorporating adjustments in the mating and survival selection strategies. It initially selects individuals based on their 'fronts', a method that may entail the front if the count of individuals surpasses a survival threshold. Within these segmented fronts, the selection process is further honed using crowding distance, determined by the Manhattan Distance in the objective space, which plays a crucial role (Gunantara, 2018). Crowding distance promotes diversity among solutions by favoring regions that are less crowded; to preserve the boundaries of the solution set, the algorithm bestows an infinite crowding distance on the boundary points (first and last) in every generation, ensuring that the extremities are maintained.

3.9 visually represents the crowding-distance calculation, where disc points indicate solutions within the same nondominated front; the procedural flow of NSGA-II is depicted in Figure 3.10, providing a comprehensive understanding of the algorithm's mechanics.

NSGA-II is a simple and straightforward algorithm. First, all individuals in the previous population ($P$) and current population ($Q$) are included in $R_t$. Then, population is sorted by nondomination algorithm described by pseudocode Appendix C.

In the context of NSGA-II, forming a new population $P_{t+1}$ from a merged population is methodological and prioritizes the highest quality solutions. The top non-dominated set, $F_1$, that contains the best solutions in the merged population is given the highest priority. All members of $F_i$ are included in $P_t$ if the size of $F_i$ is less than or equal to the desired population size $N$. If $P_i$ reaches after including all members of $F_i$, the algorithm proceeds to select members from subsequent non-dominated fronts, based on their rank order. Then solutions from $F_2$ are chosen next, followed by those from $F_3$, and so on, until the population $P_{t+1}$ reaches its capacity or no more sets can be included.

If the cumulative count of solutions from $F_1$ to the last accommodated nondominated set $F_l$ exceeds $N$, a selection must be made from the last set $F_l$. To do this, the solutions in $F_l$ are sorted by the crowded-comparison operator, which ranks solutions in descending order based on their crowding distance. The algorithm then selects the best solutions from $F_l$ to fill the remaining slots in $P_{t+1}$, until the population count reaches exactly $N$. This approach ensures that $P_{t+1}$ is composed of the best.

## 3.3.3 TOPSIS

MCDM is utilized for decision-making based on multiple criteria. The core of MCDM lies in harmonizing diverse criteria when each criterion bears significance. Among the array of MCDM techniques, TOPSIS stands out as one of the most prevalent and extensively used. TOPSIS, developed by Hwang and Yoon in 1981, is used in multicriteria system. It assesses the performance level of options based on their distance from the optimal value and least ideal value. TOPSIS has the advantage of being straightforward to understand and implement. However, it is sensitive to extreme values and does not handle criteria well. One of the main drawbacks of TOPSIS is the determination of weights. To mitigate this, entropy weighting is often used. Entropy-weighted TOPSIS is implemented through several steps (Zhu et al., 2020):

- Step 1: Calculate the determinant of a matrix, with elements determined according to Equation (3.8).

```
$X = \begin{bmatrix} X_{11} & \dots & X_{ij} & X_{in} \\ X_{21} & \dots & X_{2j} & X_{2n} \\ \vdots & & \vdots & \vdots \\ X_{i1} & \dots & X_{ij} & X_{in} \\ X_{m1} & \dots & X_{mj} & X_{mn} \end{bmatrix} (3.8)$
```

- Step 2: Calculate the normalized matrix $R = (p_{ij})_{mn}$, where $p_{ij}$ is determined by Equation (3.9).

```
$p_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_{ij}^2}}, j \in [1,n] (3.9)$
```

- Step 3: Entropy $e_i$ of the $i^{th}$ criterion is defined as Equation (3.10):

```
$e_i = -\frac{1}{\ln(m)} \sum_{i=1}^m p_{ij} \ln(p_{ij}), i=1...m; j=1...n$ (3.10)$
```

where entropy $e_i$ ranges between [0, 1]. A higher $e_i$ indicates greater differentiation for the $i^{th}$ criterion, meaning that more information can be gleaned from that criterion.
