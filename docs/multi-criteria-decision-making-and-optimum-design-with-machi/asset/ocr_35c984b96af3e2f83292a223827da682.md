# 4.2 PROBLEM STATEMENT

In the Industrial Revolution 4.0, 3D printing is an important component. Therefore, it is now critical to include 3D printing, or additive manufacturing, in education. As a typical technique, FDM is easy to use, easy to understand, and suitable for new learners, and it is often included at universities as well in short-term STEM courses. While giving students, lecturers not only have to guide students to operate the machine but also explain the factors that affect the quality of printed products. Regarding printed product quality, there are two main criteria: tensile strength and surface quality [13]. While the surface quality of FDM-printed products is mainly affected by the thickness of the printed layer, the factors that affect tensile strength are much more complex. While initial thoughts suggest that printing temperature or printing speed this issue needs to be carefully studied.

Specific to our work, it is necessary to teach 3D printing as part of supporting students their graduation thesis for the Faculty of Mechanical Engineering (FME), Industrial University of Ho Chi Minh City (IUH): practical situations at the couple have shown that tensile strength was the most important criterion. Normally, for a mechanical engineering thesis, the students design and make a few typical parts of metal, using 3D printing to produce secondary parts to speed up the process of completing the thesis. The main goal of a graduation thesis is to help students use the knowledge they have learned to manufacture a device, test it, and document the outcomes; the goal is not to mass-produce products. Therefore, the application of 3D printing to support graduation theses is appropriate and meets the training needs for Industry 4.0.

We consider researching the parameters that affect the tensile strength of FDM 3D-printed products is necessary for FME lecturers guiding students and training facilities in general. In this study, we use a two-step optimization process to help operators understand and select the optimal printing parameters. However, the process applies not only to FDM 3D printers but to additive manufacturing in general.

# 4.3 MATERIALS AND METHODS

# 4.3.1 The FDM 3D PRINTER

For this work, we used a Cubicon Style® 3D printer to fabricate specimens, as illustrated in Figure 4.1. The printer was characterized by its rectangular form with dimensions of 322 x 350 x 486 mm and a weight of 15 kg. The printer was equipped with three stepper motors for its operational control. Specifically, one of these stepmotors facilitated movement along the Z-axis through a lead screw mechanism, while the remaining two motors governed movement along the X-axis and Y-axis employing a belt mechanism. We used the default printer nozzle of 0.4 mm. The printer could achieve a maximum printing speed of 150 mm/s. In terms of its spatial printing capabilities, the printer can produce polymeric objects with dimensions up to 150 x 150 x 150 mm in length x width x height, respectively.
