However, for the use of AI to be in line with human values and beliefs, it is equally vital to look into potential risks brought on by this formidable technology and it raises (Zhao and Gómez, 2023). To ensure the long-term viability of AI, its application must be responsibly and supported by regulatory understanding. We suggest leveraging regulatory framework to encourage more socially conscious AI by keeping an eye on and reducing the dangers involved. Machine learning has been widely used in AI solutions for sustainability. For learning and creating predictions, ML takes advantage of the patterns and connections found in the historical data (Nishant, Kennedy, and Corbett, 2020). For instance, using AI and ML in the detection and classification of poverty may revolutionize the process of data collecting and analysis in turn enable faster and more effective policy responses (Adadi, 2021).

In terms of AI in business, there is a lack of agreement in the literature over whether and how AI will alter current corporate practices or even their very foundations. Approaches range from those that see a new paradigm of autonomous firms to those that maintain that no appreciable change will take place. Some authors assert that AI will increase the accuracy and efficacy of corporate actions while decreasing the need for human management and some others even think that, as a result of digitalization, boards will become virtual networks of people or be supplanted by AI-based solutions. Others, on the other hand, are still doubtful that technological advancements will be able to change normative difficulties and eliminate the need for human management (Zhao and Gómez, 2023).

## 18.4 AI-POWERED DECISION-MAKING IN SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT

The Sustainable Development Goals have several main objective areas, and unregulated AI runs the risk of slowing down progress in these areas. This risk is increased in developing nations. The current unchecked experimentation with unproven AI technology in markets and progressive societies casts a shadow that overpromises the practical possibilities of AI-powered progressive growth. “Big Tech” has already demonstrated that it cannot be trusted to self-regulate or adhere to voluntary standards, and in the absence of preventative control, trust will once be more broken, leading to negative outcomes and exploitation, harming progress toward the SDGs. Currently, AI is in an undeveloped and immature stage of development. Even AI systems have shortcomings, we consistently trust them to make crucial judgments that affect our lives. Automated decision-making that has the potential to substantially harm economic and social progress, especially in developing nations (Vaio, Palladinova, Hassan, and Escobar, 2020).

Incorporating sustainability practices into an organization is advantageous for its reputation, productivity, and access to resources such as better tools, talent, and opportunities. Many businesses see sustainability efforts as opportunities to advance their long-term interests and strengthen ties with their suppliers, employees, and communities rather as a burden that must be endured (Zhao and Gómez, 2023). Digital technologies that are powered by AI are inextricably linked to interact with, observe, and comprehend people, enterprises, economies, and lives in general.
