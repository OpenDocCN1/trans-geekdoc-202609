Recently, Travis et al. coupled diffusion MRI and quantitative T1 relaxometry to examine premature and term-birth myelination in older children (Travis et al. 2019). Conversely, magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) technique was utilized to map relaxation and myelination rates in subjects aged between 3 months and 6 years (Kuhne et al. 2021). The latest works in the literature deploy T1/T2 mapping to quantify myelination in young infants (Filimonova et al. 2023) and early adolescents (Dipnall et al. 2023).

Despite the availability of many such quantitative studies to map ongoing myelination in the pediatric brain, they are fraught with several shortcomings:

- Primarily, these works entail additional quantitative scanning techniques other than the usual diagnostic scan employed in clinics and hospitals. This increases the time, effort, and monetary cost associated with the MRI scan. The longer time also makes it difficult to image the young babies who are usually restless and uncooperative.

- Further, the current studies often focus more on older babies, such as 3-month-olds, (Deoni et al. 2011; Deoni et al. 2012; Dean et al. 2014; Dean et al. 2015; Dean et al. 2016) 1-year olds, (Deoni et al. 2015) older infants, (Kuhne et al. 2021) 8-year-olds (Travis et al. 2019), and young adolescents (Dipnall et al. 2023). There is a paucity of work dealing with the myelination of neonates and very young infants.

- Apart from this, various myelination-related challenges make the imaging and segmentation of MWM a daunting task (Devi et al. 2014a; Devi et al. 2015). This includes the apparent myelination on Magnetic Resonance (MR) images, that is, myelination as it appears on T1 and T2 scans. A time delay is observed between the visualization of myelin tissue on T1 and T2 scans (MWM is viewed later on T2 images). Besides, the age-specific process of myelin deposition necessitates numerous atlases and training images for accurately mapping myelination throughout early childhood (Barkovich et al. 1988). Moreover, myelin formation is not an all-or-none process; the myelination of WM is a slow and gradual phenomenon that cannot be fully detected on MR images (Knaap and Valk 2005). Coupled with this, there are vast differences in the myelination progress between premature babies and full-term births.

To address the above limitations, we put forth a new and automated method for the segmentation, study, and quantitative analysis of MWM in the pediatric brain. The contributions of our work are listed below:

- 1) We propose a novel automated approach for brain extraction and myelin segmentation using the weighted localized Tsallis entropy thresholding technique. We also develop a unique three-dimensional (3D) model for the visualization of WM myelination.

- 2) Further, a quantitative “myelin index” (MI) is introduced to measure the ratio of WM that is myelinated. To our knowledge, this is the first study to objectively quantify the extent of myelination by a reproducible metric based on MWM segmentation from T1 to T2 MR images.

- 3) Growth model fitting is used to analyze the differences in myelination between the right and left hemispheres of the brain.

- 4) The myelination pattern in preterm babies is closely monitored and compared with regular-term-born babies.

## 4.2 Literature Review

### 4.2.1 Segmentation of MWM in the Pediatric Brain

Although several techniques are available in the literature to segment the pediatric brain, (Prastawa et al. 2005; Xue et al. 2007; Weisenfeld and Warfield 2009; Melbourne et al. 2012; Gui et al. 2012; Gousias et al. 2013; Anbeek et al. 2013; Egekeher et al. 2013; Makropoulos et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2015) only very few approaches detect the MWM tissue separately (Prastawa et al. 2005; Weisenfeld and Warfield 2009; Melbourne et al. 2012; Gui et al. 2012; Anbeek et al. 2013; Egekeher et al. 2013). This is attributed to the various difficulties in imaging and segmenting myelin tissue along with the pertinent problems of limited dataset availability, anatomic differences in the pediatric brain, and variations caused by brain anomalies. In this section, we elaborate on the recent methods available for myelin delineation.

Initially, Prastawa et al. proposed a segmentation technique using probabilistic brain templates and graph clustering (Prastawa et al. 2005). The neonatal brain was classified into MWM, unmyelinated WM (UWM), GM, and CSF. The approach yielded an average Dice ratio of 0.61–0.77 for myelin demarcation. Likewise, Weisenfeld and Warfield postulated an algorithm for detecting the cortical and subcortical GM, MWM, UWM, and CSF based on T1 and T2 scans (Weisenfeld and Warfield 2009). The study entailed spatial atlas priors and tissue prototypes for precise delineation. The work achieved a mean Dice coefficient of 0.72 for myelin tissue.

Similarly, Melbourne et al. implemented an adaptive segmentation with manual tissue priors as atlases (Melbourne et al. 2012). The method detected eight tissues, namely, MWM, WM, deep and cortical GM, cerebellum, brainstem, CSF, and ventricles on the NeoBrainS12 database. The separate segregation of ventricles and myelin tissue is an important aspect of this approach. Conversely, Gui et al. put forward a completely atlas-free technique for pediatric brain segmentation based on morphology and tissue structure (Gui et al. 2012). Both global and tissue-specific classification was attained, that is, intracranial cavity, brainstem, cerebellum, right and left hemispheres, MWM, UWM, cortical and subcortical GM, and CSF. The algorithm was found effective in anomalous cases such as ventriculomegaly.

Anbeek et al. formulated probabilistic atlas segmentation on a large cohort of 108 newborn babies (Anbeek et al. 2013). The cortical and central GM, UWM, MWM, and CSF in ventricles and extracerebral space, brainstem, and cerebellum were identified. However, the study recorded lower accuracy values for MWM delineation with an average Dice score of 0.47. Similarly, Egekeher et al. entailed multiple atlases combined with supervised classification (i.e. k-nearest neighbors and Bayes classifier) and extensive post-processing (Egekeher et al. 2013). The pediatric brain was divided into the cortical GM, UWM, MWM, and basal ganglia and thalami (BGT), cerebellum, brainstem, ventricles, and CSF. Despite obtaining mean Dice ratios between 0.77 and 0.93 for the other tissues, myelin detection attains a low segmentation accuracy of 0.55.

Recently, Moeskops et al. performed an all-inclusive brain segmentation study targeting young children, early adults, and aged people (Moeskops et al. 2015). The following tissue classes were demarcated using deep learning networks: MWM, UWM, cortical GM, cerebellum, brainstem, and BGT. The salient aspect of the work is the use of single-modality images (either T1 or T2). This approach reported good performance for MWM with a Dice coefficient of 0.69 and 0.56 on babies at 30 weeks and 40 weeks, respectively. Alternatively, McAllister utilized synthetic MR scans to map the age-dependent increase in GM, WM, CSF, and MWM volumes (McAllister et al. 2017). A pediatric database with normal subjects aged between 0.1 and 21.5 years was created. The analysis revealed a rapid linear increase of MWM in the initial 18 months and a subsequent gradual rise during early childhood.
