## 10.3.1 Limited Annotated Dataset

DL network models necessitate a substantial volume of data, and the prerequisite for well-annotated training data underscores the dataset’s importance in various deep learning-based medical procedures (Srinivasu et al. 2021). Nevertheless, the endeavor to amass a large repository of annotated medical images for medical image processing encounters challenges (Çiçek et al. 2016). Annotating new medical images is labor-intensive and costly and requires a high level of expertise. While several large-scale datasets are publicly available, the need persists for more challenging datasets to further enhance the training of DL models, especially in scenarios involving complex objects. Existing 3D datasets, in particular, are frequently insufficient in size, with some being synthetic, emphasizing the ongoing requirement for more challenging datasets (Levin et al. 1988).

### 10.3.1.1 Solution

Expanding the size of existing medical image datasets can be accomplished through various strategies:

- (I) Employing image augmentation transformations, such as rotating images at different angles, flipping images vertically or horizontally, cropping, and shearing images. These augmentation techniques contribute to the enhanced performance of the system.
- (II) Leveraging transfer learning from efficient models can provide a viable solution to the limited data problem (Bhattacharya et al. 2021).
- (III) Synthesizing data collected from various sources offers an additional avenue for augmenting dataset size (Bhattacharya et al. 2021).

## 10.3.2 Sparse Annotations

Sparse annotation in medical image segmentation refers to situations where only a limited subset of the available data has been annotated or labeled. Fully annotating information, especially for 3D images, is a time-intensive task and is not consistently feasible. Consequently, partial labeling of information slices within 3D images is common. Training DL models using these sparsely annotated 3D images presents considerable challenges (Çiçek et al. 2016). This means limited training data, increased annotation burden, and bias in the model results. It can also lead to challenges in handling rare cases. In medical imaging, certain pathological conditions or anatomical variations may be relatively rare. Sparse annotation might inadequately represent these less common cases, making it challenging for the model to accurately segment such instances when encountered in clinical practice.

### 10.3.2.1 Solution

In cases of sparsely annotated datasets, applying a weighted loss function is a viable solution. In this method, the weights for the unlabeled data in the dataset are all set to zero, enabling the model to focus exclusively on learning from the pixels that are labeled.

## 10.3.3 Class Imbalance in Datasets

Class imbalance in a dataset occurs when the distribution of instances across different classes is uneven, meaning that some classes have significantly more instances than others. In the context of medical image segmentation, where each class may correspond to a specific anatomical structure or pathological condition, class imbalance can have several implications: model biases, reduced sensitivity to minority class, misleading evaluation metrics, and reduced model performance.

Imbalances in class distribution are inherent in various publicly available medical image datasets, posing a significant challenge in training DL models and potentially leading to misleading model accuracy. For example, in the patient data, where a disease is relatively rare, occurring in only 10% of screened patients, the overall model accuracy can be inflated, reaching local minima, as most patients do not have the disease (Bhattacharya et al. 2020; Merkow et al. 2016).

### 10.3.3.1 Solution

To address the issue of class imbalance, several strategies can be employed:

- (I) Oversampling the data and adjusting the extent of oversampling based on the degree of imbalance in the dataset.
- (II) Modifying the evaluation or performance metric to alleviate the impact of dataset imbalance.
- (III) Applying data augmentation techniques to create new data samples.
- (IV) Handling the dataset class imbalance problem by combining minority classes.

## 10.3.4 Intensity Inhomogeneities

Intensity inhomogeneities, also known as shading or nonuniformity, refer to variations in pixel intensity across an image that are not related to the underlying anatomical structures. These variations can be caused by factors such as variations in tissue thickness, patient positioning, or imperfections in imaging equipment. The impact of intensity inhomogeneities on medical image segmentation can be significant, leading to various challenges such as inaccurate segmentation boundaries, misleading texture information, difficulty in differentiating tissues, increased false positives and negatives, and compromised qualitative analysis. In images related to pathology, variations in color and intensity are commonly observed (Roy et al. 2011). Intensity inhomogeneities contribute to shading effects on the image, particularly in the segmentation of MR images. Additionally, TEM images may display brightness variations due to the presence of nonuniform support films, posing challenges in the segmentation process.

### 10.3.4.1 Solution

To mitigate intensity inhomogeneities, various algorithms are employed, and numerous nonparametric techniques are proposed in the literature (Roy et al. 2011). The use of prefiltering operations before segmentation is one approach to eliminate inhomogeneities. Moreover, advancements in scanning devices contribute to addressing and enhancing intensity inhomogeneities.

## 10.3.5 Complexities in Image Texture

The complexities in image texture refer to the intricate and detailed patterns and variations present in the visual appearance of an image. Understanding and handling these complexities is essential for image segmentation. In the realm of medical images, diverse artifacts may arise during image manipulation, primarily attributed to the use of different sensors and electronic components for image capture (Guo and Ashour 2019; Hesamian et al. 2019). The captured images often exhibit closely situated grey levels, resulting in indistinct image boundaries. Overlapping tissues and irregularities such as skin lines and hair in dermoscopic images add complexity to the identification of the region of interest in medical images.
