- neonatal phase (birth – 28 days): inverted tissue contrast compared to adult brain, that is, hypointensity of WM on T1 scans and hyperintensity on T2 images).
- isointense stage (around 6 months): marked by poor WM – GM contrast;
- adult-like appearance (more than 1 year): hyperintensity of WM on T1 images and hypointensity on T2 scans (similar to the adult brain).

Apart from this, diffusion MRI and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) are also utilized to assess WM connectivity and maturation. Recently, high angular resolution diffusion imaging has been employed to estimate the orientation and distribution of WM tracts and fibers (Dubois et al. 2021). Besides, many other quantitative techniques, such as magnetization transfer, susceptibility-weighted MRI, and perfusion imaging, are entailed to study WM myelination in the pediatric brain.

Particularly, Travis et al. combined diffusion MRI and quantitative relaxometry to analyze WM differences, especially in the corpus callosum (Travis et al. 2019). The corpus callosum was divided into four zones based on its location in the brain and the growth trajectory of each zone was compared between premature and full-term babies. Similarly, Grotheer et al. also integrated diffusion and quantitative MRI to validate the various developmental hypotheses of myelination in the child’s brain (Grotheer et al. 2022). The results of the work revealed that myelination pattern is not uniform and increases in the inferior-superior and anterior–posterior directions.

In contrast, Kuhne et al. utilized MPRAGE and MP2RAGE sequences to evaluate myelination in brain anomalies and pathologies (Kuhne et al. 2021). Rapid myelination progress was observed in the corpus callosum, while reduced myelin deposition was found in cases of hypomyelination and dysmyelination. Thus, a reference model to gauge atypical myelin and brain abnormalities was developed. Recently, many researchers have involved T1/T2 mapping to obtain specific information on WM myelination. The major merit of this technique is the widespread availability of T1 and T2 images, coupled with their faster acquisition. Dipnall et al. carried out a detailed examination of myelination tracts from late childhood to the early adolescent stage (Dipnall et al. 2023). They also studied the myelination differences between male and female children. The research work concluded that while there was a significant change in brain microstructure and WM myelination between childhood and adolescent phases, no notable variation was found based on the subject’s sex. Likewise, T1/T2 mapping was implemented in 94 neonates and infants with normal brain scans by Filimonova et al. (2023). The results showed an initial rapid rise in WM myelination during the early months, followed by a gradual reduction subsequently. The relationship between the baby's age and the extent of myelination was perceived to be nonlinear in this study.

## 4.3 Methodology

The proposed methodology is divided into four sections, namely, extraction of the brain from pediatric MRI scans along with MWM delineation, computation of MI and model fitting, study of myelination differences between the right and left brain hemispheres, and analysis of myelination in preterm born babies. The technical details of each phase are elucidated below.

### 4.3.1 Input Datasets

The sample consists of pediatric brain MR images of 40 babies acquired from the Department of Radiology, Sri Ramachandra University, Chennai, India. The babies were aged between 1 day and 2 years at the time of scan. Their age distribution is as follows:

- 30 babies born preterm (with gestational age between 30 and 33 weeks) and scanned at 40 weeks term-corrected age.
- 10 older babies aged 7 weeks, 9.6 weeks, 10 weeks, 2.9 months, 5.5 months, 10 months, 11 months, 16.3 months, 18.1 months, and 21.5 months.

The MR parameters used for imaging are presented below:

- Scanner: Signa HDxt MR machine with field strength 1.5 Tesla.
- T1 images: Repetition time (TR) – 500 ms, echo time (TE) – 12 ms, flip angle – 75°, voxel size – 0.3516 × 0.3516 × 4 mm³.
- T2 images: TR – 5400 ms, TE – 117.488 ms, flip angle – 90°, voxel size – 0.3516 × 0.3516 × 4 mm³.

### 4.3.2 Pediatric Brain Extraction and Myelin Segmentation

The proposed flow for brain extraction and demarcation of MWM is shown in Figure 4.1. Primarily, the acquired MR images are pre-processed and skull-stripped. Pre-processing is based on contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization technique. Whereas, we propose a new automatic histogram valley-based thresholding approach for skull stripping. This involves obtaining the MR scan's histogram, followed by smoothing and removal of spurious peaks. The smoothed histogram houses two peaks; the lower peak corresponds to brain tissue, while

![](img/b1cb9f9389f496c3199cc2e1e74e4626_113_0.png)

Figure 4.1 Flow chart of the proposed method.

the higher intensity peak indicates the skull. The valley between these two peaks is determined and used as intensity threshold in skull stripping.

The next stage entails brain extraction. Initially, the CSF is detected by the Chan-Vese active contour model (Chan and Vese 2001). Subsequently, the brain is identified by a series of two steps, that is, single largest component analysis and gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) based approach (Devi et al. 2014b). The largest component of the brain is obtained by equation 4.1:

$I_{brain} = C(\text{argmax } A(i)), \quad 1 \le i \le n$ (4.1)

where A(i) denotes the area of each component C(i), i = 1, 2 ... n.

For extracting the total brain volume, the GLCM matrix is computed for each component with offset d = 1 and \(\theta = 0^{\circ}\). Using this matrix, the textural features are derived. Of these, the inverse difference moment (IDM) is given in equation 4.2:

$IDM = \sum_u \sum_v \frac{1}{1+(u-v)^2} p(u, v)$ (4.2)

where p(u, v) is the GLCM matrix element at location (u, v). IDM is a measure of the component's homogeneity in the brain image. Based on experimental investigation, a homogeneity threshold of 80% is set for total brain extraction.

Subsequently, the MWM tissue is segmented from the extracted brain. For this, a novel weighted localized Tsallis entropy thresholding technique is proposed. The traditional Tsallis entropy method entails global threshold. We introduce a patch-based local thresholding approach. The brain image is first divided into many uniform blocks of equal dimension (in increments of 5 pixels). Tsallis entropy threshold is applied to each individual block and the final output is concatenated. In addition, a majority voting mechanism is utilized for detecting MWM tissue. This ensures that spurious detections and outliers are eliminated from the result (Devi et al. 2017).

To further improve segmentation performance, especially in the peripheral areas, we incorporate a new weighting mechanism. The entropy output at each block is weighed according to its similarity with the ground truth of myelin segmentation. For this, two sets of weights are obtained. While weights w1 are computed from all datasets, weights w2 are acquired after age-based segregation of the input datasets.

The total 40 input datasets are randomly split in the ratio 75 : 25 for training and testing, respectively. The leave-one-out cross-validation is used for training (Wang et al. 2015). Based on this cross-validation, weights w1 are derived as follows. For every training dataset d_t(t = 1, 2...29), the corresponding entropy result at each block size s is obtained as e_ts(t = 1, 2...29), (s = 5, 10...50). Given that the manual reference segmentation for myelin in each dataset d_t is denoted by ref_t, the similarity value is represented by equation 4.3:

simval_{ts} = \frac{2 |e_{ts} \cap ref_t|}{|e_{ts}| + |ref_t|} (4.3)

Weights w1(s) are calculated based on these similarity values as given in equation 4.4:

w_1(s) = \frac{1}{29} \left( \sum_t simval_{ts} \right) (4.4)

Thus, the final result E is computed based on the individual entropy outcomes e_s(s = 5, 10...50) as given in equation 4.5:

E = \sum_s w_1(s) * e_s (4.5)

On the other hand, weights w2 are age specific. They are designed to take into account the age-based changes in myelination of WM. The input datasets are split into two groups, that is, neonates (less than 28 days) and infants (older than one month). In each group, the leave-one-out cross-validation approach is used in weight calculation as in equations 4.6 and 4.7. Therefore:

w2_{neonate}(s) = \frac{1}{29} \left( \sum_t simval_{ts} \right), t \in neonates (4.6)

E = \sum_s w2_{neonate}(s) * e_s (4.7)

Likewise, in older infants, the weights and final MWM segmentation are given by equations 4.8 and 4.9:

w2_{infant}(s) = \frac{1}{9} \left( \sum_t simval_{ts} \right), t \in infants (4.8)

E = \sum_s w2_{infant}(s) * e_s (4.9)

As the weights w2(s) closely match the specific age range of the input dataset, they achieve more accurate delineation of myelin in the pediatric brain.
