## 2.2 Deep Learning Models for Medical Image Segmentation

### 2.2.1 Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)

CNNs are sophisticated neural network architectures designed to analyze two-dimensional (2D) images. However, it is applicable to both one-dimensional (1D) and three-dimensional (3D) data.

The core of any CNN system is the convolutional layer. Convolution, like a classic neural network, is a linear operation where weights are multiplied by the input. The data that is being input is given a linear transformation by having a 2D array of weights, also known as a mask or filter, applied to it. The size of this filter is less than that of the original data. As shown in Figure 2.1, the filter and the patch of the input that is the same as the filter undergo an element-wise multiplication to yield a single value. This process is iteratively applied to the full input image to produce a feature map, an array of features represented in two dimensions. By continuously applying the same filter to the whole image, the approach can locate a feature submerged within the image.

The term “translation invariance” describes this critical quality. One of CNN’s main benefits is that its filters do not have to be designed by hand. Instead, they may be learned automatically by back-propagation training, in which the reverse pass employs the convolution operation with spatially inverted filters (Litjens et al. 2017; Shi et al. 2021). Stacking CNN’s convolutional layers is a highly effective technique. Simple qualities like lines and boundaries are learned by the layers closest to the input, whereas more complicated features like shapes are learned by the layers farther into the model. CNN is a regularized MLP’s variant. The neurons in an MLP are all interconnected with each other. Over-fitting is possible due to the fully connected nature of these networks but can be avoided by regularization. Weights are typically added to the loss function in traditional regularization procedures. CNN, on the other hand, employs a distinct regularization method, one that makes use of the hierarchical pattern present in the data to reduce connectivity and complexity by assembling more complex patterns out of smaller and simpler patterns (Zhou et al. 2021; Kalchbrenner et al. 2014). Traditional algorithms’ filters are hand engineered, but CNN can learn them. This independence from additional feature engineering knowledge is its major strength. Some of the well-known variations of CNN include AlexNet, Inception, ResNet, VGG, SqueezeNet, and so on.

The idea behind segmentation is to categorize each pixel without giving them a thorough look. As a result, there is no spatial coherence among the pixels. DeepLab v1 was introduced by Chen et al. (2018b) to replace the two pooling strides that were originally used with just one. The padding size was also decreased from 100 to 1. Based on the VGG16 network, DeepLab v1 adds a full convolution layer in place of the final fully linked layer. The last two pooling layers were removed, and instead, they incorporated atrous convolution, a technique also employed for conditional random fields (Krähenbühl and Koltun 2011). DeepLab v2 (Chen et al. 2017b) introduced atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP) and integrated ResNet101 for its enhanced atrous convolution capability. ASPP is able to capture the context of images at varying scales. DeepLab v3 (Chen et al. 2018b) modified the ASPP module to incorporate a second channel of global picture pooling and batch normalization after convolution and before ReLU. To further enhance the segmentation results, especially at the target border, DeepLab v3+ (Chen et al. 2018a) added an efficient decoder module to DeepLab v3. SegNet (Badrinarayanan et al. 2017) is a symmetric encoder–decoder structure that accomplishes pixel-level image segmentation by using FCN's semantic segmentation task, as depicted in Figure 2.3. The structure comprises an encoding segment and a decoding segment. The encoder, constructed atop VGG16, analyzes data and objects within the network model. Each object's label

SegNet's decoder stands out for employing the max-pooling indices method during pooling to upsample low-resolution input features. Unlike FCN, which utilizes a deconvolution algorithm for nonlinear upsampling without learning, SegNet adopts a different approach. It utilizes a higher pooling index conveyed by the encoder, as depicted in Figure 2.4, which conserves space and yields commendable performance. Rather than storing the absolute maximum feature value, max-pooling indices record the position of the maximum feature value within the pooling window for each encoder map. By using the encoder’s transferred pool indices to upsample the input, a decoder produces a sparse feature map. The feature map is then convoluted with a trainable filter bank to increase its density. U-Net, in contrast, precedes convolution with concatenation by sending the complete feature map from the encoder to the decoder.
