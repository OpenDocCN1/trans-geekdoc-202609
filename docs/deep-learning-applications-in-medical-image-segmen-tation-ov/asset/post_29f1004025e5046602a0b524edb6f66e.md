It progresses in the early pediatric stages to reach full maturity. Coupled with this, there is vast variation in myelin development between subjects. This is particularly true in anomalous cases and premature births. However, despite all these concerns, it is clinically important to study myelin progression in the pediatric brain because:

- 1) Myelin formation mirrors brain maturation in the child. Age-appropriate myelination indicates that the growth and development of the brain are normal.
- 2) Abnormal myelination in the form of less-than-normal (hypomyelination), perceived delay (retarded myelination), or abnormality (dysmyelination) points to serious underlying brain disorders that must be detected and treated early.
- 3) Sometimes, even normal myelination (such as preterm births) needs close monitoring to diagnose potential developmental anomalies in the future.

In this light, we have proposed a qualitative and quantitative study of myelination. The major contributions of our work are:

- The research entails only structural brain MR images (T1 and T2 scans). We do not involve functional or quantitative MRI like other recent studies (Deoni et al. 2015; Chonka et al. 2016; Dean et al. 2016; Travis et al. 2019; Kuhne et al. 2021; Filimonova et al. 2023).
- Our approach acquires both 2D image segmentation and 3D visualization of MWM across a wide age range of babies (0–2 years).
- We introduce a novel MI to quantify the amount of WM that is myelinated at any given age. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first method to quantify the extent of WM myelination based on structural MRI scans.
- Further, an optimal growth model to accurately represent myelin progress in the pediatric brain is identified. Using this model, potential differences in hemispheric myelination are analyzed.
- We also study myelin maturation in premature babies to compare their developmental milestones with normal term-born children.

- Besides, the age range of the pediatric population is vastly different. While our method can visualize myelination even in 1-day-old babies, several approaches start with a minimum age of 3 months (Deoni et al. 2011; Deoni et al. 2012; Dean et al. 2014; Dean et al. 2016) or 1 year (Deoni et al. 2015).

In our research, we evaluate twelve growth models and the Gompertz model (MI(t) = c0 * e^(-c1 * e^(-c2 * t))) best describes myelin progress. The biological significance of these parameters is given below:

- c0 depicts the MI value at 2 years (c0 = 0.2366)
- c1 identifies the scale parameter based on initial MI value (c1 = 1.554)
- c2 shows the growth rate (c2 = 0.007592)

The MI value at term birth (MI(0)) is calculated as 0.05. Thus, we infer from the Gompertz model that 5% of the pediatric brain is myelinated at term birth and 23.66% is myelinated by 2 years. The growth rate is approximately 0.76%.

## 4.5.3 Hemispheric Differences in Myelination

The potential differences in the rate and progress of myelination between the right and left hemispheres are analyzed using the simple and complex models. The complex model fits significantly better, thereby indicating the presence of hemispheric variations. The growth rate parameters (c_r2 = 0.007498, c_l2 = 0.007636) symbolize a myelination rate of 0.75% in the right hemisphere and 0.76% in the left hemisphere. The myelination achieved at 2 years (c_r0 and c_l0) is higher for the left hemisphere compared to the right hemisphere. Therefore, we conclude that the left hemisphere myelinates rapidly relative to the right hemisphere.

A comparison with other recent publications reveals diverse results on hemispheric myelination. Some works report asymmetric myelin maturation and substantial hemispheric differences (left hemisphere myelinates faster), similar to our findings (Deoni et al. 2011). Structural asymmetries in WM pathways are also identified (Dubois et al. 2009). Whereas other studies perceive region-specific and age-specific cortical asymmetry, for example, asymmetry in the occipital lobe at 1 year and precentral/lingual gyri in later years (Deoni et al. 2015). Yet other researchers recognize no significant differences in hemispheric myelination (Deoni et al. 2012; Dean et al. 2016). Such a vast variation in the results is due to diversity in the age range of pediatric subjects, uneven sampling density, and dissimilarity in MRI techniques to study myelination between different methods.

## 4.5.4 Preterm Myelination

Currently, there is a paucity of works that quantify myelination progress in prematurely born babies. This can be attributed to the problems in segmenting and modeling preterm myelination such as high variability in premature brain development, lack of atlases and models for preterm births, and lack of availability of dedicated datasets. Thus, it is not practical to fit generic growth models for premature myelination, unlike term-born children. We obtain a percentage lag in the myelination index for preterm births to gauge their extent and level of myelination relative to equivalent-term babies. Our results reveal a myelination lag of 27.97–46.65%. A continuous monitoring and analysis of the lag in myelination is necessary to determine the age at which the myelination gap between premature and full-term babies closes. This will help determine the degree and duration of specialized medical care required for preterm neonates and infants.

## 4.6 Conclusion

The segmentation, visualization, and analysis of the myelinated regions of the pediatric brain are important from the clinical and research perspectives as they help detect normal brain maturation and growth. However, the study of MWM in pediatric subjects (including neonates, early, and late infants) is fraught with many challenges such as large variability in normal myelination, lack of uniform myelination in all areas, scarcity of specific datasets and myelin atlases, and need for additional quantitative MRI techniques. We propose a novel myelin segmentation and visualization method based only on clinical T1 and T2 MRI scans. The segmentation approach introduces a new weighted localized Tsallis entropy threshold to detect MWM. Using the myelin segmentation maps, we put forth a novel MI to quantify the extent of myelination. Furthermore, the Gompertz growth model is identified as the model of best fit to describe myelin progress in the pediatric brain based on AIC and BIC metrics. In addition, we investigate the hemispheric differences in myelination along with a close analysis of myelin development in premature babies. The results obtained show a clear demarcation in the myelination patterns of the right and left hemispheres, with the left hemisphere myelinating rapidly. Besides, we observe a 27.97–46.65% lag in the myelination of preterm children. On the whole, our study performs a qualitative and quantitative analysis of MWM that will aid clinicians to objectively identify various myelin disorders and anomalies such as hypomyelination, retarded myelination, and dysmyelination.
