In the realm of GANs, various objective functions can be applied. The initial objective function in GANs involves a minimax game between G and D, which can be expressed as follows (Equation 8.1):

```
\frac{Min}{G} \frac{Max}{D} V(D, G) = E_{x \sim p_{data}(x)}[\log D(x)] + E_{z \sim p_z(z)}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))] \tag{8.1}
```

log D(x) denotes the cross-entropy between [1 0]^T and [D(x) 1-D(x)]^T. log(1 - D(G(z))) denotes the cross-entropy between [1 0]^T and [D(G(z)) 1-D(G(z))]^T.

The ideal solution for a fixed G is given in Equation 8.2:

```
D^*_G(x) = \frac{p_{data}(x)}{p_{data}(x) + p_g(x)} \tag{8.2}
```

In certain situations, the gradient produced by Equation 8.1 may prove insufficient for effective learning by G. Early in the training process, G may generate examples that significantly deviate from the training data, leading to D's confident rejection of these initially generated instances. This often results in log(1 - D(G(z))) becoming saturated. As an alternative, instead of minimizing log(1 - D(G(z))), G can be trained to maximize log(D(G(z))). Consequently, the generator's cost can be expressed in Equation 8.3:

```
J(G) = E_{z \sim p_z(z)}[-log(D(G(z)))] = E_{x \sim p_g}[-log D(x)] \tag{8.3}
```

## 8.3.2 GAN Variants

In the following subsection, we will provide an introduction to a noteworthy GAN variant.

### 8.3.2.1 InfoGAN

The InfoGAN (Chen et al. 2016) introduced a different approach to noise vectors. Instead of using a single unstructured noise vector, it proposed decomposing the input noise into two distinct components: z, which represents incompressible noise, and c, known as the latent code. This latent code is designed to capture the essential structured semantic features found in the real data distribution. InfoGAN (Chen et al. 2016) seeks to minimize the objective function given in Equation 8.4:

```
\frac{min}{G} \frac{max}{D} V_{info}(D, G) = V(D, G) - \lambda I(c; G(z, c)) \tag{8.4}
```

- V(D, G) stands for the original GANs' objective function
- G(z, c) represents the generated example
- I denotes mutual information
- λ serves as a customizable regularization parameter.

The objective is to maximize I(c; G(z, c)), which enhances the mutual information between c and G(z, c), ensuring that c contains as many critical and meaningful features from real examples as possible. However, in practice, optimizing I(c; G(z, c)) directly is challenging since it necessitates access to the posterior distribution P(c|x).

A viable solution is to establish a lower bound for I(c; G(z, c)) by introducing an auxiliary distribution, Q(c|x), to approximate P(c|x). The final objective function for InfoGAN (Chen et al. 2016) is then defined as in Equation 8.5:

```
\frac{min}{G} \frac{max}{D} V_{info}(D, G) = V(D, G) - \lambda L_{info}(c; Q) \tag{8.5}
```

- L_info (c; Q) represents the lower bound for I(c; G(z, c)).

InfoGAN also features various variants, including causal InfoGAN (Kurutach et al. 2018) and semi-supervised InfoGAN (ss-InfoGAN) (Spurr et al. 2017).

### 8.3.2.2 DCGAN

DCGAN (Radford et al. 2016) unites GANs with CNNs, a powerful approach to computer vision. DCGAN introduces specific constraints on the network architecture of CNN to ensure stable training and to leverage learned feature representations for image classification. DCGAN enhances the quality of generated images through the following three key improvements.

- DCGAN employs strided convolutions for the discriminator and fractional strided convolutions for the generator, replacing conventional pooling layers. While CNNs are typically used for feature extraction, the CNN structure in DCGAN is tasked with generating samples, marking a departure from its usual role in feature extraction. Strided and fractional-strided convolutions efficiently transmit the majority of information to the subsequent layer, ensuring the completeness and clarity of the generated samples.

- DCGAN incorporates the batch normalization algorithm to tackle the problem of vanishing gradients. This algorithm resolves issues stemming from poor initializations, facilitates the propagation of gradients through each layer, and prevents the generator from converging all samples to a single point.

- DCGAN embraces various activation functions, including the Adam optimization method, the ReLU activation function, and the leakyReLU activation function. Empirical results affirm the strong performance of DCGAN in practical applications, underscoring the GAN structure's ability to generate samples. DCGAN is widely acknowledged as the gold standard when compared to other GAN models.

### 8.3.2.3 cGAN

GAN, as an unsupervised learning technique, derives the probability distribution patterns from unannotated datasets and expresses them in a gradual and unconstrained manner. Yet, when dealing with intricate or extensive datasets, GAN faces challenges in managing the outcomes it generates. To address this issue, a straightforward approach is to introduce constraints and establish specific objectives for the generator. GANs can be expanded into a conditional model when both the discriminator and the generator take extra information y into account. The objective function for conditional GANs (Mirza and Osindero 2014) can be compared to that of InfoGAN, as seen in Equations (8.5) and (8.6). However, in InfoGAN, the latent code c is not known in advance; it is actually discovered during training. Additionally, InfoGAN incorporates an extra network, Q, which outputs the conditional variables Q(c|x).

```
min_G max_D = E_{x ~ P_{data}(x)}[log(x/y)] + E_{z ~ P_z(z)}[log(1 - D(G(z/y)))] \tag{8.6}
```

Building upon the foundation of CGANs, it becomes possible to generate examples conditioned on various factors such as class labels, text bounding boxes, and key points.

### 8.3.2.4 ACGAN

Auxiliary Classifier Generative Adversarial Network (ACGAN) (Odena et al. 2017) is an advanced CGAN specialized in image synthesis. By simply providing a class label, it can generate an image from scratch. Notably, ACGAN produces images with higher resolution compared to earlier methods. The ACGAN's loss function is divided into two parts as given in Equations 8.7 and 8.8:

```
L_G = E[log P(S = real | X_{real})] + E[log P(S = Fake | X_{fake})] \tag{8.7}
L_C = E[log P(C = c | X_{real})] + E[log P(C = c | X_{fake})] \tag{8.8}
```

In this context, L_G represents the log-likelihood for evaluating the source's authenticity, while L_C corresponds to the log-likelihood for classifying the category or class. The training process involves the generator aiming to maximize the difference between L_C and L_G, while the discriminator is trained to maximize their sum, L_C + L_G.

ACGAN achieved a significant milestone as the first GAN variant capable of generating recognizable samples for all classes in the ImageNet dataset (Fei-Fei et al. 2010).

### 8.3.2.5 WGAN

In reference (Arjovsky et al. 2017), the authors introduced the Wasserstein distance, also known as the Earth-Mover (EM) distance, in the context of GANs for quantifying the dissimilarity between real and generated samples, as opposed to using the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence. The Wasserstein distance is defined in Equation 8.9:

```
W(p_r, p_g) = inf_{γ ~ Π(p_r, p_g)} E_{(x,y)~γ}[||x - y||] \tag{8.9}
```

where Π(p_r, p_g) represents the set of all possible joint distributions combining p_r and p_g, and γ encompasses all conceivable distributions.

The Wasserstein distance offers several advantages. It can calculate distances between distributions even when they do not overlap, possesses excellent smoothing characteristics, and mitigates the issue of gradient vanishing to some extent. Furthermore, the Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) addresses training instability problems and enhances the diversity of generated samples, eliminating the need for intricate balancing in the training of the generator (G) and discriminator (D).
