Deep learning is arguably one of the most noteworthy advancements in computer science in recent years. Its influence has permeated across numerous scientific disciplines, and it is already causing disruption and profound transformation within businesses and industries. Although significant progress has been made, there are still further advancements to be achieved in the field of deep learning. Deep learning holds the potential to enhance human lives by enabling more precise disease diagnoses, such as cancer, facilitating the discovery of new drugs, and predicting natural disasters. Many different kinds of deep architectures have been described in published works. It can be difficult to compare various designs because each has its own set of benefits that shift depending on the nature of the application and the data being processed. For instance, vision-related tasks typically favor convolutional neural networks (CNNs) (Lecun et al. 1998), while sequence and time series modeling tend to favor recurrent neural networks (RNNs) (Mikolov et al. 2010). Nonetheless, deep learning is a rapidly progressing domain. The demand for more human-like, efficient machines across a wide range of fields has led to the annual development of new architectures and learning algorithms.

Researchers have become interested in deep learning, which has demonstrated promising outcomes in a variety of applications. In this chapter, we begin by providing an overview of the many deep-learning methods currently in use for segmenting medical images. Then we explore the key applications of medical image segmentation, such as anatomy segmentation, before concluding with a discussion of the challenges of medical image segmentation.

# 2.2 Deep Learning Models for Medical Image Segmentation

# 2.2.1 Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)

CNNs are sophisticated neural network architectures designed to analyze two-dimensional (2D) images. However, it is applicable to both one-dimensional (1D) and three-dimensional (3D) data.

The core of any CNN system is the convolutional layer. Convolution, like a classic neural network, is a linear operation where weights are multiplied by the input. The data that is being input is given a linear transformation by having a 2D array of weights, also known as a mask or filter, applied to it. The size of this filter is less than that of the original data. As shown in Figure 2.1, the filter and the patch of the input that is the same as the filter undergo an element-wise multiplication to yield a single value. This process is iteratively applied to the full input image to produce a feature map, an array of features represented in two dimensions. By continuously applying the same filter to the whole image, the approach can locate a feature submerged within the image.
