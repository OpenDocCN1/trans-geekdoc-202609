Edge detection-based lung segmentation is used to highlight the edges or contours of the lungs, allowing for accurate segmentation. It depends on variations in pixel intensity between non-lung lung areas, capitalizing on sharp intensity changes near lung edges. Powell et al. originally used derivatives to identify rib cage boundaries, (Powell et al. 1988) a method subsequently enhanced by Xu et al. who applied the “second-order derivative” for contour identification to capture intricate details, (Xu and Doi 1995) albeit with a susceptibility to noise. Duryea et al. adopted a “heuristic technique” for edge tracing, (Duryea and Boone 1995) independently segmenting both lungs with an average processing duration of 0.84 seconds per image across 802 images. Li et al. further addressed challenges such as rib structure hindrance and false contours, (Li et al. 2001) achieving lung contours in 0.775 seconds per image by utilizing the first derivative. In the domain of intensity and edge-based segmentation, the challenges associated with deformed anatomical structures are addressed by methods incorporating prior understanding of texture, lung spatial, and shape characteristics. Luo et al. proposed a comprehensive approach that employs knowledge-based characteristics, (Luo et al. 2000) utilizing an object-oriented anatomical model, with robust initial contour construction and boundary detection using a snake model. Brown et al. also presented a similar method utilizing an anatomical model for both normal lung shapes and deformities. (Brown et al. 1998) While these approaches contribute to enhancing robustness in lung segmentation, they also face challenges in threshold determination, computational efficiency, and noise susceptibility in edge-based approaches. The choice of a rule-based technique depends on specific requirements and image characteristics.

# 7.3.2 Deformable Methods

These are the techniques that can adapt their shape and boundaries to accurately follow the complex and potentially irregular contours of the lungs. Unlike fixed models that rely on rigid templates or predefined shapes, deformable methods offer greater flexibility and can handle challenges like variability in lung shape and the presence of overlapped structures.
