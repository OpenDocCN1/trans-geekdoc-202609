
This 3D view offers a complete visualization of myelin development in the baby's brain from birth up to 2 years. Such a qualitative view of myelin progress also helps reinforce the neuroanatomic axioms of myelination (Welker and Patton 2012).

However, the above 3D visualization of myelination provides only a qualitative and subjective view. To attain a reliable, objective, and quantitative metric for myelin maturation, we propose a novel MI. The MI is obtained as the ratio of myelin present to the total brain. This index is age specific and increases as the baby grows from the neonatal to infancy and pediatric stages. The MI values for normal term-born babies are used in growth model fitting.

Various growth models are investigated to recognize the model that is best suited to represent the myelination of WM in children. Here, MI values are modeled as a continuous function of age (t days). It is ensured that the proposed model has biologically meaningful parameters that facilitate the interpretation of myelin progress with age. We consider the following twelve growth models that satisfy the above requirements:

- Basic models: Linear, quadratic, logarithmic, exponential, and negative exponential.
- Advanced models: Gompertz, logistic, Weibull, Richards, and three hyperbolastic models (Tabatabai et al. 2005).

Table 4.1 shows the equations for the different growth models. The parameters for these models (c₀–c₄) are adapted from Narinc (Narinc et al. 2010). Their biological significance is given below:

- c₀ – maximum MI value at 2 years
- c₁ – scale parameter (related to MI value at birth)
- c₂ – growth rate
- c₃ – shape parameter
- c₄ – parameter specific to hyperbolastic models

The growth models in Table 4.1 are fitted to the MI values. The best-suited model is obtained using Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Mathematically represented by equation 4.10:

```
AIC = n_d ln (RSS / n_d) + 2n_p (4.10)
```

Here, n_d represents the number of data points, RSS signifies the residual sum of squares and n_p indicates the number of estimated parameters. Nevertheless, this expression is suitable only for large datasets. When the ratio of n_d and n_p is less than 40, finite sample correction is done as in equation 4.11 (Wagenmakers and Farrell 2004):

```
AIC_corrected = n_d ln (RSS / n_d) + 2n_p + 2n_p(n_p + 1) / (n_d - n_p - 1) (4.11)
```

The corrected AIC metric is used in our work and simply denoted by AIC for convenience. In addition using equation 4.12,

```
BIC = n_d ln \frac{RSS}{n_d} + n_p ln(n_d) \tag{4.12}
```

Note that AIC and BIC have completely different frameworks with BIC housing a higher penalty term when n_d > e^2.

The optimal growth model is the one that achieves the lowest AIC and BIC metrics. For better analysis and interpretation of results, the Akaike and Bayesian weights are computed from the Akaike and Bayesian information criteria, respectively. For each model, the difference in AIC value is calculated as in equation 4.13:

```
Δ AIC_k = AIC_k - AIC_min, k = 1, 2 ... 12 \tag{4.13}
```

where AIC_min is the lowest AIC value of the twelve models. Further, the Akaike weights are represented as given in equation 4.14:

```
w_{AIC_k} = \frac{e^{-0.5 Δ AIC_k}}{\sum_{m=1}^{12} e^{-0.5 Δ AIC_m}} \tag{4.14}
```

Using these weights, the evidence ratio (ER) between models M_1 and M_2 is given by equation 4.15:

```
ER_{AIC} = \frac{w_{AIC_{M_1}}}{w_{AIC_{M_2}}} \tag{4.15}
```

Thus, the model M1 is ER times likely to be the optimal growth model compared to M2. Likewise, the Bayesian weights and corresponding ER_BIC are also computed as given in equation 4.16 and 4.17:

```
w_{BIC_k} = \frac{e^{-0.5\Delta BIC_k}}{\sum_{m=1}^{12} e^{-0.5\Delta BIC_m}}, k=1, 2 ... 12 \quad (4.16)
ER_{BIC} = \frac{w_{BIC_{M1}}}{w_{BIC_{M2}}} \quad (4.17)
```

Therefore, the ER is an essential metric to assess the relative likelihood of a model with respect to other models. This enables better decision-making in model selection.

## 4.3.4 Study of Hemispheric Differences in Myelination

The growth model fitting is expanded to evaluate potential hemispheric differences in myelination. There are several changes in the pediatric brain including developmental and maturational aspects, such as increase in brain size and cortical folding patterns. In this context, it is interesting to investigate the differences in myelination progress between the right and left hemispheres of the developing brain.

Primarily, we segment the hemispheres based on the inter-hemispheric fissure (IHF). The IHF is a deep groove that separates the right and left hemispheres. However, the IHF is not always oriented vertically to the image axis. There is a certain degree of rotational and translational offset. To identify the IHF, we propose a novel approach based on a pseudo midline (PML). For this, a horizontal projection of the extracted brain image (obtained in Section 3.2) is derived and its minimum is computed. The PML is designated as an imaginary line passing through this minimum point. This line is then rotated in small angular increments of 0.1° over the entire image. The rotated PML with zero or near-zero brain crossovers is considered the line of IHF. Using this IHF, the brain is split into its right and left hemispheres along with the myelinated regions, as shown in Figure 4.2.

After hemispheric separation, the MI values are obtained individually for the two hemispheres to study the inherent difference in myelin progress. The optimal growth model (obtained in Section 3.3) is fit in two ways:

- Simple model based on combined right and left hemispheric MI values.
- Complex model with two different curves for the right and left hemispheres.

The statistical F test is utilized to compare the above models and identify the model of best fit. The F ratio is calculated (as in equation 4.18:

$$F \text{ ratio} = \frac{(SS_{\text{simple}} - SS_{\text{complex}})/(\text{dof}_{\text{simple}} - \text{dof}_{\text{complex}})}{SS_{\text{complex}}/\text{dof}_{\text{complex}}} \quad (4.18)$$

Here, $SS_{\text{simple}}$ and $SS_{\text{complex}}$ refer to the sum of squares values for the two models, whereas $\text{dof}_{\text{simple}}$ and $\text{dof}_{\text{complex}}$ indicate their degrees of freedom. Subsequently, the $p$ value is derived and compared with the significance level ($\alpha = 0.05$), such that:

- If $p < \alpha$, the complex model is the best suited to describe myelination progress in the individual hemispheres of the brain.
- If $p \ge \alpha$, the simple model fits significantly better than the proposed complex model (Motulsky and Christopoulos 2003).

## 4.3.5 Study of Myelination in Premature Babies

As mentioned in Section 3.3, the computation of MI values and growth model fitting was done for full-term born babies. Nevertheless, there is considerable variation between the myelination patterns between premature and full-term births. This is attributed to the lower gestational time of preterm babies in the mother’s womb. Usually, term-born babies have a gestational age (GA) of 37–42 weeks, whereas premature babies have a much smaller GA and are segregated as follows:

- extremely preterm (GA <28 weeks)
- very preterm (GA: 28–32 weeks)
- moderate/late preterm (GA: 32–37 weeks)

The problem with premature births is that brain development is not completed at birth owing to its reduced intrauterine period. Once born, there are several physiological changes and variations in the external environment that affect brain maturation, especially WM myelination. Thus, it is essential to study preterm myelin progress and its relation to full-term myelination.

We calculate the MI values for premature babies (MI_preterm). Using the optimal growth model for myelination of full-term babies (obtained in Section 3.3), the corresponding MI values for term births at equivalent age are computed ($\widehat{\text{MI}}_{\text{term}}$).
