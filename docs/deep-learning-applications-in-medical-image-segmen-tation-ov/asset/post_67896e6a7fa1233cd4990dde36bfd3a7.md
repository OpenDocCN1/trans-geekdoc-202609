## 9.2 Methodology

The framework of the proposed approach comprises three distinct stages: pre-processing, data enhancement, and post-processing. During the pre-processing phase, resampling and data generation procedures are carried out. As a result, the image enhancement operation boosts the identification rate of subcomponents, while post-processing conducts segmentation of the enhanced cell images. Pre-processing operations were conducted on three biomedical databases: Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia International Database (ALL-IDB) (ALL-IDB 2024), JTSC (Zheng et al. 2018), and CellaVision (Zheng et al. 2018). The image samples from these datasets encompass leucocytes of five different types (lymphocytes, monocytes, neutrophils, basophils, and eosinophils), featuring varying dimensions that were standardized through resampling to a common size. Subsequently, once the resampling process is complete, two distinct appearances (Magenta and Saturation) of biological images are generated. These color channels contain high luminance information crucial for discriminating cells from background textures (Makem and Tiede et al. 2020).

The outcomes of pre-processing are directed toward the multilevel decomposition and fusion phase, where a significant enhancement in the quality of biological data occurs. We have constrained the data improvement process to four distinct levels due to the risk of image quality deterioration with further enhancements. This operation involves data decomposition and integration strategies. The medical observation decomposition is executed using latent low-rank due to its resilience to noise and its ability to minimize insufficiently sampled features (Liu and Yan 2011). The enhanced medical images are fed into a deep learning model for semantic segmentation. Various models are available for analyzing medical images, one of which is the U-net model, a fully convolutional neural network. Its architecture is segmented into encoder and decoder paths. The encoder path captures contextual information (the content of the image), while the decoder portion preserves spatial features (the location of information within the image). The encoder component consists of a sequence of convolutional and max-pooling layers. A convolutional layer serves as a fundamental unit for processing image data and detecting patterns and spatial features in the input data. The convolutional operation involves sliding a small filter (kernel) over the input data, resulting in a dot product between the filter's weights and the values in the receptive field of the input domain. The inputs provided to the convolutional operation include a cell imaging sample of size (n x n x channels) and a set of filters, each with a size of (i x i x channels). The output of the convolution yields an image or feature map with a size of (n-out x n-out x k).

The convolution operation is iterated multiple times within the network; as we progress deeper into the neural network, the size of the data matrix decreases. This reduction enables the filters of deeper layers to perceive a larger context of the data. While the height and width of the input data gradually diminish in convolution, the number of data filters increases, facilitating the extraction of complex patterns from the data.

The process of downsampling focuses on contextual features of the data and does not address spatial features. However, for medical cell segmentation, both contextual and spatial features are necessary. This requirement is met by the

upsampling operation, which preserves spatial information by converting a low-resolution image to a higher resolution one. Various upsampling techniques are available, such as bilinear interpolation, cubic interpolation, nearest neighbor interpolation, and transposed convolution. Most of these methods are based on interpolation, meaning the network does not learn on its own, and manual feature engineering is required. To achieve optimal upsampling, transposed convolution has been identified as the preferred method. Unlike other techniques, transposed convolution does not rely on preset conditions, and the network contains learnable parameters. Transposed convolution has been widely utilized in semantic segmentation to reconstruct the original sample size.

The U-net architecture is illustrated in Figure 9.5 and does not include dense layers, allowing it to accept data samples of any dimension. In our classical U-net design, various parameters are denoted by specific symbols, each carrying a distinct meaning. For instance, “2@Conv” denotes a pair of consecutive convolutional layers, while the output tensors of these convolutions are represented by “c1, c2,... cn.” The output tensors of the upsampling layers are prefixed with “u” (e.g. “u6, u7, u8, and u9”), and those of the downsampling layers are indicated by “p1, p2, p3, and p4.” The output of the encoder corresponds to an image size of 8*8*256, while that of the decoder segment is 128*128*1. This signifies that the contraction path reduces the image dimensions and increases the filter depth in deeper layers of the network, whereas the decoder segment reconstructs the original image size by incorporating skip connections with the upconvolutional layers. For example, the output of the upsampling layer u6 is obtained by concatenating the convolutional (c4) and deconvolution tensors (u6), denoted as u6 = u6 + c4. This process generates an image containing both local and global geometric features. We employed an Adam optimizer for model compilation, while the loss of the model is governed by binary cross-entropy due to the presence of only two classes: leucocytic cells and background. Learning rate decay is implemented for three consecutive epochs, and an early stopping condition is triggered if there is no improvement for five consecutive epochs.
