It is the primary cause of blindness in the elderly. A retinal blood artery blockage, known as a retinal vascular occlusion, can result in loss or distortion of vision. A set of hereditary conditions, known as retinal pigmentetosa, causes the retina's cells to gradually degrade and eventually disappear over time, resulting in tunnel vision and eventually blindness. The retina may peel away from the layer of support tissue behind it, a condition known as retinal detachment that frequently needs surgery to treat.

To identify retinal diseases early on and prevent irreversible vision loss, routine eye exams are essential. For those who have risk factors such as diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of eye illness, screenings are especially crucial. Many retinal problems can be treated early to help avoid additional vision loss and relieve discomfort (Wang et al. 2019). Therefore, early detection and precise diagnosis are crucial for the successful management and avoidance of irreversible visual impairment. Conventional approaches to diagnosing retinal problems are usually complicated, based on personal assessment, and susceptible to human fallibility. In order to overcome these constraints, there is a rising inclination toward investigating alternative sophisticated methods of image segmentation to enhance the early detection process. With the development of computer vision technology, scientists are investigating how to use fundus images and eye scans to identify different retinal illnesses using deep learning (DL). DL-based image segmentation has emerged as a viable approach to address these problems (Torre et al. 2020; Vij and Arora 2022). Artificial neural networks, which can be trained on enormous datasets, are used in DL to identify intricate patterns in images. Automating the segmentation procedure enables precise and efficient identification of retinal abnormalities (Mateen et al. 2020). This suggests that creating automated screening technologies might assist increase access to eye care. A common method involves training convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained using extensive datasets of retinal images that have been manually rated and categorized by specialists. In order to differentiate scans that show no symptoms of diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, glaucoma, or other disorders, the CNNs directly learn characteristics from pixel data (Alyoubi et al. 2020). Certain networks have proven to have accuracy levels similar to those of professional graders. DL has potential to identify individuals who require thorough eye exams by screening vast populations. It could assist in alleviating the global scarcity of resources for eye care by automating preliminary picture analysis (Sarki et al. 2020). Before the models can be used autonomously without human supervision, however, they still need to undergo thorough validation. To increase confidence in these algorithms' decisions, future research will also try to make them more explainable or easier to understand.
