### 2.3.1.7 Multi-organ

To protect vital organs during radiation planning, organs at risk (OAR) must be clearly defined. Before radiation for nasopharyngeal cancer, more than 20 organs must be precisely segmented. Since then, deep learning approaches have outperformed OAR segmentation due to recent developments in deep learning. Multi-organ segmentation is notoriously challenging due to the common occurrence of organ imbalances. The U-Net architecture has shown to be highly effective at segmenting medical images, making it the de facto standard. The features learned by ordinary convolution layers, on the other hand, are not distinguishable. To solve this issue, Wang et al. (2019d) suggest a unique architecture known as the densely connected deep U-Net (DC UNet). The DC U-Net comprises three components: dense connections, selective deconvolution layers incorporating upsampling, and a trans-convolutional filling function. To segment OARs in thoracic CT scans, Vesal et al. (2019) designed a deep learning framework for the following organs and structures: heart, oesophagus, trachea, and aorta. The method suggested employed a U-Net-inspired network featuring a bottleneck comprised of DCs and aggregated residual connections, leveraging global context and dense data. Zhang et al. (2020) introduced an effective cascaded V-Net model aimed at enhancing multi-organ segmentation performance, achieved through the establishment of dense block-level skip connections within the cascaded V-Net architecture. Crespi et al. (2023) proposed exhibiting three forms of ensembles of single-organ models able to construct multi-organ masks leveraging the different specializations of their components. Positive findings indicating that this may be a viable approach to the problem of developing effective multi-organ segmentation techniques. Xing et al. (2023) proposed Diff-UNet, a unique system for segmenting volumetric images. The method efficiently captures semantic information from the input volume through a conventional U-shaped architecture, resulting in superior pixel-level representations essential for medical volumetric segmentation. The writers also create a Step Uncertainty Fusion (SUF) module for inference, merging the outputs of diffusion models at each step to enhance the stability of prediction results from the diffusion model.

The authors also performed a thorough assessment of three datasets, demonstrating that Diff-UNet significantly surpasses other current methods in classifying multimodal brain tumors in MRI scans, liver tumors, and multi-organ CT volumes. ## 2.4 Current Challenges in Segmentation of Medical Images

When employing deep learning-engineered models for medical image segmentation, various obstacles arise. In this section, we will explore some fundamental challenges encountered in medical image segmentation, as outlined below.

Deep learning models generally necessitate a substantial volume of annotated training data to attain peak performance. However, obtaining a sufficiently large and diverse dataset with accurately annotated anatomical structures can be challenging, particularly for rare or uncommon structures.

- Class imbalance occurs when certain structures or lesions are underrepresented in the training data compared to others. Imbalanced class distributions can negatively impact the performance of deep learning models, leading to biased segmentations. Additionally, medical images often exhibit high variability in terms of shape, size, and appearance, which makes it challenging to develop models that can handle such variations effectively.

- Medical images often contain structures with complex shapes, ambiguous boundaries, or overlap with neighboring structures, such as organs, vessels, or lesions. Developing deep learning models that can handle such complex scenarios and accurately segment overlapping structures is a significant challenge.

- Anatomical structures can exhibit significant inter- and intra-patient variabilities in terms of size, shape, and appearance. Deep learning models need to be robust enough to handle such variations to generalize well across different individuals and clinical scenarios.

- Deep learning models trained on a specific dataset may struggle to generalize well to unseen medical images, especially when there are differences in imaging protocols, equipment, or patient populations. Achieving robustness and generalizability across different datasets and clinical settings is a challenging task.

- Deep learning models are frequently viewed as opaque systems, posing difficulties in interpreting and elucidating their segmentation decisions, especially in crucial medical contexts. Providing insights and explanations for the model's decisions is crucial for building trust and ensuring the clinical acceptance of deep learning-based segmentation algorithms.

- Even if a deep learning-based segmentation algorithm demonstrates promising results in research settings, its successful translation and adoption into clinical practice can be challenging. Factors such as integration with existing healthcare systems, validation in clinical trials, user friendliness, and acceptance by medical professionals need to be addressed for successful clinical adoption.

- Choosing an appropriate network architecture and optimizing its hyperparameters is a critical step in developing an effective segmentation model. Determining the optimal network depth, width, skip connections, and other architectural choices to handle anatomical structure complexity is a challenging task.
