### 8.2.2 Deep Learning Methods for Segmentation of Images

Medical images enable detailed examination of organ structures and facilitate quantitative analysis to assess clinical features related to shape and volume, particularly in studies of organs like the brain and heart. This analysis is achieved through the segmentation of organs and anatomical features. Various DL methodologies have been developed for segmenting organs in medical image analysis.

Several studies have proposed using DL for brain segmentation. For instance, H. Chen et al. (2018b) introduced a voxel-wise residual network (VoxResNet), which incorporates residual units to address the degradation issue that can occur when training very deep neural networks. This approach ensures performance improvements with increased network depth are fully realized. Authors (Borne et al. (n.d.)) proposed a cascaded V-Net approach for brain tumor segmentation, breaking the task into two steps: segmenting the entire tumor and delineating its distinct regions.

In the domain of retinal imaging, segmentation of blood vessels is crucial for diagnosing various eye conditions. The pioneering work of Liskowski and Krawiec (Liskowski and Krawiec 2016) employed a deep CNN for retinal blood vessel segmentation. Following this, CNNs have been extensively used for this task. Authors in Guo et al. (2019) presented a method involving pre-processing techniques to enhance low-contrast retinal images using a multi-scale, multilevel CNN with short connections to extract retinal vessels. Authors in Yan, Yang and Cheng (2019) developed a three-stage DL model to segment both thick and thin retinal vessels.

Lung image segmentation is essential for the accurate analysis of lung CT images in lung cancer detection. Authors in Ait Skourt, El Hassani, and Majda (2018) used the U-Net architecture for lung segmentation in CT images. Authors in Yahyatabar, Jouvét, and Cheriet (2020) tackled lung segmentation in chest X-rays, introducing Dense-U-Net based on U-Net to enhance information flow, facilitating faster model convergence and reducing parameters while maintaining high performance. Authors in Guo et al. (2019) introduced CE-Net, which uses dense atrous convolution and residual multi-kernel pooling for feature extraction, applied to various 2D medical image segmentation tasks, including lung and retinal optical coherence tomography (OCT) layer segmentation.

In abdominal imaging from CT and MRI scans, segmentation identifies structures such as the liver, pancreas, and kidneys. Authors in Christ et al. (2017) developed cascaded fully convolutional neural networks (CFCNs) for automatic segmentation of the liver and lesions in abdominal images. This network uses two cascaded fully convolutional networks (FCNs), where the first segments the liver’s region of interest (ROI), and the second identifies lesions within this ROI. Authors in Zhao et al. (2020) introduced a multi-scale supervised 3D U-Net (MSS) for segmenting kidneys and kidney tumors in CT images. Authors in Man et al. (2019) proposed a method inspired by a deep Q-network (DQN) with a deformable U-Net for pancreas segmentation, using 3D volume split into 2D slices along three axes.

For cardiac imaging, achieving automated heart region segmentation is crucial due to the significant threat posed by cardiac diseases. Authors in Xu, Wu, and Feng (2018) combined Faster R-CNN for rapid detection with 3D U-Net for powerful segmentation, introducing Conditional Function (CFUN) for comprehensive heart segmentation. Authors in Yan, Yang, and Cheng (2019) developed a deep-supervised 3D U-Net to enhance contextual information extraction at various network depths. Authors in Xia et al. (2019) introduced a two-stage framework for automated segmentation of atrial catheters, using a 3D U-Net for initial atrial center identification and another 3D U-Net for precise segmentation.

In breast ultrasound imaging, authors in Xia et al. (2019) divided images into four primary tissues – skin, fibroglandular tissue, mass, and fatty tissue – using three orthogonal image planes. A CNN was used to identify the tissue class of the central pixel within image blocks.

## 8.3 Generative Adversarial Networks

This section presents the core principles of GANs and elucidates various notable variations. These variants encompass a spectrum of advancements within the GAN framework, incorporating conditional generative adversarial networks (CGANs), deep convolutional generative adversarial networks (DCGANs), information generative adversarial networks (InfoGANs), auxiliary classifier generative adversarial networks (AC-GANs), Wasserstein generative adversarial networks (Wasserstein-GANs), cycle generative adversarial networks (CycleGANs), and progressively growing generative adversarial networks (ProGANs), among others.

### 8.3.1 Vanilla GAN

One of the most highly contested subjects within the field of ML is the concept of GANs, a generative technique introduced by Goodfellow and his colleagues in 2014 (Goodfellow et al. 2020). The remarkable success of GANs in a range of applications such as image processing (Ledig et al. 2017; Kazeminia et al. 2020), artistic style transfer (Knyaz et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2019), classification (Israel et al. 2017), medical imaging, and image generation (Li et al. 2018; Lin et al. 2017) underscores the substantial progress GANs have made in various computer vision tasks. GAN models have been proposed in numerous variations across different domains over time. In recent years, GANs have emerged as one of the most actively researched algorithms in the realm of DL.

Within GANs, there are two crucial components: the generator and the discriminator. These two components can be implemented using any differentiable system that transforms data from one space to another, although neural networks are commonly employed for this purpose. MLPs were utilized in the generator G and discriminator D of the initial GANs. The role of the generator is to create new data samples while attempting to mimic the distribution of real-world examples. On the other hand, the discriminator typically functions as a binary classifier, aiming to distinguish between generated instances and real instances.

GANs are optimized through a Minimax optimization problem. This optimization process converges at a saddle point, which represents the maximum for the discriminator and the minimum for the generator. In essence, the goal of GAN optimization is to reach Nash equilibrium, at which point the generator accurately captures the distribution of real-world examples.
