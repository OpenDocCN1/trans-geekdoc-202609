Because of this, DL methods have been developed to take advantage of image features (such as variations in contrast, orientation, form, and texture patterns) more effectively using data-driven means.

Recently, Artificial Intelligence (AI) and DL models have excelled in medical image segmentation, becoming the current state-of-the-art. There is now a shift occurring between systems that rely on manually created features (ML) and systems that derive features directly from data (DL). Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are the foundation of the most popular models in use today. The U-Net convolutional architecture (Ronneberger et al. 2015) in particular has been widely accepted due to its capacity to output comprehensive delineations with a relatively small quantity of training data. However, new attention-based paradigms have emerged alongside the growing interest in models based on pure and hybrid Transformers (Shamshad et al. 2023).

Since its inception in 2014, generative adversarial networks (GANs), a DL model, has garnered substantial attention in both academic and industrial circles, experiencing rapid growth in popularity. ## 8.2 Overview of Image Segmentation Techniques

The recent medical image segmentation approaches are reviewed in this section.

### 8.2.1 Traditional Methods for Segmentation of Images

Traditional approaches for medical image segmentation encompass a range of established techniques rooted in classical computer vision and image processing. These methods, such as thresholding, edge-based models, region-growing models, and graph-cut models, rely on mathematical and algorithmic principles to partition medical images into distinct regions or structures.

Thresholding involves dividing an input image into regions based on a chosen threshold, and multiple thresholds can be employed in multi-threshold segmentation (Senthilkumaran and Vaithgei 2016). Selecting an appropriate threshold is challenging, as higher values may lead to fragmented boundaries and overlook subtle segments. Thresholding is sensitive to noise and lacks spatial linkage awareness between different image characteristics. Thresholding has been applied in a number of applications, like brain tumor detection from MRI images and its combination with other techniques. Wittke et al. (2007) employed classification-based thresholding to produce a binary image from histological slide sections of prostate carcinoma. While thresholding is easy and fast, finding the optimal threshold automatically can be complex, and iterative thresholding may be necessary for noisy images like CT scans or ultrasound images. Overall, thresholding is a widely used method but requires careful consideration for effective application.

Edge-based methods rely on edge detection to identify boundaries and object contours. Various edge detection operators rely on gradient (derivative) functions, including but not limited to Roberts, Prewitt, Sobel, Marr–Hildreth, Laplacian, and Canny edge detectors (Song et al. 2017). The Canny edge detector, in particular, is highlighted for its optimization involving edge enhancement and tracing. Edge-based segmentation addresses challenges like under-segmentation, over-segmentation, and false edges. Kattire et al. (Shah (n.d.)) presented a two-stage method wherein they initially acquire edge information by considering the magnitude and direction of the image gradient, and subsequently generate the edge map based on the texture. While edge-based methods are simple, they may perform poorly in the presence of noise, and some edge detectors are suitable for X-ray pre-processing.

In the Region Growing Method, pixels are grouped into homogeneous regions by iteratively expanding from seed points. The growth of each region is guided by a similarity criterion, often defined as the difference between a pixel’s intensity value and the mean intensity of the region. However, region-growing methods often need manual seed setting, making them sensitive to noise and resulting in regions with holes. Challenges include finding appropriate thresholding values, especially in noisy images. Despite their simplicity and ease of implementation, region-growing approaches have drawbacks, including the need for manual interaction, sensitivity to initialization, high processing time, and the potential for non-connected regions. Tan et al. (2017) suggested an approach for detecting tumors in PET images by employing adaptive region growth with a maximum curvature strategy (ARG-MC).

Graph-cut-based segmentation methods have proven effective for optimal global segmentation. These methods transform segmentation into a maximum flow problem with polynomial time complexity, treating the image as a graph where nodes represent pixels/voxels, including special nodes for the source (S) and sink (t) connected by t-edges and other nodes connected by n-edges. Object (O) and background (B) seeds are denoted, and the goal is to achieve an optimal cut distinguishing objects from the background. Popular algorithms include normalized cuts and minimum cuts.
