# 7.5.2 CT Scan Datasets

With the advancement of medical imaging technology, CT scan datasets have emerged as vital tools for examining lung diseases, offering researchers access to a rich repository of medical images and associated clinical data. Some of the commonly used CT datasets for lung diseases are the following.

**LIDC-IDRI:** Armato et al. introduced the LIDC-IDRI dataset in “The Lung Image Database Consortium (LIDC) and Image Database Resource Initiative (IDRI): a completed reference database of lung nodules on CT scans.” This dataset is a collaborative effort by the Lung Tissue Research Consortium, consisting of 1,018 low-dose lung CT scans from 1,200 patients. Enhanced with lesion annotations meticulously provided by experienced thoracic radiologists, the dataset includes associated characteristics such as calcification and malignancy. This resource plays a crucial role in advancing research in lung tissue analysis and serves as a valuable reference for studying various lung conditions. The CT images in this dataset are in the DICOM format, with a total size of 125 GB and a resolution of [65,764]×512×512 voxels for each scan, along with its associated metadata file. An average scan has 240 slices, with an average slice thickness of 1.73 mm. (Armato et al. 2010).

**LUNA 16:** The LUNA16 dataset, specialized in lung nodule detection, contains 888 sets of 3D CT images. Focused on pulmonary nodule segmentation, LUNA16 provides a rich resource for the creation and assessment of algorithms targeting the detection and characterization of pulmonary nodules.

**LOLA11,** an acronym for Lobe and Lung Analysis Challenge, (Lola11 n.d.) is a dataset specifically designed for challenges related to the analysis of lung lobes. Comprising 55 volumetric chest CT scans from various sources, this dataset offers a diverse range of scans that include significant pathologies and abnormalities.

**SPIE-AAPM:** Initiated by the “SPIE Medical Imaging Conference in collaboration with the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM),” enables researchers to evaluate and contrast their methods in employing quantitative image analysis to categorize nodules as either benign or malignant.
