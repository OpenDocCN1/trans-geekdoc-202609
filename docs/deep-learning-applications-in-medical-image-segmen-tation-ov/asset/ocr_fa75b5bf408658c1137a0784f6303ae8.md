# 6.1 Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) and associated retinal illnesses pose important worldwide health issues, impacting millions of people and resulting in profound vision impairment if not treated (Aziz et al. 2021). The retina is a light-sensitive tissue located in the back of the eye that is essential for vision. However, over time, several medical disorders might harm the fragile retina, which could endanger vision. DR is a prevalent retinal condition that is particularly prevalent in the diabetic population. Elevated blood sugar levels linked to the condition may lead to gradual alterations in the retina by harming small blood vessels. DR can progress to the point of blindness if left untreated. (Aziz et al. 2021) In addition to diabetes, there are a number of additional retinal disorders that can arise and affect vision. Diabetes-related high blood sugar levels can lead to the blockage, enlargement, or leakage of fluid or blood from small blood vessels in the retina. An individual is more likely to acquire diabetes the longer they have the disease. Depending on the extent of retinal damage, DR can go through many phases. Microaneurysms, or tiny vascular bulges, appear in the early stages. As the disease advances, more severe retinal abnormalities such as hemorrhages, cotton-wool patches, and hard exudates appear. The most advanced form of retinopathy is proliferative retinopathy, in which new fragile blood vessels sprout on the retina or optic disc. These aberrant blood vessels can cause blindness by leaking blood or scarring the retina. Another vision-threatening consequence of proliferative retinopathy is retinal detachment, which occurs when the retina rips away from the back of the eye.
