## Analysis of Deep Learning Models for Lung Field Segmentation

Tairah Andrabi and Sajid Yousuf Bhat

Department of Computer Science, University of Kashmir, Srinagar, Jammu & Kashmir, India

### 7.1 Introduction

Lung diseases cover a broad spectrum of health issues impacting breathing and overall well-being. Ranging from infectious diseases such as pneumonia to persistent disorders such as emphysema, “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD),” and even potentially life-threatening conditions such as lung cancer and COVID-19, these pose significant challenges to healthcare globally. To diagnose diseases related to the lungs, healthcare professionals utilize an array of advanced medical imaging modalities, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), chest X-rays (CXR), computed tomography (CT) scans, and positron emission tomography (PET). Among these, Chest X-rays stand out as a pivotal diagnostic tool. Chest X-rays (CXR), also known as chest radiographs, are a fundamental imaging modality in the field of medical diagnostics. Chest X-rays (CXRs) provide crucial insights about the heart, lungs, ribs, and surrounding tissue. They are known for their cost-effectiveness, widespread availability, low radiation dose, and ability to provide valuable insights that other imaging techniques may not offer as easily. Due to these advantages, a substantial volume of X-rays is generated daily, making them a cornerstone of medical diagnostics. Despite its affordability, interpreting CXRs demands considerable expertise and poses challenges due to their complexity. Manual examination of X-rays is both time-consuming and labor-demanding, emphasizing the requirement for an automated diagnostic system.

Computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) tools are a category of software applications designed to assist healthcare professionals, particularly radiologists, in interpreting medical images and making accurate diagnoses. CAD has seen rapid advancements, and its incorporation into clinical practice not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also accelerates the decision-making process, leading to more prompt interventions and improved patient outcomes.

In recent years, medical image analysis has seen remarkable progress, with image segmentation, an integral aspect, extracting crucial and meaningful information from medical images. Lung field segmentation (LFS), a prerequisite step, precisely delineates the region of interest. However, this task comes with its own set of challenges such as (i) variations in patient positioning, (ii) the presence of superimposed anatomical structures such as the heart, diaphragm, clavicles, and rib cage influenced by factors due to age and gender (Agrawal and Choudhary 2023), (iii) potential distortions in lung appearance due to underlying pathologies, and (iv) variations in image quality, contrast, and brightness settings across different imaging devices. Furthermore, achieving precise segmentation of the lung apex is complicated by the diverse intensity levels present in the upper clavicle bone region. Addressing these obstacles is crucial for ensuring the accuracy and reliability of segmentation algorithms.

Over the past 40 years, LFS has attracted significant attention, witnessing advancements from basic rule-based approaches to sophisticated deep-learning methods, as reflected in various high-quality publications. Van Ginneken et al. provided a foundational classification of LFS techniques, organizing them into four primary categories (Van Ginneken et al. 2001): “(i) rule-based, (ii) pixel-classification (PC)-based, (iii) deformable model-based, and (iv) hybrid methods.” Initially, lung segmentation methods in CAD systems relied on specific rules that defined the relationships between the lungs and neighboring structures. Later, in the 1990s, pixel-level classification emerged, categorizing individual pixels as either lung or non-lung based on manually derived features (considered shallow learning). In the 2000s, deformable model-based methods utilized models capable of adapting under internal and external influences to outline lung shapes. Despite the existence of several LFS methods, ongoing research strives to elevate their efficacy. Presently, researchers focus on automatic feature extraction through deep learning, emphasizing a promising area for advancement within this domain.

LFS using deep learning employs a diverse range of sophisticated models such as “convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), encoder-decoder architectures,” and other advanced architectures. Among these, U-Net based on encoder-decoder architecture has emerged as a popular choice for semantic segmentation, particularly in the context of lung segmentation. Its effectiveness in capturing contextual information makes it well suited for the intricate structures present in medical images obtained from chest X-rays or CT scans. DeepLab, a state-of-the-art architecture utilizing atrous convolutions, has also been adapted for lung segmentation tasks. Notably, DeepLab excels in handling diverse image resolutions, showcasing success in precisely segmenting lung fields.
