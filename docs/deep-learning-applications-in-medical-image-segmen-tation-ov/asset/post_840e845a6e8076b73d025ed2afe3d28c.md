Global thresholding assigns a single threshold value to the entire image, while adaptive thresholding calculates threshold values locally based on the characteristics of small regions within an image. ### 5.2.2 Region Growing

Region growing is a bottom-up segmentation approach that groups pixels into regions based on similarity criteria. The process typically starts with seed points that can be manually selected or automatically generated. Neighboring pixels are then iteratively added to the region if they meet predefined similarity conditions, such as having similar intensity values or gradients. Region-growing algorithms continue expanding regions until no more pixels can be added, resulting in coherent and homogeneous segments.

### 5.2.3 Edge Detection

Edge detection techniques focus on identifying abrupt changes or discontinuities in pixel intensity, which often correspond to object boundaries in the image. Two popular edge detection algorithms are (i) the Sobel operator which is named after Irwin Sobel, and (ii) the Canny edge detector named after John Canny. The former detects edges by calculating the gradient magnitude of an image, highlighting regions with high-intensity gradients. The latter, in contrast, is a multistage method that combines gradient computation, noise reduction, non-maximum suppression, and hysteresis thresholding to detect edges while minimizing false positives.

### 5.2.4 Clustering

Clustering-based segmentation methods involve grouping pixels together based on their similar features, such as color, intensity, or texture. One commonly used clustering technique is the K-means algorithm, which iteratively assigns pixels to clusters based on their proximity to the mean value of the cluster. GMMs extend the K-means algorithm by modeling the distribution of pixel intensities as a mixture of Gaussian distributions, allowing for more flexible cluster shapes.

### 5.2.5 Watershed Transform

The watershed transform is a morphological segmentation method inspired by hydrology, where pixel intensities are treated as elevation values and flooding is simulated from immersion points (markers) to delineate regions based on watershed basins. This technique is particularly useful for segmenting objects with distinct boundaries and has applications in medical imaging, remote sensing, and industrial inspection.

Classical image segmentation techniques also present numerous challenges, such as over-segmentation and under-segmentation, which can occur in images with complex backgrounds or uneven lighting conditions. In addition, classical segmentation algorithms often involve manual tuning of parameters, which is often time-taking and may be prone to human error, leading to suboptimal results. Moreover, classical techniques may find it difficult to segment images with a lot of noise or artifacts, resulting in inaccurate segmentation results. Finally, some classical segmentation algorithms may not scale well to large images or datasets, limiting their applicability in real-world scenarios.

## 5.3 Deep Learning-Based Image Segmentation Methods for Medical Images

In recent years, deep learning techniques such as CNNs have revolutionized the area of image segmentation, particularly in the context of medical imaging. The architecture of CNNs is well suited for learning hierarchical representations of visual data, making them particularly effective for tasks such as medical image segmentation. In medical image segmentation, CNNs are used to automatically extract and learn key features from raw image data without requiring a manual or handcrafted feature extraction. They can effectively capture complex patterns and spatial relationships in medical images, allowing for more accurate segmentation of anatomical structures, lesions, and abnormalities. Several CNN architectures have been developed or adapted specifically for medical image segmentation tasks (Kayalibay et al. 2017; Malhotra et al. 2022). One popular architecture is U-Net, which has a symmetric expanding path for accurate localization and a contracting path for recording context (Ronneberger et al. 2015; Siddique et al. 2021). U-Net has been widely used in biomedical image segmentation because of its capability to generate high-quality segmentation masks.

Another common approach is the use of FCNs, which uses convolutional layers in place of fully connected layers (Dai et al. 2016). This allows FCNs to process images of arbitrary sizes and produce dense pixel-wise predictions. FCNs have been effectively used in medical image segmentation tasks, allowing for precise delineation of anomalies and structures from a variety of imaging modalities. Deep learning techniques that are used for medical image segmentation also often incorporate advanced techniques such as attention mechanisms, conditional random fields (CRFs), and multi-scale architectures to improve segmentation accuracy and robustness (Guo et al. 2020).

Despite all these advantages of deep learning methods, there are still several challenges to be addressed while applying them for medical image segmentation. These include the requirement of large annotated datasets, high-end computational resources such as graphics processing unit (GPU), and the interpretability of results. But the recent progress in deep learning-based approaches and the availability of large-scale medical imaging datasets provide hope for further improving the accuracy and applicability of DL-based segmentation methods in processing medical images (Bhattacharya et al. 2021).

Deep learning methods, particularly CNNs and their variants, have brought significant advancement to the field of medical image segmentation by allowing for automated and accurate delineation of structures and abnormalities from medical images. These methods have the potential to revolutionize medical diagnostics, treatment planning, and patient care by providing valuable insights from imaging data to clinicians.

The following section presents elaborate discussions on some deep learning algorithms in the area of medical image segmentation.

### 5.3.1 Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)

O'Shea and Nash (2015) proposed CNNs, a benchmark algorithm in the domain of image classification. CNNs are specifically designed to handle structured grid-like data, such as images (Khan et al. 2020). A CNN architecture typically consists of alternate layers of convolution and pooling, and one or more fully connected layers at the end. Sometimes, a global average pooling is used in place of a fully connected layer (Khan et al. 2020). CNNs are frequently used for tasks such as object identification, image classification, and segmentation. They have had a significant impact on the field of computer vision. The simplified architecture of CNN is given in Figure 5.2.

#### 5.3.1.1 Convolutional Layer

A core component of CNNs is the convolutional layer, where a set of special filters called convolutional kernels are applied to the input image as shown in Figure 5.3. These kernels slide over the image, computing the dot product between its weights and the values in the receptive field. This way, it checks every small part of the image to see how much it matches the pattern the kernel is looking for (Khan et al. 2020). If the kernel is symmetrical, the operation becomes a correlation operation. This convolutional kernel divides the image into small receptive fields, starts extraction of feature motifs, and convolves with the image using specific weights to capture relevant information.

#### 5.3.1.2 Activation Function

After the convolution operation, an activation function such as Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) is applied to each element. The activation function brings nonlinearity into the neural network, enabling CNNs to learn complex and non-linear relationships among input features. Apart from ReLU, there are many other activation functions such as sigmoid, tanh, maxout, SWISH, and variants of ReLU – such as leaky ReLU, ELU, and PReLU – that are used to introduce nonlinearity (Gu et al. 2018). This step is important as it helps the model learn complex patterns and features from the data.

#### 5.3.1.3 Pooling Layers

Pooling layers are commonly inserted between successive convolutional layers to reduce the spatial dimensions of the feature maps while retaining important information. Common pooling operations include max pooling and average pooling, which downsample the feature maps by taking the maximum or average value within each pooling window.

#### 5.3.1.4 Fully Connected Layers

In the final stages of the network, one or more fully connected layers are typically added to perform high-level reasoning and decision-making. These layers connect every neuron in one layer to every neuron in the next layer, allowing the network to learn intricate relationships between features extracted by the convolutional layers.
