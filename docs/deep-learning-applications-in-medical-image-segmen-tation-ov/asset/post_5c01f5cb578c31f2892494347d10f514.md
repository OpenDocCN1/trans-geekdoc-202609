
Gaussian noise alters intensity values by randomly sampling from a Gaussian distribution. Salt-and-pepper noise randomly assigns pixel values to either white or black. Uniform noise is incorporated by adjusting pixel values through random sampling from a uniform distribution.

## Random Cropping and Erasing

Random cropping augmentation entails choosing a small section from the original image and resizing it to match the original image's dimensions. As a result, this augmentation technique can also be termed scaling or zooming. Random erasing augmentation involves randomly eliminating sections of the image.

## Color Modification

Color modification for image augmentation encompasses various techniques. For instance, an RGB-colored image is represented as an array, with three color channels denoting red, green, and blue intensity levels. This setup allows for altering the image’s color while retaining its spatial characteristics. This process, known as color space shifting, can be executed across multiple color spaces, as the original color channels are combined differently in each space. One approach to augmenting an RGB-colored image through color modification involves transforming the pixel values within the image’s color channels using filters applied to a histogram, thereby creating new images with altered color space attributes. Another method involves applying three shifts (integer values) from RGB filters and incorporating each shift into a channel within the original image. Additionally, image augmentation can involve adjusting hue, saturation, and value components, isolating specific color channels (e.g. blue, red, or green), and converting between different color spaces. However, converting a color image to grayscale should be done cautiously, as this conversion may reduce performance by up to 3% (Chatfield et al. 2014).

## Generative Models

Basic data augmentation methods outlined earlier are inadequate when handling intricate data such as medical images. Several studies have been conducted to propose data augmentation techniques more appropriate for medical applications. The overarching objective is to replicate a data distribution that closely resembles real-world data, making it challenging, if not impossible, to differentiate between newly generated samples and authentic data. Recent advancements in deep generative models have rendered them especially appealing for facilitating data augmentation tasks. For instance, generative adversarial networks (GANs) (Goodfellow et al. 2020) have showcased their capacity to produce life-like images, leading to their widespread adoption in medical applications (Sandfort et al. 2019; Mahapatra et al. 2019). However, GANs also come with their limitations, including issues like learning instability, challenges in convergence, and susceptibility to mode collapse (Mescheder et al. 2018), wherein the generator generates only a limited set of samples. Variational autoencoders (VAEs) (Kingma and Welling 2014) represent another category of deep generative models, albeit receiving less focus in the realm of data augmentation. VAEs excel in output diversity compared to GANs and avoid mode collapse. However, a notable drawback is their tendency to often produce blurred and unclear output images, which arises from the regularization term incorporated in the loss function. In recent times, a novel category of deep generative models known as diffusion models (DMs) (Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015; Ho et al. 2020) has surfaced, showing considerable potential in generating varied and lifelike outputs. Despite this promise, DMs are still in the early stages of development and have not gained significant traction in medical applications. However, they are anticipated to offer a compelling alternative to existing generative models. A drawback of DMs is their substantial computational expense and lengthy sampling duration. Guibas et al. (Guibas et al. 2017) suggest a two-step process for creating artificial fundus photographs along with corresponding blood vessel segmentation masks. The authors exclusively evaluated the generated images based on a segmentation task, attaining an F1 score of 0.887 as opposed to 0.898 achieved with real images. This marginal difference indicates the comparable quality of the generated images. Platscher et al. (Platscher et al. 2022) conducted a comparative evaluation of these three frameworks, demonstrating their capacity to generate high-fidelity images ideal for medical image segmentation and translation tasks, leading to a 9.1% improvement in Dice score. These frameworks hold the potential to substantially decrease the necessity for manual annotation of medical images, consequently reducing the time and cost associated with data annotation. Huo et al. (Huo et al. 2022) introduce a progressive VAE-based framework (PAVAE) intended for creating artificial brain lesions alongside their associated segmentation masks. The authors propose a two-phase strategy, where the first phase involves generating synthetic segmentation masks using a conditional adversarial VAE. In the following stage, photorealistic lesion images are generated by incorporating them with the lesion mask through "mask embedding blocks." These blocks embed the lesion mask into the feature space during generation, similar to the approach used in Spatially-Adaptive Denormalization (SPADE). A segmentation network is trained using a combination of genuine and synthetic lesions, resulting in an enhanced Dice score compared to a model trained solely on authentic images from 66.69% to 74.18%. Fernandez and colleagues (Fernandez et al. 2022) present brainSPADE, a generative model designed to create labeled brain MRI images suitable for training segmentation models. This model merges a DM with a VAE-GAN, leveraging SPADE normalization in the GAN component to integrate the segmentation mask effectively. The model comprises two main parts: a segmentation map generator and an image generator. The segmentation map generator, a VAE, initially processes a segmentation map, extracting and structuring its latent space. This space is refined via diffusion and denoising with Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) to emphasize meaningful information while filtering out noise. A VAE decoder subsequently generates an artificial segmentation map from this refined latent space. The image generator, a SPADE model, establishes a style latent space from any provided style and incorporates it with the artificial segmentation map to produce the ultimate output image. The effectiveness of brainSPADE is evaluated in a segmentation task using nnU-Net, showcasing similar performance when trained on synthetic data as compared to real data, with a notable performance improvement observed when both types of data are merged.
