Thresholding has been applied in a number of applications, like brain tumor detection from MRI images and its combination with other techniques. Wittke et al. (Wittke et al. 2007) employed classification-based thresholding to produce a binary image from histological slide sections of prostate carcinoma. While thresholding is easy and fast, finding the optimal threshold automatically can be complex, and iterative thresholding may be necessary for noisy images like CT scans or ultrasound images. Overall, thresholding is a widely used method but requires careful consideration for effective application.

Edge-based methods rely on edge detection to identify boundaries and object contours. Various edge detection operators rely on gradient (derivative) functions, including but not limited to Roberts, Prewitt, Sobel, Marr–Hildreth, Laplacian, and Canny edge detectors (Song et al. 2017). The Canny edge detector, in particular, is highlighted for its optimization involving edge enhancement and tracing. Edge-based segmentation addresses challenges like under-segmentation, over-segmentation, and false edges. Kattire et al. (Shah (n.d.)) presented a two-stage method wherein they initially acquire edge information by considering the magnitude and direction of the image gradient, and subsequently generate the edge map based on the texture. While edge-based methods are simple, they may perform poorly in the presence of noise, and some edge detectors are suitable for X-ray pre-processing.

In the Region Growing Method, pixels are grouped into homogeneous regions by iteratively expanding from seed points. The growth of each region is guided by a similarity criterion, often defined as the difference between a pixel’s intensity value and the mean intensity of the region. However, region-growing methods often need manual seed setting, making them sensitive to noise and resulting in regions with holes. Challenges include finding appropriate thresholding values, especially in noisy images. Despite their simplicity and ease of implementation, region-growing approaches have drawbacks, including the need for manual interaction, sensitivity to initialization, high processing time, and the potential for non-connected regions. Tan et al. (Tan et al. 2017) suggested an approach for detecting tumors in PET images by employing adaptive region growth with a maximum curvature strategy (ARG-MC).

Graph-cut-based segmentation methods have proven effective for optimal global segmentation. These methods transform segmentation into a maximum flow problem with polynomial time complexity, treating the image as a graph where nodes represent pixels/voxels, including special nodes for the source (S) and sink (t) connected by t-edges and other nodes connected by n-edges. Object (O) and background (B) seeds are denoted, and the goal is to achieve an optimal cut distinguishing objects from the background. Popular algorithms include normalized cuts and minimum cuts.
