### 5.3.1.5 Softmax Activation

For classification tasks, the output layer of the CNN often utilizes a softmax activation function, which normalizes the output scores across classes into a probability distribution. This enables the network to output probabilities indicating the likelihood of each class.

## 5.3.2 U-Net

U-Net is a CNN architecture developed by Ronneberger et al. (2015) as depicted in figure 5.4. It is a widely used architecture for biomedical image segmentation and semantic segmentation tasks. U-Net is based on the FCN.

U-Net has an encoder–decoder network architecture in the shape of a U. It has four encoder and decoder blocks joined by a bridge. The encoder and decoder networks’ information flow is completed by the bridge. At every block, the encoder network doubles feature channels and cuts spatial dimensions in half, functioning as a feature extractor by activating convolutional layers before ReLU. The skip connections improve gradient flow and conserve information for improved semantic features, and the max-pooling lowers computing costs. By upscaling and concatenating skip connection feature maps, the decoder network creates segmentation masks. Convolutions and sigmoid activation are then used for pixel-wise classification. The researcher found U-Net found a wide range of applications in the domain of brain tumor detection. Akter et al. proposed a deep CNN and U-Net for image classification and segmentation of brain tumor images and they could achieve accuracy of 90.8% (Akter et al. 2024). Cheng et al. proposed a deep network incorporating VGG-19 network in the coding stage of U-Net for edge detection in brain tumor images. They also added an edge detection module to enhance the performance of the proposed model. The experimental result shows that the proposed model could achieve promising results across different standard datasets (Cheng et al.).

In the field of ever-evolving deep learning techniques, U-Net has gained wide acceptance in the domain of medical image segmentation.

## 5.3.3 GoogleNet or Inception

GoogleNet, also known as Inception-v1, marks a significant development in the evolution of CNN architectures, particularly in the realm of image classification as shown in figure 5.5. Introduced by researchers at Google in 2014, GoogleNet revolutionized deep learning by offering an efficient and scalable solution for processing large-scale image datasets.

At the core of GoogleNet lies the groundbreaking concept of the Inception module, which fundamentally altered the way CNNs process information. Unlike traditional convolutional layers that utilize fixed-size filters, Inception modules incorporate multiple parallel convolutional branches with varying filter sizes. This configuration empowers the network to capture features at different spatial scales concurrently, heightening its capacity to identify intricate patterns and objects within images.

A key design principle underpinning GoogleNet is its commitment to computational efficiency without compromising performance. To achieve this, GoogleNet incorporates dimensionality reduction techniques, such as 1x1 convolutions, prior to applying larger convolutions. By diminishing the number of input channels, these convolutions alleviate the computational burden while preserving informative content.

Furthermore, GoogleNet adopts a novel architectural structure that fosters both depth and width in the network. Instead of merely stacking layers upon layers, the architecture features multiple levels of abstraction, with deeper layers capturing more abstract and elevated features. This hierarchical representation empowers the network to discern increasingly intricate patterns, resulting in enhanced classification accuracy.

Another noteworthy aspect of GoogleNet is its utilization of global average pooling instead of conventional fully connected layers at the conclusion of the network. Global average pooling computes the average value of each feature map, significantly reducing the number of parameters in the network and mitigating the risk of overfitting. This refined solution not only simplifies the model but also fortifies its generalization capability.

GoogleNet has emerged as a trailblazer in the domain of deep learning, providing an exemplary instance of innovative architectural design in CNNs. Its exceptional performance on standard benchmark datasets has positioned it as a leading model in the field, and its broad range of applications across various domains makes it invaluable in healthcare, autonomous vehicles, security, and e-commerce. GoogleNet has made significant strides in optimizing computational resources, organizing feature representation hierarchically, and implementing auxiliary supervision methods. These innovations have positioned it as a fundamental building block in the creation of advanced neural network architectures not only for image classification but also for other applications.

## 5.4 Deep Learning Algorithms Employed in the Segmentation of Brain Tumor Images

Brain tumor segmentation refers to a process in medical image processing where algorithms are applied to delineate and identify regions of interest (tumor tissues) within brain images, typically acquired through techniques such as MRI or CT. Segmentation involves partitioning the brain image into multiple regions or classes, such as tumor tissue, healthy tissue, edema, and necrosis. This is important for various medical applications, including diagnosis, treatment planning, and monitoring of brain tumors. The rising popularity of machine learning and deep learning methods, notably CNNs, for brain tumor segmentation stems from their capacity to grasp intricate patterns from extensive datasets and their adeptness in processing high-dimensional medical images. These algorithms are trained on annotated datasets containing manually outlined tumor regions by experts. Subsequently, they can autonomously segment tumors in previously unseen images.

Deepak and Ameer (2019) presented a deep transfer learning approach for classifying glioma, meningioma, and pituitary tumors from brain MRI images, achieving a mean classification accuracy of 98% and outperforming existing methods. Transfer learning proves effective even with limited medical image data, with insights into misclassifications provided for further analysis. Cheng et al. (2015) proposed a method for improving brain tumor classification in T1-weighted contrast-enhanced MRI images by augmenting the tumor region and splitting it into ring-form subregions. This approach significantly enhanced classification accuracy compared to traditional methods, demonstrating its feasibility and effectiveness for computer-aided diagnosis. Cheng et al. (2016) introduced a novel content-based image retrieval (CBIR) for classifying brain tumors in T1-weighted contrast-enhanced MRI images, achieving a mean average precision of 94.68% by augmenting tumor regions, enabling adaptive spatial division for feature extraction, and applying Fisher kernel framework for similarity measurement.
