## 1.2.7 Colonoscopy Datasets

The human GI tract consists of various segments, including the large bowel, which can be affected by different anomalies and diseases, including colorectal cancer. Both women and men are at increased risk for developing colorectal cancer, making it the third most frequent form of cancer overall. Polyps, which are precursors to colorectal cancer, are found in almost half of individuals aged 50 or above during screening colonoscopies, and their prevalence increases with age. Colonoscopy is considered the standard method for detecting and assessing these polyps, followed by biopsy and removal. Early detection of diseases significantly impacts the survival rate for colorectal cancer, making polyp detection crucial. The chance of developing colorectal cancer can be reduced by improving polyp detection. Therefore, early automated diagnosis of polyps can help prevent colorectal cancer and increase survival rates. There are multiple datasets available for colonoscopy-related research, such as the Computer Vision Center (CVC) CVC-ClinicDB, CVC-ColonDB, Kvasir-SEG, and ETIS-Larib datasets. We will briefly discuss some of them here:

### 1.2.7.1 Kvasir-SEG

The Kvasir-SEG dataset is tailored specifically for the segmentation of polyps in GI endoscopy images. It offers freely accessible GI polyp images accompanied by their respective segmentation masks. These annotations have been meticulously crafted and validated by an expert gastroenterologist. The dataset, with a size of 46.2 MB, comprises 1000 polyp images alongside their corresponding ground truth sourced from the Kvasir Dataset v2. The images within Kvasir-SEG exhibit varying resolutions, spanning from 332 × 487 to 1920 × 1072 pixels. Organized into two distinct folders, the dataset features image files and their associated masks sharing identical filenames. The image files utilize JPEG compression for efficient storage and seamless online browsing capabilities. Researchers and educators can easily access and download this open-access dataset for research and educational purposes. The Kvasir-SEG dataset serves as a valuable resource for researchers and developers aiming to advance the field of polyp segmentation, detection, localization, and classification. With the need for multiple datasets to compare computer vision algorithms, this dataset can be used effectively for both training and validation purposes. It enables the development of cutting-edge solutions specifically tailored to colonoscopy images captured by various manufacturers. Further exploration in this area holds the potential to reduce the polyp miss rate and enhance the quality of examinations. Moreover, the versatility of the Kvasir-SEG dataset extends beyond its primary focus, making it suitable for general segmentation and bounding box detection research. Its availability alongside numerous datasets from diverse fields, including medical and nonmedical domains, adds to its value and applicability.

### 1.2.7.2 CVC-ClinicDB and CVC-ColonDB

CVC-ClinicDB and CVC-ColonDB are datasets specifically designed for polyp segmentation in endoscopic images. The CVC-ClinicDB collection includes 29 distinct sequences totaling 612 still images at a resolution of 384 × 288. Every image in the dataset is accompanied by a meticulously annotated ground truth that accurately delineates the boundaries of the polyp. CVC-ClinicDB contains endoscopic images captured during routine colonoscopy examinations. On the other hand, the CVC-ColonDB dataset contains 380 images. The images in CVC-ColonDB have a resolution of 576 × 720 pixels. The images of both these datasets exhibit a variety of polyp types, sizes, shapes, and textures. The challenges in the CVC-ClinicDB as well as in the CVC-ColonDB dataset include polyp detection, accurate delineation of polyp boundaries, and differentiating polyps from other structures or artifacts.

## 1.3 Augmentation Techniques Used in Medical Image Segmentation

Developing deep learning methods using medical images has long been an appealing avenue of study, aiming to aid clinicians in swift examinations and precise diagnoses. However, these methods require extensive datasets encompassing diverse variations during training. Unfortunately, medical image availability is often limited due to various factors like insufficient patient numbers for certain conditions, reluctance from patients to share their images, and shortages of medical equipment. Consequently, this scarcity leads to dataset bias, overfitting, and inaccurate outcomes. To mitigate these challenges, data augmentation emerges as a common remedy, with various techniques applied across different image types in existing literature. Nevertheless, determining the most effective augmentation technique for specific image types remains ambiguous due to variations in diseases, network architectures, and dataset sizes used in different studies. In this section, we will discuss some of the augmentation techniques used in medical image segmentation.

### Rotation

Rotation-based image augmentations entail rotating an image while preserving its initial orientation. This rotation takes place within a fresh coordinate system, ensuring the retention of the pixels’ relative positions. The rotation may proceed either clockwise or counterclockwise around an axis within a range spanning from 1° to 359°. Nevertheless, with increasing rotation angles, there is a potential risk of losing image labels. Consequently, the reliability of this augmentation technique hinges on the extent of rotation. While rotation transformations might not be ideal for recognizing digits 6 and 9 in digit recognition tasks, they are typically deemed safe for medical images.

### Flipping

Flipping is a method that produces a mirrored version of an image by reversing the positions of its pixels relative to one of the two axes (in the case of 2D images). Although it can be performed along either the vertical or the horizontal axis, vertical flipping is seldom preferred due to potential inconsistencies in the interchange of the top and bottom regions of an image (Nalepa et al. 2019).

### Translation

Translation entails moving images along an axis by implementing a translation vector. This approach preserves the relative pixel positions, thereby reducing positional bias (Shorten and Khoshgoftaar 2019) and preventing models from excessively focusing on features in a specific spatial location (Nalepa et al. 2019).

### Scaling

Images are subjected to scaling along different axes through the application of scaling factors, which might differ or remain uniform across each axis. Specifically, scaling modifications can be described as either reducing in size (when the scaling factor < 1) or enlarging (when the scaling factor > 1).

### Shearing

This translation moves one edge of an image along either the vertical or the horizontal axis, leading to a parallelogram shape. Vertical shear displaces an edge along the vertical axis, while horizontal shear shifts an edge along the horizontal axis. The extent of shear is dictated by a shear angle.

### Augmentation Using Intensities

This augmentation includes modifying contrast or brightness, blurring, standardizing intensity, histogram equalization, sharpening, and introducing noise.
