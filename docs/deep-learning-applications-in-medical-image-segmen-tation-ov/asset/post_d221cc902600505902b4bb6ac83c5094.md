## 7.3.5 Pixel Classifier-Based Segmentation

From the early 1990s onward, various “Machine learning techniques such as k-nearest neighbors (kNNs) and Support vector machines (SVMs)” have been extensively employed for LFS. This pixel classifier (PC)-based approaches use grayscale values to assign pixels to anatomical structures, ensuring stable performance even in situations involving absent or significantly deformed anatomical structures, assuming the classifier is adequately trained. Training these classifiers is, however, challenging due to difficulties in obtaining diverse and high-quality annotated medical training data. PC-based methods are categorized into two groups: one based on shallow learning and the other based on deep learning, depending on the process of extracting features, which can be either manual or automatic (Figure 7.4).

## 7.3.6 Shallow Learning

In shallow learning, machine learning methods are employed to extract important information. The challenge lies in identifying a fitting class for the extracted features. With growing trends in research, (McNitt-Gray et al. 1993) aimed to build a segmentation technique using the “pattern classification approach.” Numerous classifiers include “linear discriminant, k-nearest neighbor, and feed-forward back-propagation neural network (FFBPNN)” to categorize pixels based on local texture and gray-level features. Each classifier was trained on a dataset comprising 17 CXR images and trained classifiers were evaluated on a distinct set of 16 images. Although the neural network outperformed KNN and LDA, accurately predicting over 76% of pixels, limitations existed. Vittitoe et al. addressed these limitations by introducing Markov random field modeling (MRFM) to identify lung regions and non-lung regions, achieving 94.8%-pixel classification accuracy on a test set of 115 CXRs, (Vittitoe et al. 1998) outperforming some previous methods. However, the computational expense of MRFM was acknowledged as a drawback. Finally, Shi et al. and Jangam et al. in their respective works individually modified the fuzzy c-means clustering algorithm for the segmentation of lungs. Shi et al. implemented an unsupervised approach using a “Gaussian kernel-based fuzzy C-means,” (Shi et al. 2009) achieving 97.8% accuracy on 52 CXRs from the JSRT dataset. On the other hand, Jangam et al. proposed an “optimized fuzzy c-means clustering” method, (Jangam and Rao 2019) incorporating a level set algorithm and spatial fuzzy clustering for segmentation. However, Jangam et al.’s method had a longer computation time of 25–30 seconds. Both studies differ in computational efficiency.

## 7.4 Deep Learning Approaches

### 7.4.1 Lung Field Segmentation in Chest X-rays

Accurate lung segmentation is crucial in medical imaging because losing even a small part of the lung during the process can have serious consequences. Shallow learning methods for segmentation rely on manually defined features, which can be tedious and error-prone. In contrast, deep learning automatically extracts features from the data, rendering it a robust and efficient approach for lung segmentation.

Deep learning approaches for lung segmentation use advanced neural network architectures to automatically discern lung structures in CXR images. These models comprise several layers of nonlinear processing, which include the input layer, intermediate layers, and output layer. Deep learning offers various computationally efficient approaches for the computer vision task of lung segmentation, including convolutional neural networks such as “Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs), dilated convolutions like DeepLab v3 and encoder–decoder-based models like UNet” and its variants. These architectures significantly contribute to improving segmentation accuracy. Additionally, innovative methods, such as “GANs,” “attention-based mechanisms,” and “multistage or hybrid frameworks,” further enhance the segmentation process. The networks learn to distinguish between lung and non-lung regions, addressing challenges such as variations in anatomy and image quality. Training is typically performed on large datasets, allowing the model to generalize to diverse cases.

### 7.4.1.1 CNN-Based Approaches

In the past few years, with the rise of deep learning techniques, especially “CNNs,” the field of artificial intelligence has undergone a remarkable evolution. This study emphasizes the effectiveness of CNNs in automating segmentation processes, thereby reducing the manual effort and time required. Certainly, CNNs can be employed for various applications, including but not limited to image recognition, computer vision, natural language processing, and speech recognition. In medical image analysis, CNNs prove effective for different organ segmentation, automatically extracting features and identifying intricate patterns challenging for human detection. Crucially, this capability enhances accurate and efficient lung segmentation, vital for diagnosing, treating, and monitoring deadly lung diseases. To address challenges such as model overfitting, reducing parameters, and handling imbalanced data, Novikov et al. (2018) developed a robust “fully convolutional network (FCN)” architecture. The three variants – All-Dropout, incorporating Gaussian dropout layers to prevent overfitting; All-Convolutional, which replaced pooling layers with convolutional layers for denser feature extraction; and “InvertedNet” (Novikov et al. 2018) utilizing inverted residual blocks and Exponential Linear Units (ELUs) for accelerated training – were thoroughly evaluated. The study used the JSRT dataset, which comprises 247 chest X-rays. The training time for InvertedNet was 33 hours, involving 3,140,771 parameters. The study’s findings revealed that InvertedNet performed better than others, showing good results in terms of the Jaccard score, as proven by the study’s results.

Furthermore, Adel Sulaiman and colleagues introduced a lung segmentation model using deep CNNs. The model incorporates convolutional and concatenate blocks (Sulaiman et al. 2023) with convolutional layers capturing essential features and a transpose layer enhancing spatial resolution of feature maps. Performance metrics such as accuracy, Intersection over Union (IoU), and Dice coefficient were used to evaluate the model’s effectiveness, yielding impressive results. Trained with k-fold validation (k = 5), the model achieved 97% accuracy with minimal loss.

The neural network architecture known as encoder–decoder, widely used for image segmentation, consists of two essential parts: the encoder and the decoder. The encoder, often referred to as the contracting path, utilizes convolutional layers to downsample the input image, extracting crucial features and reducing spatial dimensions. Conversely, the decoder, known as the expansion path, employs upsampling layers to enhance spatial resolution and generate a segmentation mask. U-Net, a specialized encoder–decoder network initially created for biomedical image segmentation by “Olaf Ronneberger” (Ronneberger et al. 2015) incorporates skip connections and additional convolutional layers to refine segmentation accuracy.
