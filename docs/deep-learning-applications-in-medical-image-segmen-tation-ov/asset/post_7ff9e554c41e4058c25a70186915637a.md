### 4.1 Introduction

The pediatric brain represents the growing and maturational stage of overall brain development. The phase from birth to 2 years is very important in a child’s brain growth and is characterized by an increase in brain size, volume, nerve maturation, and impulse transmission. Particularly, the stage from birth to the first 28 days is called neonatal period, while the duration from 29 days to 1 year is early infancy, and the second year of life is called late infancy. These three phases are crucial in brain development and are marked by extensive maturation of brain tissues.

The human brain comprises three major tissues: grey matter (GM), white matter (WM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (Devi et al. 2016b). Each component has a distinct functional functionality. The GM is composed of several elevations and grooves that play a major role in human cognition, sensory perception, and learning. WM is located in the interior of the brain and aids nerve impulse transmission. This is enabled by the myelin sheaths in the WM that speed up the conduction of nerve signals. The CSF surrounds the brain in the cranial cavity and protects the brain from injuries and shocks. Although each brain tissue has a unique physiological role in brain development, the maturation of WM (especially, its myelination) is considered clinically significant.

Myelination denotes the formation of a protective myelin sheath around the neuronal axon. This sheath has many junctions called the nodes of Ranvier that enable faster impulse transmission. In fact, this myelination is a unique aspect of higher animals or vertebrates that enables them to recognize and respond faster to stimuli. The characteristics of normal human myelination are as follows:

- Myelination begins in the prenatal stage of the baby (i.e. before birth). The WM of the brain is not completely myelinated at birth. It progresses during the neonatal and infancy stages and continues into the early pediatric phase (Cowan 2002). Most of the myelination is completed during the initial 2 years of the child, making its study in the pediatric brain a clinically relevant area.
- Besides, myelination progresses in a predefined and orderly fashion. To understand this, three simple neuroanatomic axioms were laid down: myelin formation proceeds from the caudal to rostral, posterior to anterior, and central to peripheral areas (Welker and Patton 2012). Nevertheless, there are a few exceptions to this general rule.
- In addition, the development of myelin is not uniform across all human beings. A certain degree of variation between babies in the myelination process is normal and expected (Knaap and Valk 2005).

Furthermore, the reduced incidence of malformation of myelin results in various disorders such as hypomyelination (indicates a lack or deficiency of myelin formation), dysmyelination (represents irregular or abnormal myelination), and retarded myelination (i.e. delayed myelin deposition) (Knaap and Valk 2005). Thus, it is imperative to closely study, monitor, and analyze myelin progress in babies.

The segmentation and analysis of myelinated WM (MWM) was initially based on manual and histological tissue analyses. However, this process is cumbersome and time-intensive. The usage of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enables the visualization and quantification of brain tissues in greater detail (Devi et al. 2016a; Devi et al. 2016c). Several qualitative works based on MRI are currently available for the study of MWM in the human brain. These involve a mere description of the appearance and progression of age-specific myelination (Knaap and Valk 2005; Welker and Patton 2012). The quantitative studies on myelination entail various advanced techniques such as relaxation time mapping and myelin water fraction (MWF) imaging. For instance, Deoni et al. utilized a novel multi-component driven equilibrium single pulse observation (mcDESPOT) to map myelin deposition (Deoni et al. 2011). Subsequently, they used relaxation time mapping and MWF imaging to evaluate cortical myelination in young children (Deoni et al. 2012; Deoni et al. 2015). Likewise, Chonka et al. investigated the interdependency between cortical maturation and WM myelin formation based on MWF imaging (Chonka et al. 2016). Dean et al. analyzed WM and myelin growth in male babies using MWF maps (Dean et al. 2014).
