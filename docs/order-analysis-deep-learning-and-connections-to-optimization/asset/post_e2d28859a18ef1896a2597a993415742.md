
## Example 1.2

(a) If one defines the componentwise partial order $\leq$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by

$$ \leq := \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n \mid x_i \leq y_i \text{ for all } i \in \{1, \dots, n\}\}
$$

then the linear space $\mathbb{R}^n$ becomes a partially ordered linear space.

(b) For $-\infty < a < b < \infty$, let $C[a, b]$ denote the linear space of continuous real-valued functions on $[a, b]$. With the natural partial order $\leq$ on $C[a, b]$ given by

$$ \leq := \{(x, y) \in C[a, b] \times C[a, b] \mid x(t) \leq y(t) \text{ for all } t \in [a, b]\}
$$

the space $C[a, b]$ becomes a partially ordered linear space.

## Definition 1.9 Let $C$ be a nonempty subset of a real linear space $Y$.

(a) The set $C$ is called a *cone* iff

$$ y \in C, \lambda \geq 0 \implies \lambda y \in C $$

(see Fig. 1.7).

(b) A cone $C$ is called *pointed* iff

$$ y \in C, -y \in C \implies y = 0 $$

(see Fig. 1.8).

(c) A cone $C$ is called *reproducing* iff $C - C = Y$. In this case, one also says that $C$ generates $Y$.

## 1.2 Preordered Linear Spaces and Convex Cones

Fig. 1.8 Part of the surface of a pointed cone (see [72], Fig. 4.2])![](img/e4a2e491dbba0df71ab29f149cd5b_24_0.png) and $C := \{x \in C[0, 1] \mid x(t) \geq 0 \text{ for all } t \in [0, 1]\}$ are pointed cones.

Convex cones can be simply characterized.

**Proposition 1.1 (See [72], Theorem 4.3]) A cone $C$ in a real linear space is convex if and only if for all $x, y \in C$, $x + y \in C$ (i.e., $C + C = C$).

A central connection between preorders and convex cones is established in the following:

**Proposition 1.2 (See [72], Theorem D.3]) Let $Y$ be a real linear space.

(a) If $\leq$ is a preorder on $Y$, then the set $C := \{y \in Y \mid 0 \leq y\}$ is a convex cone. If, in addition, $\leq$ is a partial order, then $C$ is pointed.

(b) If $C$ is a convex cone in $Y$, then its binary relation $\leq := \{(x, y) \in Y \times Y \mid y - x \in C\}$ is a preorder on $Y$. If, in addition, $C$ is pointed, then $\leq$ is a partial order.

**Remark 1.1** Let $C$ be a convex cone in a real linear space $Y$. By Proposition 1.2 (a), we obtain for a preorder $\leq$ on $Y$ and for all $y^1, y^2 \in Y, y^1 \leq y^2 \iff y^2 - y^1 \in C$. If, in addition, $C$ is pointed, this implication also holds for a partial order by Proposition 1.2 (b).

**Definition 1.10** A convex cone characterizing the preorder on a real linear space is called a *preorder cone* (or a *positive cone*). A pointed convex cone characterizing the partial order on a real linear space is called a *partial order cone*.

## 1.3 Normed Spaces

Fig. 1.11 Cone generated by a set $S$ (see [72], Fig. 4.3])![](img/e4a2e491dbba0df71ab29f149cd5b_26_0.png)

**Definition 1.11** Let $S$ be a nonempty subset of a real linear space. The set $conc(S) := \{\lambda s \mid \lambda \geq 0 \text{ and } s \in S\}$ is called the cone *generated by S* (Compare. Fig. 1.11).

If a real linear space is preordered, then a preorder can also be introduced on its dual space.

**Definition 1.12** Let $Y$ be a real linear space with a preorder cone $C$. The cone $C' := \{\ell \in Y' \mid \ell(y) \geq 0 \text{ for all } y \in C\}$ is called the dual cone for $C$ (here $Y'$ denotes the algebraic dual of $Y$).

Although normed spaces are the most important space in the second part of this book, we first start with topological spaces in general. **Definition 1.13** Let $Y$ be a nonempty set.

(a) A topology $\mathcal{T}$ on $Y$ is defined to be a set of subsets of $Y$ that satisfy the following axioms:
- (i) Union of sets in $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$.
- (ii) Finite intersection of sets in $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$.
- (iii) $\varnothing \in \mathcal{T}$.
In this case, $(Y, \mathcal{T})$ is called a topological space and the elements of $\mathcal{T}$ are open sets.

(b) Let $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ be two topologies on $Y$. $\mathcal{S}$ is called *finer than* $\mathcal{T}$ (or $\mathcal{T}$ called *coarser than* $\mathcal{S}$) iff every $\mathcal{S}$-open set is $\mathcal{T}$-open.

(c) Let $(Y, T)$ be a topological space, let $S$ be a subset of $Y$, and let $y \in Y$ be a given element. $S$ is called a neighborhood of $y$ iff there is an open set $T$ with $y \in S$. The set of all interior elements of $S$ is called the interior of $S$, and is denoted by $int(S)$. The set $S$ is called closed iff $Y \setminus S$ is open. The set of all closure elements of $Y$ for which every neighborhood meets $S$ is called the closure of $S$, and is denoted by $cl(S)$.

A nonempty preorder $I$, the order relation is reflexive and transitive is called directed iff two arbitrary elements in $I$ are majorized in $I$. A map from a directed set $I$ to the nonempty set $Y$ is called a net and it is denoted by $(y_i)_{i \in I}$. A net $(y_i)_{i \in I}$ in a topological space $(Y, T)$ is called to converge to some $y \in Y$ iff for every neighborhood $U$ of $y$ there is $n \in I$ so that $y_i \in U$ for all $i \geq n$. An element $y \in Y$ in a topological space $(Y, T)$ is called a cluster point of $y$ iff every neighborhood $U$ of $y$ and every $n \in I$ there is an $i \in I$ with $i \geq n$ so that $y_i \in U$.

(e) A nonempty subset $S$ of a topological space $(Y, T)$ is called compact iff every net in $S$ has a cluster point in $S$.

(f) Let $(X, S)$ and $(Y, T)$ be two topological spaces. A map $f : X \to Y$ is called continuous at $x \in X$ iff for every neighborhood $V$ of $f(x)$ there is a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ with $f(U) \subseteq V$. $f : X \to Y$ is called continuous over $X$ iff $f$ is continuous at every $x \in X$.

Next, we consider a topological space $(X, T)$ where $X$ is now a real linear space. In this case, we require that the topological and linear structure of the space are compatible.

**Definition 1.14** $Y$ be a real linear space and $T$ be a topology on $Y$.

(a) $(Y, T)$ is called a real linear space iff addition and multiplication with reals are continuous, i.e., the maps
$(x, y) \mapsto x + y$ with $x, y \in Y$,
$(\alpha, y) \mapsto \alpha y$ with $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ and $y \in Y$
are continuous on $Y \times Y$ and $\mathbb{R} \times Y$, respectively. In many situations, we use, for simplicity, the notation $Y$ instead of $(Y, T)$ for a real topological linear space.

(b) A subset $S$ of a topological linear space $Y$ is called bounded iff for $0$-neighborhood $U$ there is a $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ with the property $S \subseteq \lambda U$.

(c) A nonempty subset $S$ of a real topological space $Y$ is called complete if every Cauchy net in $S$ converges to $y \in S$ (i.e., for every net $(y_i)_{i \in I}$ in $S$ with $\lim_{i,j\in I} (y_i - y_j) = 0$ there is an $y \in S$ with $\lim_{i\to\infty} y_i$).

**Proposition 1.3** Let $S$ be a nonempty convex set of a real topological linear space $Y$. The closure $cl(S)$ is convex. For $int(S) \neq \varnothing$ we have
(a) $int(S) = core(S)$.
(b) $cl(S) = S$.

If the $S$ is not convex, then $int(S)$ and $core(S)$ may not be equal. For instance, for the nonconvex $S$ in Example 1.1, we obtain $int(S) \neq core(S)$ (compare Fig. 1.5).
