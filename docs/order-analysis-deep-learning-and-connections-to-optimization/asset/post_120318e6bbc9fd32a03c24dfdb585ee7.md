**Definition 1.16** Let $Y$ be a real space, and $X$ a nonempty subset of the algebraic dual space $Y'$.

- (a) For every $x' \in X$, there is a seminorm $p : Y \to \mathbb{R}$ given by
$p(y) = |x'(y)| \text{ for all } y \in Y.$

The coarsest topology on $Y$ making all these linear functionals continuous is the weak topology on $Y$ generated by $X$, and is denoted $\sigma(Y, X)$ (it is the weakest topology on $Y$ in which all linear functionals belong to $X$ are continuous).

- (b) If $Y$ is equipped with a topology, the subspace $Y^*$ of all linear functionals which belong to $Y^*$ is called the topological dual space of $Y$. For $X = Y^*$, $\sigma(Y, Y^*)$ is simply called the weak topology on $Y$.

**Definition 1.17** A real normal space $(Y, |\cdot|)$ is called reflexive if the canonical embedding $J_Y : Y \to Y^{**}$ (bidual space) defined is a real Banach space. For applications, the following assertion is important (see Holmes [64], p. 126–127):

$J_Y(y)(\varphi) := \varphi(y) \text{ for all } y \in Y \text{ and all } \varphi \in Y^*.$

Every reflexive real normal space is complete, and, therefore, it is a Banach space. (see Holmes [64], p. 126–127):

**Proposition 1.5** A real Banach space $(Y, |\cdot|)$ is reflexive if and only if the closed unit ball $\mathcal{B}(0, 1)$ is weakly compact.

In this proposition, the term “weakly compact” means compact with respect to the weak topology. A nonempty subset of a real normal space is weakly compact if and only if it is sequentially convergent. To clarify this, we recall convergence.

**Definition 1.18** Let $(Y, |\cdot|)$ be a real normal space. A sequence $(y_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ of elements of $Y$ is called weakly convergent to $y \in Y$ iff for all continuous linear functionals $\ell$ on $Y$

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \ell(y_n) = \ell(y).$$

In this case, $y$ is called a weak limit of the sequence $(y_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$.

## 1.3 Normed Spaces

**Definition 1.19** Let $(Y, \|\cdot\|)$ be a real normed space. A nonempty subset $S$ of $Y$ is called weakly sequentially compact iff every sequence in $S$ contains a weakly convergent subsequence whose weak limit belongs to $S$.

In a reflexive Banach space, a simple sufficient condition for the weak compactness of a nonempty subset can be given (for instance, compare Werner [140], Corollary 6.1.9).

**Proposition 1.6** Every nonempty convex bounded subset of a reflexive Banach space is weakly sequentially compact.

Next, we extend the notion of a convex functional.

**Definition 1.20** Let $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ be a real normed space, let $S$ be a nonempty convex subset of $X$, and let $f : S \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a given functional. If for every $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ the sublevel set $S_{\alpha} := \{x \in S \mid f(x) \leq \alpha\}$ is convex, then the functional $f$ is called quasiconvex.

**Definition 1.21** Let $S$ be a nonempty subset of a real linear space $X$, and $f : S \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a given functional. An element $\bar{x} \in S$ is called a *minimal point* of $f$ on $S$ iff

$f(\bar{x}) \leq f(x) \text{ for all } x \in S.$

In this case, $\bar{x}$ is called a *minimal solution* of the optimization problem $\min f(x)$, and the functional $f(\bar{x})$ is called a *minimal value* of the optimization problem. For $S \neq X$, the set $S$ is called a *constraint set*. Moreover, $\bar{x} \in S$ is called a *maximal solution* of the optimization problem $\max f(x)$ iff

$f(x) \leq f(\bar{x}) \text{ for all } x \in S,$

and in this case, $f(\bar{x})$ is said to be a *maximal value* of this optimization problem.

Now we give a sufficient condition for the existence of at least one minimal point (compare [72], Theorem 2.1).

**Theorem 1.2** Let $S$ be a nonempty convex bounded subset of a reflexive Banach space, and let $f : S \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a continuous quasiconvex functional. Then $f$ has at least one minimal point on $S$.

**Proposition 1.7** Let $(Y, \|\cdot\|)$ be a real normed space. With the norm $\|\cdot\|^* : Y^* \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ given by

$\|\ell\|^* = \sup_{y \in Y} \frac{|\ell(y)|}{\|y\|} \text{ for all } \ \ell \in Y^*,$

$(Y^*, \|\cdot\|^*)$ is a real Banach space.

## 14 Preliminaries

A significant class of normed spaces are Hilbert spaces.

**Definition 1.22** Let $Y$ be a real linear space.

- (a) A map $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : Y \times Y \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is called an inner product iff the conditions are satisfied (for all $x, y, z \in Y$ and all $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$):
  (i) $\langle y, y \rangle > 0$ for $y \neq 0$.
  (ii) $\langle x, y \rangle = \langle y, x \rangle$.
  (iii) $\langle \lambda x, y \rangle = \lambda\langle x, y \rangle$.
  (iv) $\langle x+y, z \rangle = \langle x, z \rangle + \langle y, z \rangle$.

- (b) If the real linear space $Y$ equipped with an inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ is complete, the pair $(Y, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ is a Hilbert space (a real normed space with the norm $\|y\| := \sqrt{\langle y, y \rangle}$ for all $y \in Y$).

**Example 1.5** For a given $n \in \mathbb{N}$, consider the real linear space $S^n$ of real symmetric $(n)$ matrices with the inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ given by

$\langle A, B \rangle := \text{trace}(AB) \text{ for all } A, B \in S^n.$

Then $(S^n, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. A partial order on $S^n$ is induced by the convex cone

$S_+ := \{A \in S^n \mid A \text{ is positive semidefinite}\}.$

This partial order is called the Löwner cone. However, there is also a convex cone of interest in applications. The convex cone

$C^n := \{A \in S^n \mid x^T Ax \geq 0 \text{ for all } x \in \mathbb{R}^n_}.$

is called the positive cone.

Next, we recall the notions of a directional derivative and a subdifferential.

**Definition 1.23** Let $S$ be a nonempty subset of a real linear space $X$, and $f : S \to \mathbb{R}$ be a convex function. For two elements $\tilde{x} \in S$ and $h \in X$ the mathematical

$f'(\tilde{x})(h) := \lim_{\lambda \to 0_+} f(\tilde{x} + \lambda h) - f(\tilde{x}).$

exists, $f'(\tilde{x})(h)$ is called the directional derivative of $f$ at $\tilde{x}$ in the direction $h$. If this limit exists for all $h \in X$, then $f$ is called directionally differentiable at $\tilde{x}$ (see Fig. 1.12).

Notice in Definition 1.23 that for arbitrary null sequences $(\lambda_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ with $\lambda_n > 0$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, one has the additional property $\tilde{x} + \lambda_nh \in S$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. For instance, this property is fulfilled if $S$ equals the whole space $X$.
