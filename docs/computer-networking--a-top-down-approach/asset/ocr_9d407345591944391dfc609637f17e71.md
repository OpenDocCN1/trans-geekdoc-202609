# *Processing Delay*

The time required to examine the packet's header and determine where to direct the packet is part of the **processing delay**. The processing delay can also include other factors, such as the time needed to check for bit-level errors in the packet that occurred in transmitting the packet's bits from the upstream node to router A. Processing delays in high-speed routers are typically on the order of microseconds or less. After this nodal processing, the router directs the packet to the queue that precedes the link to router B. (In Chapter 4 we'll study the details of how a router operates.)

# *Queueing Delay*

At the queue, the packet experiences a **queueing delay** as it waits to be transmitted onto the link. The length of the queueing delay of a specific packet will depend on the number of earlier-arriving packets that are queued and waiting for transmission onto the link. If the queue is empty and no other packet is currently being transmitted, then our packet's queueing delay will be zero. On the other hand, if the traffic is heavy and many other packets are also waiting to be transmitted, the queueing delay will be long. We will see shortly that the number of packets that an arriving packet might expect to find is a function of the intensity and nature of the traffic arriving at the queue. Queueing delays can be on the order of microseconds to milliseconds in practice.
