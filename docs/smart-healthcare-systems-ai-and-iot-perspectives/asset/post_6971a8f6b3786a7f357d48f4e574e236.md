## 20.2 RELATED WORKS

This section reviews existing literature to evaluate and compare various fall detection systems, algorithms or approaches to prevent older people from falling. For instance, Xu et al. (Xu et al., 2019) recognize human body motion and archive human fall detection depending on the data and skeleton tracking technologies of Microsoft Kinect V2 sensor (Saha and Ghosh, 2009; Ghosh et al., 2020). Firstly, the human joints recorded by the skeleton tracker are analyzed using the Kinect's depth data. The fall is then detected based on the position recognition by an optimized back propagation neural network (BPNN). The data produced by the Kinect tracker is used to train the neural network. Finally, experimental verification was done for posture recognition and fall detection. However, testing is performed using a separate body tracker. The network lacks sufficient samples to learn about events that do not occur frequently in the datasets, such as falling on ones' knees or identifying falls from an individual's perspective, which are examples of analyzed samples. This may also apply to any false negatives, with the network's inability to accurately learn particular features serves as the only possible explanation for the error.

Kweski and Kwolek (2013) have implemented a fuzzy inference on data from Kinect and a wearable motion-sensing device with an accelerometer and gyroscope for fall detection. The foreground items are recognized using depth images captured by Kinect, which can extract images in a dark room. Both the user's privacy and subtle fall detection are maintained—the findings of the trial show that fall detection is effective, the system required the user to enter a calibration pose during the skeleton tracking initialization, which makes it less usable especially for older people restricting the subject's position. Another technique for detecting falls in older people' homes uses Microsoft Kinect and a two-stage fall detection system (Stone and Skubic, 2014) have been built. A variety of skilled actors' falls and a few naturally occurring falls allowed for the characterization of performance in four diverse circumstances. Results achieved satisfactory, however, larger distances from the Kinect presented several challenges for fall detection, including the depth image's decreased resolution, creating more challenges to the foreground segmentation, the depth estimate's decreased precision, and a higher probability of ambient light overpowering the actively emitted infrared pattern of the Kinect, which results in depth estimates not being returned, whereas our proposed approach deals only with RGB images that are self-sufficient to identify the patterns required for detecting falls without adding the complexity of processing other image attributes like luminosity, normal, depth, etc.

The technology proposed by (Mastorakis and Makris, 2012) can accurately and consistently detecting falls without accounting for false actions (such as lying on the floor) in real-time. Calculations on both speed and inactivity are used to determine whether a fall fallen. Their main contribution lies in evaluating nobody based on changes in the 3D bounding box. This eliminates the need for prior scene by directly leveraging the 3D bounding box because the set of identified activities is sufficient to fully implement the fall detection procedure. However, with this, detection is only local, and a carer is not alerted. It lacks extensive monitoring capabilities, prolonged data transmission, and the ability to gather data for large-scale fall pattern mining because it is not a part of bigger telemonitoring centers.

To identify falls, Nghem et al. (Nghem et al., 2012) suggest a head recognition method using depth from Kinect camera, which identifies fall locations and based on these locations, identifies individual falls by an algorithm designed to calculate head speed, the body content, and the distance between them and the floor. Scanning the head on the exterior contour of the human searches for falls positions quickly; thus, head detection is a variation of the histogram of oriented gradients for the head. However, scalable computing resources are needed for modern telemedicine, which aims to meet the increasing demand for monitoring elderly patients. Nghem et al. just justified the data and not consider alerting the caregiver for the recovery of the sufferer.

Salah et al. propose a fall identification system for older people that use an ML architecture with accelerometers (Salah et al., 2022). The results obtained yielded 96.3% accuracy and 96.4% specificity. In addition, execution times of less than 40 ms are recorded. However, the range of different sensors used is restricted in this case due to hardware constraint. For the elderly residents of nursing homes, De Rae et al. propose a Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE)-centered fall recognition and alerting system (De Rae et al., 2022). The authors point out that current systems depend on wearable devices, pressure sensors, gyroscopes, and accelerometers. These devices, however, may not be appropriate for those with cognitive impairments may be difficult and uncomfortable for adults to work. The subject-wise accuracy, or percentage of wise decisions, ranges from 84.89% to 92.65%. Tong et al. (Tong et al., 2022) discuss the advantages, disadvantages, and potential paths for future research on cell phone-based systems that use accelerometers. Somkunwar et al. propose an ML method (Somkunwar et al., 2023) comprising a camera, a computer vision for feature extraction. In contrast, the computer vision algorithm is used to detect falls based on changes in the form. The results show that the accuracy of Random Forest (RF), Naive Bayes, Vector Machine (SVM), k-Nearest Neighbour (kNN), and Ada Boost, respectively, are 82%, 39.75%, 66.25%, 68.0%, and 64.75%. However, determining the head and body center vertical acceleration is challenging, whereas the combination of the same can be built.
