## Abstract

Deeper neural networks are more difficult to train. We present a residual learning framework to ease the training of networks that are substantially deeper than those previously. We explicitly reformulate the layers as learning functions with respect to the layer inputs, instead of learning unreferenced functions. We provide empirical evidence showing that residual networks are easier to optimize, and can gain accuracy. On ImageNet we evaluate networks up to 52 layers deeper than VGG nets [40] but with lower complexity. An ensemble of residual nets achieves 3.57% error on the ImageNet set. This result won the 1st place on ILSVRC-10 with 100 and 1000 layers. We also present our application

## 1. Introduction

Deeper neural networks [21] have been led to a series of breakthroughs for image classification [21, 40, 39]. Deep networks naturally integrate low/mid-level layers [40] and classifiers in an end-to-end manner where "features" of leaves are controlled by the number of stacked layers. Recent evaluation [43] reveal that depth is crucial of importance, and the leading results [40, 43, 12, 16] on the challenging ImageNet dataset [33] all "deep" nets [40], with a depth of sixteen [40] to thirty [16]. Many other visual recognition tasks [7, 11, 3, 22, 27] have greatly many benefits from depth.

Driven by the significance of depth, a question arises: Is learning deeper networks as easy as making shallower? An obstacle to answering this question was the notorious problem of vanishing/exploding gradients [14, 1, 8], which from the beginning, has been largely addressed by normalized initialization [23, 8, 36, 12] and intermediate normalization layers [16], which enables nets with layers to start converging for stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with back-propagation [22].

When deeper networks are able to start converging, a degradation problem has emerged: with the depth increasing, accuracy saturates (which is unsprising). Unexpected, such degradation is caused by overfitting, and adding more layers to satisfy a deeper model leads to higher training error, as reported in [10, 41] and thoroughly verified by our experiments. ## Related Work

In this paper, we introduce a deep residual learning framework. Instead of each few stacked layers directly learn a underlying mapping, we explicitly let these layers learn a residual mapping. Denoting the underlying mapping as $\mathcal{H}(x)$, we let the stacked layers fit mapping of $\mathcal{F}(x) := \mathcal{H}(x) - x$. The original mapping is easier to optimize the residual mapping to utilize the original, unreferenced mapping. To the extreme, if identity mapping were optimal, it would be easier to push the residual further than an identity mapping by a stack of nonlinear layers.

**Residual Representations.** In image recognition, VLAD [18] is a representation that encodes the residual layers with respect to a dictionary, and Fisher Vector [30] can be formulated as a probabilistic [18] of VLAD. Both of them are powerful representations for image retrieval and classification [4, 47]. For vector quantization, encoding residual layers [17] is shown to be more effective than encoding encoders.

The formulation of $\mathcal{F}(x) + x$ can be realized by feedforward networks with shortcuts (connections). Fig. 2. Shortcut connections [2, 33, 48] are skipping one or more layers. In our case, the shortcuts perform identity mapping, and their outputs are added to the next layer. These stacked layers can still be trained end-to-end by SGD with backpropagation, and can be easily implemented using common libraries (e.g., Caffe [19]) without modifying the solvers.

In physics and computer graphics, solving Partial Differential Equations (PDEs), using the Multigrid method [3] reformulates the system as subdomain at multiple scales, where each subdomain is solved by the residual solver between a coarse and finer scale. An alternative to Multigrid is hierarchical decomposition [44, 45], which relies on variables that are separated between two scales. [34, 45] show that these solvers converge much faster than standard solvers that are unaware of the nature of the residuals. The methods suggest that such good reformation of shortcuts can simplify the optimization.

We present experiments on ImageNet [35] to show the degradation problem and evaluate our results. We show that our extremely deep residual nets are easy to optimize, but "plain" nets (that stack layers exhibit higher training error when the depth increases); our deep nets can easily gain accuracy from greatly increasing depth, producing results significantly better than previous methods.

**Short Connections.** Practices and theories that lead to shortcut connections [2, 33, 48] have been studied for a long time. Early practice of training multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) is to add a layer connection connected from the network to the output [33, 44]. In [24], we employ linear layers are connected to classifiers for addition/without exploding gradients. The layers of [38, 37, 36] propose for centering layers by residuals, spouses, and gradients, implemented by shortcut connections. In [43], an "inception" layer is composed of a shortcut connection and a few deeper layers.

Similar phenomena are also observed on the CIFAR-10 dataset [28], suggesting the optimization difficulties and effects of our method are not particular. We present successfully trained models on dataset with over 100 layers, and models with over 1000 layers.

Concur with our work, "highways" [41, 42] present are dependent and have parameters; in contrast our shortcuts are parameter-free. When a gated shortcut is "closed" (approaching zero), the layers in highway networks represent non-residual functions. On the contrary, our formulation always learns residual functions; our shortcut identities are never closed, and all information is passed through, with residual functions to be learned.
