### INTRODUCTION

The central function of any organization is decision-making at the operational, tactical, and strategic levels. Such decisions are supported by forecasts that are fundamentally different. For instance, long-term, aggregate predictions are necessary for strategic decisions, but in short-term, extremely precise forecasts are necessary for highly dynamic operational decisions (Athanasopoulos et al., 2017). The way these projections are produced is impacted by variations in time granularity. Strategic projections for the long term are typically produced by taking into account high-level unstructured data from the business environment. These mostly rely on the judgement and experience of senior management, as reliable long-term statistical forecasts that capture the market dynamics can be quite tough to make (Kolsarici and Vakratsas, 2015). Short-term operational forecasts, on the other hand, are typically produced utilising organised but constrained data sources, including historical power demand load. Most of this is based on statistical techniques. Furthermore, the forecasting literature is well aware that different forecast horizons necessitate different approaches because these forecasts are generated by different methodologies and are based on different information sets, and it is expected that they may not agree, even within the same decision-making level (Mir et al., 2020).

Decisions resulting from this disagreement may not be in line. For instance, daily electricity projections that assist with production and inventory decisions can offer a steady overall picture of the market. On the other hand, market growth may be anticipated by the strategic level predictions, which might have a variety of effects on the investment plan and financial choices (Château, 2022). Decisions must be made together if a corporation such as ESKOM (the South African Power utility supplier) is to be able to satisfy its growth in all possible operational, tactical, and strategic projections. If not, this misalignment may result in waste, missed opportunities, and extra expenses. There are several difficulties in forecasting a hierarchy of time series. First, there may be noticeable differences in the properties displayed by the series at various hierarchical levels, which correspond to varying degrees of aggregation yet, hourly electricity demand, load, and supply data are marked by skewness, high volatility, and intermittency, according to Mircetic et al. (2022). As a result, aggregated series will inherently evolve more smoothly, be less skewed, and exhibit more obvious signs of seasonality and weather dependence. The forecasting method that should be used at each level of the hierarchy is affected by these differences (Athanasopoulos et al., 2017).

Summing the forecasts of the respective series at the lower levels would yield the aggregates’ forecasts. But the results of this bottom-up strategy are usually not very good. This relates to a second major challenge: to provide coherent decision-making at various hierarchical levels, the prediction of each aggregated series must equal the sum of the forecasts of the corresponding disaggregated series. If the forecasts are generated independently for every series in the hierarchy, this aggregation condition is unlikely to be met. Finally, in many applications, such as the hourly electricity demand load, the hierarchy can consist of thousands, or even millions, of time series at the bottom level, which has implications in the choice of forecasting method in terms of computational load.
