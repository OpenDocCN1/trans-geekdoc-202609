# AI Approach Towards Optimal Finding of Renewable Sources of Energy

In recent years, Perovskite Solar Cells (PSCs) have garnered significant attention due to their noteworthy attributes such as low cost, high absorption coefficient, tunable bandgap, long carrier lifetime and diffusion length, high defect tolerance, low exciton energy, and high carrier mobility. The configuration of PSCs comprises an absorber layer, often referred to as the perovskite layer, positioned between the hole transport layer (HTL) and the electron transport layer (ETL). Substantial advancements in PSC power conversion efficiency (PCE) have been achieved, increasing from 3.8% to 25.7% through extensive research efforts capitalizing on the superior properties of these cells. This rapid progress positions PSCs as a practical and appealing alternative to traditional solar technology (Figure 1 illustrates the structure of PSC). While PSCs have demonstrated high efficiency within a relatively short timeframe, they still confront several challenges before becoming commercially viable. The perovskite material possesses the general molecular structure of ABX3, where A represents an organic or inorganic monovalent cation, B is a divalent metal ion, and X is a halogen anion. The crystal structure of perovskite material is outlined in Figure 1, with commonly used elements such as Cesium (Cs+), Rubidium (Rb+), Methylammonium (MA+/CH3NH3+), and Formamidinium (FA+/NH2CHNH2+) at the A site; lead (Pb+), Tin (Sn+) typically used at the B site; and Iodide (I–), Bromide (Br–), Chloride (Cl–) at the X site. Essentially, there exist infinite combinations and possibilities for perovskites in solar cell applications.

A notable characteristic of perovskite materials is their tunable bandgap, allowing them to absorb a broad range of photons. Altering the perovskite’s composition enables the adjustment of its bandgap, facilitating the tuning of the material’s absorption properties. Achieving optimal efficiency in PSCs requires meticulous attention to various aspects of fabrication, including the composition of perovskite materials, interface engineering, and device architecture.

Identifying a perovskite material with an ideal bandgap matching the solar spectrum is crucial for the efficiency of solar cells. Perovskites exhibit the capacity to fine-tune their bandgap within the range of 1.5 eV to 3.2 eV, with the most efficient bandgap for perovskite solar cells being approximately 1.6 eV. This specific bandgap optimally absorbs a diverse range of photons present in the solar spectrum (Cozzi et al., 2020). Consequently, tuning the composition of perovskites is essential to optimize their properties. However, relying solely on trial and error for such an intricate scenario is impractical and illogical.

Structural instability poses a significant challenge to the commercialization of Perovskite Solar Cells (PSCs) as it can lead to cell degradation over time. Factors such as temperature, humidity, and light exposure contribute to this instability. The deterioration of perovskite materials negatively impacts the efficiency and lifespan of solar cells. Additionally, the use of Pb, a toxic substance, in PSCs is an environmental concern as it can leach out over time and contaminate the surroundings. To address these issues, researchers are actively developing new perovskite materials that are more stable and less toxic. Strategies include creating lead-free perovskite materials, utilizing alternative materials to stabilize the perovskite structure, and employing encapsulation methods to shield solar cells from environmental factors.

In recent years, Machine Learning (ML) techniques have been employed to predict the bandgap, Power Conversion Efficiency (PCE), and stability of PSCs with high accuracy, significantly expediting the development and optimization of PSCs.
