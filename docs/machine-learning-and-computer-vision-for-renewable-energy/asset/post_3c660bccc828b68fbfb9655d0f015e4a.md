
## 2.3 Blue Wind Energy for Urbanization: Potential of Blue Wind Energy for Urban Areas

Energy is necessary for our everyday existence, since it is used to power our houses, electronics, companies, and other activities. Rising energy demand, driven by industrialization, urbanization, and increased public awareness of environmental concerns associated to energy consumption, highlights the need for continuous improvements in energy-generation technology (Watson, 2022). This initiative seeks to transition from traditional fossil fuels to renewable energy sources, with the goal of producing plentiful, clean, and environmentally beneficial energy naturally. Among the different renewable energy sources, wind power is fast gaining prominence (Cruz-Rojas et al., 2023).

Wind turbines are used to generate energy for local power systems, with an emphasis on spinning reserve dependability and environmental consciousness. Wind energy systems, in general, work best on a small to medium scale, making them well-suited for use in rural and suburban locations (Ozcanli et al., 2020). The previous research efforts concentrated on power density studies for micro-wind turbines erected in suburban areas. Recent researches have revealed an untapped potential for urban wind energy, as wind speed in metropolitan locations provides an extra aspect (Sharma & Singh, 2022). Buildings in metropolitan areas contribute to increased turbulence intensity, needing specific attention for successful use of this energy source. Rapid building constructions, widespread urban growth, rising technology, and the desire for comfortable, high-quality living have increased energy use and sparked environmental concerns (Rosiello & Maleki, 2021). So, given that buildings and urban environments contribute considerably to overall energy consumption, it is critical to prioritize energy-efficient solutions in building and urban design (Fischer & Preonas, 2010). Wind power has emerged as one of the most efficient techniques of ensuring energy efficiency in buildings. Wind, which is frequently connected with renewable energy sources, has not been widely used in construction applications until recently (Grubb et al., 2021).

## 3. COMPUTER VISION AND MACHINE LEARNING FOR RENEWABLE ENERGY: NOVEL CONCEPTS

The burning of fossil fuels, which currently satisfies a significant portion of the world's energy requirements, stands as the primary contributor to the escalating levels of greenhouse gas emissions and the subsequent global temperature rise (Raihan et al., 2022). Addressing this challenge, the increased utilization of sustainable energy sources, particularly solar and wind power, presents a financially viable trajectory in alignment with the climate objectives outlined in the Paris Agreement. Nevertheless, the pace at which renewable energy adoption has progressed has struggled to keep pace with the continually expanding energy demands, resulting in a limited share of total energy derived from renewable sources (Singh, 2023). Consequently, expediting the shift to sustainable energy sources becomes imperative. Facilitating this transition necessitates the development of energy technologies, infrastructure, and policies that support and encourage the capture, storage, conversion, and effective management of renewable energy (Dai et al., 2016).

The careful selection of acceptable materials, particularly those used in photovoltaic applications, is critical in the pursuit of sustainable energy solutions. Following that, these selected materials must be synthesized with high yield and quality to match the standards for devices such as solar panels. The typical timescale for the materials discovery process is 15-20 years, implying plenty of room for improvement. Furthermore, the devices must be refined for durability and repeatability in order to fit easily into energy systems such as solar farms. To maintain economic viability, this integration demands effective control of energy use and generation patterns (Elavarasan et al., 2020).

## 4. SUSTAINABLE URBANIZATION AND ENERGY DEMAND-IMPORTANCE OF TRANSITIONING TO GREEN ENERGY SOURCES

As the severity of global warming and climate change increases, as evidenced by frequent and drastic changes in the natural environment affecting humans and other living beings around the world, international organizations such as the United Nations Organization, the G20, the G8, and individual nations become more aware of the environmental consequences of extensive fossil fuel consumption. This understanding emphasizes the economic and societal implications of climate change, particularly global warming (Zwane et al., 2022). The United Nations, notably through its climate change-focused environmental program, has continually worked to encourage greater international agreement. This agreement focuses on the need to meet an overall global carbon reduction target within the period given by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC). The goal is to gain international consensus that each country should have some responsibility for attaining the carbon emission reduction objective (Mohammadian, 2022).

Renewable energy and energy efficiency stand out as critical components of the transition to a low-consumption, low-carbon society. To make this transition easier, government policies should prioritize achieving region-specific objectives for renewable energy generation and energy efficiency. However, these objectives range between metropolitan regions, and there is a lack of comprehensive understanding of the link between energy transition and urbanization levels (Amran et al., 2020). The emphasis on growing and safeguarding energy in the current global context is around increasing energy producing capacity and, ideally, adding cleaner and more environmentally friendly energy sources into the growth process. Through a complex system, advancement and planning procedures frequently cause changes in the landscape and have a human influence on the surroundings (Hainsch et al., 2022). Resource extraction and land management operations in the building of energy infrastructure, as well as human monitoring of these systems, can have long-term and often unexpected implications. Despite increased interest in alternative energy sources, many developing countries continue to rely largely on fossil fuels for energy generation (Atalay et al., 2016). So, energy production has the capacity to influence land productivity, land cover, human migration, and other aspects of the energy production system, resulting in a complex interaction of various variables. The interdependence of land use, energy choices, infrastructure development, and the people for whom these facilities are developed necessitates careful thought. It is critical to examine and comprehend the interactions between these components (Singh, 2022).

## 5. INTEGRATION OF AI AND IOT IN ENERGY MANAGEMENT

The substantial portion of blue-wind as renewable energy sources and energy storage technologies should be unified in micro-grids to the inside electrical grids for the sake of to create a society with zero CO2 emissions. This is because of integration enables energy exchange so that the reducing demands on the transmission system (Xiao et al., 2019). However, managing energy inside and across these micro-grids is difficult due to a variety of uncertainties and there are erroneous data on energy output and consumption.

These uncertainties may make the energy optimization in this setting more difficult (Scarlat et al., 2015) and advancement of IoT platforms, in combination with artificial intelligence (AI), big data analytics and cloud computing, opens up new operational possibilities for smart grids. Even so, the centralized data processing on the cloud results in significant communication delays and overhead (Connolly et al., 2010). The edge computing also important for decentralizes computational activities at the network’s edge, and to putting them closer to the user. These activities like which include data collecting, pre-processing and forecasting simulation, improve energy management decision-making processes (Salleh et al., 2020). As the result of which edge-cloud orchestration appears as a potential and effective architecture for improving energy trading flexibility, scalability, and performance. These AI-powered solutions for blue-wind have the potential to drastically cut energy prices and carbon footprints by actively managing use patterns in both residential and commercial settings (Singh, 2023). So, the AI plays a role in predictive maintenance of energy infrastructure, which is crucial for averting failures and prolonging asset lifespan, resulting in a more stable and dependable power supply.

### 5.1 Role of AI in Optimizing Energy Production and Consumption

Artificial intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing the management of energy systems and its capacity to handle large information, forecast trends and promote real-time decision-making makes it a valuable tool for navigating the complexities of modern energy networks (Bujang et al., 2016). The Artificial Intelligence has the potential to transform the energy sector in a variety of ways in which they including optimizing output, anticipating demand, enhancing grid efficiency and lowering operating costs. Also, the predictive skills of AI are critical in the field of renewable energy for forecasting weather patterns and maximizing the production of solar and wind energy (Lawrenz et al., 2018). The advances in enormous data processing, significant computer power, information technology, and the improvement of machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) algorithms are ascribed to the increase in AI technologies.

The Artificial Intelligence technologies essential the potential to help the energy business which capitalize on the expanding opportunities connected with the Internet of Things (IoT) and the incorporation of renewable energy sources. The supercomputers, power electronics, cyber technologies, information systems connectivity between the control center and equipment are all part of the smart energy sector’s contemporary infrastructure (Westman et al., 2019). Despite these difficulties, the global economy is largely reliant on energy production, distribution planning, and financial sustainability (Cohen et al., 2021).
