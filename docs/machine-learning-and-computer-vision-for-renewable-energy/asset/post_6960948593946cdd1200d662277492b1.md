### ABSTRACT

The urgent need for a cleaner, more sustainable energy source has been sparked by the fast-dwindling supply of fossil fuels and the catastrophic consequences of climate change. The thriving market of hydrogen as a fruitful alternative is experiencing as an important environmentally friendly industrial feedstock. There are significant techno-financial and environmental obstacles to the commercialization of hydrogen as a fuel. Microalgal biohydrogen generation has drawn increasing attention as a means of overcoming these difficulties. Different metabolic pathways can be used to make microalgal biohydrogen, but a concise explanation of the economics and technical aspects of improving microalgae-driven biohydrogen generation is highly needed. Thus, assured prolonged sustainability can be achieved by improvising economical hydrogen generation technologies through the amendment of vital trial and error factors, possible gene-level, metabolic engineering modification and AIML employment.

### INTRODUCTION

Recent climate change scenario has propelled the necessity to explore for possible fossil fuel substitute with an environment friendly source of energy. Researchers have pointed towards hydrogen (H2) as potential contender following its superior level energy output and reasonable heating capacity (Wu et al., 2012; Chandrasekhar et al., 2020). The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) portrayed

that most of the industrially manufactured hydrogen is attained from steam-methane reforming, which generates greenhouse gases, resulting in climate change. The same is applicable for oil and coal gasification (CG) process. Hydrogen production via electrolysis method is also not a carbon-neutral one as it demands a considerable amount of energy (IRENA, 2018). Whereas hydrogen attained via microbial routes (microalgal biohydrogen production) has a lot of advantages like elevated growth rate, less energy need, more environment-friendly nature and no pretreatment process as of lignin based material (Muhammad et al., 2021; Ahmed et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2020; El-Dalatony et al., 2020; Jiménez-Llanos et al., 2020; Mona et al., 2020; Salakkam et al., 2021). Therefore, this review unveiled:

- the different metabolic routes of improved biohydrogen manufacture;
- the foremost challenges and notable limitations; and
- future insights, opportunities and economic considerations.

### Biohydrogen Manufacturing Pathways From Biomass

Biohydrogen mass-production from microalgae based biomass is strong economical strategy in comparison to existing conventional energy approaches (Ge et al., 2020). This can be achieved by either direct microalgae based production approach or by using that biomass as potential microbial fermentative substrate. The second one is having higher energy yield than the first one, but both of them can serve in combo as well (Oncel et al., 2015). These areas are elaborated in the following sub-sections.

### A. Photofermentation

Photofermentation utilizes sunlight in place of sugars as an energy source. This procedure can be portrayed by following equation:

16ATP + N2 + 16H2O + 10H+ + 8e- → 16ADP + 2NH4+ + 16pi + H2

Here, heterotrophic fermentation as well as the catabolic reaction of the in-house substrate supplies electron to green algae. Additionally, light energy along with oxidative carbon metabolism contributes for the electron extraction. Generated electrons successfully transferred to the plastoquinone pool coupled with chloroplast based nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate-plastoquinone oxidoreductase. Basically, the transferred electron to the [Fe]-hydrogenase and ferredoxin yields in elevated redox potential (Baeyens et al., 2020). Induced [Fe]-hydrogenase aids in high amount of biohydrogen generation in a dark as well as anoxic atmosphere.

Photofermentation was termed to be the most efficacious process compared to dark-photo fermentation, if one consider the hydrogen yield [~141.42 ml (g TS)-1] (Zhang et al., 2020). In contrast, the aforementioned process is less efficient in daylight conversion and ultimate product yield, when compared to electrochemical as well as biophotolysis method (Zhang et al., 2020).

### B. Dark Fermentation

Carbohydrate-rich green microalgae routinely produce biohydrogen by a light-independent process popularly known as dark fermentation. The complete conversion of glucose molecule can be shown by following equation:

C6H12O6 + 6H2O = 12H2 + 6CO2

The dark fermentation then produces 4 mol of hydrogen along with possible by-products under organic acids group like acetic acid (Mona et al., 2020). Two pathways contribute to the production of biohydrogen at some stage in the dark fermentation pathway. The hydrogenase pathway catalyses one of them through the reoxidation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide hydride, while the other one occurs through the catabolic transformation of formic acid (El-Dalatony et al., 2020). Though this process has shown a higher reaction rate, but due to the low hydrogen concentration yield a final purification step is necessary (Mona et al., 2020). On the other hand, an elevated outcome can be achieved by the optimal pre-treatment and after proper enzymolysis regarding Alternantheraphiloxeroides (alligator weed) (Song et al., 2020).

### C. Biophotolysis Process

Photosystem II in green algae and cyanobacteria possess the potential to employ energy from light to break water and produce biohydrogen. Here, electrons obtained from this process lead through the electron transport chain comprising of Photosystem I and II. The ferredoxin molecule is then reduced and adenosine triphosphate is produced. These participate in numerous metabolic processes that generate hydrogen. The term “biophotolysis” relates to a process driven by light that falls into two groups, viz., direct as well as indirect biophotolysis (Razu et al., 2019).

In the photosynthesis process, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (microalgae) and Synechocystis (cyanobacteria) promptly biophotolyze the water in their environment to produce hydrogen and oxygen. The microalgae can be easily cultivated and have negligible nutrient needs. They are also effective carbon dioxide fixers. Additionally, they grow in saltwater or wastewater in a similar fashion as they do in freshwater (Eroglu et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2016). Some of the principal hurdles include requirement of massive cultivation, inhabitation of ultimate product following accumulation of oxygen, excess light intensity need, and poor photochemical efficiency (Jiménez-Llanos et al., 2020).

Microalgae under anoxic conditions perform indirect biophotolysis where oxygen evolution materializes in a different stage from hydrogen production. The initial phase portrays photosynthesis, where carbon dioxide and water are transformed to chemical energy and oxygen. Later on, the organic materials are further processed into hydrogen, carbon dioxide and additional metabolites. Major barriers in this case are reduced hydrogenate uptake, high operational costs, and generation of carbon dioxide by hydrogenase enzyme resulting in low yield of hydrogen (Razu et al., 2019). Alternatively, biohydrogen can also be generated by oleaginous microalgae combined with the additional production of lipid (Sengmee et al., 2017).

### D. Electrochemical Procedure

The microbial electrolytic cell generates biohydrogen with the help of electro-chemically active microalgae capable of carbon dioxide (CO2) and electron production. These electrons are conveyed via the anode directing towards the cathode and finally coalesce with hydrogen (H2) atoms that release H+. An outer voltage feed is essential to obtain biohydrogen at the cathode as the process is not spontaneous. Microbial electrolytic cells are well efficient in manufacturing 12 mol H2/mol substrate, which is pretty low in comparison to the traditional one as it consumes residual organic ingredients (Park et al., 2021).
