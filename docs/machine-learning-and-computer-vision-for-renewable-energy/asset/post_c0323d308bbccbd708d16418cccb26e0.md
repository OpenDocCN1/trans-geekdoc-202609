## ABSTRACT

The limitations of traditional deep learning models in processing vast volumes of data and modelling complicated temporal dependencies make it difficult to effectively satisfy these objectives for short-term load forecasting (STLF). This chapter utilises deep learning, which enables the following: k-means clustering to comprehend hourly electricity demand load trend, extraction of complex features with non-linear interactions that impact electricity demand load, handling of long-term dependencies through the modelling of temporal hierarchies in the time series data via long short-term memory, and recurrent neural network to capture dependencies across time steps. The performance of the state-of-the-art hourly demand forecast models with the k-means variant is compared with that of the RNN-LSTM-CNN-copula. Furthermore, it is noted that RNN-LSTM-CNN-copula is a viable deep learning model for energy consumption forecast problems because of its capacity to learn the spatio-temporal dependencies in the hourly electrical demand load data, which results in an accurate energy demand forecast.

### INTRODUCTION

Buildings, industry, and transportation are the three primary economic sectors that together account for the majority of a country’s energy use. Buildings, being the greatest energy consumer, contribute for 36% of all carbon emissions and 40% of global yearly energy consumption, primarily in urban areas, according to data from the World Watch Institute (Aldhshan et al., 2021). Building energy consumption is expected to grow at a pace of 1.5% annual growth in OECD countries and 2.1% annual growth in non-OECD countries between 2012 and 2020. In particular, compared to residential structures, the overall energy consumption of commercial and institutional buildings in higher education is 30% and 45% greater, respectively.

Lower operating expenses are achieved by reducing energy use and avoiding utility penalties through the installation of suitable energy management systems and end-user energy efficiency control schemes. According to Kamal-Chaoui and Robert (2009), numerous countries have increased the enforcement of energy rules and construction norms. This has resulted in the development of innovative, energy-efficient building designs that guarantee lower energy consumption, energy expenses, and environmental effect (carbon emissions). Larger investments in smart infrastructures that support energy efficiency are required since, according to the World Energy Council (2016), “electricity demand would double by 2060, while overall per capita energy demand would begin to fall.” The combination of electric vehicles, decentralized generating and storage systems, energy management systems, and cutting-edge information and communication technology has made buildings more intelligent from a smart grid standpoint. Thus, it is now crucial to design and deploy smart technologies for buildings and power grids in order to meet the world’s energy needs in a way that is both economically viable and efficient while reducing carbon emissions.

Buildings equipped with smart meters to track energy usage at precise intervals have recently made research on energy consumption forecasting in buildings more important. In order to understand energy consumption patterns for use in energy demand forecasting, demand response, heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) optimization, fault diagnosis, and detection, large-scale, high-dimensional energy consumption data from smart meters collected at various granularities is helpful (Razavi et al., 2019). The utilities can plan resources and balance supply and demand with the help of accurate and trustworthy energy demand forecasts, which guarantees the security and stability of the electrical grid as well as the dependability of service delivery. However, Hamed et al. (2022) have declared that overestimation and underestimation of the energy demand lead to a severe impact on the economic and industrial developments. Precise energy demand modeling and prediction facilitates context-aware control methods, precise demand response tactics, electrical supply management, and effective energy management in smart buildings. In order to improve the energy efficiency of buildings for a sustainable economy through a conservation-minded society, reasonable use of available energy resources, and an effective national energy strategy, energy consumption forecast models have thus become an essential component of the Building Energy Management System (BEMS) (Mariano-Hernández et al., 2020). Nevertheless, Somu et al. (2021) avowed that the non-linear, dynamic, and complex nature of energy consumption data, along with the presence of trend, seasonal and irregular patterns, and dependence on various exogenous factors such as climatic conditions, nature of the day, socio-economic factors, etc. presents accurate and reliable energy consumption forecast as an interesting research problem.

Three types of energy consumption forecast models may be found in the literature:
- Engineering methods: these are challenging and time-consuming; Energy Plus and Ecotect are two examples. They rely on complicated building and environmental data and use physical and thermodynamic rules.
- Statistical methods: Inaccurate and rigid in its relationship between energy use and pertinent variables such as occupancy and climatic data; Examples include regression models (linear regression) and time series (autoregressive models).
- artificial intelligence techniques: Learns the consumption patterns from the historical energy consumption data, i.e., discover the non-linear relationship between the input (historical data) and output (target consumption); Examples: artificial neural network, support vector regression, etc. Among these, artificial intelligence approaches have become ‘active research hotspot’ due to their efficiency and flexibility over engineering and statistical methods (Liu et al., 2020).

However, the recent COVID-19 outbreak has affected the patterns of the electric power system; as a result, the expected demand for a typical weekday now matches that of a typical weekend day, as most workers remain at home and work from there (Jogunola et al., 2022). As a result, the measured difference between the weekend's peak and lowest energy demand was 24 percent. This unforeseen incident has made demand forecasting more challenging, which has exacerbated the electrical grid's instability.

Bunn and Farmer (1985) show that a 1% increase in load forecasting error can result in a GBP 10 M increase in annual operating costs (Chitalia et al, 2022). Furthermore, the surge of distributed energy resources, coupled with several constraints such as weather, occupant behaviors, electricity prices, and desired comfort levels, made it more difficult to accurately predict energy consumption to balance supply and demand (Jogunola et al., 2020). Furthermore, precise energy consumption forecast is a crucial prerequisite for energy service providers to supply residential and commercial facilities with a range of flexible and supplementary energy management services. As a result, there would be financial savings, more options for customers, and effective grid management for service providers. Additional advantages encompass a consistent power source, effective energy distribution, effective energy management, and control methodologies (Jogunola et al, 2021).

### LITERATURE REVIEW

Statistical techniques such as linear regression, exponential smoothing, autoregressive integrated moving averages (ARIMA), and seasonal autoregressive integrated moving averages (SARIMA) are frequently utilized to predict past consumption data (Ahmadi et al., 2023). These models are based on linear connections between past data and predicted data. Yet, machine learning and deep learning models have surpassed statistical methods due to the intricate non-linear patterns present in electricity consumption data. Deep learning approaches are straightforward and quickly process calculations, demonstrating their effectiveness as models according to Singaravel et al. (2018). Deep learning models can handle long-term dependencies, in addition to non-linear relationships, making them well-suited for energy forecasting (Ensafi et al., 2022; Kohzadi et al., 1996).

Multilayer perceptrons and deep feedforward networks are the cornerstones of deep learning. Consequently, the fundamental components of a deep learning model are layers of algorithms known as artificial neural networks (Wongrin et al., 2022). The CNN and RNN layers are two of the most crucial layers. CNNs have been widely employed recently for a variety of applications, including as image recognition (Sharma, Jain, and Mishra, 2018) and time series forecasting (Le et al., 2019, Nawar et al., 2023) among others. Deep learning has successfully modeled RNNs for sequence models, including the LSTM and the gated recurrent units (GRU) (Shiri et al., 2023).
