When used for solar modelling from sky images, these methods have been demonstrated to help with the nowcasting task of matching a picture to a solar value; however, they have little effect on the forecasting task. Combining images is another type of data augmentation technique. One of the k-nearest neighbours or a random sample can be combined with the original images. It was shown that mixing images made the greatest difference in nowcasting predictions, much like colour space transformation techniques. Deep learning methods for solar energy based on vision also require careful consideration of the sampling strategy (Lin et. al., 2018; Liu et al., 2018; Zhai et. al., 2019) . Sky image datasets and satellite observations are uneven in various ways, just like a lot of real-world datasets. For example, the distribution of cloud types at a given geographic location is highly dependent on the local weather. As a result, certain cloud patterns in the original dataset were either overrepresented or underrepresented. (Mujeeb et al., 2019; Kubby et. al, 2017; Paoli et. al, 2010) uniformly sampled mini-batches from a training set that is indicative of the dataset will teach the model a site-specific distribution, which may affect how well it performs in various climates.

A dataset’s quality, in addition to its size, can significantly affect the model’s performance and generalisation qualities. Incorrectly labelled samples or mis measured irradiance, for example, can cause an incorrect supervision signal during model training, and they can also negatively impact the model’s exploitation if it is used in untested environments. These factors make quality control a crucial component of machine learning initiatives (Coimbra et. al, 2018; Qing et. al, 2018; Rana et. al., 2020). The causes of anomalies in vision-based solar power monitoring include operational disturbances like dust, snow, dew, and temperature response, as well as equipment failures like misaligned shade rings, spectral selectivity, and temperature response. Rain, snow, animals, and soiling are just a few of the external hazards that sky cameras are subject to, which can hinder the collection of cloud cover observations. (Yu et. al, 2019; Zambrano et. al, 2020). One common method to filter these anomalies in a dataset is to inspect the outliers. Outliers can be defined, for instance, as samples that are significantly limited in the large original RGB feature space due to the curse of dimensionality. One way to accomplish this is to gauge the separation between samples within a feature space. To circumvent this issue a filtering method based on the CVAE model was developed (Sapitang et. al, 2010; Senthil et. al., 2019; Shimray et. al, 2017). An autoencoder was trained to reconstruct sky images using a feature space with low dimensions. Outliers that result from this low-dimensional representation are then manually y verified to facilitate the separation of real anomalies from visually uncommon sky images. however, this filtering technique may miss common anomalies, like rain or dust in sandy areas. Visual tools were created more recently to complement the different approaches to quality control (Zhang et. al, 2016; Peng et. al, 2017).
