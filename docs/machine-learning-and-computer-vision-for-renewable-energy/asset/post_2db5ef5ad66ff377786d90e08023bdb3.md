## ML IN THE DEVELOPMENT OF TRADITIONAL SOLAR CELLS

Abadi et al. (Balle et al., 2018) introduced an innovative approach to predict device performance and unveil structure-property correlations in organic photovoltaics (OPVs) by combining Machine Learning (ML) and Taguchi Design Experiments (TODE) (Wasmer et al., 2018). Their database comprised 240 small-molecule organic solar cells with 220 diverse donor materials blended with two fullerene-based acceptors (PC61BM and PC71BM). Using ten quantum chemical features related to energy conversion as descriptors, the model employed TODE and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) algorithms to derive design guidelines. The predictions indicated that reducing the optical band gap enhances organic solar cell performance by increasing photon absorption. Additionally, the model identified optimal values for 1L (energetic difference of LUMO and LUMO+1) and 1H (energetic difference of HOMO and HOMO-1) corresponding to each optical band gap. Lower values of 1L and 1H were associated with higher Power Conversion Efficiency (PCE) in materials with a low optical band gap, and the model suggested that having 1L and 1H nearly equal to 0 leads to good PCE values.

## ALGORITHMS FOR ENERGY SOURCE IDENTIFICATION

The real challenge lies in the identification of alternative source of energy. The above discussion provides an overview of different types of models and their proper utilization in identification of various sources. A unique algorithm has been devised to identify the source with greater precision. The algorithm can be presented in the section given below

- Let X be the matrix of features of the energy sources, with n rows and m columns, where n is the number of energy sources and m is the number of features.
- Let y be the vector of labels of the energy sources, with n elements, where each element is either “solar”, “wind”, “hydro”, or “biomass”.
- Let k be the number of clusters, which is 4 in this case.
- Let C be the vector of cluster labels of the energy sources, with n elements, where each element is an integer from 0 to k–1.
- Let f be the function that maps the energy types to numbers, such that f(“solar”) = 0, f(“wind”) = 1, f(“hydro”) = 2, and f(“biomass”) = 3.
- Let y' be the vector of numerical labels of the energy sources, with n elements, where each element is f(yi), where yi is the i-th element of y.
- Let g be the linear regression model that predicts the energy type based on the cluster, such that g(Ci) = βCi + α, where Ci is the i-th element of C, β is the coefficient, and α is the intercept.
- Let y'' be the vector of predicted numerical labels of the energy sources, with n elements, where each element is g(Ci), where Ci is the i-th element of C.
- Let E be the mean squared error of the predictions, such that E = Σ (yi' - yi'')^2, where yi is the i-th element of y', and yi'' is the i-th element of y''.

The respective mathematical symbols provide their usual meanings. The KMeans clustering algorithm has been applied to classify different concerned sources of energy and the regression analysis has been carried out to predict the energy type based on the cluster. The following summarized version provides the steps of operation in detail.

- Cluster the energy sources based on their features, such that C=KMeans(X,k), where KMeans is the clustering algorithm.
- Assign a numerical value to each energy type, such that y'=f(y), where f is the mapping function.
- Train a linear regression model to predict the energy type based on the cluster, such that g=LinearRegression(C,y'), where LinearRegression is the linear regression algorithm.
- Predict the energy type based on the cluster, such that y''=g(C), where g is the linear regression model.
- Calculate the mean squared error of the predictions, such that E=MSE(y',y''), where MSE is the mean squared error function.
