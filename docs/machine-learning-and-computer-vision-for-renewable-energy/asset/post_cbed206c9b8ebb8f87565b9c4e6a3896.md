- 10. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are a type of artificial neural networks (ANN) that excel at processing and interpreting data that is organised in a grid, such as pictures or videos. CNNs use convolutional layers to extract localised features from the input data, and they are inspired by the structure and function of the brain’s visual cortex [Yao et al., 2019]. Convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers are among the layers that make up a CNN. The core components of a CNN, called convolutional layers, use filters, also known as kernels, to extract localised features from the input data. [Akram et al., 2019].

All filters are made up of tiny matrices that traverse through the input data and multiply each filter by the corresponding input pixel. This process results in the creation of a feature map that depicts the sections of the input data that are most important for the current task. Pooling layers are commonly added to networks after convolutional layers to reduce the spatial dimensions of feature maps and increase computational efficiency. Max pooling selects the highest value within a defined region of the feature map, and average pooling selects the mean value within a defined region of the feature map. These are the two most popular forms of pooling [McCann, et al, 2017]. The pooling and convolutional layers’ acquired features are used by the fully connected layers to generate the final predictions. These layers, which resemble those in a traditional neural network, forecast outcomes based on the current task using the learned features. The CNN weights are changed during training, just like those of other neural network architectures, to minimise the discrepancy between the expected and actual output. The weights are then updated in light of this information after the gradients of the loss function with respect to the weights are computed using the back-propagation algorithm [Ajit et al., 2020].

## SOLAR FORECASTING USING COMPUTER VISION

When sky features are taken from spatial data captured by light-sensing apparatuses such as satellite imagery, ground-based imagers, or both, the method of solar forecasting using computer vision is referred to as image-based (Niall et al, Leron et al, 2021). On the other hand, while statistical techniques rely on a moving average and autoregressive covariate structure, physical approaches are based on the sun’s position. Most intraday variability in irradiance is explained by the dynamics of cloud cover. Researchers have developed computer vision techniques based on observations of clouds to better predict corresponding fluctuations in solar power. From past observations, the partial spatial configuration can be inferred due to the deterministic nature of cloud motion of the cloud cover in the future (Quentin et al, 2022). In addition to the intrinsic stochastic component of cloud modelling, predicting cloud displacement provides a major benefit over forecasting methods that rely solely on local meteorological data, which are unable to forecast future events with sufficient accuracy beyond the solar time series’ short-term autocorrelation period. Consequently, in order to produce solar predictions, the forecasting approach is necessary, that are genuinely valuable at various spatiotemporal scales (Vishnu et al., 2020; Vincent et al., 2020).

Satellites and sky cameras are the primary cloud observation sources for solar forecasting with a short time horizon. A fish-eye lens on a sky camera allows it to record the spatial arrangement of a few square kilometres of local cloud cover at a time, while a satellite provides maps of a continent’s atmospheric radiation at a lower temporal resolution of five to fifteen minutes interval of less than a minute. Due to their differing technical features and perspectives on cloud cover, for solar forecasting, these two imaging methods are available right now, employed in tandem, even though it is anticipated that their spatiotemporal resolution will rise and eventually catch up to that of ground-level imagery. In reference to the forecast horizon, methods utilising sky cameras seek to offer forecasts of the local solar variability as far ahead as thirty minutes, contingent upon the local cloud velocity; in contrast, satellite imagery, with its broader field of view, offers longer-range forecasts, reaching several hours in advance (Gaétan et al., 2019). Sky cams are most appropriate for places with an elevated solar power density, like solar or hybrid power plants, unless they are part of a network arrangement that covers a wider area. On the other hand, predictions of the nation’s solar output can be obtained through satellite-based methods, which can be advantageous for energy trading and grid balancing. Some of the comparable methods utilised in conventional computer vision techniques—cloud segmentation, cloud localization, cloud properties estimation, cloud tracking, and cloud motion modelling—are all based on sky or satellite images (Gaétan et al., 2019; Dragomir et al., 2018).

For sky images, the cloud mask is often generated using techniques for thresholding based on statistics of pixel values. In addition to comparable thresholding analyses, the effective cloud albedo, also known as the cloud index (0: no cloud, 1: dense cloud), can also be used to improve cloud segmentation in satellite imagery. This is calculated based on the percentage of light reflected by a surface, the percentage of light reflected by an observation, and the highest empirical percentage of light reflected by a surface. The cloud transmittance is indicated by this continuous variable, which can also be estimated for sky camera applications taking advantage of a clear-sky model and a pyrheliometer. Next, multiple sky cameras in stereovision mode are used to carry out cloud geolocation. Subsequently, to predict the future deterministic or probabilistic effects of cloud cover dynamics on solar output, cloud velocity vectors can be estimated using block matching techniques or optical flow (Vignesh et al., 2019; Ahmad et al., 2019).
