
## INTRODUCTION TO SOLAR POWER AND NEED FOR ITS FORECASTING

Solar power, derived from sunlight through photovoltaic (PV) or concentrated solar power systems, standing as a sustainable and competitive renewable energy source. Solar photovoltaic (PV) energy specifically is highly regarded for its sustainability. However, a key challenge lies in its intermittency due to weather conditions, notably variations in temperature and irradiance, significantly affecting electric power production quality. Accurate short-term forecasting of PV module power output becomes crucial for efficiently managing daily/hourly power grid production, delivery, and storage. Moreover, such forecasting holds significance in decision-making within energy markets. The ability to predict the power output of PV modules aids in optimizing grid operations and enables better resource allocation, enhancing the reliability and efficiency of solar power utilization.

Solar photovoltaic (PV) systems have witnessed substantial growth in electricity generation, marked by a rise in the number of installations. These systems are now commonly found in open spaces, rooftops, integrated into buildings and vehicles, and organized into expansive arrays within solar power plants. Despite the positive impacts of solar PV on diversifying power generation and addressing global warming, the swift adoption of this technology brings along certain challenges. As such, it varies based on site-specific elements as well as weather trends. Power instability is a risk that increases as renewable energy sources become more and more prevalent in the energy mix, which emphasizes how important it is to control the flow of renewable energy. The practice of projecting how much electricity a solar photovoltaic (PV) system will produce over a given period of time is known as solar PV forecasting. This entails predicting a PV system’s output based on a variety of input characteristics using mathematical models. Differences between the available power generation and the desired load may arise from the ambiguity of the load consumption as well as the intermittent power output of the solar PV production. Therefore, it is crucial to predict or forecast solar PV power generation capacity accurately. Precise solar photovoltaic forecasting is crucial for multiple reasons. Initially, it enables independent systems or grid operators to predict variations in the electrical supply and modify the distribution of power in line with that. This supports the upkeep of the power grid’s stability and dependability, as well as that of standalone systems that are disconnected from it. Second, precise projections can minimize the requirement for backup power sources, maximize the usage of renewable energy sources, and eventually lower customer electricity costs.

Solar energy production is a complex and dynamic process, making projecting solar PV a tough challenge. Inaccuracies in forecasting can give rise to a mismatch between the available power generation and the demand, potentially causing power outages or other disruptions. Moreover, inaccurate forecasts may result in the underutilization of solar PV systems, restricting the integration of renewable energy into the power grid and augmenting reliance on fossil fuel-based sources of electricity. In 1954, a team led by Pearson at US Bell Labs pioneered the development of the initial crystalline silicon solar cell, boasting a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 4.5%. This event marked a crucial breakthrough in the utilization of solar electricity. The PCE of contemporary mono-crystalline/poly-crystalline silicon solar cells in industrial applications has now surpassed 20%. However, these silicon-based solar cells are comparatively costly to produce, delicate, prone to damage, environmentally impactful, and exhibit limited temperature tolerance. A unit photovoltaic cell harvests photons from incident solar radiation and creates electron-hole pairs (EHP), which result in the production of photo-electric current as an output. PV cells are joined in series to form solar panels, which can be used alone or in conjunction with other panels to create larger power plants.

### PHYSICAL MODELS (WHITE BOX)

Physical models are grounded in the fundamental physical principles that govern the behavior of solar PV systems. These models consider the essential physical properties of the solar PV system, such as panel efficiency, the angle of incidence of the sun's rays, and panel shading. Additionally, environmental factors influencing solar energy production, including temperature, cloud cover, and humidity, are taken into account. Typically formulated through mathematical equations, these models describe the physical behavior of the solar PV system. However, the complexity of these equations necessitates a deep understanding of the underlying physics and system characteristics (installation details, electrical configuration, geographic location, and technical specifications) of the modelled PV system. Moreover, their reliance on weather conditions for forecasting makes their performance heavily dependent on the accuracy of weather forecast data

### DATA DRIVEN MODELS

Data-driven forecasting models leverage historical data analysis to derive insights, patterns, and relationships, enabling the prediction of solar PV system outputs. These models, grounded in statistics and algorithms, learn patterns and relationships from data to make accurate predictions. Effective implementation requires substantial amounts of high-quality data, and prediction accuracy can be influenced by biases or incomplete data. Data-driven models can be further categorized into statistical and machine learning models. The distinction between statistical modelling and machine learning can be blurry, with some researchers considering them interchangeable depending on the specific context and user perspective. As a normal trend of classification, the statistics takes a major and significant role in the machine learning based analysis.

### A: Statistical Models

Statistical models rely on the statistical analysis of historical data to identify trends and patterns for forecasting future solar PV power output. These models employ statistical techniques and mathematical algorithms to fit historical data and establish relationships between various variables, such as solar irradiance, weather conditions, and energy production. Numerous types of statistical models are used in solar PV forecasting, including autoregressive models (AR), moving average models (MA), and Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA). ARIMA models, commonly employed for time series analysis and forecasting, assume that the current value of a time series depends on its past values. The common tasks involved in the analysis of such model is the resource integration and their proper classification based on the model prepared. Various associated metrics produces relevant outputs for the further analysis. The statistical features derived can also be used as the inputs to different machine learning procedures to analyse the outputs further to greater precision level.

### B: Machine Learning Models (Black Box)

Contrasting with statistical models, machine learning models rely on more intricate algorithms that enable them to autonomously learn patterns and relationships within the data. These models excel in handling complex and nonlinear connections between input and output variables, often yielding more precise forecasts compared to statistical models. Consequently, they have demonstrated efficacy in producing accurate PV forecasting. Noteworthy machine learning models applied in solar PV forecasting encompass artificial neural networks (ANN), support vector machines (SVM), and random forests (RF).

A fundamental distinction between statistical models and machine learning models lies in the level of human involvement required. Statistical models typically demand more human input concerning feature selection and engineering, designing the model architecture, and tuning hyper parameters. Conversely, machine learning models can be trained on raw data with minimal preprocessing, and the model architecture, along with hyper parameters, can often be automatically optimized using techniques like grid search or Bayesian optimization. Another contrasting aspect between the two approaches is their capacity to handle uncertainty. Statistical models often offer more transparency and can furnish estimates of uncertainty in their forecasts. In contrast, machine learning models are often more opaque or "black-box," providing limited information on how predictions were derived. However, techniques such as Monte Carlo simulation can be employed to generate probabilistic forecasts from machine learning models. The machine learning approach prompts the classification on larger and potential scale of precision. The training of model is very crucial and equally follow for supervised and non-supervised technique as well. In case of energy classification some relevant attributes can be used such as the characteristics of source of energy, energy flow trends, intensity of energy flow, direction of the energy etc. Such features of energy can be utilized properly in carrying out the training of models.
