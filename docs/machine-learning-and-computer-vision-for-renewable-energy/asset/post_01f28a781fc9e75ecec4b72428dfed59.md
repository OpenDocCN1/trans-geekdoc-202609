## 1.4 Biomass and Bio-Energy

Biomass and bioenergy research aims to harness energy from organic materials, such as agricultural residues and dedicated energy crops. Advances in biofuel production, biogas technologies, and the development of bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) are key areas. Sustainable practices and efforts to prevent competition with food production are critical concerns (Hakeem, et al., 2016) (Hakeem, et al., 2014) (Ozturk, et al., 2017) (De, et al., 2014).

### Definition and Types of Biomass

Biomass refers to organic matter derived from living organisms, primarily plants and animals, that can be used as a source of energy. This renewable energy resource is abundant and diverse, including various forms:

- **Woody Biomass:** Derived from trees and forestry residues, woody biomass includes forest pellets, chips, and logs. It is an established feedstock for bioenergy production.
- **Agricultural Biomass:** Crop residues (for example, straw, grain stover) and dedicated energy crops (such as switchgrass, miscanthus) contribute to agricultural biomass, providing sustainable opportunities.
- **Animal Waste:** Livestock and poultry waste, such as manure, can be processed for bioenergy production through anaerobic digestion or combustion.
- **Municipal Solid Waste (MSW):** Organic components of MSW, including food and yard waste, can be treated for bioenergy production.
- **Algae:** Algae biomass, rich in oils, can be used for biofuel production, providing a potential source for sustainable transportation fuels.
- **Bio-energy Technologies:** Several technologies are used to convert biomass into usable energy, suitable for various feedstocks and uses (Naqvi, et al., 2018) (Scalat, et al., 2015):
  - **Combustion:** Biomass combustion includes burning organic matter to produce heat and power. It is commonly used for heating applications and power generation in biomass power plants.
  - **Gasification:** Biomass gasification converts organic matter into a combustible gas (syngas) by reacting it with a regulated amount of oxygen or steam. Syngas can be used for power generation or converted into biofuels.
  - **Anaerobic Digestion:** This biological process breaks down organic matter in the absence of oxygen, producing biogas rich in methane. Anaerobic digestion is widely applied to treat organic waste and create renewable fuel.
  - **Biochemical Conversion:** Biochemical processes, such as fermentation, involving hydrolysis and microbial digestion, are used to convert biomass into biofuels like ethanol and biodiesel.
  - **Pyrolysis:** Pyrolysis involves heating biomass in the absence of oxygen to produce bio-oil, biochar, and syngas. Bio-oil can be further processed into biofuels.

### Future Prospects

The future of biomass and bioenergy is promising, with continuous advancements and innovations in technology and methods:

- **Advanced Biofuels:** Research focuses on developing advanced biofuels with improved energy density, compatibility with existing infrastructure, and reduced environmental impact.
- **Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS):** BECCS is gaining attention as a means to achieve negative emissions by capturing and storing carbon dioxide from bioenergy processes.
- **Integrated Biorefineries:** Integrated biorefineries aim to maximize the value of biomass by producing a range of bio-based products, including biofuels, biochemicals, and materials.
- **International Collaboration:** Global cooperation is essential to address challenges and share best practices in biomass and bioenergy development.

Biomass and bioenergy play a vital role in the transition to a sustainable and low-carbon energy future.

## 1.5 Geothermal Energy

Geothermal energy, tapping into the Earth’s internal heat, is gaining attention for its reliability and reduced environmental impact (Klemeš, et al., 2015) (Barbier, et al., 2002) (Dickson, et al., 2013) (Fridleifsson, et al., 2001). Research focuses on enhanced geothermal systems (EGS), advanced drilling technologies, and exploring new geothermal reservoirs. The integration of geothermal energy into district heating systems is a growing trend (Figure 6 shows geothermal energy).

### Understanding Geothermal Energy

**The Earth's Internal Heat:** Geothermal energy taps into the Earth’s internal heat, which originates from the planet's formation and the decay of radioactive isotopes. The heat is stored in the Earth’s crust and mantle, and it manifests in various forms, such as volcanic activity, hot springs, and geysers.

**Types of Geothermal Resources:** Geothermal resources can be classified into three main types: hydrothermal systems, geopressured systems, and hot dry rock systems. Hydrothermal systems are the most common and accessible, including the use of heat and energy from the Earth’s crust to produce power.

### Historical Development of Geothermal Energy

Early Uses of Geothermal Energy: Throughout history, various civilizations recognized and utilized the potential of geothermal energy for heating and healing purposes. Indigenous peoples in regions with geothermal activity used hot springs for bathing, cooking, and therapeutic purposes.

Emergence of Geothermal Power Plants: The first attempts to generate power from geothermal sources occurred before the early 20th century. The development of the first geothermal power plants, such as the one in Larderello, Italy, marked a significant achievement in the effective use of geothermal energy for power generation.

### Geothermal Technology

Geothermal Power Plants: Geothermal power plants use various technologies to harness the Earth's heat for power generation. The most common types include dry steam, flash steam, and binary cycle power plants. Each technology has its advantages and is suited to different geothermal resource environments.

Direct Use Applications: Beyond electricity generation, geothermal energy finds applications in direct uses such as space heating, greenhouse heating, aquaculture pond heating, and industrial processes. These applications utilize heat directly from the Earth without the intermediate step of electricity generation.

### Environmental Impact

Reduced Greenhouse Gas Emissions: Geothermal energy is recognized for its lower environmental impact compared to conventional fossil fuels. The process of extracting and converting geothermal heat into power releases minimal greenhouse gases, contributing to efforts to combat climate change.
