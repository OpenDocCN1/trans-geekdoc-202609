# ### PAIR OF PARADOXES

This section identifies two kinds of paradox which arise within the processing of data. In general, a paradox may be defined as a statement that is self-contradictory or contrary to expectation. Data-processing paradoxes are not, however, an indication of logical error, but rather a symptom that human intuition refuses to accept that certain mathematical relationships are unavoidable. Such paradoxes must be resolved if erroneous conclusions are to be avoided.

# ### Simpson's Paradox

The first paradox is the well-known **Simpson's paradox**, which arises when the amalgamated data reverse the conclusion suggested by the separate data. It is described in the following way.

> > In data analysis, the phenomenon that a conclusion is reversed when data are amalgregated is usually referred to as Simpson's paradox.

This paradox is intimately connected with the issue of **confounding** and has important implications for the interpretation of data from observational studies.

# ### Lord's Paradox

The second paradox is **Lord's paradox**, which is less well known than the first. The paradox is described in the following quotation taken from a well-known article by Lord.

> > Lord's paradox is a statistical paradox that arises when two different methods of analysis yield conflicting conclusions about the same data.

The resolution of this paradox has been the subject of much debate, but it serves to emphasise the importance of careful specification of the model and the research question.
