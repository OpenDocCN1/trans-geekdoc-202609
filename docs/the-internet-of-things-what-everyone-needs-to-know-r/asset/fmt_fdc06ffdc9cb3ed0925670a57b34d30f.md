
# Overcoming these challenges to enable the effective regulation of IoT technologies

Overcoming these challenges to enable the effective regulation of IoT technologies is no small feat. Data governance alone is an enormous challenge, with some estimates suggesting that IoT devices are generating over 5 quintillion bytes of data from billions of devices daily according to Cisco, a figure that is expected to increase exponentially going forward.[^(17)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-489) This level of data accumulation leads to various concerns—including who has access to that data, to what extent they can use it (for analytics, resale, etc.), and also how securely the data should be kept—each requiring various responses at multiple governance levels.[^(18)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-490) For example, in the US context, and depending on the device or application in question, an alphabet soup of agencies have some sort of authority over IoT (FDA, FCC, FTC, etc.).[^(19)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-491) And there is the related issue of too restrictive regulations hurting innovation even as some commentators such as Bruce Schneier have argued that IoT laws can, in fact, spur innovation.[^(20)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-492) For example, buyers express higher confidence in products approved by the FDA.[^(21)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-493) An increase in consumer trust toward IoT technology—perhaps driven by government-backed standards or certification schemes—could similarly boost trust and thereby sales.

Still, despite the difficulties involved, it is no secret that a large majority of consumers today (more than 90% in one 2017 survey) have little confidence in the security of IoT devices.[^(22)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-494) Recent attacks such as the Mirai botnet introduced in the preface and discussed in [chapter 3](#12_chapter3.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-chapter-3) reinforce the perception (too often accurate) of ubiquitous vulnerabilities in the IoT. In response, there are three predominant approaches to protecting digital privacy and security in the IoT explored throughout the rest of this chapter, namely: (1) allowing free market innovation to help provide a suite of solutions; (2) relying on state, federal, and international regulations to enforce security and privacy standards; or (3) leveraging a combination of the above to ensure adequate protection within a polycentric approach.

As we will see, a market-based approach focuses on educating consumers, encouraging industry best practices, and using current enforcement tools to better protect consumers’ data integrity.[^(23)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-495) While the free market encourages innovation, it is unlikely to spawn the comprehensive protection desired by consumers. There is a lack of market mechanisms to effectively incentivize corporations and manufacturers to improve digital security.[^(24)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-496) Witness the failure of the market to adequately punish firms that experience cyber attacks, as documented in a 2018 National Bureau of Economic Research study that found an immediate decrease of only 1% in the stock price of impacted organizations.[^(25)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-497)

No single avenue will be able to manage the full range of threats to the IoT. An all-of-the-above approach is needed to enhance digital privacy and security.[^(26)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-498) There is increasing recognition that such a polycentric approach is the preferable route forward. This multilevel, multipurpose, multifunctional, and multisectoral model, championed by scholars including Nobel laureate Elinor Ostrom and Professor Vincent Ostrom, challenges orthodoxy by demonstrating the benefits of self-organization, networking regulations “at multiple scales,”[^(27)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-499) and examining the extent to which national and private control can coexist with communal management. As originally explained by Professor Vincent Ostrom, “a polycentric political system would be composed of: (1) many autonomous units formally independent of one another, (2) choosing to act in ways that take account of others, (3) through processes of cooperation, competition, conflict, and conflict resolution.”[^(28)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-500) In many ways, this governance model—which has been applied to diverse collective action problems ranging from managing inner-city police precincts[^(29)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-501) to adapting to climate change[^(30)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-502) and even mitigating orbital debris[^(31)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-503)—is well situated to addressing dynamic (and oftentimes fragmented) IoT issues given the number of diverse actors and technologies in play. Examples of these applications include issues with the Domain Name System itself, for which the tech community developed a security upgrade that still has not been implemented more than twenty years later owing to the fact that “it requires most sites to adopt it before anyone sees benefits.”[^(32)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-504) No governance system is perfect and polycentrism has its disadvantages such as concerns over gridlock and a lack of defined hierarchy.[^(33)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-505) But such an approach could help move the debate regarding the Security of Things in a more productive direction as part of an overarching campaign to promote some measure of cyber peace.[^(34)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-506)

## Can self-organization work in the IoT?

One important aspect of polycentric governance is self-regulation.[^(35)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-507) Under certain circumstances, self-regulation has the capacity to adapt better and faster than black letter law to rapidly changing technological and social forces. It can also be more efficient and cost-effective than command-and-control-style regulation,[^(36)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-508) though it is not a panacea given that such efforts are voluntary and are subject to market forces (e.g., changing consumer demands).[^(37)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-509) One need only review Facebook’s promises to reform, and its failures to do so, to see why calls for an end to the self-governance of Internet platforms are gaining traction.[^(38)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-510) Still, some regulators such as Maureen Ohlhausen, the former head of the FTC, came out in favor of permitting IoT providers to self-regulate,[^(39)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-511) a perspective mirrored by the US Senate in a resolution that highlighted both the central role of the private sector in developing and regulating IoT applications and the degree of importance accorded this issue by policymakers.[^(40)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-512)
