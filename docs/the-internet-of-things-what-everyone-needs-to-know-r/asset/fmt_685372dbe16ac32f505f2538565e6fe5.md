
# Enterprises acting as norm entrepreneurs

Enterprises acting as norm entrepreneurs can play an important role in improving IoE safety by helping to pioneer and spread standards and norms,[^(85)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-806) and even organizing rating and labeling systems that clearly “explain the user’s security responsibilities.”[^(86)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-807) Cyber risk-mitigation strategies favored by factions of the US Congress, such as cyber risk insurance, can help firms limit their exposure in the event of a data breach,[^(87)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-808) but they may do little to enhance overall cybersecurity absent a proactive strategy that infuses best practices from the beginning.[^(88)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-809) Other concerns must also be overcome, such as the “war exclusion,” which may limit recovery due to covered entities being classified as “collateral damage” in a cyber war.[^(89)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-810) Further, the type and extent of investment must be analyzed using robust information-sharing mechanisms to instill technical, budgetary, and organizational best practices.[^(90)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-811) More also needs to be done to clarify liability structures and, in so doing, boundaries of responsibility and accountability,[^(91)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-812) including on the part of Internet Service Providers (ISPs) that serve as gatekeepers “sit\[ting\] between our homes and the rest of the Internet.”[^(92)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-813) Above all, tech firms should practice the precautionary principle, which states that “when the potential of harm is great, we should err on the side of not deploying new technology without proof of security.”[^(93)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-814)

# Government promotion of cybersecurity due diligence

At the next level up, governments can do more to promote cybersecurity due diligence in the IoE, such as by establishing a Cyber Peace Corps, National Cybersecurity Safety Board,[^(94)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-815) or even an International Criminal Tribunal for Cyberspace.[^(95)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-816) States are already getting into the game. Aside from California (discussed in [chapter 5](#14_chapter5.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-chapter-5)), Ohio is incentivizing companies that invest in cybersecurity best practices and frameworks by providing them with a safe harbor.[^(96)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-817) Twenty-five states have also passed anti-DDoS laws, with another five focusing on the urgent problem of ransomware with a five-fold increase in ransomware variants in 2016 alone.[^(97)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-818) Other states are focusing on removing insecure features from IoT devices and bolstering incident response plans.[^(98)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-819) Similarly, more than twenty-five nations are experimenting with various bottom-up approaches to cybersecurity risk management modeled after the NIST CSF.[^(99)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-820) The federal government also has an invaluable role in solving collective action and free-riding problems discussed throughout this book.[^(100)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-821) For example, it could create baseline standards, outcome-based rules, norms, and organizing agencies.[^(101)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-822) The courts could also help foster cybersecurity due diligence, up to and including holding executives responsible for data breaches happening on their watch.[^(102)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-823) Of course, none of this is simple,[^(103)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-824) and it’s easy to go too far; as Schneier points out, “If computers were subject to the same product liability regulations as stepladders, they probably wouldn’t be available on the market yet.”[^(104)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-825) But given the stakes, the US government can and must do more to lead on IoT governance,[^(105)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-826) or others will step into the void.

# Global cybersecurity cooperation

Globally, the G7 continued its work on cybersecurity in 2016, publishing its view that “no country should conduct or knowingly support ICT (information and communication technology)-enabled theft of intellectual property” and that all G7 nations should work to “preserve the global nature of the Internet” including the free flow of information in a nod to the notion of cyberspace as a “global networked commons.”[^(106)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-827) Bilateral relationships such as the G2 (United States and China), minilateral clubs (including the Five Eyes \[FVEY\] and NATO), and ultimately the international community must also be proactive in this polycentric effort to spread cybersecurity due diligence and promote cyber peace. In part because of the perception that cyber risk is “escalating out of control,”[^(107)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-828) there exists an opportunity to engage in constructive international dialogue on norm building. One idea is to build from the 2015 Paris Agreement model, in which individual nations and clubs could announce “Cyber Peace Pledges” at annual Conference of the Parties (COP) gatherings to help build momentum toward global agreements on IoT governance, which would include clarifying how existing international law applies to the IoT. All this could set the stage for an eventual, if still fanciful, UN Framework Convention on Cyber Peace. Even this outcome, though, would not be the finish line, but just another mile marker—successfully governing the IoE requires all of us to do our part.

# What can I do? We need you! Take control, demand better, and be a responsible netizen.

In the 1970s, there were widespread concerns about food safety. The British scientist Sir Albert Howard was making the case about returning nutrients to the soil, helping to jump-start the organic movement.[^(108)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-829) However, just as we are now seeing with IoT certification schemes, the first consumer-facing organic food programs were decentralized, largely state-by-state, affairs. It took nearly twenty years, until 1990, for Congress to pass the Organic Foods Production Act (OFPA) and finally usher in national organic food standards. Final regulations defining the production and handling of organic food were not enacted until 2002.[^(109)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-830) In all, the quest to adequately label organic food took nearly a quarter century, which is blazingly fast compared to, for example, automobile safety. One of the first popular reports pushing for improving car safety was published by *Popular Science* in 1950.[^(110)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-831) But it then took nearly two decades before the crash test results were published to inform consumers of the variable safety performances of cars. The first US law mandating the use of seat belts was not passed until 1984.
