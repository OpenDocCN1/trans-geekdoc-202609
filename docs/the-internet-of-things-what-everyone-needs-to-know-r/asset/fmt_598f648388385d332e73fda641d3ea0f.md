
# 网络安全与网络和平

Headlines regularly attest to a seemingly constant, and deepening, state of cyber insecurity. From the 2019 Marriott breach, to North Korean–sponsored ransomware, Chinese espionage against Airbus, and Hamas-organized cyber attacks against Israel, in all, humanity does not seem to be doing a particularly good job at managing cyber attacks.[²](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-723) The news is not all bad, though. There are reports that efforts such as the National Initiative for Cybersecurity Education (NICE) have led to improved cyber hygiene, as have more robust behavior-based threat analytics.[³](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-724) More boards of directors also seem to be taking their cybersecurity oversight responsibilities seriously,[⁴](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-725) while omnibus privacy regulations like GDPR, discussed in [chapter 5](#14_chapter5.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-chapter-5), are forcing more firms to re-examine their data governance practices worldwide.[⁵](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-726) Yet the cybersecurity talent crisis is continuing, and IoT threats remain acute with more nation states, for example, exploiting vulnerabilities to launch cyber attacks.[⁶](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-727) What hope is there, then, for peace in such an uncertain and fast-evolving Internet ecosystem?

As has been argued, only limited efforts have been made to date at defining “cyber peace.”[⁷](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-728) One example of such an effort is the International Telecommunication Union, which defined the term in part as “a universal order of cyberspace” built on a “wholesome state of tranquility, \[i.e.\] the absence of disorder or disturbance and violence.”[⁸](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-729) Although certainly desirable, such an outcome is politically and technically unlikely, at least in the near term. Nor can cyber peace (sometimes also called “digital peace”)[⁹](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-730) be understood merely as the “absence of violence” online, which was the starting point for how Professor Johan Galtung described the field of peace studies he helped to create in 1969.[^(10)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-731) As such, cyber peace is not defined here as the absence of conflict online, a state of affairs that may be called *negative* cyber peace.[^(11)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-732) Rather, by working together through polycentric partnerships of the kind described in [chapter 5](#14_chapter5.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-chapter-5), we can mitigate the risk of cyber conflict by laying the groundwork for a *positive* cyber peace that respects human rights, spreads Internet access along with cybersecurity best practices, promotes stability, and strengthens governance mechanisms by fostering multi-stakeholder collaboration.[^(12)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-733)

All this is (needless to say) easier said than done, and as such, promoting cyber peace requires tackling a host of thorny governance challenges discussed throughout this book, from incentivizing manufacturers to harden their Internet-connected devices against cyber attacks to defining and operationalizing concepts like corporate social responsibility, sustainability, and international norms such as due diligence. An all-of-the-above approach is needed to meet this challenge. The effort needs to embrace polycentric principles, including subsidiarity, which is the notion that “a central authority should have a subsidiary function, performing only those tasks which cannot be performed effectively at a more immediate or local level.”[^(13)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-734)

Consider the 2018 Paris Call for Trust and Security in Cyberspace, which was a broad statement of principles designed to help guide the international community toward greater cyber stability and, perhaps one day, cyber peace. It was signed by more than 50 nations (with the notable exception of the United States), and over “130 companies and 90 universities and nongovernmental groups.”[^(14)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-735) The Call was criticized for how it papered over continuing concerns over cyber sovereignty,[^(15)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-736) as seen in French president Emmanuel Macron’s accompanying remarks that “giant platforms could become not just gateways but also gatekeepers.”[^(16)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-737) Nevertheless, the Call has had an impact in catalyzing and shaping the conversation around the scope and meaning of cyber peace. For example, it focuses on improving “cyber hygiene,” along with “the security of digital products and services,” and the “integrity of the Internet,”[^(17)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-738) all of which are integral to enhancing IoT security. And it is just one of myriad private sector initiatives to promote cybersecurity.[^(18)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-739)

Under this framing, our goal, in other words, should not be to reach some predetermined finish line, but rather to promote progress along a cyber peace spectrum and toward a more robust and sustainable Digital Ecosystem of Everything. Such a spectrum would include systems in place to “deter hostile or malicious activity in cyberspace”[^(19)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-740) and in so doing promote stability, human rights, and international security both online and offline.[^(20)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-741) To realize this vision of an “open, interoperable, reliable, and secure”[^(21)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-742) IoE, it will be necessary to leverage civil society, academia, and, yes, put into place appropriate regulations. There is a regime complex of organizations engaged in this effort from which to draw, including the Online Trust Alliance,[^(22)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-743) Cyber Peace Alliance,[^(23)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-744) ICT4Peace,[^(24)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-745) and the Ostrom Workshop’s Cyber Peace Working Group, to name just a few.[^(25)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-746) There is even growing support for movements such as a Cyber Peace Corps, building from successful programs like the Peace Corps and AmeriCorps.[^(26)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-747) Fostering such efforts can help ensure that, to paraphrase Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., the moral arc of the universe is long, but that it bends toward cyber peace.[^(27)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-748)

## Taking out the crystal ball—let’s envision the state of the IoT in 2050; what do current trend lines reveal?

By some estimates, in the late 2000s, something of a milestone was reached—for the first time in history, the total number of Internet-connected devices outnumbered humans on the planet.[^(28)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-749) Estimates vary, as was discussed in [chapter 1](#10_chapter1.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-chapter-1), but using this data, the ratio between people to smart devices went from 0.08 in 2003 to 3.47 by 2015 to 6.58 by 2020, the same year that consumers are projected to spend some $3 trillion on such devices.[^(29)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-750) If these trends continue, there could be a 400:1 ratio by 2050.
