
Likewise, IoT devices are often full of sensors that are constantly collecting and reacting to data, such as a request for Alexa to “say grace” before a meal.[^(20)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-20) Combined with intelligence, that data can even help a device be responsive to what you want out of it. For instance, what if you could lace up your Bluetooth-enabled Nike sneakers with an app—and then have their tightness automatically adjust as they collect and process data about whether and how much your feet are swelling during a basketball game?[^(21)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-21) While some of this data collection and usage is intuitive to a device’s intended function, IoT devices and services may also be sweeping up more than what you’d expect. For instance, smart speakers may not only be collecting and storing data shared through voice interactions but also, to the extent they are connected with other smart home sensors, information about when lights are turned on or off, the channel that TVs are set to, or when front door locks are engaged.[^(22)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-22) Such information, it almost goes without saying, would be of interest not only to marketers but also to criminals, and even provide a new dimension to domestic disputes.

Such statistics and examples related to the rise of smart consumer devices tell only a part of the story.[^(23)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-23) Beyond saving you the step of retying your shoes, what can devices, integrated software functionality, and data *do* when they are combined in interesting new ways? Consider Virginia Tech’s Goodwin Hall, which is equipped with hundreds of sensors that together collect more than 12GB daily—they are even able “to identify occupants based on gait\[.\]”[^(24)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-24) In other words, such smart floors and walls can now identify you based on the way you walk. Even underground, our world is quietly evolving—South Bend, Indiana, for example, now boasts one of the smartest sewer systems in the world.[^(25)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-25) From the deepest depths of the oceans, to the final frontier of space, these systems of intelligent devices and services, saturated with data, are set to transform our lives and our world.[^(26)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-26)

Beyond the new capabilities and opportunities that an ever-expanding “Internet of Everything” presents, we should also ask what challenges are in store for us. In other words, as Socrates famously quipped during his trial, “The unexamined life is not worth living.”[^(27)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-27) Consider October 2016, when a distributed denial of service (DDoS) attack emanating from a vast network of small, cheap, Internet-connected devices, which collectively came to be known as the Mirai botnet,[^(28)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-28) paralyzed Internet servers run by a tech firm called Dyn. That, in and of itself, might not have been a big deal, but the consequences certainly were. Because Dyn managed (and continues to operate) a significant part of Internet infrastructure, access to a number of important Internet services was slowed, or even stopped entirely, for much of the eastern United States during the attack. The Mirai botnet was so successful, and noteworthy, because it took advantage of security weaknesses in Internet-connected devices such as security cameras and home routers. Initially, some thought that the attack was politically motivated, but investigators determined that, in fact, it was not a shadowy criminal group or nation state behind the botnet; instead, it was three college students, trying to get an edge in the Minecraft computer game.[^(29)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-29) “They didn’t realize the power they were unleashing,” said FBI agent Bill Walton. “This was the Manhattan Project.”[^(30)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-30)

In the context of IoT and more broadly, cyber attacks are continuing to increase in cost and complexity,[^(31)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-31) and some have both economic and national security implications. IoT might be used to send spam, eavesdrop on kids, and power DDoS attacks. A 2017 study found that nearly half of IoT security buyers have experienced a breach in the last two years; moreover, the financial impact of those breaches was reportedly significant—“13.4% of the total revenues for smaller companies and hundreds of millions of dollars for the biggest firms.”[^(32)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-32) Compromised IoT technologies also escalate the risk of kinetic attacks with greater potential consequences. As Bruce Schneier has argued, “With smart homes, attacks can mean property damage . . . \[but\] \[w\]ith cars, planes, and medical devices, they can mean death.”[^(33)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-33) Some scenarios with smart ovens might also be catastrophic.

Consider that, in 2014, for only the second time in history to that point, a cyber attack was confirmed to have caused physical damage.[^(34)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-34) This time, the target was not Iran’s nuclear program, but a steel mill in Germany. Specifically, a blast furnace was compromised, causing “ ‘massive’—though unspecified—damage.”[^(35)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-35) Attackers had gained access to the plant through the firm’s business network, highlighting the insecurity that can stem from interconnected systems even when a firewall is in place. IoT devices might be targeted directly or serve as an entryway to a network. For instance, there have been unconfirmed reports of similar incidents to that of the German steel mill, such as one involving a petrochemical factory that was compromised by a coffee maker,[^(36)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-36) and a Las Vegas casino that was hacked through a smart aquarium.[^(37)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-37) Widely varying estimates for the cost of these attacks range from $275 billion to $22.5 trillion,[^(38)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-38) with US government entities regularly warning that the danger from cyber attacks now “exceed the danger of physical attacks.”[^(39)](#17_Notes.xhtml#actrade-9780190943813-miscMatter-10-note-39)
