## 5 Minimally Invasive and Non-Invasive Sensor Technologies for Predicting Heart Failure

An Overview

Huseyin Enes Salman, Mahmoud Khatib A.A. Al-Ruweidi, Hassen M. Ouakad, and Huseyin Cagatay Yalcin

### 5.1 Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is one of most prevalent clinical syndromes, in which the heart muscles are not capable of providing adequate blood flow to the circulatory system. The symptoms of HF are reported as ankle swelling, dyspnea, and fatigue due to elevated jugular venous pressure, pulmonary crackles, and peripheral edema [1–3]. The estimated number of patients suffering from HF worldwide is reported as 26 million [4]. Frequent hospitalization and poor life quality are serious risks for HF patients. It has been reported that only about 10% of patients with chronic HF survive 10 years after diagnosis [5, 6]. HF is a chronic and progressive disease and, therefore, HF patients have high rehospitalization rates and emergency visits. About 25% of HF patients are readmitted within a month and at least half of these readmissions are due to HF related symptoms [7]. These facts demonstrate the high economic burden and hospital workload for HF treatment.

Hospitalization is required for HF patients in case of congestion, which is the excessive accumulation of body fluid [8]. Congestion is mainly related to water and sodium retention in the body, leading to high left ventricular end-diastolic pressure [9, 10]. The symptoms of congestion can be listed as dyspnea, rales, and edema [11]. The most-used therapy for congestion is to intravenously inject diuretics to minimize the fluid accumulation in the body [8]. The diuretics help kidneys to excrete more sodium into the urine, and the excessive sodium results in more water absorption from the blood, which reduces the amount of fluid in the body.

The current gold standard for HF diagnosis is to measure intracardiac filling pressures using implantable pressure sensors via cardiac catheterization [12]. However, the invasive nature of cardiac catheterization prevents its routine usage for HF patients [10]. In addition, the cost of the implantable pressure sensors is more than US$ 20,000, which is not affordable for millions of HF patients [7]. This demonstrates the need for an inexpensive, non-invasive, easy-to-apply, and accurate method for HF diagnosis.

In the literature, alternative non-invasive modalities are suggested to monitor HF patients and diagnose the critical HF stages which require hospitalization. This way, unnecessary hospitalizations can be prevented by remotely monitoring the patient using non-invasive and minimally invasive sensors [3]. The efficiency of remote monitoring is still open to debate, but many studies have indicated a reduced rehospitalization rate and increased life quality for patients with external monitoring [3]. For this purpose, external devices or wearables with embedded non-invasive sensors are employed. By retrieving data from the non-invasive sensors, the health status of an HF patient is followed, and clinical therapy is offered in emergency cases, which are identified by evaluating the abnormal changes in measured data. This method is also known as home monitoring or remote patient monitoring [3].

In this chapter, we explain the non-invasive and minimally invasive sensor technologies and techniques employed for HF diagnosis. We also summarize landmark studies and clinical trials which prove the potential of non-invasive monitoring of HF patients.

### 5.2 Methods

The methods for identifying worsening HF can be listed as body weight measurements, electrocardiography (ECG), bioimpedance monitoring, activity tracking, implanted pressure sensors, lung ultrasound monitoring, measurements with sound and Doppler sensors, seismocardiography, ballistocardiography, photoplethysmography, and measurement of natriuretic peptides levels in circulating blood. These methods are explained in detail in this chapter.

### 5.2.1 Body Weight Measurement

The state of worsening HF is characterized by excessive reduction in circulatory volume and body water retention [3, 13]. Depending on the change in water balance in the body, a sudden increase in weight is observed in an emergency case. If the increase rate in body weight is higher than 2 kg/48 hours, it can be evaluated as a deterioration due to the impending HF decompensation [3].

Many remote monitoring studies used body weight measurements as a rehospitalization indicator for HF patients [14–24]. Weight measurements can be easily performed on a daily basis using weight scales or shoe-integrated pressure sensors. However, pressure sensors used in shoes can lead to inaccuracy in long-term monitoring [25] as there are several factors reducing the accuracy of measurements, such as different levels of activity in daily life and individual-dependent posture and gait disturbances. Alternatively, weight measurements can be performed daily by patients using bathroom scales as a part of HF self-management [26, 27].

### 5.2.2 Electrocardiographic (ECG) Measurement

ECG measurement is a widely used non-invasive method for HF diagnosis [28, 29]. The method is based on measuring the electrical activity of the heart using electrodes placed on the chest skin [30]. The electrodes identify voltage differences resulting from heart muscle depolarization and repolarization during the heartbeat [31]. In 12-lead ECG measurements, 10 electrodes are placed on limbs and the chest. The electric potential of the heart is measured by capitalizing on the different angles between the heart and electrodes.

Measurements are generally recorded for 10 seconds so that the electrical depolarization is monitored during the entire cardiac cycle [32, 33]. The main components of ECG signals are P-wave, QRS complex, and T-wave.

Several ECG parameters are utilized for predicting HF and also its worsening in HF patients [3]. The most-used ECG parameters are reduced heart rate variability [34], increased heart rate [35], increase in QRS/T angle [36], atrial and ventricular tachyarrhythmias [37, 38], increased ECG left ventricle mass index [28, 39], QT prolongation [40], increased QRS duration [41, 42], and left ventricle strain [20, 43, 44]. Reduced heart rate variability can be determined using the time differences in heart rate between day and night [34]. The heart rate variability can be determined by examining the standard deviation of sequential 5-minute R-R interval means. If the ECG left ventricle mass exceeds 70 ± 9 g/m² in men and 61 ± 8 g/m² in women [28, 39], or the corrected QT duration is longer than 450 ms in men and 460 ms in women [40], there is a potential risk factor for the presence of HF. QRS durations higher than 100 ms [41, 42, 45], T-wave inversions, and ST segment depression [20] are also evaluated as indications of HF.

### 5.2.3 Bioimpedance Monitoring

Bioimpedance analysis is a non-invasive method for assessing the body composition and fluid overload [46]. Impedance is an electrical parameter defining the resistance to the electrical current flow. In bioimpedance monitoring, a high-frequency and low-amplitude electrical current is used in the body to measure the bioimpedances [47]. Body tissues and fluids have different resistances which change the flow behavior of the electrical current. In an HF decompensation scenario, fluid retention leads to pulmonary congestion, and this fluid overload in the lungs influences the bioimpedance of the thoracic area. The blood and fluids have lower resistance to electrical current flow compared to thoracic tissues and air. This means that bioimpedance decreases with increasing fluid content. Therefore, there is an inverse correlation between the bioimpedance and the amount of fluid [48–50]. Healthy and relatively dry lungs are expected to have higher impedances than lungs with pulmonary edema that develop as a result of HF [51]. The bioimpedance monitoring is important for HF patients, because the thoracic impedance changes start in advance of the observation of serious symptoms and hospitalization.

The traditional non-invasive bioimpedance analysis, also known as transthoracic impedance cardiography, is performed using four or six electrodes placed on the skin for measuring the changes in thorax resistance. Low-amplitude constant electrical current is sent from one of the electrodes and measured by the other electrodes, enabling the voltage difference and impedance through the thorax to be determined [47].
