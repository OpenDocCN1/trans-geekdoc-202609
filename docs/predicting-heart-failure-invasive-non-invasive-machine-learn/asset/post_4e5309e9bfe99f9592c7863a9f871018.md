
## 9.1.1.2 HF Classifications

The American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines (2013) have classified HF into two categories according to the measurement of the left ventricle ejection fraction (EF): HF with preserved (≥50%) ejection fraction (HFpEF) – the heart pumps blood normally but is too stiff to fill properly that leads to impaired filling of the chambers of the heart; and HF with reduced (≤40%) ejection fraction (HFrEF) – the heart is too weak to efficiently pump blood out of the left ventricle [1, 22]. Ejection fraction reflects the pump function of the heart; it represents the amount of blood being pumped from the left ventricle (the heart’s main pumping chamber) with each contraction and is expressed as a percentage. HF with mid-range or mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF) is an intermediate stage with an EF of 40–49%, that generally progresses either to HFpEF at 25% of cases or HFrEF at 33% of cases [23].

The most common classification for HF severity is the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification. The NYHA classification has been widely used for almost a century and was updated in 1994 [24]. It is a fundamental tool for stratifying the risk of HF and also determines if a patient is eligible for a clinical trial or if the type of medication and devices are appropriate [25]. It classifies HF into the categories shown in Figure 9.2 [26].

- Class I – HF does not cause limitations to physical activity. Ordinary activities do not cause fatigue or shortness of breath.
- Class II – HF causes slight limitation to physical activity with symptoms such as fatigue and shortness of breath. Patients with NYHA class II show no symptoms at rest.
- Class III – HF causes marked limitations of physical activity. Patients with NYHA class III show no symptoms at rest, but less than ordinary activity causes symptoms of HF.
- Class IV – HF patients are unable to carry on any physical activity without HF symptoms and even have symptoms when at rest.

Figure 9.2 NYHA classification. Based on The Criteria Committee of the New York Heart Association [24].

Depending on the time of onset, HF can be classified as acute or chronic. Acute HF can be defined as “the rapid onset or change of symptoms and signs of HF that requires urgent medical care” [5]. Furthermore, acute HF is a condition with an unfavorable prognosis and is characterized by high mortality and rehospitalization rates [27]. Patients who have stable HF for some time, without change in clinical status, are often said to have chronic HF [5].

## 9.1.1.3 Evaluation and Diagnosis

Due to the increased incidence and the large financial costs required to diagnose HF, the diagnosis and treatment of the disease are extremely critical issues. The diagnosis of HF can be difficult, especially in the early stages and there is not a single diagnostic test for HF. Furthermore, it might be particularly difficult to recognize and associate symptoms and signs in obese patients, in elderly people, and in patients who suffer from chronic lung disease and need to be assessed at each visit meticulously [16].

Initially, clinical symptoms and signs are estimated by clinicians by performing a physical examination of the patient. Also, standard biochemical and hematological tests have to be requested. These tests include complete blood analysis, urinalysis, and a complete metabolic profile for levels of serum electrolytes (including sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium), serum urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, glucose, fasting lipid profile, and thyroid-stimulating hormone. Moreover the kidney and liver function have to be tested [28]. Apart from a detailed medical history, the electrocardiogram (ECG) is fundamental to reveal arrhythmias, conduction delays, or ischemia, while the echocardiogram is also a standard test to confirm systolic or diastolic dysfunction [29]. Also, the initial evaluation may include a chest X-ray, a coronary angiography (or a computed tomography coronary angiography (CTCA)) when coronary artery disease is suspected. In addition, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging has become a very useful tool for investigating HF due to its high spatial resolution and the use of late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) [30, 31]. Static images using T1 and T2 weighted black blood algorithms are valuable for tissue characterization. With CMR, clinicians are informed about anatomic structures, perfusion, and ventricular function while LGE can specify the exact causes of HF, depending on the pattern of scar formation such as amyloidosis, sarcoid, eosinophilic myocarditis, etc. and provide important prognostic information beyond other biomarkers [32, 33].

The measurement of serial natriuretic peptide (B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) or N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP)) is also included in the HF-specific laboratory tests (specifically in patients with suspected HF) in order to address the management of HF. According to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) [5], systolic HF can be effectively ruled out with the levels of BNP or NT-proBNP. As BNP levels increase, the specificity increases and the diagnosis of HF becomes easier [34]. Standard management of the HF patient involves a follow-up 2–12 times a year and patients are advised to self-monitor their weight and symptoms.

## 9.1.2 Applications of ML in HF

### 9.1.2.1 ML in Medicine

Medicine has always been characterized by (big) data that is suitable for the application of ML. ML is appropriate to clinical research since it is more flexible than traditional statistical methods. ML provides great potential for healthcare experts to exploit massive amounts of data from numerous sources, integrate them in a single system, curate them, and provide assessment and recommendations.

ML models have the potential to improve healthcare efficiency in numerous ways. Prognostic models could empower healthcare experts to select better treatment options for their patients. Additionally, diagnostic models could be used in screening, risk stratification, and recommending appropriate tests. This would decrease the burden on clinicians, save resources, and reduce costs.

According to Obermeyer and Emanuel [35], increasing the ability to transform data into knowledge will affect at least three areas of medicine: (i) it will dramatically improve prognosis, (ii) it will improve diagnostic accuracy and (iii) it will reduce much of the work of radiologists and anatomical pathologists, since these clinicians mainly focus on analyzing digitized images that can easily be processed by algorithms instead.

ML techniques can be grouped into two subcategories: supervised learning and unsupervised learning [36, 37]. In supervised learning, the model is trained by having as input a data set comprised of instances; every instance has a known outcome. The data set may consist of various features (discrete or continuous). The goal of the model is to predict a certain output that can also be discrete or continuous. In the case of a discrete output, we are referring to a classification problem and in the case where the output has a continuous value (range), we have a regression problem. By contrast, in unsupervised learning there is not a single output to predict; the data are unlabeled. The algorithms try to find patterns in the data. Clustering, the most common application, uses algorithms that try to group data in clusters based on similarities they might have.

Early diagnosis of HF, outcome prediction, hospital readmission, and mortality prediction in HF patients are the areas that benefit from the application of supervised ML techniques in the medical field with a discrete outcome.

### 9.1.2.2 ML Applications in HF Diagnosis/Prediction

Models based on ML techniques can be useful in helping physicians in HF diagnosis. Several studies have been conducted to build a model that can diagnose and detect HF at an early stage based on various ML algorithms (Table 9.1).

In 2010, Yang et al. [38] proposed a model based on support vector machines (SVM) to diagnose HF at an early stage using 289 patient cases. Samples were classified into three groups: the control group (70 subjects), the HF-prone group (59 subjects), and the HF group (160 subjects). The accuracy of the model was 87.50% and 65.85% in identifying subjects in HF-prone group and HF group, respectively. In 2012, Son et al. [39] tested an approach that combined rough set (RS)-based model with decision trees on 88 control subjects and 71 subjects with congestive HF. In their study, they utilized data with no missing values and features consisting of demographics information and clinical laboratory findings. The experimental results showed that their method performed with an accuracy of 97.50%, sensitivity 97.20%, and specificity 97.70%. In 2017, Choi et al. [40] applied deep learning (DL) methods and more specifically implemented a recurrent neural network (RNN) for the early detection of HF in 3884 HF subjects and 28,903 controls using electronic health record (EHR) data. The EHR data set included demographics, habits, clinical and laboratory findings, medications, and International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 codes. Their model achieved 77.70% area under the ROC curve (AUC), with a 12-month observation window, higher than regularized logistic regression (LR), neural network (NN), SVM and K-nearest neighbor (k-NN) classifier approaches. More recently, in 2020, Le et al. [41] implemented a multilayer perceptron (MLP) NN achieving an accuracy of 88.00%. The data set (299 subjects) they utilized comprised demographics, laboratory findings, lifestyle habits, and ejection fraction. Also in 2020, Potter et al. [42] applied the RF classifier to build a model able to predict Stage B HF based on energy waveform ECG. They utilized 254 subjects as the training set (135 with HF, 119 controls) and 65 subjects as the test set (27 with HF, 38 controls). Their model achieved AUC 76.00%, sensitivity 93.00%, and specificity 61.00%. Pecchia et al. [43] conducted a study utilizing the short-term heart rate variability (HRV) measures. They analyzed 1914.40 hours of ECG signals from 83 patients, of which 54 were used as controls and 29 were subjects suffering from chronic HF with NYHA classification I, II, and III. They designed a classifier based on the classification and regression trees (CART) method with 94.80% accuracy, 89.70% sensitivity, and 100% specificity on identifying normal subjects vs. subjects with NYHA classification I, II and III.
