
## 13.5 Summary of the Cardiac and Wearable Device Data Sets

There is a large number of data sets publicly available for ECG recording. Recently, several data sets have been released for research and PhysioNet has a large collection of ECG data sets. A team from Chapman University and Shaoxing People's Hospital (Shaoxing Hospital Zhejiang University School of Medicine) has collected and disseminated a novel database that contains 12-lead ECGs of 10,646 patients with a 500 Hz sampling rate that features 11 common rhythms and 67 additional cardiovascular conditions, all labeled by professional experts. The data set consists of 10-second, 12-dimension ECGs and labels for rhythms and other conditions for each subject [59]. Another large recent ECG data set is the PTB-XL ECG dataset [60] containing 21,837 clinical 12-lead ECGs from 18,885 patients of 10-second length. The raw waveform ECG data was annotated by two cardiologists, who assigned potentially multiple ECG statements in each record.

An ECG image data set was released for CVD and COVID-19 patients with 1937 distinct patient records [61]. The data were collected using the ECG device EDAN SERIES-3 from different healthcare institutes across Pakistan. The data set was manually reviewed by experts to categorize them into five distinct categories (COVID-19, abnormal heartbeat, myocardial infarction (MI), previous history of MI, and normal person). ECG data were collected using a 12-lead ECG device which can be used for machine learning-based model development for CVD detection among COVID and other CVD patients.

Bogu et al. [5] reported heart rate and steps data from 106 individuals using wearable devices. There were 25 COVID-19 +, 11 non-COVID-19 illnesses with self-reported symptoms, and 70 healthy individuals with no symptoms or illness during the COVID-19 period (February 2020 to June 2020) were reported in this data set. Resting heart rate was computed by selecting the heart rate intervals where steps were zero for 12 minutes ahead of an interrogated time point.

Similarly, Mishra et al. [10] used the data from consumer smartwatches for the pre-symptomatic detection of COVID-19. Using retrospective smartwatch data, they showed that 63% of the COVID-19 cases could have been detected before symptom onset in real-time via a two-tiered warning system based on the occurrence of extreme elevations in the resting heart rate relative to the individual baseline. The investigation was carried out on nearly 5300 participants; however, 32 patients infected with COVID-19 were identified from the cohort, and the rest were in the control group.

The MyHeart Counts study [62] used an iOS app among 110,056 users from March 9, 2015, to October 28, 2015. The study cohort described here is composed of contributions from study participants who designated broad sharing of their data (n = 34,189). Among consenting participants, 4900 (10.2%) completed a 6-minute walk test at the end of day 7 in the study. The 6-minute walk test activity not only collects distance traveled but also accelerometry during the walk. Additionally, MyHeart Counts users were able to upload data from wearable devices compatible with HealthKit. Table 13.1 summarizes some recent cardiac and wearable device data sets.

## 13.6 Machine Learning for Improving Cardiac Health of HF Patients

The advances in machine learning for biomedical applications have stimulated the use of wearables for continuous monitoring and abnormality detection [35]. The authors have worked on solutions to detect heart attacks amongst drivers using models trained on public databases (MIT-BIH ST Change Database) and a conventional machine learning model (support vector machine algorithm), showing an accuracy of about 97.4% and 96.3% in detecting ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-elevation MI (NSTEMI), respectively [72].

The authors have also worked on another solution to continuously monitor the heart sound and use it to train the machine learning model on the 2016 PhysioNet Challenge database, consisting of different classes of heart sounds, to detect abnormal heart sounds with an accuracy of 97% [73]. There have been studies to use machine learning techniques to classify HF patients using the eight strongest derived predictors (age, creatinine, hemoglobin, weight, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, and income) of mortality, these have shown much more promising classification of HF patients than conventional methods, in terms of prognostication and response to medications [74]. In the future, the life-saving LVAD for HF patients can be further improved in HF prediction, using machine learning techniques with the help of a miniature high-configuration low-power-consuming microcontroller (Figure 13.10).

Machine learning techniques can be useful even in the management aspects of HF patients, e.g. identification of patients who might adhere to the prescribed medications or might need additional measures for treatment adherence. Machine learning approaches can also be utilized to identify HF patients at high risk for additional comorbidities (such as depression) or in remote HF monitoring systems to improve HF clinical outcomes. To protect implantable devices from cybersecurity assaults, effective machine learning approaches have been used. When compared with traditional methodologies, machine learning approaches have been successful in developing score models with improved measure estimates regarding the prediction of cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) responders.

ECG-based wearables, such as the Apple Watch (Apple, Cupertino, CA), mobile ECG equipment, and temporary patches are all helping to advance ambulatory heart rhythm assessment (e.g. Zio patch from iRhythm Technologies, San Francisco, CA). Wearables can generate a multiple-lead ECG with a single touch, allowing patients to self-screen in the event of any symptomatic episode. As mentioned in section 13.4.5, machine learning models are used with AliveCor's KardiaMobile, which combines cellphones as a mobile ECG monitor. In the iTransmit13 and iREAD14 tests, these wireless devices had 100% percent and 96.6% sensitivity and 97% and 94.1% specificity for AF detection, respectively, using machine learning models. Tison et al. in [76] trained a deep neural network using PPG heart rate recordings exhibited a sensitivity of 98.0% and specificity of 90.2% in detecting AF, compared to a 12-lead ECG-based analysis.
