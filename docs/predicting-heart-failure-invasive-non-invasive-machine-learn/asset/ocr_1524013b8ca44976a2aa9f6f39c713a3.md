The increase of lung tissue density causes a change from the dominant A-line to diffuse B-lines in what is known as sliding [86]. B-lines, previously known as comet tails or lung cosmists, are defined as discrete laser-like vertical lines that arise from the bottom of screen and move synchronously with lung sliding [81, 87]. Lung B-lines are not exclusive to HF symptoms, and can be seen in many other conditions such as pulmonary edema [88], pneumonia [89], systemic sclerosis [90], acute lung injury [91], and other respiratory diseases [92].

There are multiple factors that affect the quality and quantity of B-lines appearances in LUS images, such as the position of the patients, the equipment utilized, clip-length, probe selection, the experience of the practitioner, and the number of zones [93]. The current settings to examine designated thoracic zones can be as low as two up to 36 zones. The optimum recommendation is eight zones.

The visualization of B-lines is possible with cardiac, convex, or linear probes [94]. There are two main scanning approaches in LUS, but either way the probe has to be placed in intercostal spaces. The first approach is the longitudinal scan, while the second is the transverse (oblique) scan. The ribs and their shadows are a part of the image in the longitudinal scan. On the other hand, the transversal approach allows the practitioner to visualize a wider view of the pleural lines uninterrupted by rib shadows [95]. The most important reference point is the pleural line with its lung sliding as it would be impossible to obtain any information about lung aeration without clearly visualizing it. Patients should be positioned either sitting upright, semi-recumbent or supine. In addition, patients should maintain the same position if serial examinations are being performed as the B-lines appearances in the imaging are greatly influenced by the patient's position [96, 97]. The length of the imaging clip is optimally 6 seconds, if the patient is breathing normally [98].

In HF diagnosis and monitoring, the B-lines should be diffuse, bilateral, and multiple. Diffuse means that the lines should be adjacent in two areas, bilateral means that they should be visible in the left and right hemithorax, and multiple means that they should be in abundance [95]. There are many quantification systems that have been developed to classify and assess B-lines numbers in order to identify the level of risk that at patient is at during and after hospital discharge. The methods can be categorized as: (i) score methods and (ii) count-based methods. Score methods often rely on a minimum number of B-lines in one intercostal space, labeling it as a positive zone and adding up the number of positive zones. In addition, lines can be counted one by one, or upon confluence, or as percentage, cumulatively known as count-based methods [99]. These wortification systems can be important in predicting mortality and hospitalization of worsening HF, and are a prognostic marker for HF as shown by many studies [100–108].

There are many protocols for LUS for diagnosis and prognosis monitoring of HF. The most famous protocols being used are: (i) bedside lung ultrasound in emergency (BLUE) [109], (ii) fluid administration limited by lung sonography (FALLS-protocol) [110], (iii) cardiac arrest ultrasound exam (C.A.U.S.E.) [84], (iv) lung and cardiac ultrasound (LuCUS) [111].
