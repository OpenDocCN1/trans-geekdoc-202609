### 3.1 Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a general nomenclature given to the group that includes diseases of the heart or blood vessels and describes any disease that affects the circulatory system. CVDs include heart attacks, congenital heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral artery disease, and heart failure. Although there are several causes of CVD, the most common causes are atherosclerosis and/or hypertension which are the leading cause of death worldwide. Hypertension can be very dangerous because in most cases there can be no apparent symptoms at the early stages that will prompt patients to seek medical care. Although it is a disease with a fairly silent course, a few symptoms including headache, ringing in the ears, nosebleeds, and visual impairment can be warning signs [1]. In the last two decades, mortality rates from CVDs have decreased in high-income countries. Still, morbidity and mortality rates have increased surprisingly rapidly in low- and middle-income countries. More than 17 million people die from CVDs each year, which is around 31% of all deaths worldwide. 75% of CVD deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries and occur almost equally in men and women. If the current situation is allowed to persist, it is estimated that, by 2030, an estimated 23.6 million people will die from CVD [2–5].

Although these diseases are generally seen in older adults, primary prevention efforts should begin early in life, especially since atherosclerosis dates back to childhood. Therefore, there is increasing emphasis on eliminating risk factors through, for example, healthy eating, exercise, and smoking avoidance in atherosclerosis prevention. There are many causes of CVDs, and the main reasons are tobacco use, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, and the harmful use of alcohol. Many studies have proven that dietary habits are directly related to CVDs. Inadequate intake of whole grains, high consumption of salt, saturated fatty acid, and fried food in the diet of individuals are important parameters that increase the risk of CVDs. There is an increasing demand for the design of fast, portable, and low-cost biosensing devices for the treatment and early diagnosis of CVD. Biosensors can play a significant role in the early diagnosis of CVD as an alternative to time-consuming and expensive laboratory analysis. In recent years, many biosensors have been improved to determine cardiac markers. One of the biggest challenges in CVD is estimating an individual's risk of suffering from CVD. There is great attention in research to find early diagnostic biomarkers that can be detected in the blood and predict CVD risk. The best known of these biomarkers is C-reactive protein (CRP), followed by cardiac troponin (cTn) I or T, interleukin-6, interleukin 1, myoglobin, lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, myeloperoxidase, and low-density lipoprotein. ### 3.2 Specific Biomarkers for Cardiovascular Research

Nowadays, different reasons, such as particular lifestyles and dietary habits, cause an increase in the risk factors and incidence of CVDs. In addition, individual risk factors, such as age and other diseases, play an essential role for patients [17].

Biomarkers are biological materials found in bodily fluids, and they indicate the normal or pathological processes or responses of the body [18]. Biomarkers are critical tools for diagnosing and monitoring many diseases. CVDs involve various disorders, both acute and chronic. The effective, accurate, and rapid diagnosis of acute conditions with biomarkers in the short term can be life-saving [19]. Besides this, for evaluating long-term risks related to CVDs, which can occur in the later stages of patients’ lives, biomarkers are widely used to improve patient’s life quality. Cardiovascular research with biomarkers includes, in addition to diagnostic studies, post-disease risk and damage monitoring, such as evaluation of damages after stroke, the severity of cardiac damage, and cardiac arrest-related brain injury in Figure 3.2 [20].

There are various biomarkers available for cardiovascular research that have been used for years and are emerging and new. In Table 3.1, the most significant and specific biomarkers for cardiovascular research are classified.

It is possible to categorize biomarkers based on the physiologic processes. For example, inflammation-related biomarkers are CRP, sST2, GDF-15, TNF-α, etc. LDL and MPO are related to oxidative stress [24]. Considered from another angle, lipid-based biomarkers (HDL, LDL, etc.), CRP, and cTnI are considered widely used traditional biomarkers, while sST2, TNF-α, Gal-3, and GDF-15 are emerging biomarkers. Copeptin is another promising biomarker for cardiovascular research, and there are ongoing studies on copeptin that demonstrate its use as a diagnostic biomarker for MI, in addition to the well-known and clinically established biomarkers such as myoglobin and cTnI [22, 34]. Stability and ease of measure of copeptin are considered an alternative biomarker. However, the changes on the levels of copeptin during the perioperative course should be monitored for establishing copeptin as a guiding biomarker [34, 35]. On the other hand, myoglobin, H-FABP, and MPO are add-on biomarkers that are more valuable with a multibiomarker approach [30].

Essentially, an ideal cardiovascular biomarker should be highly specific with definite reference limits in order to provide valuable information for clinical assessment [24].
