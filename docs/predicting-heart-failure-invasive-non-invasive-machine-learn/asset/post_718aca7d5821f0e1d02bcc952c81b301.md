The linear range of sensor was in the range of 0.02 to 0.09 ng/mL, with the detection limit of 0.008 ng/mL [85].

A new immunosensor based on bismuth citrate-modified graphite screen-printed electrodes (SPEs) has been fabricated for the voltammetric detection of CRP using quantum dots (QDs) tags in human serum by Kokkinos et al. The modified SPE was used both as a biological recognition element and as a QDs reader. Quantification of the target protein was achieved by the acidic dissolution of PbS QDs and anodic stripping voltammetric detection of released Pb(II). Determination was performed at bismuth nanodomains formed on the sensor surface during the electrolytic preconcentration step, as bismuth citrate was reduced to metallic bismuth simultaneously with the deposition of Pb on the surface of the immunosensor. The result was linear over the range 0.2–100 ng mL⁻¹ CRP and the limit of detection was 0.05 ng mL⁻¹ CRP in the optimum conditions [110].

Nanoparticles (NPs) of commercial lipase from Candida rugosa, glycerol kinase (GK) from Cellulomonas species, and glycerol-3-phosphate oxidase (GPO) from Aerococcus viridans were prepared with a pencil graphite electrode (PGE) by Narwal and Pundir. The characterization of PGE before and after co-immobilization of enzyme nanoparticles (ENPs) was achieved by SEM, CV, and EIS. A linear relationship was obtained between a wide range (0.1–45 mM) in the optimum conditions within 2.5 s at a pH 7.0 and a temperature of 35 °C [159].

In addition to these, there are other biosensors technologies such as fluorimetric, SPR, and chemiluminescence for the determination of CVD biomarkers. For example, in CRP analysis, fluorimetric biosensors can offer affordable and accurate analysis but their lower sensitivity is a drawback [167]. CRP is a significant biomarker in CVDs and is much studied. In a study, a chemiluminescence based biosensor was developed for CRP detection using and improving conventional enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [168]. SPR biosensors are popular optical techniques and details of their applications on cardiovascular biomarkers such as cTnI and myoglobin are available in the literature [169, 170].

## 3.5 Future Outlook

Further research is needed to work on novel biomarkers and to offer multistrategy strategies for better early treatment protocols. The application of novel detection biosensors, such as electrochemical biosensors, piezoelectric biosensors, thermometric biosensors, and optical biosensors, is still in the developmental stages, and there is the potential for rapid growth. Turning these discoveries into clinical practice will be critical for reducing the incidence of CVDs. Point-of-care devices provide ease of application for patients and medical staff through user-friendly diagnostic and monitoring kits. User-friendly devices will likely become commonplace in the future. Smartphone health applications are attracting attention from scientists, focusing on their integration with apps and biosensor systems. Wearable sensors will also make life easier in the future and, thus, many patients’ lives will be saved thanks to the early detection of CVDs.
