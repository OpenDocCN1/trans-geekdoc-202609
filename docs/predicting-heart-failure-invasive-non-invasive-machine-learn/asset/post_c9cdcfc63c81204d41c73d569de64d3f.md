## 11 Commercial Non-Invasive and Invasive Devices for Heart Failure Prediction
A Review
Jayakanth Kunhoth, Nandhini Subramanian, and Ahmed Bouridane

### 11.1 Introduction

According to statistics provided in Roth et al. [1], the total number of cardiovascular cases has doubled, and death-related cardiovascular diseases have increased by 50% globally in the last three decades. Heart failure is a chronic condition where the heart fails to pump sufficient blood and oxygen for the body. A report from the American Heart Association reveals that one in five people is prone to heart failure and 50% of these patients will live a maximum of 5 years after experiencing heart failure [2]. The different risks which can lead to heart failures are high systolic blood pressure (hypertension), high fasting plasma glucose, high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high body mass index (BMI), impaired kidney function, ambient and household air pollution, tobacco, dietary risks, low physical activity, sedentary behavior, alcohol, chronic stress, adverse childhood experiences, and excess body/abdominal fat (obesity) [1, 3]. Age, ethnicity, family history/genetics, and gender are some of the non-modifiable factors leading to heart failure [3]. Fatigue, breathlessness, elevated jugular venous pressure, peripheral edema, etc. are the common symptoms of heart failure. Chronic heart failures (CHFs) have become a burden to current healthcare systems, mainly because of the increased hospital admissions. Not only do the admissions themselves increase for acute diseases, but frequent visits to the outpatient clinic are also required to monitor the status of the patient. Furthermore, during the current pandemic, in-person monitoring and frequent hospital visits are not possible, as they are highly risky. Frequent testing and analysis of physiological values, as well as the electronic health records of individuals, aid in assessing the condition of heart failure patients. These health records include various readings such as heartbeats per minute, the fluid level in the lungs, the oxygen level in the blood, ejection fraction levels, etc.

Current technological advancements in electronic health record data acquisition and management allow the recording of laboratory testing reports, medical imaging data, and personal health information into an electronic format with the help of multiple sensors. These recorded health data can be further processed to prediction and monitoring of various conditions and stages of heart failure using mathematical models, data mining techniques, and machine learning methods. Numerous efforts have been taken over the last decades to improve the quality of service to patients using invasive and non-invasive remote monitoring techniques for heart failure. Invasive devices are implanted inside the patient's body. They collect physiological readings from the human body and assist the patient with or without remote communication from an external source. On the other hand, non-invasive devices include various wearables or bands that are externally attached to an individual's body and record important physiological data for predicting heart failure. Currently available non-invasive methods for heart failure monitoring include monitoring of the heartbeat using electrocardiography, monitoring blood pressure (BP) daily using BP sensor devices, bioimpedance monitoring, wearables like smartwatches, pocket devices and Fitbits, and mobile health apps for telemonitoring. Invasive devices are effective and precise but can be used for limited cases only. Non-invasive methods do not need to be implanted and can be used easily with reduced risk, precision [4], and other performance metrics. Non-invasive devices are cost-effective and safe compared to invasive devices [5].

In the literature, researchers have proposed several devices [6–9] for monitoring and predicting heart failure conditions by measuring various physiological signals including heart rate, BP, pulmonary artery pressure, electrocardiogram (ECG), etc. This chapter briefly reviews the current invasive and non-invasive devices that are commercially available for monitoring the risk and health status of heart failure patients.

### 11.2 Non-Invasive Devices for Monitoring Heart Failure Patients

Recent advancements in sensor technology have made the development of high-performance, low-cost microsensors easier. This has positively impacted the development of various non-invasive devices or external devices for assisting heart failure patients. External devices for heart failure monitoring include various wearables, such as activity bands, smartwatches, ECG patches, and vests that can estimate heart rate, BP, and pulmonary fluid levels as well as tracking the activity of the users. This section discusses various available external devices for monitoring and predicting the risk, hospital readmission, and mortality of heart failure patients.

### 11.2.1 Electrocardiography

Electrocardiography is the process of generating an ECG to detect the problems in the rhythm of the heart and, by extension, for early prediction of a heart attack as well as heart failure. The results of the study conducted by James et al. [10] showed that ECG is a simple and useful test for predicting heart failure in its early stages. The findings of the study are based on the fact that, unlike non-heart failure patients, heart failure patients have “significantly prolonged QRS duration, prolonged QT duration, prolonged QTc, and more rightward T wave axis.” Moreover, ECG features have been extensively used in the literature to detect heart failure. Several artificial intelligence technology-based heart failure prediction systems developed in the literature are based on ECG features [11]. These systems utilize traditional machine learning [12] as well as deep learning methodologies [13] for automated prediction of various heart failure conditions using patients’ ECG features.
