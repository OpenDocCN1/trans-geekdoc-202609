# 9.1.1.2 HF Classifications

The American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines (2013) have classified HF into two categories according to the measurement of the left ventricle ejection fraction (EF): HF with preserved (≥50%) ejection fraction (HFpEF) – the heart pumps blood normally but is too stiff to fill properly that leads to impaired filling of the chambers of the heart; and HF with reduced (≤40%) ejection fraction (HFrEF) – the heart is too weak to efficiently pump blood out of the left ventricle [1, 22]. Ejection fraction reflects the pump function of the heart; it represents the amount of blood being pumped from the left ventricle (the heart’s main pumping chamber) with each contraction and is expressed as a percentage. HF with mid-range or mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF) is an intermediate stage with an EF of 40–49%, that generally progresses either to HFpEF at 25% of cases or HFrEF at 33% of cases [23].

The most common classification for HF severity is the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification. The NYHA classification has been widely used for almost a century and was updated in 1994 [24]. It is a fundamental tool for stratifying the risk of HF and also determines if a patient is eligible for a clinical trial or if the type of medication and devices are appropriate [25]. Depending on the time of onset, HF can be classified as acute or chronic. Acute HF can be defined as “the rapid onset or change of symptoms and signs of HF that requires urgent medical care” [5]. Furthermore, acute HF is a condition with an unfavorable prognosis and is characterized by high mortality and rehospitalization rates [27]. Patients who have stable HF for some time, without change in clinical status, are often said to have chronic HF [5].

# 9.1.1.3 Evaluation and Diagnosis

Due to the increased incidence and the large financial costs required to diagnose HF, the diagnosis and treatment of the disease are extremely critical issues. The diagnosis of HF can be difficult, especially in the early stages and there is not a single diagnostic test for HF. Furthermore, it might be particularly difficult to recognized and associate symptoms and signs in obese patients, in elderly people, and in patients who suffer from chronic lung disease and need to be assessed at each visit meticulously [16].
