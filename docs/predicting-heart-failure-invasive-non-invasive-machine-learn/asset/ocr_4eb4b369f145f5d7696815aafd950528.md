The ultrasound device comprises an external apparatus and a slide implanted underneath the skin with piezoelectric transducers on both sides and separated by tissue, as shown in Figure 4.10. This technology depends on the generation and propagation of sound via the tissues. Transmission of energy through tissue requires an external transducer (Transmitter (Tx)) placed on the tissue surface and counteracting the ultrasonic frequency to the biosensor chip directly under the skin (Receiver (Rx)). The power source activates a piezoelectric transducer that converts electricity into ultrasonic waves. These ultrasonic waves channel energy through the tissue toward a receiver implanted inside the body. The transducer's receiver transforms ultrasonic strength into electricity. The low-loss rectifier network rectifies and filters the output voltage of power-up the biosensor chip and charging battery.

An incorrect choice of the transmission frequency can adversely affect the skin attenuation and sizes of the emitter and receiver transducers (Tx and Rx) and Rayleigh distance. Therefore, the maximum power that a transducer can provide complete at its resonant frequency ($f_r$) depends on the transducer's material and size:

```
$$f_r = \frac{C}{2d}$$ (4.1)
```

where:
C = acoustic velocity
d = transducer thickness.

According to this equation, increasing ($f_r$) minimizes the transducer volume and boosts the Rayleigh range at the cost of boost tissue absorption.
