Both wearable and implantable sensors provide continuous and remote monitoring for patients without clinical procedures [50]. Implantable biosensors are implanted in the body, while wearable biosensors are worn on the body, such as in clothes, contact lenses, watches, rings, etc. [51].

In classical biosensors, pretreatment steps are often required after sampling, and carrying the samples near the devices/sensors is also required. Although the wearable/implantable biosensors utilize the same transducers as classical biosensors such as optical, electrochemical, and thermal, miniaturization and wearability are the features that make them differ from other biosensors. These new technology biosensors offer unique advantages over other classical biosensors: real-time and continuous data acquisition, saving money and time, reducing the waste of disposable medical materials, and more efficient and quality measurements [52]. Increases and changes in patients’ and professionals’ healthcare demands have also been significant in developing this type of biosensors. Wearable/implantable biosensors, which can also be integrated with devices such as smartphones, can evaluate the data they measure and provide information to emergency services or patient relatives in critical situations (Figure 3.3) [53]. They are used in CVDs to detect heart rate, electrocardiography, and blood pressure [53].

However, some potential technical issues include biocompatibility, biofouling, errors in measurements with small volume samples, and challenges with analysis of saliva, tears, and sweat [49]. Analysis of biological fluids has always been challenging because of the complexity of the medium. Clinical tests based on blood samples are reliable but involve invasive strategies that can be uncomfortable for patients. Saliva, tears, and sweat are examined as non- or minimally invasive biosensor options for diagnostics. Problems such as contamination of saliva samples with foods and the presence of several metabolites with concentration correlation issues – although they can be detected in sweat samples – demonstrate the improvement requirements for wearable sensors [49].

## 3.4 Applications of Biosensors for Cardiovascular Research

In the last decade, various electrochemical studies investigated the biodetection of biomarkers related to CVDs. Some of these studies examined the biosensor, nanomaterial, molecularly imprinted polymers, and aptamer-based approaches, while others focused on methods of detecting cardiac biomarkers. Reviewing recently published studies on different aspects of electrochemical methods, there is considerable research on the application of electrochemical techniques to detect cardiovascular biomarkers. Table 3.2 provides extensive information on electrochemical biosensing of cardiac biomarkers and diverse electrode modification techniques.

Chi and co-workers have developed a label-free photoelectrochemical (PEC) immunosensor using a CdS:Mn sensitized graphene (G)/Cu2MoS4 composite detection probe for the quantification of cTnI. The superior sensitivity of the immunosensor and the large electrochemical behavior of G/Cu2MoS4 allowed cTnI to be determined effectively. Consequently, cTnI was found at a concentration of 0.18 pg mL⁻¹ in phosphate buffer conditions [60]. Nandini et al. also reported an electrochemical biosensor based on nanohybrid containing functionalized graphene oxide modified graphite electrode for cholesterol biosensing. The proposed Gr/GO-SH/AuNs/CHOx shows a detection limit of 0.2 nM with a linear range from 0.05 mM to 11.45 mM. Conclusively, the proposed sensor has been effectively applied for the determination of cholesterol in real samples [93]. In another study, Tertis et al. presented a sensitive and selective multipulse amperometric sensor for the determination of interleukin 6 in human serum based on AuNPs and PPy-NPs. The linear concentration range for interleukin 6 was from 1.0 pg mL⁻¹ to 15 μg mL⁻¹ with the detection limit (S/N = 3) of 0.33 pg mL⁻¹ at the optimized conditions (0.1 M Tris–HCl, pH 7.2) [122]. In the electrochemical aptasensor suggested by Taghdisi et al., the detection was based on the Y-shaped structure of DApt-CS conjugate using screen-printed gold electrodes (SPGEs). Differential pulse voltammetry displayed that the aptasensor is able to find out 27 pM of myoglobin in a linear range of 100 pM-40 nM. The results demonstrated that the DApt-CS-modified electrodes show excellent selectivity, accuracy and are very selective in myoglobin determination [141]. These comb structured gold microelectrode arrays (CSGMs) were characterized using electrochemical techniques by Kongsuphol et al. The resulting biosensor was used for the detection of TNF-α under optimum experimental parameters. TNF-α was measured and allowed the LOD of 1.0 pg mL⁻¹ by using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). Finally, this CSGM/Magnetic beads/Ab sensor can achieve a dynamic range between 1 and 1000 pg mL⁻¹ from non-diluted serum samples [155].

Karimi and co-workers have designed a biomimetic nanomolecularly imprinted polymer for the sensitive detection of cTnT via a graphene screen-printed electrode. The cavities created to detect the target molecule were produced by electropolymerization of a conductive aniline and carboxylated aniline co-polymer matrix on the graphene oxide (GO) electrode by cyclic voltammetry.
