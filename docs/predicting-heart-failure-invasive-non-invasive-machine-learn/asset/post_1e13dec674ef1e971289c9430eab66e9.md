## 13.4 Integrating Wearables Technology into the Healthcare System

Different commercially available wearable devices can monitor steps, heart rate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, and electrocardiogram (ECG) signals, which can help the clinician in decision-making and clinical intervention. Wearable devices typically, however, focus on predicting HF or cardiac arrhythmia with the help of clinicians. There is a limited number of devices available that can be used to automatically detect a clinical abnormality of the patient reliably without human intervention. Most of such devices are implantable devices; however, these devices are very expensive and only a few of them are available on the market. Therefore, a safe, reliable, and effective automatic system like a wearable device to keep track of patients' cardiac health is very important. ### 13.4.1 Activity Monitors

The most popular form of activity monitoring devices available on the market are a ring, wristband, wristwatch, chest strap, and smart insole or shoe. These devices use three degrees of freedom accelerometers to identify the activity type of the user. They are suitable for activity monitoring and fall detection, but the type of movement significantly affects the accuracy of these devices, particularly at lower motion activities. Wearable devices, although initially designed for activity monitoring, can now be utilized to monitor several other body parameters and every day the list of parameters increases as do their accuracy and reliability. However, these devices are not yet directly used for HF identification.

### 13.4.2 Heart Rate Monitors

Most of the wearable devices utilize an optical heart rate monitor using the PPG technique, which uses the incident of light of particular wavelengths entering the skin, then measuring the perfusion of blood in the dermis and subcutaneous tissue by capturing the different amounts of light refracted by varying volumes of blood flow occurring due to heart pumps (Figure 13.6). It uses a light-emitting diode (LED) to illuminate a capillary bed and thus monitor pulsatile changes in light absorption by a photodiode, which is similar to a pulse oximeter [13]. It was reported recently that the accuracy of heart rate monitors from wearable PPG devices depends on the type of activity being performed and the heart rate range of the user.

The signal acquired using the PPG techniques can be used to generate metrics about heart rate, blood pressure, breathing rate, and several other usable metrics, as shown in Figure 13.7.

### 13.4.3 Wearable Devices for HF Prognostic Application

It was reported from a study of 50 patients with HF that the step-counts for two weeks can significantly differentiate New York Heart Association classes [39]. This cohort used accelerometer data to monitor the physical activity during HF as a prognostic measure. Another retrospective study [40] on 189 patients with self-reported HF showed that physical activity has a significant relation with HF-related mortality. This study was also carried out using accelerometer data. A similar prospective study was done on 170 Japanese HF patients, which showed that activity data acquired through the wrist-worn devices were associated with mortality; in both the studies randomized controlled clinical trials and physical activity monitoring using the smartwatches were used for epidemiological investigation (e.g. increased aerobic capacity and better health-related quality of life) [41]. Regular, daily physical activity can lower the risk of heart disease as, primarily, it can help to control weight and reduce the chances of developing other straining heart conditions such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and type 2 diabetes [42, 43]. Although it can be summarized from these studies that wearing devices for HF prognostic application has a strong relationship with reducing HF-related mortality, to the best of our knowledge, there is no clinical HF-related service available yet in the world to routinely use the wearables to assess the patient activity.

### 13.4.4 Monitoring Blood Pressure

Hypertension is one of the major causes of HF. Clinical therapy has mostly been offered based on a single assessment in the clinic or in home visit by the clinicians. In general, wearable devices still lack reliability in measuring blood pressure; however, the Omron™ HeartGuide (Figure 13.8) has met the American National Standards Institute criteria for measuring blood pressure and such wearable devices can be used to monitor potential HF patients and also the implication of therapeutic treatment on blood pressure.
