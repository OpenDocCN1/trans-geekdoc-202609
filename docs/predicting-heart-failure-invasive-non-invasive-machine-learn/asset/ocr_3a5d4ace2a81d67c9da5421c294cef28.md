In the literature, alternative non-invasive modalities are suggested to monitor HF patients and diagnose the critical HF stages which require hospitalization. This way, unnecessary hospitalizations can be prevented by remotely monitoring the patient using non-invasive and minimally invasive sensors [3]. The efficiency of remote monitoring is still open to debate, but many studies have indicated a reduced rehospitalization rate and increased life quality for patients with external monitoring [3]. For this purpose, external devices or wearables with embedded non-invasive sensors are employed. By retrieving data from the non-invasive sensors, the health status of an HF patient is followed, and clinical therapy is offered in emergency cases, which are identified by evaluating the abnormal changes in measured data. This method is also known as home monitoring or remote patient monitoring [3].

In this chapter, we explain the non-invasive and minimally invasive sensor technologies and techniques employed for HF diagnosis. We also summarize landmark studies and clinical trials which prove the potential of non-invasive monitoring of HF patients.

# 5.2 Methods

The methods for identifying worsening HF can be listed as body weight measurements, electrocardiography (ECG), bioimpedance monitoring, activity tracking, implanted pressure sensors, lung ultrasound monitoring, measurements with sound and Doppler sensors, seismocardiography, ballistocardiography, photoplethysmography, and measurement of natriuretic peptides levels in circulating blood. These methods are explained in detail in this chapter.

# 5.2.1 Body Weight Measurement

The state of worsening HF is characterized by excessive reduction in circulatory volume and body water retention [3, 13]. Depending on the change in water balance in the body, a sudden increase in weight is observed in an emergency case. If the increase rate in body weight is higher than 2 kg/48 hours, it can be evaluated as a deterioration due to the impending HF decompensation [3].

Many remote monitoring studies used body weight measurements as a rehospitalization indicator for HF patients [14–24]. Weight measurements can be easily performed on a daily basis using weight scales or shoe-integrated pressure sensors. However, pressure sensors used in shoes can lead to inaccuracy in long-term monitoring [25] as there are several factors reducing the accuracy of measurements, such as different levels of activity in daily life and individual-dependent posture and gait disturbances. Alternatively, weight measurements can be performed daily by patients using bathroom scales as a part of HF self-management [26, 27].
