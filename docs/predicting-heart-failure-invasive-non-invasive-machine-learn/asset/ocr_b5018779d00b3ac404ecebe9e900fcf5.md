# 6.2.2.2.1 Examples of Unsupervised Learning in Cardiology

Unsupervised learning is often combined with more advanced techniques, such as deep learning, when applied to large, complex data sets in cardiology. One example is a comprehensive unsupervised ML algorithm which was able to successfully differentiate heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) from healthy subjects by analyzing LV long axis myocardial velocity patterns on stress echocardiography [14]. This study built an unsupervised ML algorithm from 156 patients using 22 descriptors with 300 features collected from tissue Doppler-derived longitudinal myocardial velocity traces at rest and during exercise [14]. The algorithm found naturally occurring patterns and relationships within the details to identify a continuum of disease from normal cardiac function to patients with HFpEF [14]. The spectrum varied in age, body mass index, left ventricular mass index, and left ventricular filling pressures and symptom burden as measured by the 6-minute walk test [14].

Clustering-based algorithms are the more common examples of unsupervised learning in cardiology. One example is an unsupervised ML algorithm used to identify distinct hypertensive subgroups associated with the myocardial substrate for HFpEF [21]. The ML algorithm clustered 1273 patients into two mutually exclusive pheno-groups, based on 47 variables including clinical characteristics, cardiac structure, and function [21]. These subgroups may represent different genotypes that could benefit from targeted therapies to prevent HFpEF onset [21]. Another example is in type 2 diabetes mellitus where an unsupervised ML algorithm was applied to the echocardiographic images of 842 patients from two prospective cohorts to identify three clusters of patients from 12 echocardiographic variables [22]. Cluster 1 patients had the lowest LV mass index, the highest LV ejection fraction (LVEF), and were predominantly male; Cluster 2 patients were the oldest and had the highest strain and E/e' ratios; Cluster 3 patients had the highest LV mass index and the lowest LVEF [22]. A cluster analysis was then performed which found clusters 2 and 3 were associated with worse cardiovascular mortality and hospitalizations compared with cluster 1 (HR 2.37) [22]. Hierarchical clustering algorithms have also been studied to identify distinct clusters in diastolic heart failure. One study performed a cluster analysis and developed an ML model of LV diastolic dysfunction from 130 patients that was subsequently tested in a prospective cohort of 44 patients [23]. Speckle-tracking echocardiography (STE) was used to measure simultaneous changes in left atrial and ventricular volumes, longitudinal strains, and strain rates [23]. STE strongly correlated with conventional indices and clustered patients into three groups with differing severities of diastolic dysfunction and LV filling pressures [23]. Another example is a study that identified two distinct groups using an unsupervised, hierarchical cluster analysis of echocardiographic parameters of diastolic dysfunction in 866 patients [24]. Compared with conventional classification, the clustering algorithm improved prediction of all-cause and cardiac mortality [24]. Similarly, other studies have used unsupervised clustering-based ML algorithms to identify differences in clinical, structural, and invasive hemodynamics, which have been correlated with hospitalizations and with all-cause and cardiac mortality [3, 25].
