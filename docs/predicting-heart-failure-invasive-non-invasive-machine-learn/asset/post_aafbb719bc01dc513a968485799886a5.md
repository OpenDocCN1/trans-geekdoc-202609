
It is clear from Table 11.2 that a significant number of studies have utilized physiological data from multiple non-invasive devices for remote monitoring of heart failure patients. Except for Gingele et al. [6] and Ong et al. [45], it is observed in the studies that utilization of multiple devices for telemonitoring has generated positive outcomes for improving the health of heart failure patients, including reducing the hospital readmission rate, reducing the mortality rate, and improving the lifestyle of the patients, etc. Gingele et al. [6] utilized ECG along with a body weight scale and BP monitor for recording the physiological values, while Ong et al. [45] utilized a pulse meter along with ECG. However, neither study observed any positive effects in the subjects after monitoring. On the other hand, the study conducted by Inglis et al. [44] utilized the same combination of invasive devices as Ong et al. [45] for recording the physiological readings, but the observed results of Inglis et al.'s study indicated the telemonitoring system having positively impacted the lifestyle of heart failure patients and reduced hospital admission rate. Galiner et al. [41] examined the effectiveness of a telemonitoring approach along with a therapeutic coaching program for monitoring heart failure patients. All of the subjects were provided with a body weight scale and a questionnaire consisting of eight questions related to the systems. The body weight, as well as answers to the questionnaire, were uploaded to a server for automatic assessment. However, after 3 years, it was observed that the telemonitoring method that was implemented had not lowered the rate of hospitalization or death.

### 11.2.7 Role of Mobile Health Apps in Heart Failure Self-Care

According to the statistics provided by Forbes in 2017 [46], there exist more than 50,000 mobile health (mHealth) apps on the market that are focused on ameliorating the mental and physical fitness of individuals. Some of the available apps have been developed for general well-being while the rest are for specific purposes such as maternal care, child-care, chronic disease management, etc. Numerous studies have analyzed the role of mHealth apps for self-care in heart failure patients [47–49]. Among these, a few mHealth apps are focused on educating heart failure patients. Most of the mHealth apps that focus on therapeutic education rely on text messaging. Multiple automated messages are sent to patients to educate them on the diet to be followed, symptoms, and how to differentiate these symptoms from other diseases which have similar symptoms. Furthermore, the apps provide questionnaires that are related to heart failure symptoms, which the patients must complete. Some of the apps have integrated the physiological values from home-based devices such as body weight scales and BP monitors to provide additional care to heart failure patients. The observed results of the studies have shown that mHealth apps have improved the knowledge about heart failure and its associated issues in heart failure patients as well as increasing their involvement in self-care. However, most of the mHealth apps in the literature have conducted only a pilot study in a few subjects to analyze the usability and effects of self-care.

There are several commercially available mHealth apps for heart failure self-care. The majority of these are available free in popular application stores such as Google’s Play Store and Apple’s AppStore. A systematic app search was conducted in Wali et al. [50] to examine various commercially available heart failure apps. They found that more than 70 apps were currently available in the market for heart failure self-care. Among these, only about 25 apps have sufficient features to help heart failure patients. Moreover, more than half of the available apps did not even have a basic weight management feature, which is one of the fundamental attributes considered necessary for evaluating the health of heart failure patients. According to the study, existing mHealth apps lack patient-centered design and require advanced features based on the user’s need to guarantee the usability of the app. Among the commercially available apps, a few have been introduced by scientific societies such as the American Heart Association, Swiss Federation of Cardiology, Heart Failure Society of America, etc., but the rest have been developed by private technology companies. The app search conducted by Wali et al. [50] found that the app Heart Failure Storylines, developed by the Heart Failure Society of America, has better functionalities than other commercially available apps. The latest analysis of commercially available heart failure apps conducted by Mortara et al. [51] also rated the Heart Failure Storylines app as the better one for heart failure patients along with the HF Path app introduced by American Heart Association.

### 11.3.1 RV Pressure Monitoring

RV pressure sensors are used in measuring the diastolic and systolic pressure of the right ventricle. During the opening of the pulmonary valve, the diastolic pressure of the PA is measured, which is helpful in calculating the left ventricle diastolic pressure and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, and thereby arriving at the intracardiac filling pressures. A strong relationship between the RV pressure and the PA pressure is observed during invasive catheterization [52–54]. Recording and reporting of ventricular pressure are performed at end-systole and end-diastole. The normal range of the RV systolic pressure is 25–30 mmHg and the RV end-diastolic pressure is 5–7 mmHg. The left ventricle systolic pressure is normally equal to the systolic arterial pressure and the left ventricular end-diastolic pressure is less than 10 mmHg. With the increase in age, the left ventricle systolic pressure also increases [55].

With these findings as the base, the Chronicle implantable hemodynamic monitor (Chronicle IHM) was developed by Medtronic, Inc., Minneapolis, Minnesota. The Chronicle IHM consists of two parts – a device that is placed in the pectoral area like a pacemaker, and a pressure sensor on the tip (like a transvenous lead) placed in the RV outflow tract or septum. This device is not compatible with MRI scanning, cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), or atrial pacing devices, which is a disadvantage in using this device.

A study on 32 heart failure patients was initially undertaken and the results showed a decrease of 57% in hospital admissions. From this result, a clinical trial – the COMPASS-HF (Chronicle Offers Management to Patients with Advanced Signs and Symptoms of Heart Failure) – was conducted on advanced heart failure patients from the New York Heart Association (NYHA) to study the effectiveness of the Chronicle IHM sensors [56]. Varied subjects were selected for the study (274 patients) and were divided into Chronicle and control groups. The trial did not show significant result changes and it was concluded that the Chronicle IHM might add value in addition to the existing management routine.
