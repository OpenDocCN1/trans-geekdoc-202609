## 3.3 Types of Biosensors for Cardiovascular Biomarkers Detection

### 3.3.1 Electrochemical Biosensors

The basic principle of electrochemical biosensors is based on the generated electrical signal resulting from the electrochemical redox reaction between the biorecognition element and analyte [36]. The analyte concentration is directly proportional to the measured electrochemical response, which allows the quantitative analysis [37]. Three electrode electrochemical cell systems can use various transducers based on voltammetric, potentiometric, conductometric, and impedimetric techniques [38].

Electrochemical biosensors are label-free, affordable, easily applicable, and highly sensitive with a low limit of detection (LOD) value, and offer accurate, rapid, and robust analysis [39, 40]. They are also easy to miniaturize and apply to devices that use small sample volumes to detect more than one analyte simultaneously [37, 40]. Electrochemical biosensors include the following:

- Amperometry-based electrochemical biosensors measure the current flow changes at a constant potential, and they are the most widely used biosensors [36, 38]. They are suitable for mass production, and provide more sensitive analysis than potentiometry-based biosensors [38].
- Voltammetry-based electrochemical biosensors determine the current change as a function of the applied potential. They are highly specific, sensitive sensors with noise-free and reproducible measurements [37, 41].
- Potentiometry-based electrochemical biosensors’ measurement principle is based on the potential difference at a zero current flow [38]. They are used for the determination of both ions and biological molecules [42].
- Impedance-based electrochemical biosensors focus on the changes of resistance and capacitance generated by various biomolecular interactions between proteins or antigen and antibody etc. [36, 42].
- Conductometry-based electrochemical biosensors focus on electrical conductivity changes, but are not widely preferred for enzymatic analysis [36, 38].

### 3.3.2 Piezoelectric Biosensors

Piezoelectricity is the ability of some materials to produce an electric potential, charge, or field after mechanical stress [38, 43]. Crystals and certain ceramics, such as bone, quartz, lithium niobate, polylactic acids, zinc oxide, aluminum nitride and phosphate, and polyvinylidene fluoride, are examples of the most common piezoelectric materials [41, 43, 44]. In a piezoelectric biosensor, biorecognition material is immobilized on the piezoelectric surface that consists of a gold electrode coated quartz crystal. When an electric potential is applied due to the interaction between analyte and biorecognition material, an elastic deformation and mass change occur, resulting in a mechanical vibration effect [38, 42, 43]. The frequency of oscillation and crystal mass are related, and increased mass due to analyte absorption can cause a change in the oscillation frequency [38]. Quantitative determination with piezoelectric biosensors is based on the ratio between analyte amount and oscillation frequency [44].

Piezoelectric biosensors are advantageous because they offer easy, rapid, label-free, and low-cost analysis [41]. Nonetheless, issues about the regeneration of crystal surface, incubation time, and multiple immobilization steps may cause setbacks [41]. Piezoelectric biosensors are used to determine a wide variety of materials, such as drugs, pesticides, nucleic acids, bacteria, proteins, pesticides, etc. [45].

### 3.3.3 Thermal/Thermometric Biosensors

Thermal biosensors are not very commonly used biosensors. In thermal biosensors, a reaction that results in heat production or absorption occurs between the biorecognition element and the analyte [42]. A thermistor is used for the measurement of temperature changes. It is highly sensitive and can detect changes between 0.0001 and 0.05 °C [38]. Thermal biosensors are able to measure concentrations as low as 10^-5 M without frequent recalibration [36, 38]. However, they do not show sensitivity for electrochemical and optical characteristics of the analyte [38].

### 3.3.4 Optical Biosensors

Optical biosensors are biosensors whose transduction principle is based on optical processes. The interaction between analyte and biorecognition elements is changed into an output signal by optical transducers [46]. The measurement of the change in optical characteristics of materials such as polarization, amplitude, frequency, and radiation phase is used for quantitative determination [47]. Changes in the optical characteristics resulting from the molecular interactions between biological elements affect the measured output signal. For example, luminescent biosensors focus on the changes of luminescence related to the produced radiation due to the biomolecular interaction [47]. Optical biosensors can be either label-free or fluorescent-based. In fluorescent-based biosensors, the labeling of biomarkers with fluorescent tags provides a highly sensitive analysis. On the other hand, fluorescent signal bias is considered an issue [44]. Label-free biosensors are capable of rapid and real-time analysis with an ultra-small sample volume [42, 44].

Optical biosensors can employ different techniques such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR), surface plasmon resonance imaging (SPRi), localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS), laser light scattering, reflection spectroscopy, and chemiluminescence, etc. [38, 47]. All of them have their advantages and disadvantages. Which is preferred is based on the analyte and characteristics.

Nevertheless, SPR is considered the most effective and most preferred technique which is affordable and highly selective with the ability to detect various interactions [37, 48].

Optical biosensors offer significant advantages over other biosensors: in addition to being highly selective, they are sensitive and biocompatible, provide a minimally invasive method for in vivo applications, and overcome the electromagnetic interference issue [42].
