

## 6 Artificial Intelligence Techniques in Cardiology

An Overview

Ikram-Ul Haq and Bo Xu

### 6.1 Introduction

The unique marvel of human intelligence lies in its innate ability to reason, meta-reason, and seamlessly synthesize abstract concepts, distinct from the binary logic of computer science. Artificial intelligence (AI) is a rapidly evolving transdisciplinary field. It integrates computer science, statistics, psychology, neuroscience, material science, mechanical engineering, and computer hardware design to develop algorithms that aim to simulate human intuition, decision-making, and object recognition [1]. The concepts and methodologies underpinning AI have been well-established for some time, but recent technological advancements that support processing large volumes of data have driven a surge in interest in AI [2]. Machine learning (ML) refers to a collection of techniques which enable AI.

The overarching objectives of AI in medicine are threefold: to optimize patient care, to improve efficiency, and to improve clinical outcomes. More specifically, there are growing applications in the fields of basic science drug discovery, genetic-based treatments, radiology, and surgical specialties [1, 2]. In cardiology, there has been a growth in the potential sources of new patient data, as well as advances in investigations and therapies, which position the field well to uniquely benefit from AI: for clinicians, these benefits potentially translate into improving the accuracy of diagnosis, risk assessment, prognostication, and decision-making for treatments; for health systems, these benefits potentially translate into improved workflows and technical protocols, reducing medical errors and costs; for patients, these benefits potentially translate into promoting optimal prevention and treatment of cardiovascular disease.

This chapter aims to introduce the various AI techniques and their respective applications in cardiovascular medicine, to introduce the steps involved in building AI algorithms, as well as to discuss the current limitations and pitfalls.

### 6.2 Artificial Intelligence Techniques

#### 6.2.1 Artificial Intelligence and Statistical Models

AI can be understood as an extension of statistical modeling, with the differences related less to methodologies and more to the respective objectives and the questions they aim to answer (Figure 6.1). Statistics is the mathematical study of data, and statistical models use data to infer relationships and extrapolate values [1]. However, statistical models emphasize understanding the relationships between data points rather than extrapolating values [2]. In comparison, the primary goal of AI is to predict future values using existing data, with less emphasis being placed on understanding the relationships between variables [3]. However, there are exceptions to this rule with some ML techniques, such as LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) regression which allow relationships between data points to be analyzed.

At the most superficial level, AI can be divided into general and applied (or narrow) AI (Figure 6.2) [4]. General AI refers to the ability of an algorithmic system to process, learn, and solve previously unseen problems; this is yet to be achieved. Applied or narrow AI is more common and refers to algorithmic processes being developed for specific and specialized purposes [2].

#### 6.2.2 Machine Learning

ML techniques vary in their objectives, applications, and architectures (Table 6.1). The type, structure, and depth of input data influence which algorithm is most appropriate in answering a research question. Some algorithms, such as supervised learning algorithms are better suited for analyzing structured data over languages, and others, including deep learning, are more suited to images with spatial resolution [5]. In brief, ML is an iterative process that involves approximating complex mathematical relationships between inputs and outputs by learning from a large data set to predict values from new, unseen data [6]. Supervised, unsupervised, semi-supervised, reinforcement, and deep learning are terms describing how ML algorithms learn from data.

##### 6.2.2.1 Supervised Learning

Supervised learning refers to algorithms learning rules between a set of inputs and outputs using a labeled training data set (referred to as mapping) in order to predict outcomes for new inputs. The labeled training data set typically consists of predictor variables, features, and labeled outcomes (Table 6.1). Features refer to pieces of information that make up data and the quality, accuracy, and richness of features determine the overall effectiveness of supervised ML algorithms [7].

The aim in training a supervised ML algorithm is to optimize its generalizability such that the model defines the underlying relationship but not to the extent of introducing inadvertent bias from poorly curated data sets [1]. Hence, a variance–bias trade-off exists where variance refers to how well the model performs with new, subtly different data, and bias refers to how well the model fits to the training data [7]. Overfitting refers to a model with a high bias and a low variance: it is overfitted to the training data set such that it struggles to adapt to new, subtly different data. Obtaining large sets of reliably labeled data is often the hardest and most expensive aspect of creating supervised ML algorithms. It is important that training is monitored to ensure an optimal balance of bias and variance [7]. The most common tasks performed by supervised models include classification and regression.

###### 6.2.2.1.1 Classification

Classification is used when the output of an ML algorithm is a categorical variable and involves classifying data points into one or more categories. Linear separability is a key concept underpinning this approach and refers to the notion of using a line or a plane to separate data (Figure 6.3). The line or plane separating data into classes is referred to as a decision boundary, and the area chosen to define a class is referred to as a decision surface (Figure 6.3). If a data point falls within a decision surface, it is assigned to that class.

Support vector machines (SVMs) are commonly used technique in supervised classification ML algorithms and use hyperplanes to separate data points. An optimal hyperplane separates classes in a way that creates maximum separation between the different classes. SVMs are relatively straightforward but are less useful in analyzing more complex, multidimensional data. Instead, kernel methods are typically used for complex multidimensional data where samples cannot be linearly separated.

###### 6.2.2.1.3 Examples of Supervised Learning in Cardiology

There are a growing number of studies applying supervised ML algorithms to cardiology, including in personalized medicine, imaging, and disease prognostication. Personalized medicine involves tailoring investigations, treatments, and predictions to individual patients, thereby minimizing reductionism. Reductionism refers to treating all patients with the same disease states identically, irrespective of their genetics, lifestyle, background, and determinants of cardiovascular health [6]. One aspect of precision medicine involves creating a digital twin of the patient which integrates genomics, wearable devices, electronic health records, imaging data, and other data points to create a simulated model of the patient that is updated in real time through statistical and mechanistic modeling [2]. Traditional cardiovascular risk models extrapolate risk from a sample population, inevitably ignoring subtle clinical confounders. In contrast, AI algorithms can personalize risk calculations by including complex and subtle clinical factors.

One example is a study using supervised ML algorithms to predict 5-year all-cause mortality (ACM) in patients with supervised coronary artery disease (CAD) using cardiac computed tomographic angiography (CCTA) images [8]. Included in the development of this algorithm were 25 clinical and 44 CCTA parameters, and the algorithm was found to be superior to the Framingham risk score (FRS), segment stenosis score (SSS), segment involvement score (SIS), and the modified Duke index (MDI) in predicting 5-year ACM (ML: 0.79 vs. FRS: 0.61, SSS: 0.64, SIS: 0.64, DI: 0.62; p < 0.001) [8]. Another example is a study that combined CCTA stenosis and plaque measurements to develop a supervised ML algorithm to predict myocardial flow reserve (MFR) [9]. In the study, 153 arteries in 51 patients undergoing CCTA were analyzed to build this composite risk score and the algorithm was found to be superior to quantitative CCTA stenosis in predicting MFR by 13N-ammonia PET scan (ML AUC = 0.83, CTA stenosis = 0.66; p = 0.005) [9]. A similar multicenter trial evaluated a boosted ensemble ML algorithm built from quantitative CCTA measurements to predict lesion-specific ischemia [10]. In the trial, 254 patients underwent CCTA and invasive coronary angiography with fractional flow reserve (FFR) measurement in 484 vessels [10]. The ML algorithm had a superior area under the curve (AUC) of 0.84 compared with individual CCTA measurements (AUC: 0.76; p < 0.006) [10].

Supervised ML algorithms have also been applied to other cardiac imaging modalities. A recent study used a combination of supervised and deep learning to develop an ML algorithm using 91,232 single-lead ECGs from 53,549 patients to classify a single ambulatory electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring lead into 12 rhythm classes with an AUC of 0.97, when tested in an independent data set with a consensus committee of board-certified cardiologists [11]. In echocardiography, supervised ML algorithms have been developed to identify and classify different echo views, including one study successfully classifying 15 standard views with an accuracy of 91.7% after training the supervised ML algorithm on 267 transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) studies [12].
