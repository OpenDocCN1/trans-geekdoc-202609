The increase of lung tissue density causes a change from the dominant A-line to diffuse B-lines in what is known as sliding [86]. B-lines, previously known as comet tails or lung comets, are defined as discrete laser-like vertical lines that arise from the bottom of screen and move synchronously with lung sliding [81, 87]. Lung B-lines are not exclusive to HF symptoms, and can be seen in many other conditions such as pulmonary edema [88], pneumonia [89], systemic sclerosis [90], acute lung injury [91], and other respiratory diseases [92].

There are multiple factors that affect the quality and quantity of B-lines appearances in LUS images, such as the position of the patients, the equipment utilized, clip-length, probe selection, the experience of the practitioner, and the number of zones [93]. The current settings to examine designated thoracic zones can be as low as two up to 36 zones. The optimum recommendation is eight zones.

The visualization of B-lines is possible with cardiac, convex, or linear probes [94]. There are two main scanning approaches in LUS, but either way the probe has to be placed in intercostal spaces. The first approach is the longitudinal scan, while the second is the transverse (oblique) scan. The ribs and their shadows are a part of the image in the longitudinal scan. On the other hand, the transversal approach allows the practitioner to visualize a wider view of the pleural lines uninterrupted by rib shadows [95]. The most important reference point is the pleural line with its lung sliding as it would be impossible to obtain any information about lung aeration without clearly visualizing it. Patients should be positioned either sitting upright, semi-recumbent or supine. In addition, patients should maintain the same position if serial examinations are being performed as the B-lines appearances in the imaging are greatly influenced by the patient's position [96, 97]. The length of the imaging clip is optimally 6 seconds, if the patient is breathing normally [98].

In HF diagnosis and monitoring, the B-lines should be diffuse, bilateral, and multiple. Diffuse means that the lines should be adjacent in two areas, bilateral means that they should be visible in the left and right hemithorax, and multiple means that they should be in abundance [95]. There are many quantification systems that have been developed to classify and assess B-lines numbers in order to identify the level of risk that a patient is at during and after hospital discharge. The methods can be categorized as:
- score methods
- count-based methods

Score methods often rely on a minimum number of B-lines in one intercostal space, labeling it as a positive zone and adding up the number of positive zones. In addition, lines can be counted one by one, or upon confluence, or as percentage, cumulatively known as count-based methods [99]. These quantification systems can be important in predicting mortality and hospitalization of worsening HF, and are a prognostic marker for HF as shown by many studies [100–108].

There are many protocols for LUS for diagnosis and prognosis monitoring of HF. The most famous protocols being used are:
- bedside lung ultrasound in emergency (BLUE) [109]
- fluid administration limited by lung sonography (FALLS-protocol) [110]
- cardiac arrest ultrasound exam (C.A.U.S.E.) [84]
- lung and cardiac ultrasound (LuCUS) [111]

LUS is a powerful and sensitive technique for the prediction and prognosis monitoring of HF. It is easy-to-apply, widely available, and has a short learning curve [112]. Although LUS is a powerful tool for pulmonary and cardiology imaging, there are some limitations, including the dependence on the skill of the practitioner, the quality and the setting of the hardware [113], and the lack of specificity as B-lines can be present in many other conditions. Nevertheless, the future is open for LUS to be further developed and specific with the integration of new algorithms and reliability on deep learning methods [114].

## 5.2.7 Sound Sensors

The flow of blood in and out of the heart and the movement of the structures responsible for controlling this flow allow the heart to make sounds [115]. There are four types of heart sounds:
- primary heart sounds in healthy adults, including the first heart sound (S1) and the second heart sound (S2) [115–118]
- extra heart sounds including gallop rhythms of the third heart sound (S3) and fourth heart sound (S4) [119–123]
- heart murmurs, which are present in congenital abnormalities [124–128]
- other abnormal sounds such as adventitious sounds, click [129, 130], rubs [131], snaps [132], knocks [133], and plops [134–136]

Before the invention of the stethoscope in 1816 by Laënnec, listening to the sounds of heart was not possible without physicians applying their ears to the chest of the patient [137]. Stethoscopes enabled listening to and interpreting heart sounds for diagnosis. The contribution of scientists and introduction of physical concepts and technological additions have allowed us to arrive at sound sensors for the classification of heart sounds.

The normal sounds in healthy adults are S1 and S2 [138]. The presence of other sounds could be an indicator for cardiovascular problems. For instance, the presence of S3 sound could be an indicator for pathological problems such as anemia, thyrotoxicosis, mitral regurgitation, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, aortic and tricuspid regurgitation, and LV dysfunction [135]. The S4 sound can result from hypertension, aortic stenosis and regurgitation, severe mitral regurgitation, cardiomyopathy, and ischemic heart disease [139]. The ability to distinguish the different heart sounds is uniform across all practitioners, especially as it is difficult to distinguish some sounds, like the S3 sound, which is crucial for HF diagnosis. Therefore, it became important to move toward the automatic classification of heart sound signals in order to achieve better diagnosis, through signals detected with, for example, stethoscopes, electret microphones or piezoelectric acoustic sensors [140–142].

Over the past few decades, multiple sensors have emerged for better detection of heart sounds based on phonocardiography and echocardiography. The sensors focus on improving signal processing and noise reduction. In 2004, Reed et al. developed a model for the production of heart sounds through a prototype based on wavelet decomposition of sounds and a neural network-based classifier that could detect abnormal heart sounds, which was very useful in predicting HF [143]. In another work, a chest-worn accelerometer with high sensitivity for cardiorespiratory sound was based on an asymmetrical gapped cantilever, which is composed of a bottom mechanical layer and a top piezoelectric layer separated by a gap with a built-in amplifier to amplify the output of the voltage sensor [144]. Mubarak et al. published their work centered on improving the quality of phonocardiogram (PCG) signals and the removal of noisy and corrupted signals, which allowed them to accurately identify and localize S1 and S2 sounds based on homomorphic filtering using support vector machine [145]. Gupta et al. utilized commercially available Audicor (Inovise Medical, Oregon, USA) devices that use 10 second PCG to detect S3 sounds. They used a proprietary algorithm and the S3 intensity is graded on a scale of 0–10. S3 sound is considered present when its intensity is greater than 5. They were able to reproduce the data upon replacing the old sensors with new pairs of sensors [146]. In their work, Chen et al. used a deep neural network (DNN) to recognize S1 and S2 sounds based only on acoustical characters through converting signals first into a sequence of Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs). The K-means algorithm is applied to cluster MFCC features into two groups to refine their representation and discriminative capability. The refined features are then fed to a DNN classifier to perform S1 and S2 recognition [147]. In a non-invasive pilot study on HF patients with audible S3, Siejeko et al. studied the feasibility of heart sound measurements from an accelerometer within an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) pulse generator. In their findings S1, S2, and S3 vibrations were detected in all accelerometer locations including 16 subjects. However, S3 was not audible because when the signal bandwidth was increased, a substantial portion of S2 was infrasonic (below 20 Hz). In their conclusions they found that heart sounds, including S3 amplitude and heart sound time intervals, can be measured using pulse generator embedded circuitry at implant sites without special-purpose leads [148].

## 5.2.8 Seismocardiography

Seismocardiography (SCG) is a non-invasive method based on measuring the vibration on the chest in response to the movement of heart and blood in the arterial tree [149]. Vibration measurements can be performed using a MEMS-based 3-axis accelerometer placed on the chest, particularly around the mid-sternum [7, 150]. Since SCG measurements are not confounded by body fluid, there is no need to use multiple sensors. The waveforms of SCG signals consist of parts that correspond to the opening and closure states of the aortic valve, and the ejection of blood from the left ventricle to the aorta. The hypothesis of SCG studies depends on the similarity of signals for HF patients before and after physical exercise [7], because HF patients have less reserve to increase cardiac performance during exercise. In other words, SCG signals of HF patients are similar during exercise and rest conditions. On the other hand, healthy individuals have relatively altered signal amplitudes during exercise and rest. By comparing the recorded signals during rest and exercise, the HF severity of the patient can be predicted in a non-invasive way. Recorded time signals are converted into frequency domain for detailed investigation. During exercise, high-frequency components in SCG waves are more visible in healthy individuals compared with the SCG signals of HF patients [7].
