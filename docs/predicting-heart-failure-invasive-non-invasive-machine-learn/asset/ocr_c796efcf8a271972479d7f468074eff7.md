# 13.4.6.1 Practical Challenges to Wearable Devices

There are hundreds of off-the-shelf smartwatches, smart bands, chest straps/patches available which can monitor physical activity, sleep, and heart rate. However, smartwatches and bands do not perform similarly to a chest strap or patch. It has been reported that even on healthy subjects the performance of different wearable devices in measuring heart rate varies significantly [52] and that although, on a treadmill, there is some agreement, the devices showed the greatest error during cycling [52]. Most of the them fail in measuring dynamic heart rate changes. Therefore, these devices are most suitable for measuring resting-state heart rate [53].

Due to the wide range of variability in activity, subject movements, and form factors of wearable devices (smartwatches, smart band, earbuds, ring, etc.), up to now it has been very difficult to get a high-fidelity PPG signal from them, leading to erroneous calculations of heart rate and other parameters [54]. From its beginnings in watches and wristbands, wearable technology has now moved to being available even in a ring form factor. Ōura [55] is a ring that has infrared LEDs, a temperature sensor, accelerometer, and gyroscope. As described at the beginning of this section, it can capture heart rate, temperature, and body movements and sleep patterns. Moreover, five major challenges can affect the performance of the optical monitoring devices: optical noise, sensor location, skin tone, crossover, and low perfusion [56]. The main technical challenge of processing a PPG signal is the noise, which will escalate in the presence of the motion artifact, thus affecting the analysis, which is mostly dependent on clean PPG signals. There have been studies done by the authors to reduce the motion artifacts from PPG signals using signal processing techniques and machine learning [57]. Only 1% of the incident light reflected the photodetector, 1% of which is modulated by the heart pumping-related blood flow variations. The rest of the incident light is scattered from the skin and body materials and, therefore, time-varying motion noise due to dynamic activities of the user makes it difficult to differentiate from real blood flow-related variations. The ear, lower or upper arm, and wrist are the popular wearable device locations. The earlobe is the best place among them as it can provide the best quality signals with minimum affect from the motion artifacts. However, the wrist should be choice for wearable devices to ensure the acquisition of better quality motion-free signals, while the upper arm or forearm can be the better option, compared to the wrist, for higher signal-to-noise (SNR) PPG signal acquisition as there is a higher density of blood vessels close to the surface of the skin. There is a wide variety of skin tones present among the world population and there are six classes among them according to Fitzpatrick Scale [58]. Skin tone variation changes the pattern of absorption. PPG sensor depends on the skin tone of the user's wearing the sensor. Darker skin will absorb more green light and will make it hard to produce reliable PPG signals and thereby other metrics. One of the major challenges of these wearable devices is observed with periodic activity. Most often periodicity of this activity falls with the range of heartbeats. The process of delivering blood to the capillary bed is called perfusion, which significantly varies among the population particularly with diabetes, heart problem, obesity, and other arterial problems.
