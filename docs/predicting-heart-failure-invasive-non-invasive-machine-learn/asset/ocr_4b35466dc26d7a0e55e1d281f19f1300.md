Gupta et al. utilized commercially available Audicor (Inovise Medical, Oregon, USA) devices that use 10 second PCG to detect S3 sounds. They used a proprietary algorithm and the S3 intensity is graded on a scale of 0–10. S3 sound is considered present when its intensity is greater than 5. They were able to reproduce the data upon replacing the old sensors with new pairs of sensors [146]. In their work, Chen et al. used a deep neural network (DNN) to recognize S1 and S2 sounds based only on acoustical characters through converting signals first into a sequence of Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs). The K-means algorithm is applied to cluster MFCC features into two groups to refine their representation and discriminative capability. The refined features are then fed to a DNN classifier to perform S1 and S2 recognition [147]. In a non-invasive pilot study on HF patients with audible S3, Siejeko et al. studied the feasibility of heart sound measurements from an accelerometer within an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) pulse generator. In their findings S1, S2, and S3 vibrations were detected in all accelerometer locations including 16 subjects. However, S3 was not audible because when the signal bandwidth was increased, a substantial portion of S2 was infrasonic (below 20 Hz). In their conclusions they found that heart sounds, including S3 amplitude and heart sound time intervals, can be measured using pulse generator embedded circuitry at implant sites without special-purpose leads [148].

# 5.2.8 Seismocardiography

Seismocardiography (SCG) is a non-invasive method based on measuring the vibration on the chest in response to the movement of heart and blood in the arterial tree [149]. Vibration measurements can be performed using a MEMS-based 3-axis accelerometer placed on the chest, particularly around the mid-sternum [7, 150]. Since SCG measurements are not confounded by body fluid, there is no need to use multiple sensors. The waveforms of SCG signals consist of parts that correspond to the opening and closure states of the aortic valve, and the ejection of blood from the left ventricle to the aorta. The hypothesis of SCG studies depends on the similarity of signals for HF patients before and after physical exercise [7], because HF patients have less reserve to increase cardiac performance during exercise. In other words, SCG signals of HF patients are similar during exercise and rest conditions. On the other hand, healthy individuals have relatively altered signal amplitudes during exercise and rest. By comparing the recorded signals during rest and exercise, the HF severity of the patient can be predicted in a non-invasive way. Recorded time signals are covert into frequency domain for detailed investigation. During exercise, high-frequency components in SCG waves are more visible in healthy individuals compared with the SCG signals of HF patients [7].
