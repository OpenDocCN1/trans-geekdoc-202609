While supervised learning techniques can provide a high degree of accuracy, the limited availability of annotated clinical data makes unsupervised [43] ML algorithms a necessity in performing real-time ECG classification based on unlabeled data using characterization methods like clustering or principal component analysis [22, 24, 44]. Supervised, instance-based ML algorithms utilizing non-parametric classifiers to compare the test and training data, such as k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), that label the test data based on the k closest training samples in the feature space [22, 23, 47-49], have also been employed by various studies.

Based on the processing algorithms utilized to identify features and perform classification, there have been multiple DL architectures introduced for the prediction of major HF clinical outcomes [22, 42, 50-54; Figure 12.1]. CNNs have been shown to be highly effective in image classification [55, 56] and have been utilized widely for HF detection and diagnosis [51, 57, 58]. RNNs have shown success in identifying HF patients [50] and predicting the onset of HF [59-61]. A combination of such architectures creating multiple layers of processing can be utilized to improve performance and prediction efficacy, creating a deep neural network (DNN) [55, 62], which has shown promise in HF prediction [58, 63, 64; Figure 12.1].

Importantly, many ML/DL algorithms have shown superior performance in identifying HF patients compared with traditional statistical approaches and conventional physician diagnosis. Nirschl et al. [51] reported a CNN-based DL classifier to detect HF using endo-myocardial biopsy images with 99% sensitivity and 94% specificity. The algorithm was highly accurate and shown to outperform two pathology experts by 20%. Another study, investigating the efficacy of an RNN model for prediction of HF onset and risk, reported an area under curve (AUC) of 82% compared to 79% achieved by logistic regression [61]. Additionally, the study demonstrated the successful transferability of the ML model to data across different hospitals with a conservative 3.6% drop in AUC, highlighting the adaptability of the ML algorithm to the variability in data across patient populations and hospitals [61]. Choi et al. demonstrated the utility of AI based clinical decision support systems in diagnosing HF compared to traditional method of diagnosis by HF specialists [65; Figure 12.1]. The AI based system showed high accuracy with a concordance rate of 98% between the AI system and HF specialists. Similarly, multiple other groups have shown ML/DL algorithms to outperform conventional analytical methods [66-69].

## 12.5 Invasive Monitoring Devices for Prediction of HF

Recent studies have suggested that physiological parameters, other than the traditionally utilized markers of weight or intrathoracic impedance, may be needed for timely detection of HF decompensation [70]. In fact, interventions based on pulmonary artery hemodynamic monitoring [71, 72] have shown a significant reduction in rehospitalization rates.

Invasive ambulatory hemodynamic monitors have shown promise as early-warning systems in HF, with multiple devices having been investigated, including right ventricular (COMPASS-HF [9] and REDUCE HF [73]), left atrial (HOMEOSTASIS [74] and LAPTOP-HF [11, 71]), and pulmonary artery (CHAMPION [12]) pressure measurement systems [7, 10–12]. More recently, in the MultiSENSE [75] study, an algorithm based on physiological data from sensors in the implantable cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) defibrillators, showed 70% sensitivity in predicting the risk of HF hospitalizations or worsening of HF. Although, in this case, the risk prediction algorithm (HeartLogic [75]) was constructed by combining heart sounds, respiration, thoracic impedance, heart rate, and activity information and did not involve complex ML/DL algorithms, the study highlighted the potential of utilizing physiological information from implanted CRT devices to predict impending HF decompensation.

The utility of applying ML algorithms to data acquired from invasive cardiovascular devices for the management of HF (Table 12.2) was demonstrated by the COMPANION [76] trial. In this study, an RF ML model developed to predict all-cause mortality or HF hospitalization at 12-month post-CRT implantation produced quartiles of patients with an eightfold difference in survival between those with the highest and lowest predicted probability for events [76]. Furthermore, the model also performed better at discriminating the risk of all-cause mortality or HF hospitalization than traditional methods of assessment based on the bundle branch block morphology and QRS duration [76]. While the conventional diagnosis methods failed to significantly differentiate survival outcomes in patients with CRT, the RF ML model successfully predicted clinical outcomes.

Similarly, a study to predict right-ventricular failure (RVF) following left ventricular assist device (LVAD) therapy using a tree-augmented Naive Bayes model, demonstrated an accuracy of up to 97% [77]. Bayesian prognostic models of RVF applied to patient data from the Inter-Agency Registry for Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support (INTERMACS) who had an implanted LVAD, were shown to be highly accurate in predicting acute, early, as well as late RVF with sensitivity of around 90%, and achieving a maximum specificity of 99% [77]. The Bayesian model was also shown to outperform previous risk scores such as the right ventricular failure risk score [78] (RVFRS) and Drakos [79], obtained by multivariable logistic regression analysis, when applied to the INTERMACS registry (AUC 54.7% and 49.8%, respectively) [77]. Another study by the same group showed that Bayesian prognostic models applied to the INTERMACS registry accurately (76–87%) predicted mortality as well, with an AUC ranging between 0.70–0.71 [80]. Furthermore, they developed a novel Bayesian model for risk stratification and prediction of mortality at different time points, that is 30 days, 90 days, 6 months, 1 year and 2 years, post-LVAD implantation [81]. The AI algorithm exhibited mortality prediction accuracies of 95, 90, 90, 83, and 78%, at each of the five time-points, respectively, with AUCs of 91, 82, 82, 80, and 81%, respectively, performing significantly better than the traditional logistic regression based HeartMate II Risk Score [82] (AUC 57 and 60%, at 90 days and 1 year, respectively) [81].

There are a number of implantable monitoring devices focused on the management of HF, such as CardioMEMs [83, 84], which are implantable impedance monitors [85] to gauge fluid accumulation [86, 87] and dedicated ML approaches to analyze data from CRT devices, LVADs, and implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICDs) [22, 32, 33, 43, 45, 88]. While devices like CardioMEMs have shown reduction in HF readmissions [12, 72], a major limitation of these devices is that they are invasively implanted, costly upfront, and demand additional personnel and resources to handle the device implantation and data acquisition. In addition, invasive monitoring devices require specialized hardware and data extraction features, often making them cumbersome and impractical for real-time monitoring and assessment of active, ambulatory patients, especially those who live in underserved areas. Furthermore, their cost-effectiveness remains debatable [89–91], highlighting the need for affordable non-invasive approaches for HF monitoring enabled with effective AI based disease prediction strategies.

## 12.6 Non-Invasive Wearable Monitoring Devices for Prediction of HF

The utility of wearable monitoring devices [92–96] in cardiovascular healthcare and, in particular, in HF management has been well established [16, 97–99]. Ambulatory non-invasive monitoring devices that assess various physiological parameters, including blood pressure [100], photoplethysmograms (PPGs) [101], seismocardiograms [17], ECG [95, 102], electromagnetic energy-based dielectric properties [103], oxygen saturation [104], and fluid status, weight, activity, and bioimpedance [105], have enabled access to large amounts of patient data which are ideally suited for applying AI algorithms and predicting HF onset or deterioration (Table 12.2; 98, 106–110).
