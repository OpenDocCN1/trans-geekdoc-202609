# 8.3 Detection and Diagnosis of HF

The growing number of HF patients and increasing healthcare costs indicate the importance of the early diagnosis of HF for efficient treatment planning. For the diagnosis of HF, several clinical tests including blood tests, chest radiography, echocardiography, and ECG are performed in addition to physical examination and disease history investigation of the patient [12, 13]. A research study has shown that the deep learning model trained with ECG alone has high sensitivity and specificity in detecting HF [14]. Several criteria, such as Framingham, Boston, European Society of Cardiology (ESC), and the Gothenburg criteria, are employed by evaluating the clinical test data to decide on the presence of HF [15]. According to the ESC guidelines, the patient's clinical history, observed symptoms, physical activity, and resting ECG are examined at the first step [10]. The symptoms of HF can be summarized as ankle swelling, dyspnea, fatigue due to elevated jugular venous pressure, pulmonary crackles, and peripheral edema [16–18]. If all examinations mentioned are normal, the presence of HF is not expected. If any of the initial stage evaluations are abnormal, plasma natriuretic peptides are recommended to be measured and, after this measurement, the clinicians decide on whether there is a further need for echocardiography [14]. Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) is a protein secreted by the ventricular musculature in response to volume or pressure overload. The natriuretic peptide family is important for volume and blood pressure regulation. The presence of BNP in blood plasma is being used as a biomarker for diagnosing HF. Studies have shown that the use of BPN can improve HF diagnosis compared with the use of echocardiography [19]. An effective technique for HF diagnosis is to measure the intracardiac filling pressures using implantable pressure sensors by means of cardiac catheterization [20] but the invasive nature of catheterization limits the routine usage of this method [21].

# 8.4 Prediction of HF

After the diagnosis of HF, the severity of the condition is clinically predicted and classified using either the New York Heart Association (NYHA) or the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines [22]. These guidelines are constituted to accurately predict the state of the disease and to plan the most appropriate treatment methods. NYHA guidelines basically use the physical activity capabilities and observed symptoms of the patient for the classification of HF [13]. On the other hand, in the ACC/AHA guidelines, the structural changes and symptoms are described for HF stages [19]. In other words, NYHA guidelines define the exercise capacity and symptomatic status of the patient [14], and ACC/AHA guidelines focus on the developmental stage and progression of HF [10].
