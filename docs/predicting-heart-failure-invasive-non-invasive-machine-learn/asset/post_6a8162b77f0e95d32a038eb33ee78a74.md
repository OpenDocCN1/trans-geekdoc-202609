## 4 Overview and Challenges of Wireless Communication and Power Transfer for Implanted Sensors

Mohamed Zied Chaari and Somaya Al-Maadeed

### 4.1 Introduction

Implanted sensors can measure the physiological and clinical parameters of interest, elaborate on them, and transmit information to healthcare staff through the internet to enable real-time decision-making, as shown in Figure 4.1. Each sensor is an intelligent device, and the complete structural health supervision (SHM) system is a utilization of the internet of things (IoT) paradigm [1–4]. The bioinformatics data are sent to the cloud by internet link, allowing distributed systems and big data concepts to monitor and analyze them. Implementing the SHM system using the IoT model allows new technologies to facilitate the capability and reliability of progressive health supervising technology. Remotely following critical health bioinformatics helps patient in-home care and reduces the complexities of overseeing the healthcare of older people [5, 6], and is especially practical in COVID-19. If this advanced technology were implanted in the human body before, it would have helped us face the coronavirus pandemic, as the sensors measure the body temperature. If the temperature exceeds 39 °C, the doctor is informed directly. The patient then receives an instruction to stay at home until they have done a coronavirus test, and thus the spread of infection has been reduced.

New technology implant biosensors for supervising many biological parameters and improving wireless methods have led to progress in all-inclusive patient-focused technologies such as wireless body area networks (WBANs) [7] and body sensor networks (BSNs) for long-term healthcare [8, 9]. BSN is a short-range network consisting of an array of low-power sensors that can be inserted or attached to the body and work within a short-range. WBAN, on the other hand, offers several exciting new web-based applications for remote health monitoring, healthcare, and medicine, all of which benefit from the unrestricted freedom of movement provided by WBAN. The advantage of WBAN is that the patient does not have to stay in bed for long and can now leave the hospital and transmit medical data remotely. The essential component of these networks is an implantable chip. This research harness wireless energy transfer technologies to energize the chip implant sensors underneath the skin (invasive).

### 4.2 Microchip Insertion Procedure

New improvements in biomedical chips and the latest CMOS techniques have led to invasive bioelectronics microchips for permanent human health monitoring. Microchips implanted in the human body allow doctors to continuously investigate a patient's medical vital data online, enabling savings of both money and time. In addition, the information acquired by the sensor from regular supervision helps the doctor and healthcare staff efficiently record a patient's medical history for analysis and emergencies. Figure 4.2 shows a complex implantable sensor system with various biosensors and the methods for entering the body. CMOS technologies offer many features, such as minor form elements and reliable processes, making them appropriate for implantable health applications.

Chips on this platform contain many sensors for oxygen, glucose, etc. The latest scientific study on implantable chip systems focuses on small form factors and light-weight features for ease of implementation and reasons of physical safety [10, 11]. Even though deep CMOS processes have substantially improved the overall sensor area, the power unit delivers a robust, comprehensive system.

The path to ultra-precise metering in CMOS technologies is not just limited to image sensors, wireless systems, microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), and radiofrequency systems. The implantable devices into the body demand low energy consumption and a small area fabricated by ultra-fine technology. Microchip sensor technology is very functional for biotechnology implementations in which fluorescence is frequently used for labeling, particularly for detecting neural activity or cells. These features are suitable for biomedical applications. CMOS technologies are thus entirely appropriate for an implantable microchip for biomedical fields and use in various applications.
