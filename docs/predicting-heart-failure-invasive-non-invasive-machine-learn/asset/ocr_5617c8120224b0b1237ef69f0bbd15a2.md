+   - Immune and inflammatory diseases
+   - Infiltrative diseases such as amyloidosis or sarcoidosis
+   - Metabolic causes (thyroid dysfunction, nutritional deficiencies)
+   - Genetic abnormalities
+   - Arrhythmias
+   - Valvular and structural abnormalities
+   - Hypertension [5]
+   - Life style habits such as the lack of exercise, smoking, and obesity, can exacerbate an existing problem [6].

HF is one of the most common causes of admission to hospital and is the cause of more than $30 billion spent in the US annually [7]. A total increase of 46% is estimated to be associated with more than 8 million adults with HF and can be compared to a global epidemic [8]. HF represents the primary cause of hospitalization in patients aged 65 years or older in the western world, and is accountable for more than 1 million hospitalizations per year in the US [8]. As presented in Figure 9.1 the HF prevalence is a worldwide phenomenon [9, 10].

HF imposes a significant burden on both patients and healthcare systems, with costly hospitalizations and high mortality rates [11, 12]. Hospitalizations due to HF represent 1–2% of all hospital admissions [13]. More than 20% of patients are rehospitalized within 30 days and up to 50% within 6 months [14], and 40% of patients die within a year of their first hospitalization [15]. Occurring more frequently in people over 65 [16], HF prevalence in the general population is estimated to be 4.2% [17]. Although HF is perceived as a disease of the elderly, studies have indicated that HF incidents are increasing in young people [18, 19].

Therefore, there is a great interest in reducing hospital readmissions due to HF; attempts to achieve this take different forms, from the introduction of penalties on hospitals that exceed the expected readmission rates [20] to specialized decision models [21].
