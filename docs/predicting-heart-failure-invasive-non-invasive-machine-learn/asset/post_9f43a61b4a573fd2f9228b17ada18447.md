Numerous studies have proved the positive effect of bioimpedance analysis for monitoring heart failure patients. Initially, bioimpedance analysis was conducted invasively by transmitting a weak current from the wires of pacemakers and defibrillators [26]. Now, however, non-invasive devices are pervasively used for bioimpedance analysis and heart failure monitoring. Whole-body impedance, as well as transthoracic impedance measurements, are utilized in the literature to predict the risk in heart failure patients [27, 28]. Kataoka [29] proposed a novel non-invasive method for integrating a bioimpedance index along with body weight for managing the health conditions of heart failure patients. The proposed method showed that the bioimpedance index and body weight combination have significantly increased the predictive accuracy for detecting the worsening of heart failure conditions. However, the method was evaluated in only a few subjects. A study by Gyllensten et al. [30] compared the performance of traditional algorithms based on body weight and bioimpedance measurement for early prediction of risk heart failure patients. In the study, 91 patients with CHF were made to wear a transthoracic bioimpedance vest containing textile electrodes on both sides to transmit current at variable frequencies to the body. Along with the bioimpedance measurement, body weight was also monitored for a period of 10 months. Compared with body weight, the bioimpedance measurement provided a more accurate prediction for early indication of risk in heart failure patients.

In Anand et al.'s MUSIC study [9], a non-randomized study for evaluating the efficacy of a multisensory device for monitoring patients suffering from congestive heart failure utilized the body composition measurement along with other physiological values. Another study examined the efficacy of a fluid accumulation vest (transthoracic bio-impedance vest) for monitoring heart failure patients [31]. The selected subjects of the study were made to wear the vest and take the body composition measurement. The body composition measurements were sent to the clinic using smartphones and internet connectivity. The observed output of the study in 180 patients showed that monitoring the body composition measurement using the fluid accumulation vest can predict the risk, including that of hospitalization, of the patient in advance.

## 11.2.5 Smart Wearable and Pocket-Sized Devices for Monitoring of the Heart

US FDA-approved smart wearables that are equipped with multiple sensors, including smartwatches, smart bands, vests or socks embedded with sensors, ECG patches, etc., are currently available for monitoring heart conditions. Current generation smartwatches and smart bands are equipped with activity recognition sensors and with heart rate and heart rhythm sensors. The readings from these sensors can be utilized for monitoring of and predicting risk in patients with heart failure. Triaxial accelerometers, global positioning systems, and barometers are included in the smartwatches and bands to accurately estimate the physical activity of an individual. A recent research study [32] found that the data from a pedometer is suitable for detecting the risk and severity of heart failure conditions. All the participants of the study were equipped with a smartphone and a first-generation Apple smartwatch. In one of the experiments, the participants had to do a 6-minute walk while wearing a pedometer built-in to the smartwatch. The study showed that results from the 6-minute walking test are sufficient for the risk assessment of heart failure patients. Smart wearables, including smartwatches, utilize photoplethysmography and ECG techniques to estimate heart rate and the rhythm of heartbeats. Various studies [33, 34] have shown that a high resting heart rate and heart rate variability are risk factors for heart failure patients. In this context, the heart rate variability measure from smart wearables can be utilized for early prediction in heart failure patients. However, the accuracy of smart wearables’ heart rate estimation impacts the overall precision of the prediction system. Multiple studies have been conducted to assess the accuracy of photoplethysmography sensors in smartwatches for heart rate estimation. The third generation Apple Watch and Fitbit Charge 2 have shown acceptable heart rate estimation accuracy in a study [35].

A comparative study conducted by Etwiy et al. [36] evaluated the heart rate estimation accuracy of multiple smartwatches, including Apple Watch, Garmin Forerunner, etc., and of one chest strap, Polar H7. The results obtained showed that the chest strap was more accurate than smartwatches for heart rate monitoring. The accuracy of the Apple Watch for measuring heart rate variability in heart failure patients during admission to the hospital and after discharge is currently being evaluated in a clinical trial (NCT04510779). Generally, the electrode in Apple Watches can be used to obtain a 1-lead electrocardiogram. The available ECG feature in the Apple Watch can be extended to obtain a 3-lead [37], 6-lead, or 12-lead ECG [38] by keeping the watch in various regions of the body. Zoll Medical Corporation has developed the μCor™ patch for managing heart failure as well as arrhythmia. The FDA-approved patch is equipped with a radio frequency sensor, an ECG monitor for early detection of adverse heart conditions. The radio frequency sensor is responsible for monitoring the pulmonary fluid levels and the pulmonary fluid level is utilized for assessing heart failure decompensation. μCor™ is currently under clinical trials (NCT03476187) for predicting heart failures. ## 11.2.6 Telemonitoring of Heart Failure Using Multiple Commercially Available Non-Invasive Devices

Several studies have been conducted to examine and analyze the effect of telemonitoring heart failure patients remotely using multiple non-invasive devices. Chaudhary et al. [39] conducted a study across 1653 patients for analyzing the effect of telemonitoring in heart failure patients. In the study, 826 patients were monitored via telemonitoring and the rest were given usual care. The study utilized the telephone and a body weight scale for monitoring the patients. The daily body weight and symptoms experienced by the patients were recorded by an interactive voice response system via the telephone. The obtained results show that telemonitoring did not improve the prediction. Moreover, there were no beneficial effects while using body weight as the primary metric for heart failure condition assessment.
