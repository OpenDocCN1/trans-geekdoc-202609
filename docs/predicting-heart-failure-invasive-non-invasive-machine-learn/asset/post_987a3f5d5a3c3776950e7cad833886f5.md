### 4.3.5 Ultrasonic Wireless Power Transfer

This section discusses wireless ultrasound energy transmission to miniature medical implants in the human body [32–34]. The ultrasound produces vibrations or sound waves with the help of piezoelectric transducers. The ultrasonic frequency outside the audible scale is greater than 20 kHz [35] and, thus, the ultrasonic technique provides better penetration depths and shorter wavelengths. It can also transmit electromagnetic energy through hard-to-reach materials and neither the ultrasound method nor electromagnetic interference harms human tissues. The ultrasound device comprises an external apparatus and a slide implanted underneath the skin with piezoelectric transducers on both sides and separated by tissue, as shown in Figure 4.10. This technology depends on the generation and propagation of sound via the tissues. Transmission of energy through tissue requires an external transducer (Transmitter (Tx)) placed on the tissue surface and counteracting the ultrasonic frequency to the biosensor chip directly under the skin (Receiver (Rx)). The power source activates a piezoelectric transducer that converts electricity into ultrasonic waves. These ultrasonic waves channel energy through the tissue toward a receiver implanted inside the body. The transducer's receiver transforms ultrasonic strength into electricity. The low-loss rectifier network rectifies and filters the output voltage of power-up the biosensor chip and charging battery.

An incorrect choice of the transmission frequency can adversely affect the skin attenuation and sizes of the emitter and receiver transducers (Tx and Rx) and Rayleigh distance. Therefore, the maximum power that a transducer can provide complete at its resonant frequency ($f_r$) depends on the transducer's material and size:

$$f_r = \frac{C}{2d}$$ (4.1)

where:
C = acoustic velocity
d = transducer thickness.

According to this equation, increasing ($f_r$) minimizes the transducer volume and boosts the Rayleigh range at the cost of boost tissue absorption.

Having presented the classification for wireless electricity, we will focus on inductive and radio frequency harvesting technology to charge the subcutaneous SoC implant. This work designs and realizes an inductive wireless charging system using mutual inductive radiation. An inductive link between two coils operates the SoC, despite the tissue thickness of 3-5 mm (tissue width). Although this technique has a short space, the inductive distance range can be boosted using a high-power class E amplifier to enhance the magnetic resonance.

According to the following equation, the efficiency of ultrasonic wireless power (\(\eta\)) usually relies on transducer losses, the amount of energy used by the receiver transducer, acoustic impedance matching, the efficiency of the coupling gel, losses due to rectifier losses, and skin absorption:

\(\eta = \left| \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}} \right|\) (4.2)

The proposed circuit considers all of the above technical parameters to provide adequate power to operate the applied biosensor in the patient. The real success of the implanted chips varies according to how safely they work without any harmful effect on the skin.
