Management of HF is unique among medical circumstances, in that it requires continuous management of data such as weight, heart rate, blood pressure, and volume status that may require adjusting daily medication.

Wearable health devices are already available in the market, although most of them are for fitness, these devices are creating a unique opportunity to improve HF care. With the advances in technology, some companies have started including biosignal sensors to measure biomedical signals such as heart rate and electrocardiographic information. Several trials [13–15], using commercial devices, have been conducted on using wearable devices for HF management. These studies have confirmed that wearable devices can be used to manage HF patients. However, several problems still exist, such as how to use the devices, data transmission, analyzing the data, and correlating the data with existing records [14, 15]. Advances in machine learning and accessibility to extensive patient information in the electronic medical records play an important role in improving HF management tools. Several studies have shown that machine learning can predict HF diagnosis with reasonable accuracy, classify HF cases, and identify HF a long time before the clinical diagnosis [16]. Studies exist that have used machine learning to create predictive models for HF [16, 17]. The models are trained on different data types, such as ambulatory blood pressure and activity level. The models can either identify the factors that increase the risk of HF, such as diastolic blood pressure, or identify patients at risk, or patients who are at risk of readmission to hospital [18, 19].

This chapter discusses the applications and challenges of implantable devices for HF monitoring, as well as their prospects and limitations, and lists some of the data sets recently collected from wearable devices. Finally, the application of machine learning for detecting HF using wearable devices and its prospects are discussed.

## 13.2 Applications of Implantable Devices in HF Detection

Implantable devices are extensively used to monitor patients with cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). CVDs contribute to 45% of deaths in Europe and 720,000 people suffer from heart attacks every year in the US [20, 21]. Several studies have confirmed that there is a close relationship between high blood pressure and increased CVD risk in the elderly population. Between 2004 and 2014, there was a significant increase (~34%) in deaths in the world population due to high blood pressure, which was attributed to ever-increasing socioeconomic pressure, as well as to lack of awareness of preventive or monitoring measures, etc [21]. Several implantable devices are clinically being used or are under clinical trial for monitoring HF patients invasively. Among these, low arterial pressure monitors, intrathoracic impedance monitors, and lung fluid monitors are prominent. However, very few remote monitoring systems for HF patients are approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA); the Cardio-Microelectromechanical system (CardioMEMS™) [22, 23] is one of them. This is a pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) device to directly monitor pressure with the help of implanted sensors, and the data is communicated to the healthcare provider for better patient management (Figure 13.1). Clinical trials have reported that the rate of hospitalization has reduced with the application of the CardioMEMS device [24].

Apart from cardiovascular information, blood pressure provides information about other conditions, such as sleep apnea, dementia, and hypertensive multiple sclerosis complications [25, 26]. Implantable pressure sensors with wireless communication can be effectively used for long-term monitoring. This PAP monitoring has significant research importance. It has been reported that HF can be detected several weeks earlier by monitoring the changes in blood pressure [27]. Non-invasive solutions for real-time arterial blood pressure monitoring can significantly ease the process. One major contribution toward this objective has been reported recently, namely the extraction of arterial blood pressure non-invasively using the photoplethysmogram (PPG) signal [28]. A PPG is an optically obtained plethysmogram that can be used to detect blood volume changes in the microvascular bed of tissue. It is often obtained by using a pulse oximeter which illuminates the skin and measures changes in light absorption. There have been studies conducted by the authors to detect blood pressure using PPG signals using machine learning approaches on already available labeled datasets [28, 29]. These studies reported a deep learning-based approach for predicting the continuous arterial blood pressure (ABP) waveform through a non-invasive approach using PPG signals, an approach that is suitable for wearable devices and remote patient monitoring.

A cardiac pacemaker generates electrical impulses to be delivered to the atria and ventricles to contract and pump blood to the body. By contrast, a cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator (CRT-D) is a life-saving device for HF patients who are at high risk of cardiac arrest in that it not only helps to sustain normal heart rhythms like a pacemaker, but also helps to deliver small electrical impulses to both ventricles to contract them simultaneously (Figure 13.2). To monitor the progression of HF, intracellular and extracellular ion concentrations can be monitored and clinical trials with CRT-D and these kinds of sensors can help save lives by ensuring better patient management. Wireless communication from implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) to medical personnel and patients’ caregivers has now become a reality [30].

The ICDs continuously monitor the heart signal and, if there are any abnormal heart rhythms detected, it immediately works to correct the rhythms. Moreover, if a cardiac arrest occurs and the heart stops its pumping action, these devices restore heart function by applying an electric shock to revert the situation. These devices are FDA-approved and are quite reliable for critical patient management, although patients have to maintain a very restricted lifestyle.

The rise in left atrial pressure (LAP) usually is gradual and precedes symptom onset. Therefore, accurate monitoring of LAP using an implantable LAP sensing device (as shown in Figure 13.3) could allow earlier identification. A small clinical trial with eight HF patients being monitored with LAP devices twice a day resulted in no deaths, no unplanned HF clinic visits, and no hospital admissions for HF [33].

## 13.3 Wearable Devices in HF Detection Application

Consumer health technology has grown to an annual market of billions of dollars and almost 20% of Americans are now using wearable devices for fitness tracking applications [34]. The market of wearable devices is growing very fast and is expected to be more than $27 billion by 2026 [35]. These wearable consumable devices have increasingly become more sophisticated, accurate, and closer to medical-grade devices. They will allow more personalized and precise monitoring of the patients with HF along with more frequent and appropriate adjustment of therapy and care. Moreover, early detection and diagnosis of the disease can help in immediate intervention and save a significant number of lives.
