BCG signals can be determined using a modified weighing scale capable of measuring the response at the bottom of the foot. There is a phase difference between the peaks of ECG and BCG signals due to wave propagation through the body. There are also secondary peaks in the BCG wave which are not observed in ECG signals. In the classic BCG, early peak H occurs due to the heart contraction in systole and main IJK complex is observed due to the ventricular ejection and aortic flow [151, 152]. After BCG had been studied extensively between 1955 and 1980, the method was almost extinct by 2010, mainly due to the lack of standardized measurement techniques and lack of understanding of the signal source [151]. However, BCG research has increased again in recent years, thanks to the advancements in sensing technologies such as static-charge-sensitive sensors and piezoelectric films [153–155]. These sensing technologies simplify the non-invasive measurements of BCG signals using weighing scale implemented or bed/chair implemented sensor applications that are capable of measuring BCG signals during standing or sitting [156, 157]. BCG signals vary depending on the cardiovascular health status of the individual [158, 159]. The peak amplitudes of BCG signals are significantly lower for HF patients compared with the rest or exercise states of healthy individuals [158].

### 5.2.10 Photoplethysmography

Photoplethysmogram (PPG) is an optical signal used for the quantification of blood volume changes during the heartbeat [158]. PPG signal is generally measured to monitor the oxygen saturation using a pulse oximeter [160]. The scatter of light is different for oxygenated and deoxygenated blood and the amount of oxygen can be predicted using this difference [161]. A pulse oximeter is generally used on the finger [161, 162]; a beam of light is emitted toward one side of the finger and the level of collected light is measured on the other side. As a result, the scattered light intensity provides a measure to determine the level of oxygen saturation in the body [163]. The baseline oxygen saturation decreases progressively due to inadequate cardiovascular performance of HF patients, and HF worsening may be suspected when the baseline oxygen saturation is less than 93% [164].

### 5.2.11 Monitoring Natriuretic Peptides Levels

B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and amino-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) are biomarkers of HF, and they can be used for the assessment of HF severity [165–167]. BNP is a cardiac hormone which is released from the myocytes (heart muscle cells) in response to increased wall stress [168]. For the measurements of BNP levels, minimally invasive finger-stick self-testing can be used [3].

In the case of HF, ventricular stress increases, leading to higher levels of BNP and NT-proBNP release. It has been reported that the cut-off levels for HF diagnosis are 100 pg/mL of BNP [168] or 300 pg/mL of NT-proBNP [169]. Natriuretic peptide levels less than these cut-off values are not considered to indicate HF. There are several factors which influence the level of BNP and NT-proBNP; therefore, in addition to BNP measurements, other supporting diagnostic tools should be employed for accurate HF diagnosis [2]. In case of acute coronary syndrome, renal insufficiency, RV dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, and advanced aging, higher levels of BNP are observed than the expected levels [167]. On the other hand, obesity, flash pulmonary edema, and end-stage cardiomyopathy lead to lower levels of BNP than those expected [167]. For individuals under 50 years old, presence of HF can be considered when the BNP level is higher than 400 pg/mL or NT-proBNP is higher than 450 pg/mL. For the groups between 50 and 75 years of age and older than 75 years, these threshold levels become 900 pg/mL and 1800 pg/mL, respectively [167]. It is reported that impending HF deterioration can be predicted using daily home BNP measurements [170, 171]. McDonald et al. (2018) used an averaged filter to reduce the variations in daily BNP measurements and provide better assessment of weekly changes for predicting emerging HF failure [170].

## 5.3 Clinical Trials on Remote Monitoring Using Non-Invasive Sensors

The non-invasive modalities discussed in this chapter are used for remote monitoring of HF patients. Many remote monitoring trials investigated different population groups and employed different monitoring techniques. Several researchers performed meta-analysis by combining randomized control trials (RCTs).

Klersy et al. (2009) conducted a meta-analysis, using 20 RCTs and 12 cohort studies, to identify the effect of remote patient monitoring (RPM) on the outcome of HF patients [172]. The RCTs included 6258 patients and had a 6-month follow-up period. The RCTs showed that RPM results in a significant reduction in the number of deaths (relative risk: 0.83, 95% confidence interval: 0.73 to 0.95, p = 0.006) and hospitalizations (relative risk: 0.93, 95% confidence interval: 0.87 to 0.99, p = 0.030), indicating that RPM has a clinical protective effect compared with usual care [172]. Inglis et al. (2010) performed a similar meta-analysis and reported that RPM decreased the number of deaths (2710 subjects were included, relative risk: 0.66), all-cause hospitalizations (2343 subjects were included, relative risk: 0.91), and HF-related hospitalizations (1570 subjects were included, relative risk: 0.79) [42].

The meta-analysis of Yun et al. (2018) reported that RPM reduced all-cause mortality (relative risk: 0.81) and HF-related mortality (relative risk: 0.81), but no significant change is reported for all-cause hospitalizations and HF-related hospitalizations [173]. Chaudhry et al.'s (2010) study was a randomized controlled clinical trial of RPM by means of measuring weight and collecting daily information about the symptoms [14]. They stated that there was no effect of remote monitoring system on the number of deaths and hospitalizations. The possible reason for this ineffectiveness might be related to using only weight as the indicator. The most commonly used parameter for remote monitoring is weight because of its simplicity [51]; however, it is not sufficient alone to capture the warning of impending HF decompensation [19]. Large-scale trials that use only one remote monitoring parameter tend to generate neutral results and show limited positive effect of remote monitoring. Similarly, if thoracic impedance is used as the only remote monitoring parameter, large trials result in low sensitivity and specificity [174, 175]. It can be concluded that including several monitoring parameters is critical to improve the effectiveness of RPM [51]. The findings of Pekmezaris et al. (2018) support this fact as, by utilizing blood pressure measurement and a pulse oximeter in addition to the weight scale, lower hospitalization rates are achieved [22].

In several trials which employ multiple monitoring parameters, thoracic impedance, heart rate, heart rate variability, and physical activity are used synchronously [176, 177]. In the study of Anand et al. (2012), an algorithm was developed to predict impending acute HF decompensation [178]. A total of 543 patients were monitored over three months using a device with multiple sensors measuring heart rate, respiratory rate, body impedance, and physical activity. It is reported that the algorithm predicted HF events with 63% sensitivity, 92% specificity, and a false positive rate of 0.9 per patient-year.

## 5.4 Discussion

HF is a chronic cardiovascular disease which results in frequent hospitalizations, increasing the economic burden in healthcare systems and negatively affecting the hospital workload. Many rehospitalizations are unnecessary or can be avoided by employing remote monitoring of HF patients.

Hemodynamic effects of HF decompensation begin about 30 days before hospitalization [185]. In current practice, in-hospital invasive techniques are applied for assessing the HF state by measuring ventricular pressure levels. However, filling ventricle pressures can be determined using a catheter and cannot be applied frequently because of their invasive nature. An accurate and low-cost non-invasive modality is an important requirement for the recognition of HF decompensation. The signs of HF decompensation can be sensed in the form of intrathoracic impedance alterations measured by non-invasive tools up to 15 days before hospitalization. Systematic use of non-invasive techniques allows remote monitoring of the patient's condition. With the development in sensor technologies and data science, lightweight and wearable sensors can be designed and the measurements taken can be remotely monitored by clinicians.

The exact role and effectiveness of remote monitoring remains unclear [3, 24, 182]. Although several RPM studies have reported reduced mortality rates and HF-related hospitalizations, large-scale trials tend to give neutral results for mortality and hospitalizations [19]. In future, it is necessary to elucidate the effect of remote monitoring modalities for HF prediction on large-scale RCTs. We have seen that using only one monitoring parameter is not sufficient to reduce hospitalization and mortality rates, and multiparameter methods with optimized algorithms are recommended for the increased benefit and for improving the life quality of patients [22].

The use of weight scales is common among RPM trials, but the increase in weight provides an imprecise estimation because weight can change due to many factors other than HF decompensation [186]. A relative increase in thoracic bioimpedance provides better prediction of HF-related congestion [3]. ECG is among the under-investigated techniques for HF remote monitoring [182].
