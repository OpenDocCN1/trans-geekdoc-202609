## 13.4.5 Arrhythmia Detection

Several life-threatening arrhythmias that lead to HF are atrial fibrillation (AF or AFib), ventricular tachycardia (VT or V-tach), and ventricular fibrillation (VF or VFib). However, there are very few wearable solutions available for real-time monitoring and detection of VT and VF. On the other hand, there is a good number of wearable solutions available for AF detection. The main challenges in wearable VT or VF detection are due to the complexity of developing an ECG system in wristwatches and wristbands. Moreover, irregular pulses cannot be easily detected by analyzing the PPG signal without confirming the ECG signals [46]. Ambulatory monitoring of the ECG signals is often done using Holter monitors, but Cardioskin, Carnation™ patch, VivaLNK™, and similar chest patches are becoming popular because they can be used for several days comfortably for ECG monitoring and are more convenient than the Holter devices (Figure 13.9) [47].

The Cardioskin is a wearable, wireless medical device, which allows the acquisition, recording, storage, transmission, and display of a 15-lead ECG to analyze potential cardiac pathological abnormalities. The Carnation Ambulatory Monitor (CAM) is a lightweight, bandage-sized cardiac patch monitor engineered to capture low amplitude, low-frequency electrical signals that form the P-wave. This focus on the P-wave is a major advancement for cardiologists and cardiac rhythm specialists to aid in diagnosing an arrhythmia. The CAM patch is designed to be accurate, promote patient compliance, streamline clinical workflow, and yield clinically actionable data. VivaLNK is stuck to the skin to record ECG waveforms, respiratory rate, heart rate, RR interval, and patient movement, and so has potential to help detect cardiac arrhythmias such as AF. Weighing only 0.26 ounces (7.5 grams), it has all the electronics of a single-lead ECG, an accelerometer, a rechargeable battery, and wireless connectivity. Electrodes can also be woven into a “smart textile” vest for prolonged multilead ECG monitoring.

ECG monitoring through wearables can therefore be useful for the diagnosis of AF. All three can be worn for several days at a time for continuous ECG monitoring more comfortably and for longer than a standard Holter monitor. The Apple Heart Study has utilized a PPG-based algorithm to identify irregular pulses which can be utilized to screen AF patients without previous history of AF [48]. However, these types of studies should be validated with the concurrent ECG recording in adopting these devices for clinical applications. KardiaMobile, from AliveCor (Mountain View, CA) is typically paired with a smart-phone to monitor ECG signals in mobile phones and can be used for AF detection. Philips has released a cardiac and motion monitoring module called Philips health watch, which can detect bradycardic and tachycardia with a sensitivity of 85% and 99%, respectively [49]. The E4 wristband from Empatica Inc. (Boston, MA) has a smart application that can discriminate AF from atrial tachycardia and flutter, and premature ventricular contractions with an accuracy of 0.8 using a machine learning algorithm [49]. These devices have received clearance from the US FDA.

A significant increase in the interest in remote monitoring of cardiac and coronavirus disease (COVID-19) patients and their hemodynamic monitoring was reported in patients with implantable sensors and wearable ECG sensors for detecting AF using off-the-shelf solutions HeartLogic™ and CardioMEMS [45].

## 13.4.6 Technology in Development for Early Detection of HF Decompensation

ZOLL µCor is a patch-based ECG monitor using a radiofrequency sensor and transmitter to measure pulmonary fluid content in the heart and was assessed for predicting HF decompensation in a clinical trial [1]. Several electrodes were embedded in the textile to measure heart rate, heart rate variability, respiration rate, and cardiothoracic impedance with a device-level algorithm using HeartLogic [50]. A smart sock was proposed by [51] to monitor edema and activity using the accelerometer and stretch sensor, which showed high accuracy in measuring ankle circumference and activity for a healthy cohort.

### 13.4.6.1 Practical Challenges to Wearable Devices

There are hundreds of off-the-shelf smartwatches, smart bands, chest straps/patches available which can monitor physical activity, sleep, and heart rate. However, smartwatches and bands do not perform similarly to a chest strap or patch. It has been reported that even on healthy subjects the performance of different wearable devices in measuring heart rate varies significantly [52] and that although, on a treadmill, there is some agreement, the devices showed the greatest error during cycling [52]. Most of them fail in measuring dynamic heart rate changes. Therefore, these devices are most suitable for measuring resting-state heart rate [53].

Due to the wide range of variability in activity, subject movements, and form factors of wearable devices (smartwatches, smart band, earbuds, ring, etc.), up to now it has been very difficult to get a high-fidelity PPG signal from them, leading to erroneous calculations of heart rate and other parameters [54]. From its beginnings in watches and wristbands, wearable technology has now moved to being available even in a ring form factor. Ōura [55] is a ring that has infrared LEDs, a temperature sensor, accelerometer, and gyroscope. As described at the beginning of this section, it can capture heart rate, temperature, and body movements and sleep patterns. Moreover, five major challenges can affect the performance of the optical monitoring devices: optical noise, sensor location, skin tone, crossover, and low perfusion [56]. The main technical challenge of processing a PPG signal is the noise, which will escalate in the presence of the motion artifact, thus affecting the analysis, which is mostly dependent on clean PPG signals. There have been studies done by the authors to reduce the motion artifacts from PPG signals using signal processing techniques and machine learning [57]. Only 1% of the incident light reflected the photodetector, 1% of which is modulated by the heart pumping-related blood flow variations. The rest of the incident light is scattered from the skin and body materials and, therefore, time-varying motion noise due to dynamic activities of the user makes it difficult to differentiate from real blood flow-related variations. The ear, lower or upper arm, and wrist are the popular wearable device locations. The earlobe is the best place among them as it can provide the best quality signals with minimum effect from the motion artifacts. However, the wrist should be the choice for wearable devices to ensure the acquisition of better quality motion-free signals, while the upper arm or forearm can be the better option, compared to the wrist, for higher signal-to-noise (SNR) PPG signal acquisition as there is a higher density of blood vessels close to the surface of the skin. There is a wide variety of skin tones present among the world population and there are six classes among them according to Fitzpatrick Scale [58]. Skin tone variation changes the pattern of absorption. PPG sensor depends on the skin tone of the user wearing the sensor. Darker skin will absorb more green light and will make it hard to produce reliable PPG signals and thereby other metrics. One of the major challenges of these wearable devices is observed with periodic activity. Most often periodicity of this activity falls with the range of heartbeats. The process of delivering blood to the capillary bed is called perfusion, which significantly varies among the population particularly with diabetes, heart problem, obesity, and other arterial problems.
