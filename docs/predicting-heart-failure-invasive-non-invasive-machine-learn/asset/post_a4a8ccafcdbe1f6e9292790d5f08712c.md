Therefore, individualized analysis is employed for each patient for tracking the relative increases in bioimpedance levels [10]. By remotely performing bioimpedance measurements at every hour or every day, clinicians can track the relative behavior of impedances and have the opportunity to take therapeutic action in case of a sudden and sharp decrease in thoracic impedance.

As a recent application of bioimpedance monitoring, remote dielectric sensing technology (ReDS) utilizes the changes in lung fluid status and dielectric properties of thoracic tissues for predicting the HF state [52–54]. In this application, a wearable vest is used with two sensors placed on the anterior and posterior sides. Low-power electromagnetic signals are emitted and received by the implemented sensors after passing through the thoracic tissues [55]. There is no need for direct skin contact to perform the measurements, and therefore the method can be applied over light clothing. The electromagnetic signals are mostly affected by the fluid content of the thoracic tissues in the path [55], and the predicted fluid balance in the lungs can be used for identification of congestion in case of HF decompensation [53].

### 5.2.4 Pedometers and Activity Trackers

One of the common symptoms of HF is the restricted exercise ability that prevents people performing daily tasks. Regular physical activity is recommended for HF patients to improve their functional capacity [56–58]. Walking is a safe and efficient form of physical activity, and pedometers are used for counting the number of steps [56]. In this way, the activity levels of patients can be remotely monitored using biosensing products integrated into accessories and clothing [3].

Pedometers and activity trackers are portable electronic devices which measure the distance traveled by the patient using a global positioning system (GPS) receiver. Since the step size of each individual varies, a calibration is required for determining the accurate number of steps. There are also pedometers including inertial microelectromechanical system (MEMS) sensors for detecting the number of steps utilizing the body accelerations in three spatial dimensions [58–60]. Using these portable devices, clinicians can track the total physical activity of HF patients, and in case of severe reduction in physical activity, patients can be called for medical care. This also helps HF patients in tracking their own physical data and taking preventive precautions before being faced with more serious HF symptoms.

### 5.2.5 Implanted Pressure Sensors

The concept of sensors-based device monitoring is a prime prospect for improving the outcomes of any long-lasting HF disease supervision [61]. With the rapid increase of cardiac implantable sensors in HF patients, some HF monitoring strategies have taken broader advantage of such chips [61]. Certainly, the usage of implantable sensors has several advantages over other HF monitoring standards (such as in-clinic monitoring, where the patient acts as the sensor during the live physical contact), which are mainly limited by several factors including subjectivity, variable patient ability, and poor sensitivity to symptoms during a premature HF diagnosis process [61].

In addition, implantable sensors have several advantages, including live continuous monitoring, autonomous assessment based on subjective measurements, and the ability to provide a timely profile about a particular patient with relative ease in analyzing its medical status.

One of the chief methods in identifying deteriorating HF can be implantable devices such as pressure sensors [62]. It is worth mentioning that the early detection of signs of any clinical condition deterioration permits rapid medical preventive intervention to optimize and identify an HF status. Consequently, it is not only healthcare specialists who can take advantage of a decrease in medical treatments and monotonous checks, but also patients who can profit from effective supervision of their HF status. Indeed, over the past decade, and with the increasing recognition of the value of monitoring HF, there has been an exponential growth in the insertion of devices for cardiovascular therapy [62]. Employing implantable pressure sensors in cardiovascular therapy-based devices represents an exceptional prospect for constant tending of a patient's clinical HF status through real-time measuring of physical activity and cardiac rhythms/events, as well as sensing any health malfunction by monitoring different cardiac pressures.

A key factor in diagnosing an HF is an elevation of the ventricular filling pressures and, therefore, much of the focus of HF disease monitoring has centered on achieving the lowest ventricular filling pressures possible with medical and device-based therapy. Consequently, the opportunity for continuous monitoring of cardiac pressure via implantable sensors has been shown to be an attractive opportunity. Early effort [63] established the safety and practicability of long-term cardiac pressure-based sensors monitoring and also recognized an outstanding connection between the implantable heart pressure sensors measurements and the HF attack. From this perspective, coupling a pressure transducer to a heart ventricle lead of a pacemaker or defibrillator provides the simplest form of continuous cardiac pressure monitoring. Through implanted pressure sensors, three major classes of arterial pressures can be remotely supervised to mirror a patient's HF status: (i) the right ventricular (RV) pressure, (ii) the left ventricular (LV) pressure, and (iii) the pulmonary arterial (PA) pressure.

Indeed, a patient's RV pressure can be monitored using a piezoelectric-based chip mounted at the tip of a pacemaker lead in the RV outflow area [64]. Then, the arterial diastolic pressures can be estimated by taking the value of the RV pressure at the peak time (first rate of change of the pressure with respect to the time) [65]. A particular study on a RV pressure-guided treatment group [66] showed a decrease in HF hospitalization and a reduction of about 20% in the need for intravenous diuretics. A subsequent analysis on the same group showed an approximately 20% reduction in HF events. Furthermore, high RV pressure was correlated to an augmented risk of ventricular tachyarrhythmias [67, 68].

The left atrial (LA) pressure sensor is composed of a sensor lead positioned at the interventricular septum and attached to a coil antenna for telemetry of sensor signals from the LA. In a severe HF patient trial [69], the use of LA pressure monitoring resulted in a fall in LA pressure and proved the stability, safety, and efficacy (potential risk of pulmonary embolism) of LA pressure sensors.

Various studies, including that of Abraham et al. [39], have proved that an increase in the intracardiac and the PA pressures comes before the commencement of an HF decompensation. A pressure transducer, such as the CardioMEMS HF sensor [70, 71], can be arranged in any branch of the pulmonary artery. Such a sensor allows monitoring of the PA pressure to improve the outcomes of HF patients. The trial by Smeets et al. [10] showed that daily measurement of PA pressures in addition to ordinary care led to an approximate 40% reduction in HF-related hospitalization and improved quality of life scores compared with the control group, while the duration of an in-patient stay was significantly shortened from almost 4 to 2 days.

The CardioMEMS HF sensor [39, 72, 73] is the first clinically proven HF monitoring system confirmed to significantly decrease HF hospital admissions and improve quality of life [39]. Additionally, it has been clinically demonstrated to reduce hospital admissions by about 60% over an average of 1 year [72]. This HF system, almost batteryless, revealed many benefits, including the use of sensor information without the patient needing to visit the clinic or hospital as the system regularly communicates the measurements wirelessly to the respective care person/doctor.

In summary, remote wireless pressure sensors-based systems are valuable apparatuses that can support several HF clinics by keeping the respective patients regulated, out of the medical health centers, and a relatively stable quality of life [74]. As cardiac pressure sensors-based monitoring systems become automated in terms of data collection, smarter and smaller remote nursing will evolve into an essential piece of HF patients' health evaluation, especially with the recently developed emphasis on virtual visits in light of the COVID-19 pandemic [74].

### 5.2.6 Lung Ultrasound

Lung ultrasound (LUS) is an accurate non-invasive method that can be used for quantification of extravascular lung water (EVLW) in patients with known or suspected HF [75]. It is a safe, efficient, and reliable method [76].

The concept of LUS in critical care and cardiovascular medicine is perhaps seen as new idea emerging over the past few decades, but its origin dates back much further – to 1946 [77]. It was neglected for a long time [78] due to the belief that lungs are an inadequate medium for ultrasound waves because of high air content [79]. The discovery of B-lines artifacts and utilizing their presence and summation as an indicator for HF made LUS significant in cardiology. The study of ultrasound includes three essential modes that form soft tissue imaging: (i) amplitude mode (A-mode), (ii) motion mode (M-mode), (iii) brightness mode (B-mode) [80]. In this section, we are limiting discussion to the third mode only.

The LUS for HF applications relies mostly on B-lines artifacts detection. The presence of multiple and diffuse B-lines is correlated with the presence of EVLW [81, 82].
