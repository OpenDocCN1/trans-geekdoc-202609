With the advances in sensor technologies, it is also possible to predict the HF worsening state remotely before the hospitalization and clinical tests. For this purpose, non-invasive or minimally invasive sensor measurements are performed via remote patient monitoring (RPM) systems [15]. These RPM methods continuously measure the blood pressure [24], body weight [25], physical activity [19], bioimpedance [26–28], and ventricular filling pressures [29–31] of HF patients remotely, and predict the impending HF decompensation before there are serious symptoms. It is reported that RPM trials have reduced the all-cause and HF-related mortality of HF patients [32, 33], prevented unnecessary HF-related hospitalizations [34], and increased the life quality of HF patients [35].

## 8.5 ML Techniques for Detection and Diagnosis of HF

In recent years, ML has gained a growing importance in cardiovascular medicine, especially for the detection and diagnosis of HF. This is mainly due to the huge amounts of data recorded using different medical devices, such as electronic health records (EHRs), multimodality images, and mobile health sensors, which requires powerful and smart algorithms to extract pertinent information, predict HF, and detect and classify HF [36, 37]. Based on the nature of the ML algorithms used for detection and diagnosis of HF, four classes can be identified:

- 1. **Supervised learning models**: Annotated datasets are used to train this kind of model, in which for each item of input data there is a label referring to the nature of HF. After training the ML model, a test data set is used for its validation which includes labels. Accordingly, different ML metrics are then used to estimate the accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, etc. Classification and regression are notable supervised learning models [38].

- 2. **Unsupervised learning models**: Data sets employed for training this kind of model do not include labels. Their main role is to discriminate between the different HF types by using algorithms relying on clustering, one-class classification, dimensionality reduction, etc. [39].

- 3. **Semi-supervised learning models**: These combine supervised learning and unsupervised learning through the use of both unlabeled data and labeled data during the training process. Explicitly, this is usually achieved by using a lesser amount of annotated data with a hefty amount of unlabeled data [9].

- 4. **Reinforcement learning models**: The main advantage of these models is that they do not require an annotated data set for their training. In addition, the results are not directly associated with the input data, hence these models can learn from experience. Specifically, these models can detect the HF by (i) improving their presentation using their association with the environment, and (ii) figuring out the manner for discussing their faults and getting the adequate outcome by assessing and testing different prospects [39].

In this context, ML and big data analytics play an essential role in modern medicine. ML provides efficient solutions to alleviate the difficulties usually encountered by physicians when they are struggling to compute and analyze big data recorded using medical devices [40]. For instance, using ML in the field of cardiology means that detection, diagnosis, and prediction of HF can be effectively conducted. In doing so, different steps should be carefully followed:

- 1. **Data collection**: Different kinds of data can be collected to help in predicting HF. Usually, they are classified into three categories: *personal data*, such as gender, age, weight, smoking/no-smoking, etc.; *clinical data*, which are gleaned using wearables, imaging techniques, echocardiography (ECG), and EHRs; and *omics data*, which are gathered by analyzing cardiac tissue or blood and include genomics, translatomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, proteomics, and lipidomics [41].

- 2. **Data preprocessing**: This step is important as it is responsible for cleaning data. Therefore, it removes noise, filters data by removing incomplete samples, irrelevant and redundant features, and/or replaces incomplete data with the mean or median of their neighbors. This step helps later in simplifying the data analysis process and ensuring data quality and consistency [42].

- 3. **Feature extraction and selection**: This is of significant importance in most classification and regression applications as it aims to extract the most relevant features that enable an accurate and robust HF analysis. Based on the objective of the ML-based solution and collected data, various kinds of data can be extracted in time domain, frequency domain, entropy domain, multimodal domain, etc. in order to rank the features corresponding to the most important HF risk factors [42].

- 4. **Classification**: This aims to categorize and classify extracted features into different classes with reference to the nature of the ML model deployed (supervised, unsupervised, semi-supervised, or reinforcement learning), and hence identify the HF (left-sided, right-sided, diastolic, or systolic). Conventional ML models have been widely used in the previous two decades, such as support vector machine (SVM), decision tree (DT), K-nearest neighbor (k-NN), random forest (RF), Naive Bayes, and ensemble classifiers. However, nowadays, increasing attention is devoted to using deep learning models such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), denoising autoencoder (DAE), deep belief networks (DBNs), and long short-term memory (LSTM). Moreover, deep reinforcement learning has more recently begun to receive much attention for developing data-driven clinical decision support tools [43].

## 8.6 HF Diagnosis Using Biopsy Images

The diagnosis of HF usually relies on physical examination, lab tests, and clinical history. However, in some patients, these diagnosis methods fail to identify signs of HF. Alternatively, endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) is the gold standard for identifying and grading HF. Nirschl et al. proposed an automated deep learning model for the recognition of HF from whole slide images of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) tissue [44]. The introduced neural network architecture is an adaptation of the work of Janowczyk and Madabhushi [45]. The network is formed by alternating convolutional, batch normalization, and rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation layers. The model has more than 13,000 tunable weights trained based on a data set of 1144 regions of interests (ROI) from 209 whole slide images from 94 patients with HF and 115 subjects without HF. Several CNN architectures were used such as AlexNet, GoogLeNet, ResNet50, and ResNet50. These models were compared with the traditional feature engineered approach using WND-CHARM [46]. The latter extracts 4059 features and an RF classifier is trained based on these features. The held-out test set was blindly and independently evaluated by two expert pathologists to compare with the ML models. The clinicians were given unlimited time to complete the task. Cohen’s kappa inter-rater agreement between the two pathologists was 0.40 with an individual accuracy of 75% at patient level. The performance metrics used are accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and AUC (area under ROC curve). The performance of the deep learning model in terms of AUC was 0.97 compared to 0.95 for RF and 0.75 for the pathologists. The CNN model shows 99% sensitivity and 94% specificity. These results are obtained at ROI level. The analysis at patient level also showed an advantage of the deep learning approach.
