The CardioMEMS HF sensor [39, 72, 73] is the first clinically proven HF monitoring system confirmed to significantly decrease HF hospital admissions and improve quality of life [39]. This HF system, almost batteryless, revealed many benefits, including the use of sensor information without the patient needing to visit the clinic or hospital as the system regularly communicates the measurements wirelessly to the respective care person/doctor.

In summary, remote wireless pressure sensors-based systems are valuable apparatuses that can support several HF clinics by keeping the respective patients regulated, out of the medical health centers, and a relatively stable quality of life [74]. As cardiac pressure sensors-based monitoring systems become automated in terms of data collection, smarter and smaller remote nursing will evolve into an essential piece of HF patients’ health evaluation, especially with the recently developed emphasis on virtual visits in light of the COVID-19 pandemic [74].

# 5.2.6 Lung Ultrasound

Lung ultrasound (LUS) is an accurate non-invasive method that can be used for quantification of extravascular lung water (EVLW) in patients with known or suspected HF [75]. It is a safe, efficient, and reliable method [76].

The concept of LUS in critical care and cardiovascular medicine is perhaps seen as new idea emerging over the past few decades, but its origin dates back to much further – to 1946 [77]. It was neglected for a long time [78] due to the belief that lungs are an inadequate medium for ultrasound waves because of high air content [79]. The discovery of B-lines artifacts and utilizing their presence and summation as an indicator for HF made LUS significant in cardiology. The study of ultrasound includes three essential modes that form soft tissue imaging: (i) amplitude mode (A-mode), (ii) motion mode (M-mode), (iii) brightness mode (B-mode) [80]. In this section, we are limiting discussion to the third mode only.

The LUS for HF applications relies mostly on B-lines artifacts detection. The presence of multiple and diffuse B-lines is correlated with the presence of EVLW [81, 82]. In a typical B-mode, the ultrasound pulses construct a 2D image [83]. In a normally aerated lung, ultrasound images will show A-lines consisting of regularly spaced horizontal lines and reverberation artifacts between two bordering surfaces, which are the probe/body surface and the pleura/air in alveoli [84]. An image for a lung that contains A-line artifacts is usually called dry lung.
