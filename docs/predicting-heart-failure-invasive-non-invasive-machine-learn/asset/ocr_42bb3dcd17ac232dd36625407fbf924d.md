LUS is a powerful tool for pulmonary and cardiology imaging, there are some limitations, including the dependence on the skill of the practitioner, the quality and the setting of the hardware [113], and the lack of specificity as B-lines can be present in many other conditions. Nevertheless, the future is open for LUS to be further developed and specific with the integration of new algorithms and reliability on deep learning methods [114].

# 5.2.7 Sound Sensors

The flow of blood in and out of the heart and the movement of the structures responsible for controlling this flow allow the heart to make sounds [115]. There are four types of heart sounds: (i) primary heart sounds in healthy adults, including the first heart sound (S1) and the second heart sound (S2) [115–118], (ii) extra heart sounds including gallop rhythms of the third heart sound (S3) and fourth heart sound (S4) [119–123], (iii) heart murmurs, which are present in congenital abnormalities [124–128], (iv) other abnormal sounds such as adventitious sounds, click [129, 130], rubs [131], snaps [132], knocks [133], and plops [134–136].

Before the invention of the stethoscope in 1816 by Laënnec, listening to the sounds of heart was not possible without physicians applying their ears to the chest of the patient [137]. Stethoscopes enabled listening to and interpreting heart sounds for diagnosis. The contribution of scientists and introduction of physical concepts and technological additions have allowed us to arrive at sound sensors for the classification of heart sounds.

The normal sounds in healthy adults are S1 and S2 [138]. The presence of other sounds could be an indicator for cardiovascular problems. For instance, the presence of S3 sound could be an indicator for pathological problems such as anemia, thyrotoxicosis, mitral regurgitation, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, aortic and tricuspid regurgitation, and LV dysfunction [135]. The S4 sound can result from hypertension, aortic stenosis and regurgitation, severe mitral regurgitation, cardiomyopathy, and ischemic heart disease [139]. The ability to distinguish the different heart sounds is uniform across all practitioners, especially as it is difficult to distinguish some sounds, like the S3 sound, which is crucial for HF diagnosis. Therefore, it became important to move toward the automatic classification of heart sound signals in order to achieve better diagnosis, through signals detected with, for example, stethoscopes, electret microphones or piezoelectric acoustic sensors [140–142].

Over the past few decades, multiple sensors have emerged for better detection of heart sounds based on phonocardiography and echocardiography. The sensors focus on improving signal processing and noise reduction. In 2004, Reed et al. developed a model for the production of heart sounds through a prototype based on wavelet decomposition of sounds and a neural network-based classifier that could detect abnormal heart sounds, which was very useful in predicting HF [143].
