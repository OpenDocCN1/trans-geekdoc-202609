
## Applications of Machine Learning for Predicting Heart Failure

Sabri Boughorbel, Yassine Himeur, Huseyin Enes Salman, Faycal Bensaali, Faisal Farooq, and Huseyin Cagatay Yalcin

### 8.1 Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a chronic disease in which the cardiac system fails to pump the normal blood level in the body, leading to a reduced oxygen level in organs and tissues. It is a global health burden that creates a major challenge for the healthcare systems worldwide. The global prevalence of HF is 64.34 million cases, corresponding to 8.52 cases per 1000 inhabitants. This translates to 9.9 million years lost due to disability. In terms of costs, the HF burden accounts for US$346.17 billion [1]. In the Arab Gulf Region, the prevalence of HF is about 1–2% of the population [2] accounting for more than 4 million patients. The average affected population is 10 years younger than in developed countries [2, 3]. Some of the main risk factors of HF are sex, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and previous heart conditions. While, the prevalence of HF as a function of age peaks for older people over 70, a recent study showed that HF risk in the younger population is higher for obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and smoking. These results emphasize the importance of preventive measures across adult life [4, 5].

Due to the progressive and chronic nature of HF, high hospitalization rates and emergency visits are reported for HF patients. About one-fourth of HF patients are readmitted for medical care within a month, and at least half of these readmissions are related to HF symptoms [6]. High hospitalization rates increase the work burden of medical professionals and also increase healthcare expenditure. The healthcare costs of HF management are estimated to be about 1–2% of all healthcare expenditure [7, 8].

The early prediction of HF risk is important in enabling preventive action and limiting the consequences of HF. The traditional method for developing risk factor models for HF is typically using logistic regression models with general health data. Such an approach suffers from a limited predictive power when a large population is used to fit the models. Since logistic regression is a linear model, it cannot capture non-linearity in complex and noisy data. Machine learning (ML) approaches provide good alternatives to traditional HF risk scoring and prediction. High model capacity allows for a better predictive performance limiting the underfitting problem observed with logistic regression models. Several ML approaches incorporate interpretability elements to help the user understanding which variables are the most predictive. In the section 8.2, we provide the reader with a brief introduction to the field of ML. In the following sections we give an introduction to the use of ML for the diagnosis of HF.

### 8.2 Brief Explanation of ML Techniques for Medicine

ML is the field responsible for developing methods and tools that can learn and make decisions based on data. It can be viewed as the intersection of several fields such as computer science and statistics. Software development has recently contributed significantly to the rapid progress of the field. Before the emergence of ML, intelligent programs were hard-coding rules derived from domain experts. These expert systems quickly became very complex and incapable of coping with real-world applications. ML automatically derives these rules from the data. Hidden patterns can be discovered in the data and be used to detect disease, anomalies, and other events. In ML, there is no need to explicitly write these rules as ML inherently captures them during model training.

Because of the digitalization of healthcare, capturing, sharing, and delivering massive amounts of data has become a high priority. ML plays an essential role by addressing the challenges posed by the huge quantity of data. It tends to demonstrate that collecting and processing various data types from different sources, such as electronic health record data, raw patient data, diagnosis results, resource/patients provision, healthcare personnel information, along with a variety of “big data” pertaining to human-to-human interactions (from cellular operators, traffic, airlines, social media, etc.) can efficiently improve healthcare services [9]. In this context, ML helps healthcare organizations meet the growing demand for medical care, improving operations, and lowering costs. At the same time, ML innovation helps healthcare experts in (i) effectively predicting, detecting, and treating diseases, (ii) speeding up decision-making while guaranteeing more accurate, precise, and personalized cares, and (iii) optimizing the use and allocation of scarce healthcare resources [10, 11]. ML techniques are capable of automating tedious and repetitive tasks such as speech-to-text translation, structuring clinical notes, finding landmarks in radiology and pathology images, generating alerts and reminders, etc. Recently, chatbots have been successfully used to initiate remote consultations and tackle the growing demand for medical care during the pandemic. ML can offload many tasks from clinicians, allowing them to free up time to improve patient care.

The first step in building ML models for HF diagnosis is to prepare a training data set. We consider the example of HF diagnosis using electrocardiogram (ECG) data. The training dataset is retrospectively collected and here the subject outcomes are known. This means that for each recorded ECG in the data set, it is already known whether it corresponds to a patient with HF or it corresponds to a subject who did not have HF. The information attached to each ECG sample (HF or no HF) is typically called the labels. This scenario, where labels are available, is known as supervised learning. When labels are not available, an unsupervised learning problem is addressed. Once the data is annotated and ready, an ML model can be trained to classify whether the ECG corresponds to HF or not. There is extensive literature and also prior work on the different types of ML models. Each model has a set of parameters to be estimated based on the training data. Once trained, the model can be applied to new data and predict the label (HF event or no HF event).

### 8.3 Detection and Diagnosis of HF

The growing number of HF patients and increasing healthcare costs indicate the importance of the early diagnosis of HF for efficient treatment planning. For the diagnosis of HF, several clinical tests including blood tests, chest radiography, echocardiography, and ECG are performed in addition to physical examination and disease history investigation of the patient [12, 13]. A research study has shown that the deep learning model trained with ECG alone has high sensitivity and specificity in detecting HF [14]. Several criteria, such as Framingham, Boston, European Society of Cardiology (ESC), and the Gothenburg criteria, are employed by evaluating the clinical test data to decide on the presence of HF [15]. According to the ESC guidelines, the patient's clinical history, observed symptoms, physical activity, and resting ECG are examined at the first step [10]. The symptoms of HF can be summarized as ankle swelling, dyspnea, fatigue due to elevated jugular venous pressure, pulmonary crackles, and peripheral edema [16–18]. If all examinations mentioned are normal, the presence of HF is not expected. If any of the initial stage evaluations are abnormal, plasma natriuretic peptides are recommended to be measured and, after this measurement, the clinicians decide on whether there is a further need for echocardiography [14]. Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) is a protein secreted by the ventricular musculature in response to volume or pressure overload. The natriuretic peptide family is important for volume and blood pressure regulation. The presence of BNP in blood plasma is being used as a biomarker for diagnosing HF. Studies have shown that the use of BNP can improve HF diagnosis compared with the use of echocardiography [19]. An effective technique for HF diagnosis is to measure the intracardiac filling pressures using implantable pressure sensors by means of cardiac catheterization [20] but the invasive nature of catheterization limits the routine usage of this method [21].

### 8.4 Prediction of HF

After the diagnosis of HF, the severity of the condition is clinically predicted and classified using either the New York Heart Association (NYHA) or the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines [22]. These guidelines are constituted to accurately predict the state of the disease and to plan the most appropriate treatment methods. NYHA guidelines basically use the physical activity capabilities and observed symptoms of the patient for the classification of HF [13]. On the other hand, in the ACC/AHA guidelines, the structural changes and symptoms are described for HF stages [19]. In other words, NYHA guidelines define the exercise capacity and symptomatic status of the patient [14], and ACC/AHA guidelines focus on the developmental stage and progression of HF [10].
