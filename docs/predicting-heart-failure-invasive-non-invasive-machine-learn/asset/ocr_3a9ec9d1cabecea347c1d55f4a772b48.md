Both wearable and implantable sensors provide continuous and remote monitoring for patients without clinical procedures [50]. Implantable biosensors are implanted in the body, while wearable biosensors are worn on the body, such as in clothes, contact lenses, watches, rings, etc. [51].

In classical biosensors, pretreatment steps are often required after sampling, and carrying the samples near the devices/sensors is also required. Although the wearable/implantable biosensors utilize the same transducers as classical biosensors such as optical, electrochemical, and thermal, miniaturization and wearability are the features that make them differ from other biosensors. These new technology biosensors offer unique advantages over other classical biosensors: real-time and continuous data acquirement, saving money and time, reducing the waste of disposable medical materials, and more efficient and quality measurements [52]. Increases and changes in patients’ and professionals’ healthcare demands have also been significant in developing this type of biosensors. Wearable/implantable biosensors, which can also be integrated with devices such as smartphones, can evaluate the data they measure and provide information to emergency services or patient relatives in critical situations (Figure 3.3) [53]. They are used in CVDs to detect heart rate, electrocardiography, and blood pressure [53].

However, some potential technical issues include biocompatibility, biofouling, errors in measurements with small volume samples, and challenges with analysis of saliva, tears, and sweat [49]. Analysis of biological fluids has always been challenging because of the complexity of the medium. Clinical tests based on blood samples are reliable but involve invasive strategies that can be uncomfortable for patients. Saliva, tears, and sweat are examined as non- or minimally invasive biosensor options for diagnostics. Problems such as contamination of saliva samples with foods and the presence of several metabolites with concentration correlation issues – although they can be detected in sweat samples – demonstrate the improvement requirements for wearable sensors [49].
