A biosensor is considered as a very effective analytical tool in medical diagnostics for rapid detection of CVD disease at an early stage. Biosensors have revolutionized CVD diagnosis and prognosis. A biosensing device is made to detect and measure specific target molecules. The underlying principle used in the design of biosensors is to immobilize a biological receptor material on an appropriate transducer’s surface, which further transforms this biochemical signal into measurable electronic signals such as optical, piezoelectric, electrochemical, magnetic, or acoustic signals [7–11].

A variety of CVD detection sensors is available, which are very accurate, quick in detection, easy to carry, and don’t cause any damage to the environment. Recent research studies in microfluidics and advanced nanomaterials have led to the development of more efficient diagnostics requiring little analysis time. They incorporate several clinical assays into a compact, single portable unit. The nanotechnology industry is rapidly growing and creating highly efficient tools based on multidisciplinary research involving nanofluids, drug delivery, microelectronics, and analytical chemistry [12–16].

Current CVD diagnostic techniques require blood tests to assess relevant biomarker levels. A biomarker is measured on a biosample such as a blood, urine, or tissue test. It can also be the data recording obtained from a person’s blood pressure, ECG, etc., and includes imaging tests like an echocardiogram or CT scan.

Different imaging techniques, such as electrocardiograms, computed tomography (CT), and angiograms, are also used for CVD detection.
