
There is a need to personalize the visualization so that students know where they stand when comparing with other students. Students’ need for personalized feedback is not surprising and has been highlighted in the literature (Link & Li, 2015). Moreover, past studies have described the use of multiple visualizations (Mottus et al., 2015; Ramos-Soto et al., 2015). This study provides another concrete example of “personalized feedback.” In this pilot study, students were interested in knowing precisely where they stood compared to students from previous cohorts. Therefore, “personalized feedback” is not limited to what they need to do (i.e., recommendations) but also includes how they are doing. Future projects need to consider the personalized progress of students in addition to offering personalized recommendations.

From a student perspective, the need for sophisticated visualization is clear. However, this raises a question for technical specialists: How can a simple HTML dashboard provide sophisticated features? The project team are not web developers, but there seems to be no simple solution to technically cater for sophisticated visualization. In other words, there may be a need to hire developers to further explore technical options to provide sophisticated visualizations for students. Future project teams should explore this possibility and strike a balance between simple technical design and the needs of students.

## Cost-Effective and Non-Specialist Solutions for All

This project showcases an effective solution for any teacher who wants to try a dashboard, and it is recommended that teachers who are interested in a dashboard start with a similarly simple solution. This project included a simple interface that was still cost-effective and easy to use. While many other past projects (e.g., Elaachak et al., 2015; Mottus et al., 2015; Ramos-Soto et al., 2015) included well-designed dashboards, they were technically sophisticated and required a developer or (in-house) computer scientist to develop. This project, based on results from a student pilot study, was as effective as those in previous studies and offered a satisfying dashboard experience to students. However, this dashboard, the CDR, only requires non-specialist computing knowledge to develop and maintain (`HTML` with `jQuery`). Accordingly, the CDR is appropriate for implementation by teachers without specialist computing knowledge, teaching development funds and/or teaching assistants. Such implementation could provide more opportunities for teachers and students.

## CONCLUSION

This paper describes the design, development and evaluation of a learning dashboard, the CDR. This dashboard has an easy-to-use interface, and students and subject leaders were both satisfied with the design and features. Through an in-depth discussion of the needs of stakeholders, the challenges in development and the process of evaluation, this paper provides sufficient details for learning designers, teachers and AI researchers to explore future opportunities in their contexts and to contribute to the growing body of literature on learning dashboards. The study highlights the need for consultation with stakeholders, flexibility for unexpected use and more sophisticated visualization as well as the potential for easy implementation by teachers without technical knowledge.

This paper provides two important contributions to the field: (1) From a learning dashboard development perspective, the developed CDR is a low-cost solution (instead of a costly development project designed by a contractor), and course lecturers, without much support, can replicate the design and implement CDRs in their own courses; and (2) studies on learning dashboards in the language learning context are rare.
