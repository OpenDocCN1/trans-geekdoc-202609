# FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

The findings of this research study can be found to echo how Vagle (2014) sees phenomenology when he stated that this type of qualitative study is a way of living with a certain way and philosophy in life; it is an encounter of how others live their own experiences in their own conscious intentionalities. Therefore, according to Vagle (2014), engaging in the practice of phenomenology is a craft in and of itself. This specific term, craft, coined to the practice of phenomenology is precisely what the researcher of this exploration undertook as she engaged in an attempt to solve her study’s problem, answer its three central questions, as well as ensure that each of the study’s elements aligned with its design: phenomenology. As she undertook crafting this phenomenological practice, she scrutinized every component of her inquiry through various vantage points, as expected from a phenomenologist who often seeks the essence of things on descriptive, fresh, psychological, and philosophical levels.

In that respect, the first direction and recommendation for future (novice) phenomenologists who may involve in crafting a comprehensive phenomenological exploration like this study is to regard phenomenology as a craft and hone it to the best of their ability and within their own experience, expertise, and practice. The second recommendation for future investigation in this field is that it is paramount that they take upon this onus with an inspiration of the fundamental nature of transcendental phenomenological research. This undertaking includes adopting and practicing the basic elements of descriptive data coding and analysis processes from start to finish. It is especially essential for future phenomenological researchers to keep in mind that descriptive data coding and analysis under the principles of phenomenological school of inquiry focus on the very fundamentals of being a human in a certain context or situation and perceiving that situation with being-ness under conscious intentionality, instead of being directed to an object—or of being-ness, rather than merely of doing or of-ness.

It is through this acknowledgment, understanding, and appreciation that they can then delve more deeply into the basic essence of the meanings behind the descriptions given to them by their human subjects, who know their own being-ness and their own becoming within their own paradigms of change or transformation at any level. In this way, as Vagle (2014) suggests, too, future phenomenological researchers can develop and craft a salient pattern of single themes—or thematic categories—that may help yield a comprehensive and meaningful apprehension of the very existentialist (ontological) and epistemological (knowing) layers of what their study participants really meant as they provided personal accounts of their own lived experiences about a specific phenomenon under inquiry.
