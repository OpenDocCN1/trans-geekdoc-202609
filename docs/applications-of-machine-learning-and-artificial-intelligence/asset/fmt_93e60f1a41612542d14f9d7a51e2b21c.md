
# Support Vector Machines (SVM)

Support vector machines (SVM) is a supervised Machine Learning algorithm for analyzing data and patterns. SVM constructs a set of a hyperplane in high dimensional spaces. These planes can be used for classification. It is a non-probabilistic binary classifier. For that reason, it can be applied to computer vision-related problems, especially classification. For example, human skin classification. During training, an annotated set of skin and non-skin pixels is given to the SVM for training. The SVM has a training algorithm that constructs a model that then attempts to assign pixels into the two training classes, thus making it a non-probabilistic binary linear classifier. An SVM model creates a representation of the pixels as points in space, mapped, so that skin and non-skin pixels are divided by clear and wide as a possible gap. New pixels are then mapped into the same space and predicted to fall in a category based on which side of the gap they fall on. (Han, 2006) utilized an SVM based on active learning to detect skin pixels for gesture recognition, claiming that it is transferable to other applications.

# Discussion on Supervised Machine Learning Algorithms

Supervised machine learning algorithms are dominantly used to solve various computer vision problem nowadays. Artificial neural Network can detect complex nonlinear relationship between variables. It requires less statistical training and many of the algorithms are available. These algorithms can be used for classification and regression problems. However, in artificial neural network, user does not have the independence of making all the decisions, it works as a “black box”. The result of ANN highly depends on available good “training data”. In some domain of computer vision problem there are not many training data publicly available, e.g., medical image analysis. Nonetheless, K-nearest neighbors (KNN) is a simple algorithm and classify quickly compared to ANN. It can also handle noisy data. But, as the number of attribute increases, it becomes computationally expensive. As KNN gives equal importance to all attributes, the classification result is poor. Naïve bayes is a simple and useful algorithm for large dataset, it can be used for binary and multiclass classification. However, it is more complex and computationally expensive (Uddin, Khan, Hossain, & Moni, 2019). Overfitting for the model is also a problem for Naïve Bayes algorithm.

## Human Skin Detection

Computer vision has a wide variety of implementation of supervised learning. In this section, a well-known computer vision problem- human skin detection is discussed. Human skin detection is widely used primitive in image processing. Various applications over images with human content employ skin detection as a primary step. Skin detection is the process of discriminating human skin pixels from non-skin pixels in an image or video (Zuo, Fan, Blasch, & Ling, 2017). Various human-computer interaction (HCI) applications – face detection, face tracking (Mohanty & Raghunadh, 2016; Shakir¹, George, & Tuama, 2015), gesture recognition (Rautaray & Agrawal, 2015), computational health informatics, web content filtering profoundly depends on efficient skin detection. Skin detection is also used as a primary step for detecting people in general. The main challenge of human skin detection is the large variations of human appearance with other objects. Besides, there are many factors that make skin detection a challenging problem. Among them, the notable one is variations in illumination. Variation in illumination is the most important factor that influences skin segmentation performance (St¨orring, Koˇcka, Andersen, & Granum, 2003). A variation of illumination (indoor, outdoor, shadows, presence of various light colors) produces changes in human skin tone. Shadows on the face from direct lighting may obscure some facial regions. It can be a reason for rendering a facial shape unevenly.

On the other hand, a bright facial region may be due to the natural diffusion of light, causing extra brightness on the face. Additionally, various ethnic groups have many skin tones. Skin color tone varies from person to person within the same ethnic group and also between members of different ethnic groups. Caucasians, Africans, and Asians have different skin hues ranging from white to dark to brown (Tan, Chan, Yogarajah, & Condell, 2011). Shadow in an image and complex background is another crucial part of correct skin detection. Many real-world background objects have similar skin colors. Skin color can be close to other image artifacts such as furniture, clothes, hair color, etc. The diversity is virtually unlimited. This causes skin detectors to produce false-positive detection (Kakumanu, Makrogiannis, & Bourbakis, 2007).In addition, image sources are an uncontrollable factor. Images available on the internet, newspapers may have been modified and filtered possibly multiple times. This makes color information problematic. Camera characteristics cause additional difficulty for skin detection. Because of these and other reasons, it is challenging to choose a specific trichromatic color space for any specific application. In addition, designing skin detection models to detect human skin under all possible constraints has not been achieved. For that reason, it is demanding to have a human skin detection method that can overcome the aforementioned challenges in separating skin and non-skin pixels.

Researchers have been working to find a technique that will be able to detect skin in spite of challenges. Skin detection can be considered a binary classification problem. There are two principal types of skin detection – pixel-based or region-based. In pixel-based detection, a pixel is classified compared with its neighbors as either a skin pixel or not. Skin detection that is based on specific trichromatic color space and specific color component value ranges is an example. On the other hand, region-based skin detection focuses on the spatial arrangement of skin pixels with additional information of intensity and texture. A few researchers have used texture (Dahmani & Larabi, 2014; Holden, Lee, & Owens, 2005; Kang, Lee, & Jung, 2004; Vadakkepat, Lim, De Silva, Jing, & Ling, 2008) information in combination with color-based methods. Wavelets, contourlets (Chai, Phung, & Bouzerdoum, 2001; Jaimes & Sebe, 2007) are among the textural features that have been used in conjunction with color cues. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have received wide attention more recently for image classification and object detection. But CNN's do not work well-defined relationship between a pixel and its neighbors. Since skin pixels are located close to each other spatially, CNN's lack the desired performance outcomes. Given all of the previous work, color cues have provided the dominant source of information. In spite of the emphasis on color-based analysis, a considerable number of applications will benefit from a system that can perform skin detection in the absence of color cues. Moreover, approaches mentioned above depend heavily on color information that, in the absence of global information, can lead to incorrect detection. Given the number of methods from which to choose, it is difficult to pick one which would project satisfactory accuracy under the diverse condition

## Bayesian Statistics Based Skin Detection

In Statistical Models, skin detection is considered a probabilistic problem. There are many techniques based on the general distribution of skin and skin color that use specific color models in order to better detect skin. Based on an extensive training set, these strategies estimate the probability that a certain pixel is associated with the skin. These methods use Histogram, Lookup Tables, Distance-Based Detectors, Naive Bayes, and Gaussian Distribution.
