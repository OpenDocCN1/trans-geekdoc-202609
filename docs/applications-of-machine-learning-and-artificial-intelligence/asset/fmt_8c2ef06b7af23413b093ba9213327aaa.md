
According to the University’s English language requirements, students must take two English for Academic Purposes (EAP) courses (among three of them) to learn the genre and gain fundamental academic writing and speaking skills. Both courses involve learning to use academic sources and the assessment requirements of writing academic essays and delivering academic oral presentations. The courses to be taken depend on students’ English exam results obtained in HKDSE. The CDR tool focused on these three courses, which are core English courses taken by three-quarters of students. To satisfy the intended learning outcomes of these courses, students are required to complete three assessments in each of these courses: two writing assignments and one speaking assignment. The genres of writing and speaking assignment depends on courses. For example, in one of the courses, the first two assignments were a 500-word problem-solution essay, an 800-word discursive essay; and this is followed by the academic presentation assessment. In all three courses, students are given an overall assessment grade and a grade for every writing component, including content, organization, language and referencing, or for every speaking component, including content, delivery, language and pronunciation/fluency. The CDR was developed based on the overall grade and components grades of the first writing assignment in each course. The mechanism of the CDR will be explained in detail in the next section.

## DESIGN AND DEVELOPMENT OF COURSE DIAGNOSTIC REPORT

### Development of Course Diagnostic Report

Due to the developments in AI and dashboards, a project team was established to develop a dashboard for an English for Academic Purpose program (three courses) in a Hong Kong university. This intelligent dashboard was named “Course Diagnostic Report” (CDR). This section describes the development and features of the CDR, along with key concerns/problems identified during the development process.

### Planning and Development

The CDR project has gone through several main stages of development. After the project team was assembled, with a teaching faculty and a project assistant/programmer, the team brainstormed ideas for the project, consulted stakeholders, developed prototypes and evaluated their effectiveness. This project was supported by a project grant administered by the university.

During the brainstorming stage, a range of possible algorithms, development languages and hosting platforms were suggested. Regarding algorithms, it was suggested to mine past students’ data to develop a decision tree or some other sophisticated data mining methods to predict if students would do poorly in the course. In terms of development language and hosting platform, it was originally suggested that an application on a mobile device be developed in some designated programming language. There were also suggestions to develop separate student and teacher versions so that both parties could maximize the tool. These ideas were presented to five subject leaders who coordinate these three courses. Their ideas had to be carefully considered because their support in the use of these tools, despite having project funding, was crucial for implementing the pilot successfully.

During the consultation stage, the project team presented initial ideas to the subject leaders and sought their support. While the subject leaders, in general, supported the development of CDR, their concerns/interests were quite different from those of the project team. A detailed discussion of these meetings (as a discussion on innovation in higher education) was documented in another paper by Foung (2019). From a project operation perspective, Table 1 offers an overview of the differences between the project team and subject leaders that can be resolved.

Subject leaders believed that the CDR, as a project, would impact the workload of teachers, and that was an influential starting point. Accordingly, it was decided that there would only be one student platform that students could use with minimal assistance (so that teachers do not need to solve technical problems). Specifically, the CDR platform should be simple and require no installation; hence, simple HTML pages were considered most desirable. To allow the CDR itself to provide simple justification for the feedback given to students, simple averages were suggested instead of more complicated data mining methodologies. One very specific concern of subject leaders was the language/text to be presented in the CDR. The subject leaders did not want the text to cause any stress or anxiety for the students (or subsequently to teachers). Instead, the text presented to students should be motivating and show them clear ways to improve. Therefore, the project team worked closely with the students to develop the texts to be presented in the CDR.

After the consultation stage, the project team was confident that the fundamental development principles could guide the development of the CDR. The part-time student programmer took charge of the development. The CDR consisted of several HTML pages supported with jQuery (i.e., with IF operation based on students’ input), and the student programmer developed one prototype for testing. One major challenge was that the student programmer was busy and the development requirement (i.e., page design and wording) needed to be appropriate. The student programmer was unable to make changes to the CDR after the initial draft. The project leader had a basic understanding of jQuery and HTML and was able to continue with the development process. Several prototypes were developed to obtain the subject leaders’ feedback, and further revisions were made to enhance readability or provide accurate resource links for the CDR. Both the project team and subject leaders were pleased with a final prototype for piloting (described in the Results section). The following section describes the features of this prototype.

### Piloted Course Diagnostic Report

#### Operation

The CDR was designed to be easy to use, motivating and interpretable. After considering the above-mentioned design principles and numerous rounds of consultation with subject leaders, CDR prototypes were developed for piloting with students. The features of the CDR that were piloted with students are described below.

The tool was hosted as a web page accessible through a public URL. Students were expected to use the tool to understand their progress after completing the first assignment in their course. After clicking on the URL, students are presented with a landing page with a general introduction of the CDR and radio buttons for entering their grades from the first assignment (Figure 1). The first assignments of all courses included four component grades and one overall grade. For example, the first assignment for one of the courses was a problem-solution essay, and the grades to be entered for this assignment included Content Development, Organization, Language Use, Academic Conventions and the Overall Assignment grade. After entering their grades with the radio buttons, students are shown a “diagnostic report” page with the grades entered, general comments and actions students could take to do better on future assignments. After viewing this report online, students can enter their email address and have this report sent to their email.
