
# Following Husserl’s Descriptive Phenomenological Method

Following Husserl’s descriptive phenomenological method, other contemporary pioneers of descriptive phenomenology such as Amedeo Giorgi (1979, 1986, & 2009) and Moustakas (1994) have also developed on this method, by proposing alternate approaches to phenomenological data analysis. Since its inception in the 1970’s (Giorgi, Giorgi, & Morley, 2017), the phenomenological psychological data analysis method has paved the way for a more lucid picture of the consciousness of participants having lived the experiences of a particular phenomenon. It was this descriptive data analysis method that this phenomenological study heavily relied on. That is, amongst any other type of qualitative data analysis methods commonly used in phenomenological research studies, descriptive data analysis method was selected for this inquiry.

## Rationale for Choice of Descriptive Data Coding and Analysis

There were two reasons that helped support this specific data analysis related selection:

1.  The research problem supported by the main learning theory (andragogy) and the supporting learning theory (social constructivism), both laying the foundation for the overarching theme of this study (i.e., whether or not the study participants may have felt that they had developed and/or transformed into autonomous English learners).
2.  Ontological, rather than merely epistemological, implications of the study (i.e., again, adult ESL students’ possible developmental change or their transformation—in their identity—into self-directed, independent learners of English, which will further be explained).

The latter, in particular, played a significant role in a way that it helped warrant the philosophical and psychological underpinnings of this phenomenological exploration, where both were inextricably intertwined and both helped justify the selection of the descriptive data analysis method based on the study participants’ personal accounts of their lived experiences pertinent to the phenomenon under study.

According to Giorgi et al. (2017), there is one simple criterion for a well-executed data analysis in a phenomenological research study, which is that the descriptions given by the participants are to be good enough, or adequate. Drawing on this very criterion, the analyses completed from the data collected from each of all 12 adult ESL students are expected to shed sufficient—or simply, good enough—light to a holistic and explicit understanding of the essence of the meanings from individual accounts of these students who commonly lived certain experiences regarding the phenomenon of their individual use of mobile technologies in their English language acquisition and/or development processes. Both the information obtained from audio recordings and participants’ written descriptions on the written LED protocols were analyzed into meaningful analysis units. It is crucial to note at this point that the data obtained from each of the 12 adult English language learners during the one-one-one interviews yielded the type of descriptions that were much fuller compared to those obtained from the written LED protocols, which validates what is suggested by Giorgi, Giorgi, and Morley (2017) on the degree of length, richness, authenticity, and personality of the spoken narratives in contrast with the written ones.

Giorgi et al. (2017) established that the true phenomenological nature of a research study stipulates that only first-person accounts be analyzed and that no third person opinions be included, the researcher being one of the latter. Otherwise, the process of data analysis might be significantly impacted as the data obtained might get distorted and the results skewed or are not generalized enough or transferable to other studies in the field, contrary to what was originally planned. Therefore, the best solution to employ throughout this study’s data analysis was the proper use of transcendental reduction and epoché (Husserl, 1931).

In order to bolster this argument for a successful descriptive phenomenological data analysis process, it was vital to draw on the axioms of Giorgi et al. (2017), who proposed that a researcher needs to try to describe the lived experiences based on the raw data he/she obtains—and do this extremely carefully—and once the thorough phenomenological philosophical analysis of these raw data begins, it needs to occur with the researcher’s use of the phenomenological-psychological reduction. To further support this argument during the data analysis stage, the authors claim that this type of reduction, or this special attitude of the researcher, or epoché, is only successful when the researcher is able to set aside all his/her previous knowledge or judgments in his/her consciousness and considers—and works with—merely the type of the data presented or given to his/her consciousnesses, instead of intuitions that are currently existing in his/her consciousness. In Giorgi, Giorgi, and Morley’s (2017) defense, these raw data presented to the researcher by each participant were then to be very carefully described and analyzed in their entirety.

Thus, in order to have a much clear portrayal of the reason why the aforementioned learning and developmental theories rule this study and the manner in which an individual’s consciousness to and of a certain being (or phenomenon) is shaped with a considerable alteration of his/her thinking, actions, or behaviors in the present or in the future, one could closely look into what Merleau-Ponty (1963) found with respect to the general alteration of behavior. According to Merleau-Ponty (1963), altered behavior appears or manifests itself in a multitude of a series of actions, also called manifold of appearances. Drawing on this prominent figure’s proposed concept, manifold of appearances explained under the descriptive data analysis method and that which is based on the descriptions of a specific school organization’s adult ESL learners selected for one-on-one interviews for this study can help the reader gain a much deeper and clearer understanding of the qualitatively altered and transformed thinking, consciousness, rationale, being, becoming, growth, and development—or the respondents’ new, modified version of their own selves as adults.

## Procedures and Methods of Data Analysis Applied: The 7-Step Van Kaam Method

For the data analysis phase of this study, the applied main method was the 7-step Van Kaam (1966) method,² which included the following steps: Horizonalizing, reduction into invariant constituents (Moustakas, 1994), individual textural descriptions, individual structural descriptions, and finally, formation of composite textural and structural descriptions via synthesis that led to the essence of meanings. According to Moustakas (1994), the organization and analysis of phenomenological data begins when the researcher studies the transcribed interviews and engages in the methods and procedures of phenomenal analysis.
