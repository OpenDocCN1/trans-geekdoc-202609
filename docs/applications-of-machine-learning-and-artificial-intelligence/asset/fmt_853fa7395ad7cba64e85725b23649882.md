
## Outsourcing Risks and Benefits in ISD Implementation

Other than the advantages of especially the second strategy (i.e., outsourcing) that the members of the project team will use, there might also emerge a number of outsourcing-related disadvantages. Confidential information can be compromised. Also, according to Dennis et al. (2009), in-house personnel are not learning the skills needed to create the system. Thus, there may emerge a few downsides of outsourcing during new ISD implementations. It is true that with the outsourcing strategy followed, the ownership of technical skills may shift or be completely replaced. That is, the technical know-how will be different from that of the senior management, middle management, end users, and certainly that of the team’s technical experts engaged in the new ISD implementation. It is also undeniable that budget-related issues might surface, and there is certainly a risk of jeopardizing ISD-related technical or other confidential information.

Nevertheless, outsourcing is still likely to reduce user participation to a certain degree, which is not necessarily bad. Lin and Shao (2000) pontificated that outsourcing tends to reduce user involvement and the system impact is likely to reduce user attitude. The former may not be necessarily detrimental or life-threatening to the lifecycle of a project. However, for example, the lifecycle of a software product development might certainly have been compromised due to over-involvement of end-users, consumers, or the clients. Therefore, as far as the success—or the failure—of SDLC goes, it is plausible to draw the conclusion that issues potentially arising may not necessarily stem from technical failures or lack of technical skills of the project team outsourcing to create that new system. These appear to pose inherent risks with a multilateral approach to the relationship between ISD implementation and risk management. Eventually, by outsourcing during the system implementation, some of the issues associated with ISD management may be mitigated by the project management team.

### Evaluation Plan

Marion (2002) pointed out that organizational culture can be strengthened by Total Quality Management (TQM), which refers to a strategy to increase effective productivity at schools. Effective TQM is implemented during the project controlling and closure phases of this instructor training online course design project.

The e-training program designed for all university instructors will be achieved by capitalizing on an “interpersonal dimension of an organization” (Marion, 2002, p. 245). Marion (2002) also stressed that TQM can be sustained via “tacitly acknowledging the holistic nature of an organizational culture, one that includes all elements of the production process” (p. 245) (see Figure 6).

The educational climate at today’s higher educational institutions puts emphasis on interpersonal relations, which leads each school member to believe that in the lack of a healthy organizational climate wherein communication is not sustained, obtaining productivity is unfeasible. During this project’s controlling and closure phases, the role of SMEs will not be overlooked. The substantive responsibilities of SMEs will greatly contribute to reducing any potential risks and challenges that might be associated with the project. SMEs could also alleviate challenges to this project, particularly if the project manager is not an expert in the e-learning and distant learning fields this project is executed in. The evaluation plan will help determine SMEs’ key roles and participation in the project.

One of the foci of this training instructor proposal outline is the ability to convey the message that successful organizational culture leadership will be exercised as the overall training program quality is evaluated, revisions are done, and the project comes to a conclusion. This will not be possible without the internalization of espoused moral values of universities, along with the embedded instructional skills of each online course instructor, all staff members’ habits of collective thinking, and the teaching philosophy of the educational institution (i.e., unconventional wisdom). Other values that will be respected and appreciated throughout the project quality control and closure stages will be the school culture. All will be maintained through ongoing, formative evaluations during the project implementation and through summative evaluations upon completion of the project.

## Future Research Directions

For future research in the e-learning and online course design and faculty training fields with its managerial applications, it is important to stress the use of social media tools that can greatly influence teaching and learning practices, provided that each online course instructor is encouraged and trained to effectively integrate the use of these applications into their lesson plans and hands-on classroom practices. ## Conclusion

Achieving a sustainable e-learning project implementation in any higher educational organization will involve inclusion. It is imperative that all change agents continuously include the resisting employees, colleagues, or supervisors in the entire process of change. Inviting them to participate in the change process is a precursor to successfully bringing about change across the school. Furthermore, this can lead to a participatory, democratic, and peaceful work environment for all instructors, staff, and program supervisors involved in the e-training program. Regarding participatory leadership, Goleman (1995) pointed out that democratic leaders often build consensus by continued participation. There are other steps to take during these phases of the social networking technology-based training project designed for online course instructors. The project manager of this program created a strong charter with her team, by taking from the three-tier change model formulated by Kotter (1996). This change model will be effective in overcoming potential challenges, one of which is instructors’ inherent resistance to change within the program. Kotter’s (1996) eight-step model that this proposal has been inspired by includes the following: establishing a sense of urgency, building a powerful guiding coalition, creating a vision, communicating this vision, empowerment of others to take actions on this vision, creating short-term wins, consolidation of improvements and generating further change, as well as institutionalizing new approaches for prospective change and continued academic growth at the post-pandemic higher educational institutions.
