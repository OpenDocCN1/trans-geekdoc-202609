
# Learning Analytics and Dashboards in Language Learning

Over the last two decades, the trends of data-tracing and using digital technologies in education have motivated tertiary institutions to consider ways of analyzing and visualizing the large amount of data collected from `LMSs` to aid learning and improve pedagogy (Thomas et al., 2017). A growing number of studies have been focusing on the increasingly common use of learning dashboards. Schwendimann et al. (2017) defined a learning dashboard as a combination and demonstration of single or multiple visualizations of various learning indicators about learners, processes and contexts. Kim et al.’s (2016) learning dashboard was developed to visualize factors like learners’ time and frequency of log-in and their frequency of using the dashboard. Usage frequency was found to be positively related to learners’ learning achievement. Sedrekyan et al. (2020) examined the relationship between dashboard design and the regulatory cognitive and behavioral mechanisms underlying the learning process. They reported a case study on using a learning analytics dashboard to promote learning regulation as a demonstration of their idea.

Other existing studies on dashboards (e.g., Elaachak et al., 2015; Mottus et al., 2015; Ramos-Soto et al., 2015), however, have focused on explaining and proofing concepts without reaching the aims of identifying appropriate information for stakeholders or effective methods of data presentation—let alone evaluating the impact of learning dashboards (Baker, 2007; Few, 2006; Schwendimann et al., 2017). Most of these studies concentrated on university settings, which provide a larger sampling scale and more flexibility for learning analytics (`LA`) research compared to school settings. However, these studies lacked specific visualizations for addressing learning and teaching patterns or a consensus on the usage frequency and patterns of learning dashboards (Schwendimann et al., 2017). Moreover, there have been very few `LA` studies in a Hong Kong higher education context (e.g., Choi et al., 2018; Foung, 2019; Wong & Li, 2019), where discussions about learning dashboards remain unpopular.

## Learning Analytics and Dashboards in Language Learning

`LA` has started to play a significant role in the context of language learning. Wen and Song (2020) investigated the use of `LA` for collaborative language learning in classrooms through the integration of `LA`, learning design and teacher inquiry. Their study suggested that straightforward `LA` indicators and visualization could reflect the process of collaborative writing based on sound theories, which could inform learning design and teacher inquiry. Moreover, they found that it is critical for teachers to believe in the innovative teaching approach and be keen to collaborate with researchers and to focus on their professional development for `LA` to inform the other two factors effectively. Peeters et al. (2020) studied the self-regulated learning tactics of students during peer collaboration on academic writing tasks on social networking sites by applying `LA` methods. They concluded that students’ use of self-regulated learning tactics is positively correlated with their learning performance and that relevant support mechanisms are needed in computer-supported collaborative learning. Viberg et al. (2020) introduced the `MALLAS` framework, aiming to assist learning researchers and designers while supporting second language learners in their self-regulated learning in mobile contexts using `LA`.

There are few studies on the use of learning dashboards in a language learning context. An online dashboard was used to gather information about non-native English-speaking students’ engagement with `Blackboard`’s `LMS` in a graduate-level research writing course, which assisted teachers in their course design and personalization of feedback strategies to address students’ needs and abilities (Link & Li, 2015). Similarly—but more learner-oriented—dashboards were created to examine learning factors, including course outlines, intended usage of activities and learners’ actual online behaviors, and to inform online language learners about the indicators in their language-learning patterns that lead to successful or unsuccessful learning (Thomas et al., 2017). The `TimeTracker App` was designed to help second language learners who are adult migrant workers keep track of the amount of time spent studying the target language and its influence on their engagement and motivation in the continual study of the host language (Viberg et al., 2021).

For learning dashboards to be effective and successfully implemented in any context (not confined to language learning), it is crucial to consider the factors reflected in the studies mentioned above: stakeholders’ (i.e., teachers, professional staff and students) familiarity with the use of online applications, tools and dashboards; learning and teaching indicators and goals; and user-oriented customization of dashboard design and visualization. These factors provide the foundation for designing and developing the `CDR` for our university English program.

# CONTEXT OF THE PROJECT

## Language Background of Students

This research was conducted in an English-medium university in Hong Kong, in which nearly 96% of students speak Chinese/Cantonese as their first language (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, 2020). That is, most students are English as a second language users/speakers, who have fulfilled the minimum English language requirement for university admission: Level 3 in the English Language subject of the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education Examination (`HKDSE`) for local admission; or an overall band score of 6 on the International English Language Testing System (`IELTS`) or a score of 80 on the Internet-based or 550 on the paper-based Test of English as a Foreign Language (`TOEFL`) for international admission (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, 2021). However, most of the students directly promoted from secondary schools are new to academic English in higher education.

## Exposure of Students to Online Platforms

The university has been subscribing to the `Blackboard Collaborate` as the `LMS`, `Microsoft Office 365` for internal staff and student mailing and daily work applications and `Turnitin` for assignment submission and plagiarism checking, to name a few. Upon entering the university, students are given access to the university portal, where all these online learning resources and applications can be found. Corresponding training seminars, guidelines and information technology support services are provided to students (`ITSO`, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, 2021); thus, in general, they have the necessary knowledge for using the university portal and online learning resources.

Nonetheless, students’ usage of these online learning platforms is limited to obtaining brief information, such as course updates, teachers’ scanned handwritten/typed feedback on assignments and assessment results, downloading course materials, uploading assignments, completing compulsory online exercises and checking university emails. Students are not able to track their learning progress by course, semester or academic year on these online platforms. Problems often arise when students are not interested in using these platforms or resources frequently or have even abandoned these platforms, which results in missing important online course information and updates. In light of these issues, the `CDR` tool, aiming to act as an early alert system (Foung, 2019), would attract students’ attention by providing assessment results and suggestions for improvement while contributing new ideas to make fuller use of similar learning dashboards/platforms.
