
# 21st Century Skills in K-12 STEM Education

## The Impact of COVID-19 on STEM Learning

A study by the Brookings Institute compared student’s academic achievement and growth during COVID to their academic achievement and growth from 2019 (Kuhfeld et al., 2020). While they noticed a decline in learning across the board, they found that math and other STEM subjects were the hardest hit — students were further behind in these classes than any others. The researchers speculated that subjects like reading or language arts were easier for parents to teach at home, while the more specialized STEM subjects suffered more from the lack of access to teachers and experts (Kuhfeld et al., 2020). It could also be the case that, in the transition to online learning, STEM education had to give up many of the pedagogical gains it made towards a more engaging, project-based curriculum and revert back to a lecture-based model that is more likely to leave students behind. Research has long suggested that STEM instruction is greatly improved by utilizing more real-life situation applications, promoting cooperation and group work, and establishing an increase in hands-on activities (Myers & Fouts, 1992). Unfortunately, these are the very things that online learning makes more difficult.

## The Urgent Need for 21st Century Skills

Undoubtedly, STEM students are in need of help. They are currently facing unprecedented social and emotional challenges, while at the same time the uncertainties of online and hybrid education have made it harder to deliver these students the 21st century skills that could help them cope. Many educators are barely treading water themselves, and between the difficulties of navigating new learning platforms and improvising new models for education, they have in some cases neglected to attend to the “soft” skills that are so important for student growth. Also, pedagogies that prioritize creativity, critical thinking, and collaboration may simply be harder to execute in online and hybrid class environments. 21st century skills have become more difficult to teach just when students need them most.

## Chapter Goals

The goal of this chapter is to raise awareness of the continued need for 21st century skills in K-12 STEM education and propose a few practical methodologies for incorporating them into a STEM classroom, even when that classroom must exist online. If nothing else, these skills should be considered essential components of a COVID-era education because they teach students how to adapt to unfamiliar problems and communicate difficult emotions effectively (Jerald, 2009). By incorporating more discussion of social and emotional skills into their curriculum, as well as prioritizing collaborative communication in online projects, educators can show their students how to shift between different modes of learning more fluidly and can help them mitigate feelings of isolation. Beyond that, these lessons in communication, information literacy, and adaptability are still the best way to prepare students for a world that can change in an instant, as COVID-19 has so dramatically demonstrated.

## What Are 21st Century Skills?

Ultimately, the appeal of primary and secondary education comes from its ability to prepare students for adult life and the workforce. A study by Kaufman (2019) established that high school principals predominantly believed that the role of high school education was college readiness, preparedness for the workforce, the military, and embracing a socially active life. But the same study found that many in the workforce consider incoming high school and college students to be grossly underprepared for the demands of a modern workplace (Kaufman, 2019). The consensus among most business leaders is that what is lacking are the so called “21st century skills,” which include the critical thinking, teamwork, collaboration, and communication skills unequivocally important for potential employees.

Many different researchers have articulated their own lists of essential 21st century skills, but broadly speaking the term refers to a set of competencies that allow students to navigate a rapidly changing world. These are the skills that go beyond simple course content, including such abstract processes as problem-solving, self-direction, collaboration, and adaptability. They do not entail mastery in a specific field, but rather a familiarity with a wide range of skills that are useful in almost any career or path one may choose. These skills have been the source of much discussion in education research; a comprehensive review by traced the evolving use of the term through 40 research papers (Chalkiadaki, 2018). The author concludes that 21st century skills can be generalized into four basic categories: personal skills, social skills, knowledge and information management skills, and digital literacy.

### Personal Skills

Personal skills refer to the development of a student’s individual characteristics. Of these, creativity was found to the most commonly cited 21st century skill, valued highly by both educators and employers (Chalkiadaki, 2018). In this context, creativity means the ability to innovate in a workplace as well as a sense of curiosity and imagination. Problem-solving was deemed another essential personal skill. At a time when knowledge is widely accessible, making the ability to apply that information more valuable. Self-directedness or autonomy is also considered extremely valuable, such as having the ability to self-regulate and develop. Other Personal skills such as sound reasoning, inquiry, and the ability to make informed decisions were also found to be essential.

### Social Skills

The next category of essential skills has to do with a student’s one’s ability to communicate and get along with others. These social skills include skilled and effective oral and written communication, conflict management, global awareness, and an openness to diversity. Collaboration is also deemed as equally important. A good combination of these skills leads to less conflict and misunderstandings, and more productivity in a workplace or in one’s life. Many colleges actively look for students with strong leadership and cooperation skills in their applications (Sahin, 2014). Knowledge of a second language is also important given the increasingly globalized and heterogeneous nature of the workforce (Chalkiadaki, 2018).

### Knowledge and Information Management Skills

Knowledge and information management skills are associated with one’s ability to absorb and retain information. In this age, learning is often based on technology and the internet, coined as e-learning (Balcaen, 2008). Students must be well versed in developing their skills in being able to absorb and manage information, especially from technology, as it becomes more common.

### Digital Literacy

Digital literacy is a student’s adeptness with technology and is one of the most prominent and important 21st-century skills. A student’s ability to utilize technologies has become increasingly important as technology and the world’s reliance on it evolves. The digital age has brought a new tool that aspiring students must be confident in. Digital literacy involves a series of subskills that all have to do with one’s ability to effectively utilize media, the internet, and other technologies.

## Why K-12 Is the Critical Window

Research has found that 21st century skills are easier to teach at younger ages (Chalkiadaki, 2018). Kids are more malleable and have an easier time learning 21st century skills, provided that they are in a supportive environment. On the other hand, adults already in the workforce have a much more difficult time acquiring these skills and habits. Moreover, businesses are less inclined to actively teach adults a non-specialized skill set in a competitive economy, especially when someone with those skills can be readily hired instead. Because of this, K-12 education is the best time to teach 21st century skills. Ultimately, prioritizing the instruction of 21st century skills will create a stronger and more adaptable generation of children who are more prepared for life after high school.
