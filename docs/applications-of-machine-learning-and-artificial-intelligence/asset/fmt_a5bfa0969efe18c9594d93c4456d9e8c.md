
# Student Disengagement and Teacher Challenges During the Pandemic

## Student Disengagement and Chronic Absenteeism

Student disengagement and especially chronic absenteeism are also factors that could significantly hurt students over the pandemic. There are indications that the negative relationship between absenteeism and student outcome becomes more pronounced the more days students miss school (Balfanz et al., 2020). This is worrying in that absenteeism promoted by the pandemic due to the easier aversion of consequences makes the repercussions of decreased learning time much more intense. Chronic absenteeism especially seems to be a primary indicator of lower academic performance and higher chances of dropping out. The United Nations has called the expected sharp increase in dropout rates to be a "generational catastrophe."

## Teacher Support and Adaptation Challenges

It turns out that teachers need help as well. Weak support systems have failed to coherently help adapt teachers to an online environment and left them unequipped to teach over the pandemic. While just over two-thirds of public-school teachers have gotten instruction on adapting to a virtual environment (which is still concerningly low), more teachers found it to be only somewhat useful, and in some cases, not useful at all. Most teachers have never contemplated online learning before the pandemic, let alone attempted or done it. Even if technically proficient teachers have gotten over the obstacle of technical issues, many teachers were inexperienced in how to conduct teaching online and were left to come up with teaching plans and curriculum on the fly, possibly damaging the quality of education. Teachers also have to deal with the difficulty of creating emotional bonds virtually. There are emotional bonds critical to learning and the online environment makes it especially hard for the teachers to form meaningful relationships with their students.

# Conclusion

The emergency online learning brought on by COVID-19 has brought on many unanticipated problems. From an accelerating gap in educational achievement between different socioeconomic levels to challenges posed by navigating online environments, most of the research points to the failure of emergency online learning and the disregard of schools' emergency policies. Fortunately, there are many things teachers and schools can do to improve the quality and accessibility of remote learning. Teachers may employ varying moderating variables to fit the conditions of their classrooms and schools. Many strategies can make online learning just as effective as face-to-face learning through means such as allowing more access to external resources and through the use of learner supports. Teachers and parents can allocate some of their own time to monitor students' skills and motivations. Procrastination can be prevented through the constant supervision of both teachers and parents and slightly stricter deadlines. Administrators can monitor the leniency of teachers and the progress of students by adjusting school policy with a top-down approach. More allocation of funds towards technology can help greatly improve the accessibility of virtual classrooms to students, especially those that are less inclined to have them in their households. By taking into account all of these remedies, both parents and teachers can help improve the efficacy and accessibility of online learning. These solutions can help improve the productivity of our economy and help provide a better future for all. As we have already mentioned, if we don't act now, the education achievement gap may leave those already behind in the dust. Our students' futures are at stake.

# Key Terms and Definitions

**Asynchronous Learning**: This refers to learning where students engage in learning in separate times and places.
