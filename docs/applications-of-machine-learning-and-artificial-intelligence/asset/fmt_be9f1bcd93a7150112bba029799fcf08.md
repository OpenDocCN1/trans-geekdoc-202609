
# MOOCs: Advantages, Disadvantages, and Impact on Deep Learning

## Overview

MOOCs currently offer alternative educational opportunities on the part of instructional designers, instructors, learners, and educational leaders. In general, MOOCs use strategies similar to those used by social networking sites in order to “connect the masses but with the added benefits of subject matter experts, to facilitate the content, and to coordinate a vast array of free, online materials” (Chen, Barnett, & Stephens, 2013, p. 3). According to Carr and Duderstadt (2012), “some of the areas in which MOOCs have been cited as most beneficial include increased options for accessibility, increased potential for student engagement, and expanded lifelong learning opportunities” (as cited in Chen et al., 2013, p. 4). Other than enhanced accessibility, MOOCs have additional advantages for learners from across the world, such as flexibility and the potential to “democratize higher education in some of the countries” (de Waard, 2011, as cited in Chen et al., 2013, p. 4) that are marginalized and disenfranchised both technology wise and in terms of other resources. For example, according to Leber (2013), “as online education platforms like Coursera, edX, and Udacity burst onto the scene over the past year, backers have talked up their potential to democratize higher education in the countries that have had the least access” (para 1, as cited in Chen et al., 2013, pp. 4-5). Also, contrary to common belief, even younger learners can attend MOOCs, instead of solely adults (e.g., college students, working professionals, etc.).

## Advantages

With the emergence of advanced educational technologies, MOOCs have become today’s nontraditional and rather informal modes of learning and teaching environments on demand. Thousands of educators from around the world share the same learning platform only to observe the significant levels of motivation and readiness for learning on the part of their K-12 students. Khan Academy is an excellent example of these online learning platforms, where common core standards smoothly apply. Teachers from several states share their students’ educational success stories supported by the guidelines established within common core standards that Khan Academy adopts and implements as one of the most contemporary and popular distant learning platforms. Numerous educators today utilize Khan Academy, which helps make the CCSS come to life in their classrooms. Where lesson planning and preparation of students for real-life situations and scenarios are involved, many teachers refer to these platforms as useful vehicles for teaching with common core standards.

One of the primary goals of MOOCs is to “enhance student engagement [and improve] student outcomes” (Chen et al., 2013, p. 5). Trowler and Trowler (2010) posited that student engagement is the “investment of time, effort, and other relevant resources by both students and their institutions intended to optimize the student experience and enhance the learning outcomes and development of students, and the performance and reputation of the institution” (as cited in Chen et al., 2013, p. 5). MOOCs can help online or distance course instructors deliver that.

Recent research informs that MOOCs can help instructors understand the extent to which it is critical to “recognize the learning styles of students and adapt their teaching strategies accordingly” (Chen, Barnett, & Stephens, 2013, p. 5). In the implementation process of MOOCs designed for a much larger number of students, these online course instructors should manage to understand the academic responsibilities of their students and those that they need to demonstrate themselves, along with their own instructional roles—as facilitators. In regards to the large audiences MOOCs are designed to teach, Carr (2012) pointed out that, until today, “more than a million people in the world have taken MOOCs” (Chen et al., 2013, p. 3). McAuley, Stewart, Siemens, and Cormier (2010) additionally provided that “from a pragmatic perspective, MOOCs provide access to large numbers of people who might otherwise be excluded for reasons ranging from time, to geographic location, to formal prerequisites, to financial hardship” (as cited in Chen et al., 2013, p. 6). As such, differentiating online instruction based on a solid analysis of and appreciation for a variety of students’ diverse learning styles is central to student engagement and the overall success of these courses. From a systems perspective, this is a crucial phenomenon to inquire further and in a systemic manner through ongoing, formative feedback, reviews, and promotion of lifelong learning opportunities to a wider range of learners across the globe.

## Disadvantages

Some of the challenges currently associated with MOOCs cannot be disregarded. There is an ongoing controversy that surrounds these open, online courses, particularly where “individual instruction, student performance assessment, and long-term administration and oversight” (Chen, Barnett, & Stephens, 2013, p. 6) are involved. These issues need further address, investigation and long-term, sustainable solutions that can sound results within the free, open, online education literature. Some of the higher occupational institutions today reject to join these courses, for they believe that MOOCs contradict with their overarching mission and core academic philosophy.

Furthermore, regarding their free offerings and open format to millions of students from around the world, MOOCs can actually be significantly costly to institutions. In fact, nothing comes for free, especially where business profits are involved. To illustrate, Kolowich (2013b) maintained that “course development assistance through edX can reach upwards of $250,000 per course with an additional $50,000 fee each time the course is offered” (as cited in Chen et al., 2013, p. 7). Also, Chen et al. (2013) underscored that “for instructors who develop their own courses, human resource needs [to] include course development (typically 100 hours) and course management (8-10 hours per week) in addition to existing professorial duties” (p. 7).

Therefore, considering these challenges that MOOCs pose to educational leaders, potential values of MOOCs should be more closely explored. Technologically-driven educational values of MOOCs (i.e., students’ learning development, innovation, engagement, authenticity and meaning of content, motivation, interaction, project-based learning, collaboration, differentiated instructional design, delivery, and assessment, etc.) should be scrutinized with more caution. There is certainly a future for MOOCs; however, as scholars suggest, instructional designers, editors, and learners should not hold their breaths for a breakthrough in this area in the very near future. After all, MOOCs are still in their infancy stage since the offering of Siemens and Downes’ Connectivism and Connective Knowledge course in 2008 and the launch of Coursera in April of 2012 (Chen et al., 2013).
