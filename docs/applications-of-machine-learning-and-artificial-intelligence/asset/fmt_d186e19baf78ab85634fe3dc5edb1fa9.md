## Abstract

In a rapidly changing world, especially amidst COVID-19, the educational goals must be refined and modified. This chapter derives from a study based on research findings and recognizes the individual aspects of 21st century skills that can pave the way for adaptation and transition utilizing applied skills and digital literacy. The 21st century skills (21CS) have also been in the spotlight about digital literacy, as the pandemic possessed the current need to continue instruction and learning despite the setback imposed. It was concluded that 21CS covers the requirements and works best when implemented into the current mode of instruction combined with STEM education and basic skills.

## Introduction

Prior to the recent shift to online learning, educators understood the important role that 21st century skills played in providing students with a well-rounded education. These skills have typically been framed as the social and emotional tools that allow students to think critically about new problems, communicate effectively with others, and motivate themselves to work independently. A survey of employers conducted prior to the pandemic found that the skills most valued in job applicants were these soft skills such as communication, writing, speaking, and self-directedness (World Economic Forum, 2016). For K-12 students especially, developing these skills is a crucial part of education, at least as important as any particular course content. This is especially true in STEM fields, where the rapid pace of technological change makes it almost impossible to predict what specific problems students will face in their professional work. This has led to increased interest in project-based learning or group-led learning, which are better at preparing STEM students to be flexible problem solvers, equipped to face whatever challenges may await them in the modern workplace.

But the disruptions brought about by COVID-19 have made such 21st century skills even more crucial for students, who are now tasked with navigating their own learning in the midst of unforeseen disruptions and emotional stress. With the increased autonomy of online learning, students faced new challenges to stay motivated and engaged in their classwork. A student’s involvement in social networks has been shown to have a major impact on their academic success, and the increase in social isolation has adverse effects on student well-being and performance (Stadtfeld et al., 2019). And the new stressors associated with COVID — worries about the safety of family members, economic collapse, uncertain future of jobs and even education itself — only compound the problems of isolation. A study from Switzerland, conducted in the early weeks of the pandemic, found that students were on average more depressed, more anxious, more stressed, and more lonely than they were before lockdown (Elmer et al., 2020). While not surprising, these findings do emphasize the degree of stress that students are under and help explain why academic performance has faltered amid the shift to online learning.
