
## Phonetic Data Analysis towards Deeper Learning of the Participants’ Conscious Intentionality and Perceptions of the Phenomenon Under Research

In the second cycle of coding, or pattern coding, the researcher employed meta transcriptions, which included spelling of sounds (phonemes) towards finding common speech patterns related to the lived phenomenon. In the First Cycle of the coding process, which was Descriptive Coding, all of the previous codes were identified under textural descriptions. This First Cycle of coding was completed with codes having been formed following an initial deconstruction phase of the raw data given by all participants. Next, a plethora of emerging themes was identified under structural descriptions. The next step comprised the Second Cycle of Coding: Pattern Coding. Where the second type of coding in the coding phase was involved, the researcher found all participants’ common speech patterns by meta coding. She applied the meta coding technique via IPA-based meta transcriptions.⁵ Being a linguist herself, therefore, from a linguistic perspective, the researcher employed the type of data analysis that had not previously been exercised in the CALL field and in the realm of phenomenological study design: Phonics in data analysis. This specific type of pattern coding, phonetic coding, is what makes this phenomenological exploration original, authentic, ingenious, and unique.

In order to decipher phonetic codes in this second phase of her analysis of the collected data, the researcher revisited and reread the table of descriptive coding schema (see Table 1) based on all interview transcripts combined. She had earlier structured both of these coding schemas throughout the descriptive coding phase. Next, she located the most frequently used, or the most consistent, recurring and repetitive textural descriptions provided by all of the 12 adult ESL student participants. She then analyzed the particular tones and stresses (high stress and low stress) that stood out in each of these recurring codes under each textural description. Her goal with extrapolating an array of these outstanding sounds was to find what each of these respondents felt as they lived their individual experiences pertinent to the phenomenon under her inquiry. In order to achieve this goal and complete this second cycle of the coding process (i.e., Pattern Coding), the researcher referred to the official and universal phonics alphabet system, including its related interactive charts and a unique coding system used by all linguists, including herself.

International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) Association⁶ (1999) is an organization founded for phoneticians and that practice linguistics, which assists with interactive, ubiquitous, and most recent phonetic coding charts and systems as these practitioners engage in deciphering the meanings behind certain word accents, tones, and other linguistic elements of the language used by speakers. During the Second Cycle of Coding (Pattern Coding), the researcher utilized these phonetic charts to describe the purpose, intention, and meanings of the most commonly used oral remarks that her interviewees used during the interviews. The researcher’s phonetic coding process exclusively focused on particular vowels as phonemes only, rather than consonants, which yielded emergent stress, rhythm, tone, and rhetoric from within the partaking subjects’ oral statements. She derived these tones and high or low stress sounds, by especially referring to the word accent chart, diacritics chart, and suprasegmentals chart specifically designed by the IPA organization for the type of phonetic coding distinct and particular to the English language.

In reference to IPA and for a salient practice of data analysis through each phonetic code, the researcher utilized an extensive number of resources—including a vast range of code schemas and frameworks—as she examined each of the 12 adult ESL learners’ uttered speeches as they orally responded to all four interview questions during one-on-one interviews. Based on the similarities, regularities, and consistencies, and repetitions of the responses each of the 12 student participants, specific statements were selected and placed in the same coding category. The next step applied was to determine the tones, word accents, and styles of each of these similar—or even identical—statements uttered according to the stresses (high or low stress) rhythms, as well their intonations and enunciations.

This was only possible to decipher by identification of some of the outstanding words which were then broken down into their syllabi (i.e., syncategorematic elements) that next led to identifying particularly emergent vowels of the sounds. These vowels were analyzed in detail based on their phonetic features (see Figure 2 for Vowels - Tones and Word Accents Chart).

The phonetic analysis assisted with having a deeper grasp of the essence of the meanings derived from the (textural) descriptions that each respondent provided. In other words, by use of specific phonetic codes from within the IPA system, the researcher could understand in detail what each participant meant and intended to say in their conscious internationality, and why (purpose) they said the things they said (what→noema) in a certain manner (how→noesis). The subjects’ purpose and intent with their oral statements was pivotal for the researcher to discern, which is directly congruent with the Husserlian approach to phenomenological inquiry and analysis, which can be referred to as a philosophy of phenomena experienced in an intuitive manner.

Sokolowksi (2000) later built his views upon this notion of conscious intentionality and expanded the to which an individual living an experience about a phenomenon intuits each concrete and abstract identity of things that manifest to him/her under certain circumstances and contexts either through empty or filled intentionality, and he/she realizes this based on his/her memory, perception, imagination, and anticipation. With the intent to develop on this concept of conscious intentionality where things manifest to subjects, the researcher of this phenomenological study conducted this analysis of sounds (i.e., phonemes), because she sought what her partaking study subjects felt, thought, perceived, and intended within their individual consciousness, circumstances, and contexts as they spoke to her during one-on-one conversations.

The researcher was aware that as she searched for essential features and meanings of things, she needed to move into somewhat a deeper inquiry rather than basic or original, which then led up to her digression from the original, basic, natural attitude, and thereby, resulted in a phenomenological attitude. From a philosophical viewpoint, which also perfectly aligns with the philosophical and psychological premises of this phenomenological research, as discussed earlier in this chapter, moving from the original, natural, and naïve phenomenological attitude to a more profound and philosophical attitude during the conduct of her data analysis would not only enrich her experiences along with those of her subjects—in an interdialogic manner—but also bring about a paradigmatic shift in her research-based, phenomenological attitude: from natural to transcendental (Sokolowski, 2000).
