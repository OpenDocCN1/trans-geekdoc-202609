
# Educational Achievement Gaps and the Impact of COVID-19

There has been a persistent gap in educational achievement between Black/Hispanic students and white students. In fact, this gap has been estimated to cost the U.S. economy as much as $500 billion a year in productivity (Auguste et al., 2009). While progress has been made to narrow this gap over recent years, the disparity in academic achievement is still immense, and the emergency shift to virtual school is predicted to widen it (Stanford Center for Education Policy Analysis, n.d.). This gap not only disproportionately hurts minorities but also hurts the economy as a whole. Estimates predict that if this gap had been closed in 2009, today’s GDP could be anywhere from $426 billion to $705 billion higher (Dorn et al., 2020). Closing this gap is a crucial endeavor, but with COVID-19 looming over our heads, the near future looks bleak. The average K-12 student is predicted to lose about $70,000 of lifetime earnings because of educational losses caused by COVID-19 (Dorn et al., 2020).

## Digital Divide and Access Inequality

While discussing the switch to online learning, it is important to remember that access to computers and the internet remains unequal. According to the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), nearly all privileged 15-year-olds have a computer, but almost a quarter of disadvantaged students don’t. This situation reveals the urgent need to “bridge the digital divide,” in the words of the UN Conference on Trade and Development.

It must also be noted that many countries outside the US, especially developing countries, have it even worse (Vegas et al., 2020). Before the pandemic, 90 percent of children in low-income countries and 50 percent of children in middle-income countries came out of school without mastering the necessary basic secondary-level skills (Vegas et al., 2020). In comparison, only 30 percent of children in high-income countries like the US lack this level of academic achievement. But even 30 percent of children is too many, considering that this means 30 percent of the children are going to leave school without the skills needed to lead a successful life (Vegas et al., 2020). But after the pandemic, these numbers will only get worse. The children who live in poor countries who are already behind students from other countries will now face even worse conditions. In mid-April 2020, while about 90 percent of high-income countries were providing remote learning, only 25 percent of the low-income countries were providing any type of remote learning (Vegas et al., 2020). Most of the remote learning in low-income countries was provided through TV and radio, which isn’t as effective as online learning. Although these numbers will likely improve as the pandemic subsides, there is still a substantial difference between the percentage of low-income countries, where more people of lower socioeconomic status are located, and high-income countries that provide effective remote education.

## Gender Disparities

The shift to emergency online learning affected girls disproportionately more than boys. In some countries, girls already lacked opportunities for education. When COVID-19 hit and education went online, even more discriminatory barriers were put in front of those girls who already had trouble getting education (Sheppard, 2021). Furthermore, when girls get out of school in these countries, they have higher chances of facing gender-based violence, and they gain more responsibilities at home, which makes it more difficult for them to ever get back into school. This increases the seriousness of the problem.

## Socioeconomic Privilege in Education

Wealthy families from around the world have the means to ensure that their children get a good education, regardless of the pandemic. Some such families have hired private tutors or teachers to teach their students at home without the dangers of catching the virus (Vegas et al., 2020). In most cases, students learn more with this type of personalized attention than they do in traditional classrooms. Some wealthy parents have also found online platforms which can give their children extra learning assistance. These online platforms are generally very expensive, so they aren’t an option for families of low income. Even though the wealthy families that do these only comprise a small percentage of the population, this still shows how the situation with COVID-19 is increasing the education gap between students of different socioeconomic status.

# Negative Effects of COVID-19

By April of 2020, about 94 percent of learners throughout the world were feeling the effects of the pandemic on their education (UNSDG, 2020). But education was already in a crisis even before the disruptions brought by COVID-19. Worldwide, approximately 250 million children do not or cannot attend schools, and approximately 800 million adults are illiterate (UNSDG, 2020). The pandemic only made our pre-existing problems worse; it increased the number of children who were out of school, delayed the education that many students were receiving on a day-to-day basis, and in some cases made the education children were receiving less effective.

These effects were felt throughout the world by all socio-economic classes to different degrees. Preschoolers and elementary schoolers missed out on the opportunity to learn and practice social skills that needed to be nurtured. In low socio-economic areas, most of the children whose parents worked missed the proper food and nutrition they need, many of them missed the educational opportunities that were available, and these problems will only compound in the future. In secondary, collegiate, and graduate-level education, institutions have been more able to provide continuity through online lectures, which has made it easier for higher-level institutions to adapt to the problems that COVID-19 has brought. But many local and smaller higher-level institutions lacked the proper technological infrastructure and did not receive full support.

# Remedies

Given the widening gap between students of different socioeconomic levels caused by COVID-19, it is now more important than ever to consider different learning paradigms that yield learning opportunities to students regardless of their status. Online education must be as easy to access as possible while not compromising its efficiency and effectiveness. In addition, many learners have been in a fragile emotional state during this pandemic, which providers of online education must take into account (Carter et al. 2020). If they do, then all learners will have an easier time adjusting to their online classes and an easier time learning. It is also important to remember that many learners are not as efficient when it comes to pacing their classwork themselves, and they tend to be less productive while working in a home environment (Carter et al. 2020).

Even though the crisis of COVID-19 is unique, much of the research done on the topic of online learning is still useful. Previous studies show that, in order to create an effective online learning environment for all types of students, teachers need to use careful instructional design and planning as well as a systematic model for design and development (Hodges, 2020). Also, creating an effective online environment for every student in a class will help lower the achievement gap between students of different socioeconomic levels. However, these two factors need to be implemented correctly to actually be able to help create an effective online learning environment. Unfortunately, this isn’t what is happening today, especially given the speed with which institutions had to transition to online learning.
