
# Corpus-Aided Grammar Teaching

## Theoretical Foundations

On the premise that the lexicogrammar approach is viewed as the unified system of lexis and grammar, grammatical interpretation should be studied in habit formation of textual environment including the construction of surrounding co-text (e.g., function words) and discourse functions (e.g., genres and registers) (Gledhill, 2011). This approach is particularly useful in the analysis of formulaic sequences (e.g., collocations; lexical bundles) using the knowledge of grammar and pragmatic rules and developing the interpretation task from corpora resources (Mystkowska-Wiertelak & Pawlak, 2011). Drawing on this point, the next section will review how the usage of corpora can assist grammar teaching following the teaching implications of corpus-aided grammar teaching.

## Corpus-Aided Grammar Teaching

The usage of corpus data in the context of English language teaching is subject to its usefulness in providing access to authentic language data because it is argued that the language in use is not the same as rule-specified grammar and varies in terms of forms and degree of formality (Huang, 2008). In the application of language teaching and learning materials development, corpus linguistics can be applied in the compilation of dictionaries in either printed form (e.g., *Collins COBUILD Advanced Learners’ English Dictionary*; *Macmillan English Dictionary*; *Oxford Advanced Learners’ English Dictionary*) or online search (e.g., *The Corpus of Contemporary American English*; *British National Corpus*; *Cambridge Dictionaries Online*). Apart from dictionaries, corpus linguistics is also implemented in data-driven learning as the means for enhancing the "interpretation of form" and "realization of function" via the creation of grammar exercises and grammar learning activities from corpus data (Johns, 1990, p. 293). These grammar exercises and learning activities are distinct in the nature of consciousness-raising (Meunier, 2002), and their purpose is to facilitate learners' discovery of the regular patterning of the target language in an inductive manner (Johns & King, 1991).

Our next review concerns what kinds of empirical evidence can be drawn from corpus resources to create grammar exercises and grammar learning activities.

First, language teachers can utilize corpus concordance of certain lexical items (i.e., text lines with the search word in the middle and the surrounding context) as linguistic evidence for learners to observe the usage of grammar and vocabulary, to be followed by the next stage of generalizing the patterns and rules associated with the observed search word (Liu & Jiang, 2009). Observations of concordance can be conducted inductively by directing learners' attention to on-screen computers or printed handouts and examining how lexical items are used in specific contexts (Nesi & Basturkmen, 2009).

Second, word frequency lists can be employed as the means for learners to observe the ranking of certain lexical items and to make comparisons of these items across selected language varieties and language contexts (O'Keeffe & Farr, 2003). Normally, the word frequency list is extracted in the high-frequency word list starting with the minimum frequency of ten, and high-frequency lexical items on the list may reflect characteristics of the specialized corpora in terms of usage and emphasis (Warren, 2009).

### Languaging and Contextualization

Another consideration for corpus-aided grammar teaching is placed on *languaging*, the emphasis of which is on how to mediate cognitive processes in the process of meaning-making to develop knowledge and experience through the usage of language (Swain, 2006). In terms of application in corpus-aided grammar teaching, languaging can be combined with the following stages in corpus-aided investigation:

1.  Observing the recurrences of language patterns for the chosen lexis.
2.  Generalizing the explanations on the identified language patterns via comparison methodology.
3.  Reproducing conventional or producing creative texts (Gavioli, 2001).

This concept can also be integrated in the case of contextualized grammar teaching where the teaching of grammar is among the combination of the lexicogrammar approach, the implementation of corpus, and contextualization (Liu & Jiang, 2009). Contextualization from corpus data is beneficial for grammar teaching in the sense that it provides relevant context transferred from the text for learners to interpret grammar as compared with paper-based learning materials (Charles, 2007). In particular, the embedded information from contextualization can be discourse functions, genre, speech events, and specialized language, all of which should construct relevant linguistic environment in the grammatical constructions (Perez-Llantada, 2009).

### Implementing Corpus Linguistics in Grammar Lessons

The next concern in this section is how teachers can implement corpus linguistics in a grammar lesson. According to McEnery et al. (2006), corpus linguistics can be viewed as "a new philosophical approach to linguistic enquiry" (p. 6), and this linguistic enquiry can be firstly conducted in the transition from bottom-up to top-down processing (Flowerdew, 2009). The search of specific lexical items in a bottom-up processing would result in the analysis and classification of specific patterns to the search items, while the top-down processing would rather be handled in the analysis of how genre-specific requirements influence the emergence of frequent patterns and structures. For instance, Flowerdew (2008) illustrated how the top-down selection between passive and active voice in the lexical item *focus* comes from the bottom-up search of *focus* and its relevant pattern.

The linguistic enquiry from corpus data is also designed in grammar exercises which include observation/research tasks and freer creative writing activities. In Johns (1990), the author illustrated the design of grammar exercises on the observation/research task of *that*-clauses, from which learners identify the associated verbs with *that*-clauses from concordance lines of the *New Scientist* weekly journal (the extracted corpus), subsequently compare the two groups of cited quotations of *that*-clauses, and finally complete the sentences with *that*-clauses in their own creativity.

The third way to conduct the linguistic inquiry is the extraction of the World Wide Web as the source of corpus data and the performance of online searches from there. Boulton (2012) reviewed Google as an exemplary case of concordance with which learners can extract results of linguistic data of their own needs; nevertheless, the act of Googling should consider relevant issues including the training of getting and evaluating the results, not to mention the issue of how reliable and frequent the linguistic data withdrawn from this search engine are.

## Practical Teaching Activities

This section suggests teaching activities implementing corpus-aided grammar teaching as the teaching methodology. Following the previous discussion on languaging, an attempt is made to introduce teaching activities on the aspect of grammatical interpretation in the following procedure: observation, generalization, and reproduction. On the chosen varieties of British and American English, the first teaching case demonstrates an exploration of the length of an action in moment expressions from the following formulaic sequences: `V+ momentarily`, `V+ for a moment`, and `V+ in a moment`. On a similar focus on the chosen varieties of English, the second teaching case further incorporates the function of Googling in the design of teaching activities for the comparative tasks on the following lexis: (1) `coffee house`–`coffee shop` and (2) `discount`–`concession`.
