## Lookup Tables (LUTs)

**Lookup Tables (LUTs)** is the fastest way for skin detection since it is not dependent on arithmetic operations. There is no specific definition of probability density characteristic in non-parametric techniques. Single histogram primarily based Look-UP-Table (LUT) model is a common approach in modeling skin color cluster. This technique uses a set of training skin pixels from the distribution of skin pixels in the color space chosen for the skin detection program. The training technique is simple but populating the histogram in this way calls for an extensive skin data set.

However, one major detriment of this method is their dependence on the training set that requires the gathering of a massive range of skin pixels. Furthermore, large memories are required to implement histogram-based models, especially when fine resolutions are required.

# Parametric Models

**Parametric Models** include pattern classification by Gaussian distribution. This method is a heuristic one and relies on skin color distribution that can be modeled based on Gaussian distribution in the color space. Most Gaussian distribution produces a symmetric, bell-shaped curve. The normal distribution depends on the mean `µ` and standard deviation `σ`. The mean determines the center, and the standard deviation determines height and width. The Probability Density Function (PDF) of a random variable `x` is given by `P(x)` (equation 7) (Duda, 2012).

Single Gaussian method (SGMs), Gaussian mixture models (GMMs), the cluster of Gaussian models (CGMs), Elliptical models (EMs), etc., are evolved to compensate LUT shortcomings consisting of excessive storage requirement. Similarly, they generalize thoroughly with a tremendously smaller amount of training set. In SGM, there should be a smooth Gaussian distribution around the mean vector. GMM is advanced to version more complex distributions as a normalized weighted sum of Gaussian PDFs (Yang & Ahuja, 1998). It compensates the inability of SGM in managing out-of-control conditions in a general skin segmentation problem, similarly to the truth that SGM is not capable of approximating the actual distribution due to the asymmetry of distribution in its peak (Lee & Yoo, 2002). The most appealing features of GMM models are their easy evaluation procedure and occasional memory cost. The training technique is longer than the former methods. A comparative look at the overall performance of single and combination of Gaussian distributions in (Caetano, Olabarriaga, & Barone, 2003) confirmed that the mixture method improves the performance only in a relevant operating area. However, skin color clusters have an elliptical shape, and the use of the Gaussian model leads to a high false-positive rate. # UNSUPERVISED MACHINE LEARNING

In Machine Learning, the problem of unsupervised learning is to find hidden structure in unlabeled data. Unsupervised algorithms find the undiscovered pattern that is undiscoverable by the supervised algorithm and human observation. As there is no indication of possible output, the unsupervised algorithm cannot be trained. Moreover, unsupervised learning algorithms cannot directly apply for classification or regression since those algorithms do not know the output. Unlike the supervised algorithm that is bottlenecked by the requirement of quality training data, the unsupervised can find data pattern (Islam, 2019). In some real-world applications, where the quality of training data is sparse, the unsupervised algorithm can be used. Compared to supervised learning, unsupervised algorithms have some uncharted areas. There are many unsupervised algorithms available, but the most prominent are – clustering, principal component analysis, association rule, Apriori, etc. In this chapter, clustering algorithms will be discussed in details since it has the most widely used algorithm on computer vision.
