
# Systems Thinking in Online Learning Design and Delivery

Systems thinking postulates that successful innovation or change within any educational context cannot be achieved without including the inextricably linked and interdependent parts of that organization. Based on the premises of the systems approach in online learning design and delivery, finding long-term, sustainable solutions to current problems is essential. Could (2012) pointed out that a problem is a symptom of another problem, and a systems thinker needs to figure out how far upstream he can go in order to identify a certain problem within his sphere.

## Historical Context: The AIM Project

The systems approach was first introduced with the Articulated Instructional Media (AIM) project funded by the Carnegie Corporation (1964–1968). The project was directed by Charles Wedemeyer at the University of Wisconsin in Madison. AIM is regarded as the third generation of social, collaborative, and **PMBOLD** design and delivery. The AIM project stands as a milestone in the history of **PMBOLD**. This third-generation sustainable learning project gave way to today’s social networking sites and MOOCs. Perceived to be today’s sustainable and green learning, design, and instructional applications, social media sites and MOOCs are accepted as the evolved versions of what is referred to as the genesis of **PMBOLD**.

## Social Networking in a Systematic Process

In a systematic process, learners from around the world gain opportunities to learn anytime and anywhere as they utilize social networking applications to optimize their learning and personal development. From a systems thinking perspective, all social media tools are pragmatic online technologies where collaborative learning, project and data set management, instructional design, delivery, assessment, and ongoing feedback are all sustainable and fostered. To illustrate, instructional designers and educators utilizing these tools can break instruction down into smaller components, engage in informal learning contexts with their learners and other faculty, and thus create collaborative, online learning environments outside of the classroom environment.

# Social Networking Technology with a Systems Approach to Effective Learning and Instruction: Two Instructional Systems Design (ISD) Models towards Learners’ Mastery of Learning

Where systems thinking and systems theories or models are involved, social networks can largely benefit from the following two instructional systems design (ISD) models:

1.  **Dick and Carey Model** formulated by Dick, Carey, and Carey (2009)
2.  **Forward Model** formulated by Visser and Sclundt-Bodien (2009)

## Dick and Carey Instructional Systems (ISD) Design Model

In the process of creating and implementing a successful design and toward attaining the previously targeted learning outcomes, the model urges designers to develop an effective instructional strategy. Strategy development occurs based on the information obtained from the preceding five stages of the model. Data collected through formative evaluation (i.e., one-on-one, small-group, and field trial evaluation) (Reiser & Dempsey, 2007) in the second-to-last stage of the model leads to system-wide evaluation prior to the actual delivery of the designed instruction.

This is an added value of the model, especially because mastery learning (Gagne, Wager, Golas, & Keller, 2005) is sought during educational activities. In the last stage of the entire evaluation process, the worthiness and validity of the test items and formerly set performance objectives are revised. That is, the effectiveness of instruction is reassessed, reexamined, and improved, if necessary, in order to achieve the desired goals, align with the formerly set benchmarks and standards of instruction, as well as reach each planned learning outcome. Regarding this final phase of revision, Dick, Carey, and Carey (2009) stressed that “revision is not a discrete event that occurs at the end of the ID process, but an ongoing process of using information to reassess assumptions and decisions” (p. 8). This statement provided by the authors would not fall contrary to the aforementioned iterative nature (Johnson, 2007) of the entire process.

This classroom-oriented instructional model also “reflects the fundamental design process used in many business, industry, government, and military training settings, as well as the influence of performance technology and the application of computers to instruction” (Gustafson & Branch, 2002, p. 62). Research indicates the effectiveness of the Dick & Carey ID model in the way that it aligns with and is greatly influenced by particular learning theories such as Robert Gagné’s (1985) Conditions of Learning and Fischer and Immordino-Yang’s Cognitive Information Processing (2008) theories (Dick, Carey, & Carey, 2009). According to Reiser and Dempsey (2007), besides the five types or domains of learning he discussed in his book, Gagné (1985) specifically gave a description of nine events of instruction. These are defined as teaching activities he considered critical as educators and instructional designers promote any type of learning outcomes they had targeted prior to the delivery of instruction. Similar events of instruction targeting particular academic gains are also manifested in the Dick and Carey ID model. These instructional events are independent—albeit interrelated—instructional components, which attribute fluidity to the model, particularly when designers prefer to start the process from any step of the sequence. Thus, because of its consistency and alignment with the elements of Gagné’s learning framework, as well as its congruence with brain-based learning approaches, one can find this rather classroom-based model quite powerful in educational environments. It is also noteworthy that the model includes the components of behaviorist, cognitivist, and constructivist approaches to learning.

## Backward Instructional Systems Design (ISD) Model

This ISD model predominantly focuses on identifying student learning outcomes (SLOs) in the process of designing instruction. The fundamental principle of the backward design model is to initially focus on designing effective instruction based on identifying SLOs and then work backwards to develop course materials and instruction to help students achieve the desired outcomes. The model is described as a backward design with an “approach to designing curriculum or unit that begins with the end in mind and designs toward the end” (Childre, Sands, & Pope, 2009, p. 7).

In this respect, the model is unequivocally similar to the preceding ID model. As in the case of the Dick and Carey ISD model, gaining mastery of certain skills in the Backward Design Instructional Model is observed as a quite powerful learning technique employed during educational activities. Particularly the first two types of assessment within the Backward Design instructional model can be analogized to the Dick and Carey ISD model, which enables educators to employ effective and efficient performance-based assessments and criteria-referenced tests for their learners towards gaining deeper knowledge and mastering the content.
