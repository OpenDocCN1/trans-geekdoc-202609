A recent method in [26] proposes a TNN and restricts both activations and weights to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. It uses teacher–student methods to train TNNs. In this method, the teacher and student networks have the same architecture, with each neuron according to Table 5.1. In fact, each neuron of student network imitates the equivalent neuron's behavior in the teacher network without any multiplications.

The TNNs make the model sparse by approximating small weights to zero. Sparsity in TNNs is illustrated in Figure 5.5, where $X$ and $Y$ denote inputs and outputs, respectively. weights and activations are demonstrated by lines and circles, respectively. The forward computation is done when both activation and weight are nonzero, weights and activations are shown by lines in Figure 5.5. In other words, the ternized neural network is a sparse BNN that performs computations, only when both activation and weight are nonzero.

Recent papers provide hardware for these TNN models on both FPGA and ASIC platforms. In [27], implementing TNN on FPGAs shows 3.1× more energy efficiency than the software-based designs using the MNIST, CIFAR10, SVHN, and CIFAR100 datasets.

A new low-precision neural network model, called ternary-binary network (TBN), is presented in [28]. TBN restricts the inputs and weights to values from $\{−1,0,1\}$ and $\{-1,1\}$, respectively. They show that an optimal trade-off among memory, performance, and efficiency are provided by incorporating binary weights and ternary inputs. It should be mentioned that a scaling factor is used for binary weights similar to XNOR-Net. They deployed XNOR and AND gates to perform multiplication. They have obtained ~32× memory saving and 40× speedup compared with a full-precision CNN design. Not only TBN reduces implementation cost, but also achieves remarkable accuracy on MNIST, CIFAR10, SVHN, and ImageNet by deploying TBN.

All of the aforementioned designs need to store full-precision weights to update parameters during the training phase. Therefore, full-precision memory access is required to store weights during backpropagation. GXNOR-Net [27] has tried to address this issue by introducing discretization framework. The contributions of GXNOR-Net are twofold:

First, it presents a multi-discretization function (e.g., ternary discretization) for neuronal activations. It also presents derivatives approximation method to implement backpropagation, as shown in Figure 5.7.

Second, GXNOR-Net introduces a discrete state transition (DST) methodology to eliminate the full-precision weights in the training phase. Both weights and inputs are ternarized ($\{-1,0,1\}$) in GXNOR-Net, hence they can also benefit from sparsity and be more efficient. In this method, the amount of sparsity can be controlled by D. GXNOR-Net is a gated version of XNOR-Net which makes the network sparser. GXNOR-Net and XNOR-Net use XNOR gates for multiplication, so 2 XNOR gates are required for 2-bit weights although GXNOR-Net has achieved noticeable accuracy on MNIST, CIFAR10, and SVHN datasets, there are not any results reported on large datasets, such as ImageNet and CIFAR100 datasets.

The authors in [29] introduced TOT-Net, a ternarized neural network with $\{-1,0,1\}$ for weights and activation functions and showed that it can achieve a higher level of accuracy with lower computational load over the competitors. In fact, TOT-Net introduces a simple bitwise logic (XOR gate and AND gate) for convolution computations to reduce the cost of multiplications. With GXNOR-Net as as in Figure 5.9, TOT-Net requires fewer gates for generating the output signal. TOT-Net has ternary activations and weights based on TWN approach [30]. TOT-Net also proposes a novel piecewise activation function, and optimized learning rate for different datasets to improve accuracy. This way, by using an evolutionary optimization approach, novel piecewise activation functions are explored. As an example, {Elite, Sigmoid}, and {Sigmoid, Leaky_ReLU} exhibited the best performance as piecewise activation functions for CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, respectively.
