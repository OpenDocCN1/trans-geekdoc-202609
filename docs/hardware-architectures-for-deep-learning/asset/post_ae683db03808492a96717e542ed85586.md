### Inverter-based memristive neuromorphic circuit

Writing the equations of (11.5) for all of the weights of the column forms a system with $2N$ equations and $2N$ unknowns which should be solved to map the neural weights to the conductance values of the memristor conductance. This system of equations which is utilized in the training process may be expressed as:

$$\begin{bmatrix} W_{j1p}-1 & W_{j1p} & \dots & W_{j1n} \\ W_{j1n} & W_{j1n}-1 & & W_{j1p} \\ & \dots & & \dots \\ W_{Jnp} & & W_{Jnn} & \dots & W_{Jn}-1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{j1p} \\ \sigma_{j1n} \\ \sigma_{j2p} \\ \sigma_{j2n} \\ \vdots \\ \sigma_{JNp} \\ \sigma_{Jn} \end{bmatrix} \quad (11.7)$$

Since (11.7) is a homogeneous linear system, it has a nontrivial solution only if the determinant value of its coefficients matrix is zero. We have shown that applying the following condition results in zero determinant value for the coefficients matrix of (11.7) and hence a nontrivial solution:

$$\sum_{i=1}^N(W_{jin} + W_{jip}) = 1 \quad (11.8)$$

Assuming (11.8), and aggregating all the rows of coefficients matrix of (11.7) in the first row, the system could be rewritten as:

$$\begin{bmatrix} \sum_{i=1}^N(W_{jin} + W_{jip})-1 & \sum_{i=1}^N(W_{jin} + W_{jip}) & \dots & \sum_{i=1}^N(W_{jin} + W_{jip}) \\ W_{j1n} & W_{j1n}-1 & & W_{j1p} \\ & \dots & & \dots \\ W_{Jnp} & & W_{Jnn} & \dots & W_{Jn}-1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sum_{i=1}^N(\sigma_{jin} + \sigma_{jip}) \\ \sigma_{j1n} \\ \sigma_{j2p} \\ \sigma_{j2n} \\ \vdots \\ \sigma_{JNp} \\ \sigma_{Jn} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad (11.9)$$

Since $\sum_{i=1}^N(W_{jin} + W_{jip}) = 1$, all the elements in the first row of coefficients matrix of (11.9) are zero. Therefore, the determinant of coefficients matrix is zero. Hence, the learning algorithm should train the neural network under these two constraints:

- Weights must be positive and the conductance of all the memristors must be in the range $[\sigma_{min}, \sigma_{max}]$.
- The sum of all the weights entering a neuron must be equal to 1 (based on (11.8)).

### 11.3.2 Input interface (DAC)

The input interface should convert the input signals to analog ones, hence a DAC is needed. There are several implementations for DAC (e.g., [36, 37, 40]). In order to reduce the power consumption while maintaining the performance, we suggest implementing the DAC without active components such as operational amplifier [17]. The suggested n-bit DAC structure is depicted in Figure 11.5. In this structure, the voltage of the digital inputs of the DAC ($D_0 - D_{2n-1}$) are scaled and accumulated in the node $R_0$ through the binary resistors $(R, 2R, \dots, 2^{n-1}R)$ which could be implemented using memristors in a small area. The voltage of the analog output node may be found from the KCL equation:

$$\frac{V_{AO}}{R_{Load}} + \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{V_{Di}}{2^{n-1-i}R} = 0 \quad (11.10)$$

where $V_{Di}$ is the voltage of the $i$-th digital input, and $R_{Load}$ is the equivalent load resistance of the memristive crossbar driven by the DAC. Using (11.10), one may calculate (real) $V_{AO}$ as:

$$V_{AO} = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{V_{Di}}{2^{n-1-i}R}}{R_{Load}} \quad (11.11)$$

On the other hand, in the ideal DAC (without loading), input/output voltage relation is given by:

$$\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{V_{A0ideal}-V_{Di}}{2^{n-1-i}R} = 0 \quad (11.12)$$

which may be used to obtain ideal $V_{A0}$ as:

$$V_{A0ideal} = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{V_{Di}}{2^{n-1-i}R} = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} 2^i V_{Di} \quad (2^n - 1) \quad (11.13)$$

Here, the relative error between the ideal and real outputs of the DAC is expressed as:

$$Error = \frac{V_{A0ideal}-V_{A0}}{V_{A0ideal}} = 1 - \frac{V_{A0}}{V_{A0ideal}} = \frac{n}{R_{Load}} - \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{1}{2^{n-1-i}R} \quad (11.14)$$

Assuming that the input error tolerance of the neural network is $\alpha$, the maximum value of $R$ ($R_{max}$) may be determined:

$$\frac{n}{R_{Load}} - \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{1}{2^{n-1-i}R} < \alpha \quad (11.15)$$

Rearranging the above inequality, one finds:

$$\frac{R}{R_{Load}}(n-\alpha) < \frac{2^n-1}{2^{n-1}} \quad (11.16)$$

and finally,

$$R < R_{max} = \frac{2^n-1}{2^{n-1}} \times \frac{\alpha}{n-\alpha} \times R_{Load} \quad (11.17)$$

Therefore, in designing the DAC, the $R$ value should be selected based on $R_{Load}$ and $\alpha$. In the cases $R_{max}$ is smaller than the minimum resistance of the available memristor, we should employ memristors with minimum resistance in parallel. is noteworthy that due to the compactness and small layout size of the memristor, the overhead area of parallel memristors is much smaller than the area occupied by the transistors of an op-amp.

The resistance of the binary weighted memristors grows exponentially when the resolution of DAC increases ($R, 2R, \dots, 2^{n-1}R$). Since the resistance of the memristors are limited (bounded between the maximum and minimum resistances), a large number of memristors may be needed. To overcome this problem, for higher resolutions, we suggest that the memristive branches of DAC grouped into 4-bit (partial) clusters and use a second stage for combining the results of the partial clusters.
