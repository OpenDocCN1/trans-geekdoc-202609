
## Discovering Catalytic Reaction Networks Using Deep Reinforcement Learning from First-Principles

Tian Lan and Qi An*

### ABSTRACT

Determining the reaction pathways, which is central to illustrating the working mechanisms of a catalyst, is severely hindered by the high complexity of the reaction and the extreme scarcity of the data. Here, we develop a novel artificial intelligence framework integrating deep reinforcement learning (DRL) techniques with density functional theory simulations to automate the quantitative search and evaluation on the complex catalytic reaction networks from zero knowledge. Our framework quantitatively transforms the first-principles-derived free energy landscape of the chemical reactions to a DRL environment and the corresponding actions. By interacting with this dynamic environment, our model evolves by itself from scratch to a complete reaction path. We demonstrate this framework using the Haber-Bosch process on the most active Fe(111) surface. The new path found by our framework has a lower overall free energy barrier than the previous study based on domain knowledge, demonstrating its outstanding capability in discovering complicated reaction paths. Looking forward, we anticipate that this framework will open the door to exploring the fundamental reaction mechanisms of many catalytic reactions.

### INTRODUCTION

To address the emerging energy crisis and environmental issues, it is essential to discover and optimize novel catalysts that convert raw materials efficiently into valuable chemicals and compounds, such as the ammoxidation of propene into acrylonitrile, conversion of methanol to gasoline, and the Haber–Bosch synthesis of ammonia from nitrogen gas. Traditional advances in developing industrial catalysts have been incremental. In order to make rapid progress on the optimization of highly efficient catalysts, it is essential to understand reaction mechanism and apply reaction mechanism-based rational design approaches. Tremendous experimental and theoretical efforts have been devoted to identifying reaction mechanisms, elementary steps, and rate-determining steps in many industrially important catalytic reactions. However, a catalytic reaction may involve many intermediates and branch paths, making it challenging to determine the complete reaction network. For example, the Haber–Bosch reaction on an Fe catalyst may involve ~20 important surface configurations and ~10 important reaction steps, as well as many potential branch reactions with dopants. Therefore, it is demanding to develop novel techniques that can effectively determine the reaction network in complex reactions. Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) has achieved great success in many research areas. Particularly, supervised learning has been extensively applied in computational heterogeneous catalysis and advances the understanding of the relationships between materials properties and catalytic performance (e.g., activity, selectivity, and stability of catalysts). For example, Abdelfatah et al. predicted the transition-state energy of hydrogenation reactions on metal catalysts using various machine learning models with conventional descriptors (e.g., product or reactant energy) and other descriptors (e.g., bond counts of surface species and metal descriptors). They concluded that adding bond counts and metal descriptors improves the performance of both linear and nonlinear models. Ulissi et al. proposed to determine the reaction network of a catalyst using the machine learning derived surrogate model that can effectively predict the energetics of most important reaction steps. The recent progress on applying machine learning techniques to heterogeneous catalysis was summarized in a recent review. However, the application of AI to catalytic reactions has been severely limited by the extreme scarcity of data describing the catalytic reaction networks, while a tremendous amount of data is, in general, a prerequisite for building a reliable AI model.

Using ideas from the optimal control of the incompletely known Markov decision process,24 reinforcement learning utilizes a limited amount of data to train a learning agent to interact over time with its environment to achieve a goal. Since the AlphaGo program defeated professional Go world champions,25 this technique has been extended to solve problems in video games,26 social science,27 and, most recently, some semiquantitative tasks in chemistry.28,29 However, defining a quantitative chemical environment suitable for the reinforcement learning representation of catalytic reactions is much more complicated in science and has been barely explored. Moreover, the dynamic interaction between the agent and the environment requires extensive and in-depth information that cannot be obtained directly from the experiment.

### RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

**Environment Definition.** We selected Fe-bcc(111) as the catalytic surface in our study because it is the most activated surface for NH3 synthesis.34,35 Recent DFT studies on NH3 synthesis over the Fe-bcc(111) surface suggested a plausible reaction path consisting of 24 important surface configurations and 12 transition states with a turnover frequency (TOF) of 17.7 NH3/s per 2 × 2 sites at 673 K and 20 atm pressure (1.5 Torr NH3 pressure). As the reaction reaches a steady state with NH3 pressure increasing to ∼1 atm, the calculated TOF decreases to 4.6 NH3/s.8 Although the TOF is consistent with the single-crystal experimental value of 9.7 NH3/s,7 these studies mostly relied on the exhaustive search and domain expert knowledge, whereas many plausible intermediate states and branch reaction paths are not able to be considered. The great complexity of the Haber–Bosch reaction makes it an ideal subject to challenge our DRL model.

Here, we developed a novel deep reinforcement learning (DRL) framework to determine a complex reaction network given an initial reactant and a target product from zero prior knowledge of catalytic reactions. Our framework is not constrained by the limits of data and human knowledge, but evolves by itself from scratch into a complete reaction path. Specifically, we map the evolution of the chemical reaction path into a Markov decision process taking place in a delicate environment depicted quantitatively by density functional theory (DFT) simulations. Using ammonia (NH3) synthesis on an Fe catalyst as a model system, we demonstrate that DRL efficiently constructs the complex reaction path by adopting the proper environment and training algorithms. Our agent accurately predicts a reaction network that is more plausible with lower free energy barriers than previous studies.7,8

We selected the Haber–Bosch process as the model system because NH3 is the main intermediate in the nitrogen cycle on Earth.30 The Haber–Bosch process alone is responsible for over 2% of all global energy usage and produces 160 million tons of NH3 annually.31,52 This scale of production may increase substantially if ammonia is used as an energy vector to replace fossil fuels due to its attractive features of high energy density and abundance of raw materials.35 Metal catalysts are used to accelerate the reaction, but high temperatures and pressures of atmospheric nitrogen gas (N2) and hydrogen gas (H2) (extracted from natural gas sources) are required. A great deal of research over the last century has aimed to increase the performance of the Haber–Bosch process, but recent progress has been slow. To make the process more efficient under milder conditions—which would dramatically decrease production costs and enable smaller, more widely dispersed plants—we must optimize heterogeneous catalysts, which requires understanding the reaction mechanism of the Haber–Bosch process.
