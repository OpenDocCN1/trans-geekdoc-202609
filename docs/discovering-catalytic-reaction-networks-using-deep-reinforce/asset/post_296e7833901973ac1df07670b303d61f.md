The value network (critic) \(V_{\theta_v}(s_i)\) shares the same hidden layers with the policy network, but it outputs a scalar value that quantitatively estimates whether the action is on track to generate rewards. More precisely, we define the observed return at step \(t\) up to the terminal step K as the sum of discounted future rewards \(r_{t+1}, r_{t+2} ... r_T\) with the discount factor \(\gamma = 0.99\)

\(G_t = \sum_{\tau=t+1}^{K} \gamma^{\tau-t-1} r_\tau\) (1)

and the value function, \(V_{\theta_v}(s_i)\), approximates the expected value of the return, \(E_\pi[G_t|s_i]\), given state \(s_t\).

During the training, the model updates the parameters, \(\theta_p\) and \(\theta_v\), of the policy and value network. To update \(\theta_p\), policy gradients (PGs) are taken according to the direction given by

\(PG = (G_t - V_{\theta_v}(s_i)) \nabla_{\theta_p} \log \pi_{\theta_p}(a_i|s_i)\) (2)

Here \(G_t\) is the observed return defined in eq 1 and collected in the rollout as detailed in the Rollout Sampling section. The current value network, \(V_{\theta_v}(s_i)\), is used to update the policy \(\pi_{\theta_p}(a_i|s_i)\), with the policy gradient being in a direction that will improve the policy to favor actions that increase the expected return. Moreover, the policy gradient becomes more reliable when the value function has a more accurate estimation of the expected return. To improve the value network, the parameters \(\theta_v\) are updated to minimize the mean square error between the current value function, \(V_{\theta_v}(s_i)\), and the observed return, \(G_t\). As discussed earlier, the input data is a vector with 23 entries describing the state \(s_t\). The model will accept a minimum of 300 data points (a training minibatch) in each training step. Thus, the training utilized the stochastic gradient descent on the minibatch of the shape of (300, 23).

### Rollout Sampling

For each subsequent step \(t\), the rollout data are collected using the Monte Carlo (MC) method. Based on the current state \(s_t\), the actor decides an action \(a_t\) which is drawn from the distribution generated by the policy network \(\pi_{\theta_p}\). By executing the action, the model transits to the next state \(s_{t+1}\) and claims the corresponding reward. The rollout ends when either the final state or the maximum step of a rollout K is reached. In our experiment, the maximum step K is set to be 500. We did not use approximation methods such as TD(\(\lambda\))\(^{45}\) to approximate the observed return \(G_t\), but used the Monte Carlo method to faithfully collect the return along the rollout trajectory. By the end of the rollout, training samples consisting of the list of tuples \((s_t, a_t, G_t)\) are collected.

For every policy update (training episode), the model is configured to investigate many independent MC rollouts created on-policy, collected in parallel, and finally synchronized to update the policy. The rollouts that do not reach the final state by the terminal step K will have a certain down-sampling rate for the random selection without replacement. The number of rollouts is dynamically determined in the runtime and is inversely proportional to the average length of these rollouts for the current policy update. We find our sampling methods play an important role in preventing the model from converging to the suboptimal policy biased by the locally optimized trajectories. In our experiment, we choose 0.3 as the down-sampling rate, and a size of 300 is the minimum number of data points that are required. This is equivalent to about 10 rollouts close to the final convergence.

### Curriculum Learning

We introduce two-stage learning curricula for the model to learn. For the first curriculum, to avoid unstable learning dynamics created by the initial introduction of penalties for executing impossible actions, we impose a tiny negative reward of -0.01 in the environment building. This small penalty alleviates the accumulated negative rewards that otherwise easily prevent the model from early exploration on the complex network while still holding fairly reasonable sense for the model to exploit the favorable actions. In the second curriculum, the negative rewards are increased to -0.1 for a more realistic environment, and details about the rewards can be found in Table S1. In our experiment, we run 100 000 training episodes for the first curriculum and 50 000 for the second.

### Entropy Regularization

In the policy gradient objective, we add the policy entropy (PS) to eq 2 as an extra term, which is defined as

\(PS = -\beta \sum_i \pi_{\theta_p}(a_i^i|s_i) \log(\pi_{\theta_p}(a_i^i|s_i))\) (3)

Here the superscript \(i\) denotes the action index and \(\beta\) is the entropy regularization coefficient. The use of the entropy term encourages the model to explore more on the actions that have small probabilities in the action space following the current policy. We find the entropy regularization necessary to handle our complex network with a large action space. For the first curriculum, we configured the entropy regularization coefficient to be 0.1 and this coefficient is decreased to 0.05 in the second curriculum while the model becomes more stable.

### DRL Model Convergence

The initial parameters for our DRL model are given with no prior information. But our model converges effectively to a stable and optimal strategy, as shown in Figure S8 of the SI. This is due to the following reasons. First of all, we randomly initialize the parameters via the Xavier initialization method that is designed to keep the scale of the gradients roughly the same in all layers and significantly helps the stability and convergence of the DNN.\(^{46}\) Second, we applied the A2C method, which is a synchronous version of A3C. The synchronized gradient update keeps the training more cohesive and make convergence much faster than the Vanilla policy gradient.\(^{37}\) Third, the framework combines with DFT simulations, using the detailed free energy landscape to construct the accurate reward functions over the actions. Last but not least, the curriculum learning effectively helps the convergence of our model.

### DFT Simulations

All DFT simulations are carried out using the VASP software with plane-wave basis sets for the electronic wave function.\(^{47-50}\) The energy cutoff for plane wave expansion is 500 eV in all calculations. We use the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof exchange and correlation functional\(^{51,52}\) for electronic interactions and the D3 corrections for van der Waals interactions.\(^{53}\) The Methfessel–Paxton scheme is adopted to account for the electron partial occupation, and the width of the electronic smearing is 0.2 eV. The spin polarization is allowed in all calculations to predict the magnetic moments of Fe atoms accurately. The energy convergence for the self-consistent field and the force convergence for ionic steps are \(10^{-6}\) eV and \(10^{-3}\) eV/Å, respectively. The first Brillouin zone is sampled using the \(\Gamma\)-centered mesh method with the \(4 \times 4 \times 1\) K-points used in the slab calculations. The simulation setup is consistent with the previous studies, and the details can be found in the previous work.\(^{7,8}\)

The Fe-bcc(111) slab model consists of a (\(2 \times 2\)) unit cell in the (111) plane and six layers perpendicular to the plane. The bottom three layers are fixed, and the top three layers are allowed to relax in the geometry optimization. A vacuum of 15 Å is added to the slab model to minimize the interactions between periodic images. To examine the possible size effect of the slab model, we increase the six-layer slab to nine-layer and compute the free energy of several important states along the free energy diagram (Figure 4) on this nine-layer model. The bottom three layers of this nine-layer slab are fixed in the geometry optimization. To investigate the effect of the catalytic surface, we expand the (\(2 \times 2\)) six-layer slab to a (\(4 \times 2\)) slab and compute the free energy of these important states on the enlarged model. Assuming that the free energy corrections for the three slab models are the same, we obtain the free energies of the important states and list them in Table S3 of the SI. The free energy of important states agrees very well for all three slab models, indicating that the DFT simulations are robust using the six-layer (\(2 \times 2\)) slab model.
