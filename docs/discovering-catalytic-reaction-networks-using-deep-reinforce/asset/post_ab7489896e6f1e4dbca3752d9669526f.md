To describe the surface reactions occurring at various sites, we define a total of 20 surface sites numbered 1–20, including four top sites, four sublayer sites, four bridge sites, four top–sublayer sites, and four top–sublayer–deep layer sites, as shown in Figure S1 of the Supporting Information (SI). Each adsorption position has four sites on the surface due to the application of a (2 × 2) Fe-bcc(111) surface model.

We model the evolution of the chemical reaction path as a Markov decision process defined by a state space S, an action space A, a probability transition P(s_t+1|a_t, s_t), and a reward function r that is generally defined in the space S × A. To define the environment state, we introduce an encoded state vector s_t at step t that projects the total number of 20 surface sites onto a vector. The vector also accounts for N2, H2, and NH3 gas species involved in the Haber–Bosch reaction. Figure 1(d) shows the first 20 entries of the state vector that are used to represent the 20 surface sites; the value of each entry represents the adsorbents displayed in Figure 1(e). The adsorbents H, N, NH, NH2, NH3, and N2 are encoded by integers 1–6, respectively, and an empty site is represented by the number 0. The last three entries of the vector are adopted to record the number of gas species (N2↔21, H2↔22, and NH3↔23) along the reaction path. The reaction starts with the initial state s0 which is a 4N-covered surface plus one N2 and three H2 gas molecules (Figure S2 of the SI). The model surface is consistent with the fact that the Fe surface has a high coverage of N atoms under Haber–Bosch reaction conditions. The final state sf is defined as the 4N configuration with two NH3 molecules in the gas phase (Figure S2 of the SI), representing the complete reaction N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3.

The actions connecting s0 and sf are defined in terms of four main categories: (1) H2 adsorption/desorption from the surface; (2) N2 adsorption, migration, and dissociation on the surface; (3) surface hydrogen migration to hydrogenate N, NH, or NH2 species (for example H + NH ⇌ NH2); and (4) NH3 desorption from the surface. Based on the specific surface sites for reactions, the actions are further refined to form the action space utilized in the environment. The definitions of all 17 actions are displayed in Figures S3–S6 of the SI. The defined 17 actions represent the fundamental reaction steps on the Fe-bcc (111) surface, and they are necessary to complete the reaction cycle by connecting the reactant and the product. Allowing any action order and the absence or repetition of the action, the DRL model is trying to figure out from scratch the most plausible reaction pathway and configuration via Markov decision processes. We do not consider the H-assisted N2 activation in the 17 actions since migration of H to N2 has a much higher free energy barrier (1.74 eV) than other plausible N2 activation steps. Figure S7 of the SI shows the reaction barrier calculation of H migration to surface N2 (α state) to form NH2.

## Deep Reinforcement Learning Model

Central to the DRL model is a policy $\pi_{\theta_p(a_t|s_t)}$ represented by a deep neural network with parameters $\theta_p$ that analyzes the current state and takes actions. The vector of current state s_t represents one plausible configuration along the reaction path and the action a_t changes the current configuration to the next one. The policy $\pi$ is the strategy of changing the state s. The model parameters $\theta_p$ are initialized randomly with no prior information about the environment or any domain knowledge of the reaction. The policy is then trained by a reinforcement learning strategy designed to maximize the expected value of the sum of discounted future rewards and conditioned on the success of reaching the final state.

Our reinforcement learning strategy is based on a policy gradient algorithm similar to actor–critic. We use proximal policy gradients and the Adam optimizer to compute policy gradients. A value network (critic) $V_{\theta_v}(s_t)$ is trained to predict the expected return and used to update the policy network (actor), which provides the probability distribution of actions in the current state. The architecture of our value and policy networks consists of fully connected deep neural networks (DNNs). The two deep neural networks share the common internal stack of hidden layers but differ by the final output layers. The efficiency of the DRL framework is similar to kMC calculations on the Fe(111) surface.

The key link between the DFT and the DRL in our framework is the reward system associated with actions, and it is derived from DFT calculations from the first principles. We assign a reward r associated with each action that is dependent on the states involved before and after the action; that is, the same action could end up with a very different reward depending on the state it acts on. In particular, r = e^(-ΔE) is employed for each action where ΔE (in eV) is the DFT-derived free energy barrier connecting different states under the reaction condition of T = 673 K and P(H2, N2, NH3) = (15, 5, 1) atm. The free energies are calculated from a high N coverage surface, and the simulation details on the free energy are in the Experimental Section. The value of ΔE varies according to the site occupancy on the surface represented by the state; for example, the different occupancy configurations surrounding the reaction sites can significantly change the free energy barriers incurred by the same action. Additionally, a negative reward is given if obvious obstacles prevent the action from happening. Those obstacles and constraints are similar to the geometrical boundary mask in a video game environment. For example, adding an extra H to a site that has already been occupied by an H species is not possible and, in this case, the action will not effect to change the state and will get an immediate negative reward. This is a complex network environment consisting of 23 positions with values ranging from 0 to 6, and there are a total of 17 actions defined in the competition to explore the most plausible path linking the initial state and the final state. The detailed reward values are displayed in Table S1 of the SI, and more detailed explanations for the reward definition can be found in the SI.

One of the main challenges of finding the catalytic reaction path is the subtle trade-off between exploitation and exploration. Exploitation in this work means that the model claims the rewards based on the current strategy, while exploration means finding a better strategy to produce more plausible intermediate states along the reaction path. The goal is to find a strong decision-making strategy so that our model has the highest probability of taking the optimal action. On the one hand, the optimal action trajectory guarantees reaching the end state and maximizes rewards given the observed states. On the other hand, the high probability for those optimal actions will increase the stability of constructing complex reaction networks and is crucial to confidently determine the most plausible reaction path given the start and final states. During the training stage, if we constantly choose the best action known so far, the model will not have sufficient chance to explore other plausible actions given the same state, so the model is most likely to follow semioptimal policies leading to small rewards or even bad policies that cannot reach the final state. Moreover, unlike many other problems, the catalytic reaction path found by the DRL model is expected to become significantly shorter in the late training stage compared with that in its early stage. As a result, the total number of samples from a rollout may decrease dramatically with further training. These pose great challenges for exploring this complex environment with sparse rewards, many competing actions, and a huge number of states.

To tackle the exploitation–exploration challenge, we employed several novel strategies. We applied the curriculum learning algorithm to environment building. During training, the model explores multiple environments of varying complexity associated with low to high skill levels and eventually interacts with the realistic environment. This also significantly helps to improve the stability and speed of model convergency. For the policy update, we devised a training strategy derived from the synchronous deterministic variant of A3C but with several modifications: First, for each synchronous training episode, the model is configured to investigate many independent-of-policy rollouts collected in parallel, but the number of rollouts is dynamically determined and is inversely proportional to the average length of these rollouts.
