# # Discovering Catalytic Reaction Networks Using Deep Reinforcement Learning from First-Principles

Tian Lan and Qi An*

ABSTRACT: Determining the reaction pathways, which is central to illustrating the working mechanisms of a catalyst, is severely hindered by the high complexity of the reaction and the extreme scarcity of the data. Here, we develop a novel artificial intelligence framework integrating deep reinforcement learning (DRL) techniques with density functional theory simulations to automate the quantitative search and evaluation on the complex catalytic reaction networks from zero knowledge. Our framework quantitatively transforms the first-principles-derived free energy landscape of the chemical reactions to a DRL environment and the corresponding actions. By interacting with this dynamic environment, our model evolves by itself from scratch to a complete reaction path. We demonstrate this framework using the Haber-Bosch process on the most active Fe(111) surface. The new path found by our framework has a lower overall free energy barrier than the previous study based on domain knowledge, demonstrating its outstanding capability in discovering complicated reaction paths. Looking forward, we anticipate that this framework will open the door to exploring the fundamental reaction mechanisms of many catalytic reactions.

# ## INTRODUCTION

To address the emerging energy crisis and environmental issues, it is essential to discover and optimize novel catalysts that convert raw materials efficiently into valuable chemicals and compounds, such as the ammoxidation of propene into acrylonitrile, conversion of methanol to gasoline, and the Haber–Bosch synthesis of ammonia from nitrogen gas. Traditional advances in developing industrial catalysts have been incremental. In order to make rapid progress on the optimization of highly efficient catalysts, it is essential to understand reaction mechanism and apply reaction mechanism-based rational design approaches. Tremendous experimental and theoretical efforts have been devoted to identifying reaction mechanisms, elementary steps, and rate-determining steps in many industrially important catalytic reactions. However, a catalytic reaction may involve many intermediates and branch paths, making it challenging to determine the complete reaction network. For example, the Haber–Bosch reaction on an Fe catalyst may involve ~20 important surface configurations and ~10 important reaction steps, as well as many potential branch reactions with dopants. Therefore, it is demanding to develop novel techniques that can effectively determine the reaction network in complex reactions. Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) has achieved great success in many research areas. Particularly, supervised learning has been extensively applied in computational heterogeneous catalysis and advances the understanding of

the relationships between materials properties and catalytic performance (e.g., activity, selectivity, and stability of catalysts). For example, Abdelfatah et al. predicted the transition-state energy of hydrogenation reactions on metal catalysts using various machine learning models with conventional descriptors (e.g., product or reactant energy) and other descriptors (e.g., bond counts of surface species and metal descriptors). They concluded that adding bond counts and metal descriptors improves the performance of both linear and nonlinear models. Ulissi et al. proposed to determine the reaction network of a catalyst using the machine learning derived surrogate model that can effectively predict the energetics of most important reaction steps. The recent progress on applying machine learning techniques to heterogeneous catalysis was summarized in a recent review. However, the application of AI to catalytic reactions has been severely limited by the extreme scarcity of data describing the catalytic reaction networks, while a tremendous amount of data is, in general, a prerequisite for building a reliable AI model.
