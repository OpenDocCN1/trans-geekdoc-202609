### 5.1 INTRODUCTION

For many, the talk offered by Dr. Richard P. Feynman in 1959 [1] is considered as the starting point for the development of quantum computing. Back then, Dr. Feynman pointed out that “atoms on a small scale behave like nothing on a large scale, for they satisfy the laws of quantum mechanics” and conjectured that we could perform computations by using “not just circuits, but some system involving the quantized energy levels, or the interactions of the quantized spins.” This turned to be an accurate depiction of quantum computing. The idea of quantum computing was consolidated in 1985 by David Deutsch who conceived the “universal quantum computer” as the quantum generalization of the Turing machine [2]. During that period, however, the practical implications of this research area did not weigh heavily on the public interest. It was not until 1997 that quantum computing became a major concern when Peter Shor published quantum algorithms for computing prime factorization and discrete logarithms in polynomial time [3]. The main implication of that work is that a significant part of modern cryptography will become obsolete if a large-enough quantum computer is built [4]. Banking, government, health care, commerce, and virtually any application deployed over the internet would be affected. Cryptography has entered a race against time to adapt for this new threat [5]. Adapting cryptography for resisting quantum attacks, while maintaining low-enough overheads for constrained devices, is a particularly difficult task.

In this chapter, we explore the challenges in securing wireless sensor networks (WSNs), given the potential avenue of quantum-enabled adversaries (QEA). With this term, we refer to any malicious actor with unrestricted access to a computing system capable of solving quantum algorithms in reasonable time, where the computing power of the device has a linear relation with its monetary cost. With modern threat assessments, it is mandatory to acknowledge the possibility that Internet of Things (IoT) systems can become the target of QEA. Under this assumption, the security standards governing this technology are likely to recommend the use of one or multiple quantum-safe security suites. For high-end IoT nodes, this might not be a major concern, as in some cases these platforms are potent enough for overcoming the overheads of cryptography algorithms. The underlying problem addressed in this chapter mainly exists on the side of the WSNs, whose nodes are devices of limited capabilities: processing power, storage, power supply, and bandwidth. We explore alternatives for providing WSNs with quantum-safe security services and how these can be integrated in a secure form into the IoT.

The contribution in this work is threefold. First, we study the scope of modern security solutions for constrained devices and the status of development of quantum computers. Second, we review potential cryptography solutions that could be used in constrained applications under a quantum-threat model. Lastly, we discuss the foreseeable alternatives for WSNs for integrating into the future IoT and their potential outcomes.

The rest of the chapter is organized as follows. In Section 5.2, a discussion on the state of the art in the current security solution for WSNs is provided. Section 5.3 deals with the progress in the development of quantum computers and their impact on modern cryptography solutions. In Section 5.4, we provide a survey of cryptographic algorithms used in constrained devices that are believed to resist quantum attacks, their costs, and implementation challenges. Section 5.5 contains the main discussion on the challenges for WSNs to operate in the potential scenario where IoT links have moved to support exclusively post-quantum algorithms. Finally, Section 5.6 presents a summary of our findings and conclusions.

### 5.2 LIGHTWEIGHT CRYPTOGRAPHY

Ensuring the security of any piece of information implies upholding a set of characteristics deemed necessary to guard the interests of the owner of these data. The most important of these services include:

- Confidentiality: Only authorized parties can access the data.
- Integrity and Authentication: Data cannot be modified by unauthorized parties, and the data source can be verified.
- Availability: Data are available to the authorized parties at any given time.

Malicious actors work against these services. During the transmission of data, an attacker can eavesdrop on the communication channel, modify the messages, forge messages, or disturb the data flow. All these actions can have negative effects on the communication between the parties. In order to thwart these threats, the parties can make use of cryptographic solutions for providing one or more security functions.

Most of these algorithms such as ciphers and media access control (MAC) functions require the parties to share a common piece of information-denominated key. These keys grant the user with network access by being recognized as an authorized user of the channel. The establishment of these common pieces of data is in itself a critical security service for networked environments. Providing constrained devices with security services, mainly by means of cryptography, is not an easy task. Implementing cryptographic procedures implies burdening the underlying system with security overheads. Lightweight cryptography has surged as a possible solution for this problem. To date, lightweight cryptographic realizations mostly consist of ciphers and hash functions, which aim at providing confidentiality, integrity, and authentication for constrained devices. These primitives are critical for creating affordable security solutions on WSNs and similar IoT domains. However, lightweight versions of more complex mechanisms, such as the ones required for safe-guarding network access, have been rarely studied.

Public-key cryptography (PKC), also called asymmetric cryptography, is critical for providing networks with services that would otherwise be difficult to achieve in a secure way. Key establishment protocols, authentication protocols, and digital signatures are the most popular algorithms in this field. In this group, systems based on elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) have been considered as the most practical asymmetric solution for constrained devices. The strength of ECC relies on the difficulty of finding discrete logarithms over elliptic curve groups: the discrete logarithm problem over elliptic curve groups (ECDLP). The hardness of this problem, compared with contemporary alternatives such as the integer factoring problem and the discrete logarithm problem over multiplicative groups, allows for using smaller key pairs in ECC, which derives in higher efficiency for these algorithms.
