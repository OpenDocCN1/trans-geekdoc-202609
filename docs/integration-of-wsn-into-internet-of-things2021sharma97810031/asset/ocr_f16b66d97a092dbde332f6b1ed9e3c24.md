# # 5.1 INTRODUCTION

For many, the talk offered by Dr. Richard P. Feynman in 1959 [1] is considered as the starting point for the development of quantum computing. Back then, Dr. Feynman pointed out that “atoms on a small scale behave like nothing on a large scale, for they satisfy the laws of quantum mechanics” and conjectured that we could perform computations by using “not just circuits, but some system involving the quantized energy levels, or the interactions of the quantized spins.” This turned to be an accurate depiction of quantum computing. The idea of quantum computing was consolidated in 1985 by David Deutsch who conceived the “universal quantum computer” as the quantum generalization of the Turing machine [2]. During that period, however, the practical implications of this research area did not weigh heavily on the public interest. It was not until 1997 that quantum computing became a major concern when Peter Shor published quantum algorithms for computing prime factorization and discrete logarithms in polynomial time [3]. The main implication of that work is that a significant part of modern cryptography will become obsolete if a large-enough quantum computer is built [4]. Banking, government, health care, commerce, and virtually any application deployed over the internet would be affected. Cryptography has entered a race against time to adapt for this new threat [5]. Adapting cryptography for resisting quantum attacks, while maintaining low-enough overheads for constrained devices, is a particularly difficult task.

In this chapter, we explore the challenges in securing wireless sensor networks (WSNs), given the potential avenue of quantum-enabled adversaries (QEA). With this term, we refer to any malicious actor with unrestricted access to a computing system capable of solving quantum algorithms in reasonable time, where the computing power of the device has a linear relation with its monetary cost. With modern threat assessments, it is mandatory to acknowledge the possibility that Internet of Things (IoT) systems can become the target of QEA. Under this assumption, the security standards governing this technology are likely to recommend the use of one or multiple quantum-safe security suites. For high-end IoT nodes, this might not be a major concern, as in some cases these platforms are potent enough for overcoming the overheads of cryptography algorithms. The underlying problem addressed in this chapter mainly exists on the side of the WSNs, whose nodes are devices of limited capabilities: processing power, storage, power supply, and bandwidth. We explore alternatives for providing WSNs with quantum-safe security services and how these can be integrated in a secure form into the IoT.

The contribution in this work is threefold. First, we study the scope of modern security solutions for constrained devices and the status of development of quantum computers. Second, we review potential cryptography solutions that could be used in constrained applications under a quantum-threat model. Lastly, we discuss the foreseeable alternatives for WSNs for integrating into the future IoT and their potential outcomes.
