
The point where a quantum processor outperforms a classical computer is known as quantum supremacy. In the fall of 2018, a group of researchers demonstrated, for the first time, the advantage of quantum computing by showing that “parallel quantum algorithms running in a constant time period are strictly more powerful than their classical counterparts” for certain problems [21]. Although the approach followed in this experiment represents a “highly restricted form of quantum computation,” it highlights that the development of quantum systems is progressing steadily.

The challenges in the development of quantum computing are diverse. From the algorithmic point of view, designing procedures for solving actual problems is not trivial. On the physical side, the number of qubits is fundamental but “the processor must also have low error rates on readout and logical operations” [22]. As the number of qubits grows, the error rates also increase. One of the modern strategies to deal with the quantum error is to use multiple physical qubits to construct a logical qubit by means of entanglement.

### 5.3.2 Impact of Quantum Computing in the IoT

The IoT is a collection of constrained devices that fulfill tasks of data collection, processing, and even modify their environment [23]. Constrained devices, as defined in [24], refer to small devices with limited resources in CPU, memory, and power.

A set of protocols including the IEEE 802.15.4 standard, the constrained application protocol (CoAP), and the IPv6 over low-power wireless personal area network standard (6LoWPAN) rule over the IoT operation [23]. These norms define how security services such as confidentiality, integrity, and authentication can be provided for constrained devices. This comes with two shortcomings. First, the standards rely on current cryptographic standards at the cost of impacts on the performance and energy of the underlying devices. Second, the standards need to be updated to account for the new threat found in quantum computing. Table 5.4 provides details on the security features offered by different IoT standards, and the expected impact of large quantum computers on these norms. As it can be noted, these specifications that have just begun to be adopted may soon enough find themselves in the need of major overhauls.

Some works have addressed the first shortcoming proposed: the inadequacy of current cryptography standards for use in the IoT. The approach followed in [25–27] has been to adopt lightweight cryptography primitives for improving the efficiency of the IoT protocols. However, all these examples rely on ECC that is on itself vulnerable to quantum computing. Thus, falling in the second shortcoming previously identified, cryptographic standards need to be updated to resist quantum threats.

Table 5.4 also includes some actions that might need to be taken for securing IoT against quantum attackers, for example, increasing keys and MAC lengths. However, there is not quite a clear idea as of what to do to replace the would-be unsecure PKC primitives. ## 5.4 POST-QUANTUM CRYPTOGRAPHY

Quantum computers that are capable of breaking modern security standards are not available yet, but it is estimated that they might appear by 2030 [28]. One can think that there is still time to migrate from current solutions to quantum-resistant alternatives. However, a very feasible scenario implies that a malicious actor can start storing our, today secure, communications and eventually gain access to the data when a large-enough quantum computer is eventually created.

Cryptographic algorithms that are believed to be secure in the foreseeable post-quantum world are said to pertain to PQC. As reviewed in Tables 5.2 and 5.3, whereas symmetric standards will have to be updated, asymmetric cryptography will need to be redesigned for resisting quantum attacks.

As defined in [29], we understand for PQC the subarea of cryptography that deals with the design, cryptanalysis, and implementation of cryptographic algorithms is supposed to be able to withstand attacks by quantum computers. Quantum-safe asymmetric cryptosystems have existed for some time, but their latency and memory requirements far surpass those of ECC. For this reason, they were not previously considered for practical use. Given the advances in the development of quantum processors, these constructions are now being revisited. Experts point out that we should already migrate to PQC standards so that our information remains secure in the long term [28].

Novel cryptographic proposals are often made with claims to be “secure and efficient,” but lack of auditing generates that critical vulnerabilities remain hidden for long periods of time. This is the most dangerous pitfall in the search for new cryptosystems. Ultimately, security strength is a trait that ought not to be compromised. As stated in [5], “the central challenge in post-quantum cryptography is to meet demands for cryptographic usability and flexibility without sacrificing confidence.” The most relevant public-key solutions that are believed to be post-quantum safe can be classified into five main groups depending on the underlying hard problems. These classes are described in the following.

### 5.4.1 Linear Codes

Linear codes are elements employed in data communications for correcting errors that randomly occur in the transmission of messages. Decoding a random linear code is an NP-hard problem [30]. In 1978, McEliece used an instance of this coding problem for constructing the first code-based public-key encryption system. In the McEliece cryptosystem, the private key is a random binary irreducible Goppa code, and the public key is a random generator matrix; the ciphertext is a codeword to which some errors have been added, and only with the Goppa code (the private key) the errors can be removed [31]. Other code-based proposals have been made over the years, and these are generally efficient regarding processing times, but their public keys tend to be large.

### 5.4.2 Lattices

The use of lattices in cryptography originally involved cryptanalysis tasks. As expressed in [32], “lattice algorithms can be used to break cryptographic protocols, while hard lattice problems can be used to design secure cryptosystems.” The most common instances of lattice problems are the short integer solution problem and the learning with errors problem and its variants. The popular cryptosystem NTRU also falls within this classification. As summarized by NIST [4], most lattice-based cryptosystems are relatively simple, efficient, and highly parallelizable, but it is difficult to give precise estimates of their security.
