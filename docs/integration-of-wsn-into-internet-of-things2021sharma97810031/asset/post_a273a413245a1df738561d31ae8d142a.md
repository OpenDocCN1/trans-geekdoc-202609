## 5.5 WSNs AND THE POST-QUANTUM IoT

Integrating WSNs into the IoT in a secure manner requires both platforms to support at least one of the standardized security suites: IEEE 802.15.4, CoAP, 6LoWPAN, among others. Using standardized security solutions contributes to preserve interoperability as custom approaches would reduce the application scope of a technology.

Once NIST has concluded the standardization process for quantum-safe algorithms for encryption, signature, and key exchange, it is likely that some of the security specifications ruling over the IoT will eventually be updated. This will present a challenge for WSNs that are already operating within the IoT ecosystem. As we have shown, the realizations of post-quantum algorithms carry heavy taxes for the underlying systems in terms of processing latency and storage. Therefore, new approaches for updating the resilience of WSN devices must be addressed. But also, new WSNs created for collaborative work might benefit from rethinking their security approach in order to maintain low manufacturing costs while complying with the new requirements of the medium. In this section, we evaluate the different approaches that could be used for a secure integration of WSNs into the IoT, under the assumption that the latter has evolved to integrate PQC solutions from the pool of potential candidates identified by NIST.

### 5.5.1 UPDATING CURRENT NOTES TO SUPPORT PQC ALGORITHMS

Keeping up with the consumer demand implies that many WSN motes are manufactured beforehand and stored for later deployment. This is also an economic factor considering how ordering large amounts of the underlying components of a node can drive down the manufacturing costs significantly. Therefore, remodeling a device is challenging when the sensors, power sources, communication hardware, memories, and processors cannot be changed. What can be done, however, is to update the processor software for supporting new potential security solutions. Yet, can these devices afford to do that?

The most constrained of the devices from Table 5.6 has in total 32 Kb of memory. A cryptosystem with keys of around 2 Kb would require 6% of the device's memory resources just for storing its keys. The criteria for implementing PQC in the context of constrained devices must consider the storage requirements of the algorithm.

Memory usage is relevant since it reflects on the storage required by the device; however, for PKC there is another dimension that is just as important: bandwidth. Cryptographic protocols such as the key exchange require the parties to exchange some “public keys” at least once. Larger keys imply that more bits need to be transmitted or received, where the communication costs generally outweigh the processing costs. In [48], the authors study the communication costs of the MicaZ and TelosB motes. For the MicaZ, the work found that transmitting one byte is equivalent to performing 1,360 cycles of operation and receiving one byte is equivalent to performing 1,520 cycles of operation. In the case of the TelosB, transmitting and receiving a byte were estimated to cost as much as 4,800 and 5,440 cycles of processing, respectively. Moreover, storing data is costly in terms of energy. Maintaining the memory values is costly for some technologies, not to mention that memory accesses are not free either. These estimates show that schemes with shorter key sizes can offer some advantages regarding energy consumption over solutions with shorter processing times.

However, for WSN motes, longer processing times imply higher energy expenditures. Processors are generally optimized to finish a task and go into hibernation; hence, it is desirable to complete this task in a short time. Let us consider the Cortex-M3 and Cortex-M4 32-bit processors as a case study. The former was released in 2004 and used in WSN motes such as the Preon32 and the OpenMote. The latter, released in 2010, has been employed as a popular benchmarking platform for PQC algorithms as it can be observed in Table 5.5. The shortest latency results for the Cortex-M4 were reported in [39] for the Round5 key encapsulation mechanism, totaling 982,000 cycles for a full key exchange. In contrast, a performance-oriented realization of ephemeral ECDH with 128-bit classical security from [49] requires a latency of 729,900 cycles on the Cortex-M4 processor (using the FourQ elliptic curve and clocked at 8 MHz). While the performance estimates are close if we account for the frequency variations, the ECC solution requires 80 bytes for key storage whereas the PQC alternative uses 1,348 bytes, almost 17 times as much. Summarizing, high-end WSN motes could deliver similar performance and energy results if they were updated to implement the faster PQC algorithms, but they would need to assign significantly more memory to cryptographic tasks.

### 5.5.2 PROMOTING THE BASE STATION AS UNIQUE IOT GATEWAY

WSNs are heterogeneous in nature, yet there is a common piece of equipment that can be found in any deployment of these technologies: a base station (BS). This element, which generally takes the role of a personal area network (PAN) coordinator and general collector in classical WSNs, is a device endowed with higher processing capabilities and storage than the actual sensor nodes.

These BSs have conventionally been used as the access point for the users to the harvested data. Following this approach, some WSNs could choose to have the BS as the single element fully merged into the IoT ecosystem, whereas the leaf nodes are left outside of the grid. Hence, the motes would not be concerned with the potential costs of future quantum-safe IoT standards.

On first sight this appears to be an acceptable compromise. But we must consider that the leaf nodes would not be able to rely on conventional PKC for creating secure channels, which might open the WSN to a wide range of threats. Second, not providing the motes with internet access would affect the quality of service for the user as the data would first need to be routed to the single access point. This is also detrimental to modern WSN paradigms such as Edge and Fog computing. Lastly, if the BS is the single internet access point of the WSN, this provides attackers with a clear attack target for compromising the operability of the WSN.

### 5.5.3 Expanding the Mote Capabilities

While it might not be practical to update the hardware of WSNs already on deployment, or motes already manufactured, a likely outcome of a post-quantum IoT environment is that sensor nodes will have to be redesigned to keep up with processing requirements of new security algorithms. The market of mobile devices is constantly evolving to provide further features as the user's demands grow. This can be observed, for example, in how over the past decade 32-bit microprocessors have slowly replaced the prevalent 8-bit architectures.

So far, Moore's law has been used for modeling the trend in increasing the component density of silicon devices. This has a direct relation to the final costs of new generation chips, when the research, development, and manufacturing costs are considered. Thus, it might be possible that future WSN motes could be equipped with more powerful processors while the manufacturing costs are maintained. A similar observation has been made for storage devices, as modeled by Kryder's law. On the other hand, the design of low-cost power sources and energy harvesting technologies have shown slower developments without addressing environmental concerns associated with discarding large amounts of motes. But according to Dennard's scaling, it is expected that as the transistor size decreases, the power requirement would also decrease; put the other way: if the area is kept constant, the processing efficiency is increased while spending the same energy. Hence, legacy power systems might continue to be used. However, it has been shown that Moore's law is beginning to show a deceleration [50]. This implies that if the processing demands of the sensor nodes keep increasing, eventually a single-processor system will not be able to handle all the required tasks. If new WSN motes are being considered parting from the introduction of quantum adversaries, it might be convenient to revisit the general architecture of these devices in order to offload the security tasks from the main processor by introducing coprocessors or hardware accelerators.

Another concern lies on the increased bandwidth requirements of post-quantum algorithms. Larger keys imply larger message payloads in cryptographic protocols. Also, new attack avenues convey the need to design novel protocols, with potentially more messages being exchanged. When this is combined with the largely unattended design of efficient radio links, the new algorithms could demand orders of magnitude more energy from the device than previous security solutions.
