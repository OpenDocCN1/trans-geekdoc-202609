
Individual insurance could be integrated into a smart contract or program agreed and available to all stakeholders. Accounts can be automatically validated, increase cycle time, reduce costs, and virtually eliminate litigation.

### 16.5.6 MEDICAL EXAMINATION

Having a secured centralized storage of clinical trials and patient outcomes for new treatments can dramatically improve patient care and outcomes. Immutable, time-stamped blockchain records of clinical trials and results could help reduce results sharing, data monitoring, and unethical reporting, reducing both fraud and error.

Blockchains could mobilize new innovative research initiatives by sharing patient outcomes more widely (with the patient's consent). In addition, new and innovative research initiatives would be catalyzed by a broader sharing of patient outcomes (with the patient's consent) and thus stimulate remarkable collaboration between participants and researchers.

### 16.5.7 CASE MANAGEMENT

Blockchains can also have a positive impact on patient management. Once a patient sees a physician and a treatment plan has been developed, the treatment schedule can be added to the blockchain book as part of the patient's medical records.

Then a smart contract can be developed with all elements of patient management. The contract specifies the framework for the patient's treatment goals in combination with the rules defined in the smart agreement to monitor and evaluate the patient's progress. When the patient record is integrated into the blockchain, it can provide chronological data on the provision of care and the patient's response.

Another layer of benefits can be found when a patient's health information is removed from portable social media monitoring devices and apps and then uploaded to the patient's personal blockchain registry. Again, smart contracts can measure how well patients are performing against their health goals.

The advantage of the process is that everyone in the network can see patient data, but only when the patient gives consent, which eliminates the need for centralized control and reduces the risk of PHI data theft.

## 16.6 QUANTUM COMPUTING

Quantum computing is a growing discipline with great potential in skyrocketing biomedical research, particularly healthcare analytics. The concept of quantum computing derives from physics. Back in the 1950s, Richard Feynman paved the way for quantum mechanics. Quantum mechanics had been conceptualized in computer science at the beginning of the 20th century. Essentially, quantum computing replaces the binary algorithm with a three-variable system, where 0 or 1, as well as 0 and 1, are possible. This expansion of binary computing enables more complicated and hence more realistic contexts of a function [31].

Quantum computing healthcare applications have recently attracted the attention of researchers. In general, it is considered as a tool with great potential in enhancing our understanding of genomics, physiology, pharmacology, and several other areas of medicine and health care. The practical use of quantum computing is generally unknown given that it is not available for commercial use. Innovative research facilities have access to such modalities. IBM has lately released a platform where researchers across the globe can simulate quantum computing-based data analysis, shedding light on its potential [26, 32].

The enlargement of blockchain-protected data in finance has covered several gigabytes. The growth is expected to become exponential as binary computing switches to quantum computing. Up to this point, quantum computing raises some challenges regarding the complexity and size of the blockchain. However, safety is yet to be discussed.

Recently, Rajan and Visser, researchers from the School of Mathematics and Statistics of the Victoria University of Wellington in New Zealand, published a paper on a conceptual quantum blockchain. According to them, data can be encoded in the form of photons. These photons can be linked chronologically, moving the entanglement from space to time. Quantum data can be encoded in two photons that coexist in the present and the past. As they are entangled with each other, attackers attempting to violate the quantum blockchain are incapable of accessing the previous photon that no longer exists.

The nature of blockchain is such that all blocks are interconnected in a serial manner, for example, the current block with the previous one [32]. Any interference with the current quantum state of the photon will affect the photon connected with it in the past. Even so, this paradigm stands as a strong identifier of the endless possible—conceptual for now—applications of quantum computing. Moreover, it indicates how far a solution designated to a medical issue can go and unravels novel paths of securing biomedical data (clinical trials, drug molecules, patient records, etc.) [18].

## 16.7 LIMITATIONS

### 16.7.1 TECHNICAL LIMITATIONS

While data uniformity and shared warehousing/accessibility of blockchain provide the potential for managing health data, there are challenges related to the blockchain-derived technique which necessitates thorough investigation.

Blockchain techniques could be disruptive and need a new reconsideration and large venture all over the setting, such as modifying the workflows of business or changing the current systems.

Prior to adoption, government-sponsored healthcare givers should initiate a cost–benefit analysis to realize the return on investment and other possible financial or legal issues. For instance, the same record could lie in several network nodes, placed in various nations with diverse confidentiality and data safeguarding prerequisites [33].

An additional pragmatic problem is how to prepare blockchain to warehouse health data, which is yet unknown. Initially, blockchain was developed to record transaction data that are fairly linear and limited, where the only concern was if the ongoing transaction could be traced back to the primary deal. Meanwhile, health data, like therapeutic plans and images, might be relational and huge and need further inquiry [7].

Although safeguarding the volume of quantum computing-derived data seems to be an inherent part of blockchain's evolution, we ought to consider the danger that quantum computing poses in terms of blockchain safety [18].

Quantum computing can expand deciphering the encryption of blockchain. This poses a serious threat to the so-called impregnability of blockchain. The most serious concerns come from the finance sector, the alma mater of the healthcare-related blockchain [31]. Given that quantum computers are faster than conventional computers, they can accelerate the way passwords are broken or encryptions are decrypted.

Hacking with quantum engines casts doubt on the encrypted networks’ safety standards. The expected ramifications on encrypted blockchain networks can have a considerable impact rocking the boat as far as the protection of unauthorized access is concerned [31, 34].

Quantum computers have been perceived as the Achilles' heel of the bitcoin blockchain. Their overwhelming computing acceleration advantage theoretically enables them to disrupt the activity of a wide variety of encryption methods including decentralized blockchain ledgers.

Quantum computers can accelerate the production and activation of cracking algorithms. Bitcoin was based on one of the most resistant “uncrackable” algorithms so far. However, from this perspective, quantum computing as a tool of human ingenuity increases the odds of dissolving blockchain’s privacy [24, 35].

Generally, blockchain can be a target of quantum computing because of financial interests, either independent of healthcare data or related to healthcare data, when it comes to insurance companies or employers.

Regardless of the grounds of penetrating blockchain’s impregnability, in case it is achieved, healthcare data will be at stake [36].

### 16.7.2 Legal Limitations

The blockchain feature of the powerful data uniformity caused inviolability where the data, once warehoused in the blockchain, cannot be changed or removed. Meanwhile, if the register contains health data, these individual data would be safeguarded by regulations for keeping confidentiality; several laws would not permit storing individual data permanently. The EU’s General Data Protection Regulation has reinforced the person’s rights to ask for deleting such data. A rule among the privacy principles of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development that inspired several regulations for safeguarding data allows personal removal of these data. For the susceptibility of health data, those who intend to implement blockchain for warehousing them should not disregard such a lawful obligation [7].

The liability issues behind these scenarios are quite complex given that blockchain represents one of the safest digital environments.
