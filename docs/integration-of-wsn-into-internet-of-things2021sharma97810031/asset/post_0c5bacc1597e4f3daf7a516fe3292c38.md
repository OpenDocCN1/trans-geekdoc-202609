## 16.2 HEALTH INFORMATION SYSTEMS

Computerized medical records (CMRs) involve health-related data of a patient housed by the designated healthcare professional to enable retrieval later [9]. For enhancing the management of CMRs, conventional health information systems (HISs) are used to generate new CMR instances, house, query, or retrieve the CMRs of interest [10]. HISs could be a fairly primitive solution and broadly characterized by a graphical user interface or a web service that represents the front end, with a database at the back end, in a centralized or distributed adoption. With patients more and more moving in and out of a country, enabling several interoperable stand-alone CMR solutions to exchange real-time data on health between various healthcare providers and beyond country borders, as necessary, for instance, in medical tourism centers like Singapore, emerges as an obvious need.

Electronic health records (EHRs) are wealthier in data framework than CMRs. Systematically designed HISs and EHRs are necessary for exchanging data and portability by patients. Some EHR solutions enable transferring the medical history of a patient or accessing by multiple healthcare givers at various healthcare facilities in different locations within or even outside a country [11]. Several national and international projects are enhancing the infrastructures and the HISs to meet forthcoming demands [12].

Lately, the prevalence of intelligent devices such as Android, iOS, and wearable devices has caused a significant change in the health sector. These devices can be either owned by the patients or deployed by the healthcare giver to assess the well-being of patients and enable medical monitoring and management. For instance, there is a broad range of smartphone applications (apps) in health, weight loss, fitness, and other health-related themes. Such apps primarily work as monitoring tools for recording user workouts, counting consumed calories or the number of steps, and other statistics [13].

Some other devices, such as mobile phones, wireless body sensors for further sophisticated medical functions like collecting vital signs, for example, assessing heartbeats during exercise with wristbands, or measuring blood sugar levels with self-evaluating devices are also used [14]. These physiological data could be periodically collected and transferred real-time to intelligent devices, prior to transferring them to distant health-based cloud services for more investigations. An additional case is ambient-aided life interventions for health for enabling distant individual health tracking via advanced telemedicine or telehealth services [15]. Such innovations have opened the door for personal health records (PHRs), in which patients are greatly engaged in tracking their health conditions and gathering their data via their wearables or mobile devices [16].

However, PHRs are challenged by the reliability of the data gathered by patients themselves, validation of these data by relevant healthcare professionals, or the legal liabilities for retarded diagnosis or misdiagnosing, since decisions are being taken based upon the data transferred from the patient's device which might later be discovered to be erroneous, perhaps because of a malfunctioning sensor. In spite of these challenges and consequently the legal concerns, building an interoperable HIS, shared physically between various software and smartphone applications, and used by multiple healthcare givers for housing patients' health data and exchanging data easily would be valuable for all users (healthcare givers, patients, and the health care system) [7].

Cloud computing is a promising option that is able to sustain the instant exchange of data irrespective of spatial sites, allowing on-demand access to resources, dealing with health big data, and holding big data analytics for gaining valuable perspectives on analyzing such data for research and strategic decision-making.
