## 9.4 METHODOLOGY

The work can be separated into two stages. In the first stage, the entire lifting of the currents of the selected loads and training of the neural network are done, as shown in Figure 9.27. While the second stage is related to the simulation to identify the loads; Figure 9.28 shows the block diagram representing the phases developed in that stage.

After surveying the currents of each selected appliance, the experimental part corresponded to the execution of three programs developed in MATLAB to identify these electrical charges.

The first program uses a neural network in the backpropagation configuration, which corresponds to the most used standard configuration, with two layers, the first layer is composed of eight neurons. The first test started with 2 neurons and then the amount was increased until the smallest possible difference was reached between the signal returned by the network and the standard target signal, this result was achieved with eight neurons.

The backpropagation algorithm was chosen because it seeks the minimum difference between the desired outputs (target) and the outputs obtained by the neural network (network response), with the minimum of error. The algorithm adjusts the weights between the layers of the error propagation found in each iteration.

From the original signal with 131,072 points, three signals (P, T, and S) were created each with 2,670 points to characterize eight cycles, an empirical definition. If we give exactly 256 samples per cycle, with eight cycles, it would result in 2,048 samples and a rate of 15.36kHz. As the currents were acquired at a rate of 20 kSa/s, each signal is getting 2,670 points to achieve eight cycles in the visual of the graph.

The P and T signals are used to create and train the network, then the S signal is used to simulate the network. The resulting signal Y corresponds to the signal that the neural network returns as a result of the simulation. In other words, the S signal is the signal submitted to the network, and the Y signal is the signal returned by the network.

After completing the first program, the second program is executed to extract the coefficients of the tested signal, being an approximation coefficient and three detail coefficients. Thus, each signal can be represented by its coefficients of the wavelet transform.

The wavelet transform was chosen because it is a tool known for its characteristic of decomposing functions and reconstructing them again. Discrete wavelets were chosen and, due to their simplicity, the Haar wavelet was used, also known as a special case of the Daubechies 1 wavelet, which is widely used in many applications, especially in pattern recognition.

As some signals resulted in very close coefficients, thus making it difficult to identify loads, it was decided to calculate the sum of the coefficient norms in an attempt to obtain different values for close coefficients, thus facilitating the identification process. In this way, it is possible to increase the difference between similar coefficients.

This calculation was performed by the third program, which subsequently compares this value with a database assembled from the sum of the norms of the wavelet coefficients of “standard” signals for each load. This signal corresponds to the T signal, used as a target in the neural network. In this way, it is possible to know whether the signal coming from the neural network (signal Y) really corresponds to the signal being measured (signal S). In the identification, an error of 1% is allowed between the compared signals, and if the error is greater, the algorithm will return that it was not possible to identify the load.

## 9.5 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

In all experiments carried out with the loads measured individually, the algorithm was able to correctly identify the load within the stipulated tolerance of 1%. In experiments with the sum of the signs, identification is possible, increasing the tolerance to 2%, 3%, and 11%, depending on the sign. But as tolerance is increased, the previously correctly identified signals begin to become confused.

After simulating all loads, the results were promising, since the proposed algorithm was able to identify all measured loads, as well as sets of loads measured at the same time, assuming an error of 1% between the simulated load and the standard charge. However, the proposed algorithm requires more studies and details when it comes to the identification of simulations based on the sum of individual signals. The results of these tests are summarized in Table 9.2.
