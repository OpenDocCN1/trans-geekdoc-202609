
The primordial operation in ECC systems is known as scalar multiplication and represents consecutive application of k elliptic curve group operations over an elliptic curve group generator or base point P, where k is known as the scalar. In such systems, the group operation is additive and follows well-defined formulae for a given elliptic curve model. For this reason, k additions of the base point are represented as multiplying P by the scalar k. In ECC, k is generally used as the private key and kP as the public key. With this context, finding the secret key k is akin to solving an instance of the ECDLP. The most efficient classical algorithms for this task have exponential complexity; hence, this problem is considered intractable in the scope of classical computing.

When the ECC system is tailored to constrained devices, it is said to be lightweight. This means that the solutions can observe trade-offs of performance, area, energy, or security. Generally, prioritizing one of these aspects represents compromising the others. Identifying the adequate trade-offs and creating designs that can be adapted to the application requirements are important components of lightweight cryptography.

Software and hardware realizations of PKC algorithms for constrained environments must overcome significant challenges in order to achieve optimal performance while observing the restrictions of the underlying system. ## 5.3 THE THREAT OF QUANTUM COMPUTING

The extent of the power of quantum computing is an open discussion. Theoretical understanding of quantum algorithms and their application to classical problems has started to receive attention only in the past decade. As a result, the reach of application for quantum computers is still unclear. One of the few consensuses is that quantum computing is believed to be unable to solve the classical NP-complete problems. Nonetheless, it is known that quantum computers can solve problems that were believed to be unsolvable in polynomial time.

Shor's quantum algorithms for computing integer factorization and discrete logarithms are prime examples for solving problems considered hard in the context of classical computing. Others, such as Grover's search (proposed by Lov Grover in 1996 for database search), promise speedups over their classical counterparts, quadratic in this case [16]. Both Shor's and Grover's works represent the greatest impact to modern standardized cryptography. Based on these three algorithms, National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has evaluated the state of the actual standards as it is summarized in Table 5.2.

| Algorithm | Type | Purpose | Impact from Large-Scale Quantum Computer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AES | Symmetric key | Encryption | Larger key sizes needed |
| SHA2, SHA3 | – | Hash function | Larger output needed |
| RSA | Public key | Signatures, key establishment | No longer secure |
| ECDSA, ECDH | Public key | Signatures, key exchange | No longer secure |
| DSA | Public key | Signatures, key exchange | No longer secure |

Although Grover's search has a wider range of application than Shor's, it is burdened by its difficulty of parallelization. As noted in [5], each one of Grover's quantum operations must be computed sequentially. Given a finite computation time t, if the number of quantum operations (evaluating a function) that can be performed in t is smaller than the search space (e.g., 2^k/2 for AES with k-bit keys), then many quantum processors would still be required. The number of quantum processors might be smaller than in the classical counterpart scenario but, if evaluating quantum operations results costlier than evaluating the respective classical operations, then Grover search might not turn out to be of practical use. In the worst-case scenario, standardized symmetric algorithms would require to be updated, but PKC (as we know it) might cease to exist if powerful-enough quantum computers are created.

### 5.3.1 QUANTUM COMPUTING

According to [17], quantum computation and quantum information are the study of the information processing tasks that can be accomplished using quantum mechanical systems. For simplicity, we refer to quantum computation and quantum information as quantum computing. In quantum computing, the analog of the classical bit is denominated in qubits. Unlike classical bits that can be either zero or one, qubits can exist in a superposition of basic states. As noted in [18], a simple way to represent a qubit in the physical world is the electron’s spin, which has two possible projections on any coordinate axis.

Due to entanglement, a multiple-qubit system has a state space that grows exponentially in the number of qubits. And because the qubits can exist in a superposition of states, one operator applied to such a system can operate on all the states simultaneously. As it is observed in [19], “an operator can be applied to a superposition of all possible inputs, performing an exponential number of operations simultaneously.” It is through entanglement and superposition that quantum computers might be able to overcome classical computers.

Let a “quantum mechanical system” be the quantum counterpart of a classical computing system. We can broadly identify two classes:

- Those designed for completing a specific task.
- Those created for performing general computations.

In the classical sense, application-specific processors or circuits achieve greater efficiency at the cost of reduced flexibility and face practical constraints of cost, development time, and complexity. In the quantum scenario, application-specific quantum circuits are already available, but large generic quantum processors are still uncertain of becoming a reality. Fortunately, or not, running the algorithms described in Shor’s work requires a general-purpose quantum processor. For this reason, we are interested on these realizations.
