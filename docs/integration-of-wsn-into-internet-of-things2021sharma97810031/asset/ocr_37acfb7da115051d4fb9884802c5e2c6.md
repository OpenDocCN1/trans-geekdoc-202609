# 5.5.3 Expanding the Mote Capabilities

While it might not be practical to update the hardware of WSNs already on deployment, or motes already manufactured, a likely outcome of a post-quantum IoT environment is that sensor nodes will have to be redesigned to keep up with processing requirements of new security algorithms. The market of mobile devices is constantly evolving to provide further features as the user's demands grow. This can be observed, for example, in how over the past decade 32-bit microprocessors have slowly replaced the prevalent 8-bit architectures.

So far, Moore's law has been used for modeling the trend in increasing the component density of silicon devices. This has a direct relation to the final costs of new generation chips, when the research, development, and manufacturing costs are considered. Thus, it might be possible that future WSN motes could be equipped with more powerful processors while the manufacturing costs are maintained. A similar observation has been made for storage devices, as modeled by Kryder's law. On the other hand, the design of low-cost power sources and energy harvesting technologies have shown slower developments without addressing environmental concerns associated with discarding large amounts of motes. But according to Dennard's scaling, it is expected that as the transistor size decreases, the power requirement would also decrease; put the other way: if the area is kept constant, the processing efficiency is increased while spending the same energy. Hence, legacy power systems might continue to be used. However, it has been shown that Moore's law is beginning to show a deceleration [50]. This implies that if the processing demands of the sensor nodes keep increasing, eventually a single-processor system will not be able to handle all the required tasks. If new WSN motes are being considered parting from the introduction of quantum adversaries, it might be convenient to revisit the general architecture of these devices in order to offload the security tasks from the main processor by introducing coprocessors or hardware accelerators.

Another concern lies on the increased bandwidth requirements of post-quantum algorithms. Larger keys imply larger message payloads in cryptographic protocols. Also, new attack avenues convey the need to design novel protocols, with potentially more messages being exchanged. When this is combined with the largely unattended design of efficient radio links, the new algorithms could demand orders of magnitude more energy from the device than previous security solutions.
