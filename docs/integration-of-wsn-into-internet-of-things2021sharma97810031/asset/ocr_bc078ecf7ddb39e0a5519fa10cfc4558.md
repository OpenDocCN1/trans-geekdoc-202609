# 5.3.1 QUANTUM COMPUTING

According to [17], quantum computation and quantum information are the study of the information processing tasks that can be accomplished using quantum mechanical systems. For simplicity, we refer to quantum computation and quantum information as quantum computing. In quantum computing, the analog of the classical bit is denominated in qubits. Unlike classical bits that can be either zero or one, qubits can exist in a superposition of basic states. As noted in [18], a simple way to represent a qubit in the physical world is the electron’s spin, which has two possible projections on any coordinate axis.

Due to entanglement, a multiple-qubit system has a state space that grows exponentially in the number of qubits. And because the qubits can exist in a superposition of states, one operator applied to such a system can operate on all the states simultaneously. As it is observed in [19], “an operator can be applied to a superposition of all possible inputs, performing an exponential number of operations simultaneously.” It is through entanglement and superposition that quantum computers might be able to overcome classical computers.

Let a “quantum mechanical system” be the quantum counterpart of a classical computing system. We can broadly identify two classes:

+   - Those designed for completing a specific task.
+   - Those created for performing general computations.

In the classical sense, application-specific processors or circuits achieve greater efficiency at the cost of reduced flexibility and face practical constraints of cost, development time, and complexity. In the quantum scenario, application-specific quantum circuits are already available, but large generic quantum processors are still uncertain of becoming a reality. Fortunately, or not, running the algorithms described in Shor’s work requires a general-purpose quantum processor. For this reason, we are interested on these realizations.
