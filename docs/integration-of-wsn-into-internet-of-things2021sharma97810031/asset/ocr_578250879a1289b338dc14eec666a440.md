# 9.2.4 Smart House

According to Ref [37,38], the concept of a smart house is related to the term “domotics,” which results from the combination of the Latin word “domus” (house) with “robotics” (automated control of something). This last term enriches the system, making people’s daily lives simpler, satisfying their communication, comfort, and security needs. When home automation appeared (with the first buildings in the 1980s), it was intended to control lighting, air conditioning, security, and the interconnection between these three elements.

Nowadays, the basic idea is the same, the difference is the context for which the system is designed: no longer a military or industrial context, but a domestic one. Although still little known and publicized, for the comfort and convenience it can provide, home automation promises to have many fans. In this way, it allows the use of devices to automate the routines and tasks of a home. Normally, room temperature, lighting, and sound controls are performed, distinguishing it from the normal controls by having a center that controls everything, which is sometimes coupled to a computer and/or internet [38,39].

Within the concept of a smart house, the optimization and monitoring of consumption behavior of each equipment is a fundamental element, especially at the time when alternative and localized sources of energy production, such as those obtained by photovoltaic plates, will be used on a large scale [39,40].

# 9.2.5 Monitoring Systems

One of the areas of study within the Smart Grid concept is the possibility of discriminating the electric bill by equipment connected to the installation. From this, it is possible to know which equipment is consuming more and even analyze the efficiency of each piece of equipment installed in the residence. In this way, a more rational use or even the replacement of some equipment is possible [41,42].

The various works in the area have been researching ways to identify the equipment in use to allocate their share of the energy consumed. When techniques such as videos and recordings are used to determine the type of equipment, the technique is called invasive [43–45]. The big problem with this technique is exactly the issue of privacy. On the other hand, noninvasive techniques use sensors to determine the status of equipment, thus maintaining people’s privacy [46].
