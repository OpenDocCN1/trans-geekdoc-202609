### 8.1 INTRODUCTION

In recent years, one trend that has been noticed to grow steadily and exponentially is the use of technology in aspects where it was never even imagined to be possible before. This implementation of technology in the everyday lives of people benefits not only those who use it but also those who design it. One such parameter that embodies this is big data [1,2]. The bulk use of several technologies produces data in sizes that are ludicrous to even attempt to decipher with normal existing methods; hence, big data becomes the norm [3,4]. Parallely, the use of the Internet of Things (IoT), and sensors with wireless technologies open up a completely new spectrum of possibilities for big data [5]. The amount of data that is usually garnered is in organized, unorganized, or semiorganized form and is usually controlled with only complex data analytics.

Big data finds its applications in almost every field, ranging from social media sites to medical facilities, from meteorological departments to manufacturing units, and much more [6]. Thus, it is no surprise that the amalgamation of big data with microgrid is host to even more fascinating opportunities. A microgrid is produced by combining the distributed source producers, capacity, and control elements, all working in harmony and connected together with control knobs or grids [7,8].
