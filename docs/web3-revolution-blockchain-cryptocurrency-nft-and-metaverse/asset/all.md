# Introduction

 

 

It was the 2010 when Laszlo Hanyecz bought two pizzas for 30 dollars in a small town in Florida for 10,000 Bitcoins. Today, almost 12 years later, those two pizzas are yielding to the lucky pizzeria (which hopefully has in the meantime saved the Bitcoins) an equivalent value of about 400 million dollars. On the other hand, the US dollar suffered a devaluation of about 27% at the same time. This simple fact is enough to understand that we are facing a disruptive change: we have reached a point where new technological solutions promise such changes that they can even reformulate a new social architecture on the basis of a new decentralized protocol: the Blockchain.

We are probably facing something similar to what happened during the second industrial revolution, when the masses moved from the countryside to the cities to work in factories and centralizing capital in the hands of businessmen. Centuries have passed since then, but the paradigm has not changed. In fact, inequality has even increased. In his book "Invertire la rotta: disuguaglianza e crescita economica" (Italian Edition)[\[1\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn1), Joseph Stiglitz, former Nobel laureate in Economics in 2001, reports some data: between 1980 and 2014 the real average income of the richest 1% of the population of the United States increased by 169% and its share of national income is more than doubled (from 10 to 21%). At 0.1% of the super-rich, it fared even better, with real income increasing by 281%. However, the average family income only grew by 11%, but only in the very first years: in 2014 it was just 0.7% higher than in 1989. In addition, the average hourly wage, adjusted for inflation, has increased by only 9%, despite the fact that productivity increased by 72.2% in the same period.

Similarly, the current fiat currencies managed by national governments are undergoing a slow but steady inflationary process (in some cases not so slow, such as the cases of hyperinflation in Zimbabwe and Venezuela), gradually losing their value: 1 US dollar in the 1970 had the same purchasing power of $ 6.59 in 2019, with an inflation rate of change of 558.63%[\[2\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn2). 

How to avoid the devaluation of national currencies?

 

*The centralization of information and data*

Resuming the comparison with the second industrial revolution, today, in the digital age, we are witnessing a phenomenon similar to what happened with the centralization of production activities in factories, but this time on the basis of information and data. In his book "The Fourth Industrial Revolution"[\[3\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn3), Klaus Schwab, founder and executive president of the World Economic Forum, while discussing the impact of new digital technologies on people's lives, presents an interesting picture: whether the three largest social media sites were countries, they would be in the top 10 of the most populous nations. Furthermore, in the first place we find a country of the social media category, without considering that these three social networks are all part of Meta Platforms Inc. In addition, if we consider Google, the largest search engine in the world, Microsoft, the largest provider of operating systems, Samsung, which holds the primacy of the smartphone market share and finally if we add to this analysis also the world server market, divided between the American giants Dell, Hpe, Inspur and the big Chinese Huawei and Lenovo, it is easy to understand how information, true gold of the new millennium, is centralized in the hands of a few corporations.

*The Big Brother*

In the novel "1984" the English writer George Orwell describes a society in which each individual is constantly monitored by the authorities. The Big Brother, as the fictional character is called, is not known whether he is an existing person or simply a symbol created by the party. However, in 2013 the big brother became not just a character in a science fiction novel, but something absolutely real through the work of the main world power: the United States. The discovery of a centralized mass surveillance system, in which information technology was used by the NSA[\[4\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn4) to monitor every citizen on a global scale in the name of national security, breaking all international privacy laws, became public thanks to whistleblower Edward Snowden. It was clear that data centralization was a formidable tool of power and control for anyone with access to it. However, as it turned out, everything happened in the total unawareness of the citizens.

Will it be possible, in the future, to prevent situations of this type from happening again?

 

*Web3*

If in the case of industrial revolutions, we can identify four distinct phases that occurred historically over the course of just over two centuries, in the field of information technology the transformations occur at hypersonic speeds. Although the World Wide Web officially dates back to 1990, when CERN published the first web page in history on December 20, we can already distinguish three distinct phases of the evolution of the web in just 30 years. Web 1.0 spread substantially between 1991 and 2004. In Web 1.0 the vast majority of users simply acted as a consumer of content and the first personal web pages spread. The term Web 2.0, on the other hand, was coined in 1999 by Darcy DiNucci in her article "Fragmented Future"[\[5\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn5): in Web 2.0, the user becomes an active part of the web, being invited to contribute to the contents of the sites by commenting on the articles published or by creating a user account or profile on the site in question, allowing greater participation. The main features of Web 2.0 include social networking websites, self-publishing platforms (for example, WordPress blogs and website building tools), "tagging" (which allows users to tag sites, photos or videos), "likes" buttons and social bookmarking. The interface also changes: the use of mobile devices such as smartphones or tablets instead of desktop workstations becomes increasingly common. 

 

What about Web 3.0? The term, also known as "Web3", was coined in 2014 by Ethereum co-founder and Polkadot creator Gavin Wood. Web3 revolves around the idea of a decentralized Internet as opposed to Web 2.0, where large amounts of web data and content are centralized in a fairly small group of companies often referred to as the Big Techs: Alphabet (Google), Amazon, Apple, Meta (Facebook) and Microsoft.

Decentralized Finance (DeFi) represents another key concept, where users can exchange currency without the involvement of the bank or the government.

In this chapter we will try to answer the following question as simply as possible: what is the Blockchain? We will do this with the help of some historical notes on the origin of this technology, referring to the solution offered in relation to the problem of the centralization of information and security of the exchange of valuable assets, thanks to the use of cryptography.

## 1.1 Introduction

 

The Blockchain, as the name implies, is a "chain of blocks" where each block contains a series of information. A large database, which is decentralized (more precisely, distributed). What is the purpose of creating a series of blocks containing data?

Each new technology has the goal of solving a problem, or solving it in a better way than an existing solution. What is the problem that the blockchain tries to solve? To answer this question, let's take a few steps back.

The first to propose a protocol based on such a system was David Chaum, an American computer scientist and cryptographer. The practical implication of Chaum's work was to create a digital Cryptocurrency, independent of banks and usable in an untraceable way. To develop a system of this type, it was therefore necessary to avoid the centralization of information typical of the traditional banking system, creating a decentralized system in which the information was accessible to all and at the same time not modifiable.

 

**How to design a system in which the exchange of information is traceable, immutable and independent, without the need for a third-party guarantor to certify the transaction?** 

In a system such as the one that has evolved into the current Bitcoin technology, for example, each block is uniquely linked to the previous and next block by means of a code. This means that to change the information within a block in the chain, all subsequent blocks must also be changed. However, this operation is by no means simple, as:

1.              changing the code of a block requires some effort

2.              the code change must be approved by the users who are part of the network 

These two points give us the opportunity to deep dive some specific concepts of the Blockchain. 

We said that modifying a code requires some effort. As we will see in the next chapter, this commitment in most cases occurs in the form of power consumption used to "find" the code of the block. The codes in fact exploit the principle of cryptography: this means that a certain computing power and a certain amount of time are required for a computer (network node) to decrypt the correct code. That's not all: this code is then sent to network users who must "approve" it. Subsequently, if you want to modify the information of the block, the code would automatically be modified as well, invalidating all subsequent blocks. To revalidate the chain, all codes should also be recalculated accordingly. Even if this happens, this change could only be accepted when at least 51% of the network agrees (as we will see, this situation is known as a 51% attack). In the presence of a very large network, with hundreds of thousands of nodes, such an event has a very remote probability of occurring.

Once the code associated with a block is found and validated by the network, the block can now be added to the chain. As a reward, the user or node who managed to crack the block before the others will receive a token in exchange. In the case of Bitcoin, therefore, the user will receive 1 BTC as a reward.

We summarize below the various steps in the block generation process within a Blockchain:

 

·    the aim is to create a reliable and secure system to carry out transactions without the need for intermediaries;

·    the Blockchain uses a complex coding system to create codes able to uniquely identify the blocks and connect them to the chain;

·    the codes are decrypted by special nodes, the so-called "miners" (we will talk about them in the next chapter) and sent to the network that is using the Blockchain;

·    the miners, for their efforts, receive in exchange tokens protected by cryptography;

·    each Blockchain user keeps a copy of these codes;

·    the modification of block codes is an extremely expensive operation in terms of computational resources and difficult to implement, since for example it would require validation by at least 51% of users.

 

## 1.2 A practical example

 

To clarify even better the concepts underlying the Blockchain and what its main benefits are, let's take a cue from the example reported in the book by Mark Gates[\[7\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn7). Let's imagine an online library that shares books (let's say eBooks to be more concrete) where each user contributes their eBooks and allows people to borrow them. Anyone can participate, and when they borrow books, they can also lend them to other people, without returning them to the library or to the original owner. How could records be kept of who had borrowed the books, which book they had, and who was the original owner of the book? Records should be kept of the books currently in the library, their original owners, and books that have been borrowed. To do this, one could assign a person in the group to track the exchanges using a centralized database model. In reality this condition would have a number of disadvantages:

·             the ability of the person handling the transactions and keeping the ledger should be trusted first. The greater the network, the greater the management complexity and difficulty, therefore the greater the risk of mistakes

·             it should then be trusted that this person does not fraudulently modify the records to steal the circulating books

·             I should pay this person

·             I should wait for this person to carry out all transactions. Greater complexity also means greater execution time. Otherwise, I would have to hire another person, thus also increasing the fixed management costs

Instead, the Blockchain can provide a distributed database of all library book records without the need for a person to take over management. With such a database, everyone in the library has access to the trade register, everyone sees the library's books, who the original owners were, who borrowed each book, and everyone can see if they've continued to lend that book to anyone. other. Whenever a book is borrowed from the shared library, all the books in the book database that everyone has access to are updated. There is no central database or institution needed to make it work - everyone has a copy of the database. Each time a book is borrowed, a transaction is created: there can be many transactions occurring at the same time, so these can be grouped and added to a new block. This new block is added "on top" of the previous block and linked to it. For instance:

·             Block 10 connections to block 9

·             Block 9 connections to block 8

·             Block 8 connections to block 7 Etc.

By connecting these blocks, a chain of blocks is created, hence the name "Blockchain". Still referring to our example, anyone could go to the most recent block in the chain and see all the books that were borrowed and from whom, see the transactions in the previous block (to see who had the books before them) up to start to see the original owner. There is no database or central authority: for example, if a person claims to be the owner of the book, it could be verified by simply tracing all transactions back to the first block known as the "genesis block". In a decentralized library of this type:

 

·             there is no central authority or body that manages the transaction. This means less waiting times and no librarian management costs;

·             the system thus designed allows full traceability of the books. Everyone knows who the owners are and who has the book at that moment: it is in fact almost impossible to modify the registers, unlike a centralized system;

·             anyone can make their books available, no validation by a central body is required.

 

 

## 1.3 History of Blockchain

 

When was the Blockchain born and who invented it? In this section we briefly report the main stages of the birth and evolution of this technology:

 **1982** - **David Chaum**[\[8\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn8), an American computer scientist and cryptographer, lays the foundations of the Blockchain. In his doctoral thesis at Berkley *"Computer Systems Established, Maintained and Trusted by Mutually Suspicious Groups"[**\[9\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn9)* he defines a plan for achieving consensus state between nodes in a distributed network, concatenating consensus history in chunks, and immutably marking concatenated data. The document also exposes the specific code to implement this protocol.

 

**1983** - **Blind Signature**. In his document *"Blind signatures for untraceable payments"[**\[10\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn10)* Chaum describes a system for allowing users to obtain digital currency from a bank and spend it in a way that cannot be traced by the bank or any other entity. With this form of digital signature, it is possible to hide the content of a message before it is signed, so that the signer cannot determine its content. The resulting blind signature can be publicly verified against the original undisclosed message like a normal digital signature. For his contribution, Chaum is credited as the inventor of digital currency, ideas that form the technical basis of the vision of the Cypherpunk movement that began in the late 1980s. Indeed, members of this movement advocated the widespread use of advanced cryptography and privacy-enhancing technologies as a path to social and political change.

 

**1990** - Chaum founds **DigiCash**, an Amsterdam-based electronic cash company.

 

**1994** - DigiCash sends **the first electronic payment**. 

 

**1997 - Adam Back**, a British cryptographer and later CEO of Blockstream, proposes Hashcash, an algorithm used to limit email spam. Shortly, Hashcash is a "proof-of-work" algorithm (we'll find out in the second chapter what that means) based on hash codes (again, a little bit of patience). In practice, when email is used, a code (or "stamp") is added to the email header to show that the sender took a modest amount of CPU time to calculate this code before sending the e-mail. By virtue of that effort, he is unlikely to be a spammer. At this point the recipient can check the validity of the stamp with a negligible computational cost. However, the only known way to find the code is through what is commonly referred to as "brute force," which is by calculating random codes until the exact code is found. This concept will later be used in the Bitcoin blockchain as part of the mining algorithm.

 

**Early 90´: Nick Szabo**, an American computer scientist and cryptographer, introduces the concept of Smart Contracts as distributable value in addition to electronic money. A Smart Contract is a digital contract that is executed automatically and based on predefined criteria. 

 

**1998** - Szabo designs a mechanism for a decentralized digital currency which he calls "Bit Gold". Bit gold was never implemented, however it was called "a direct precursor of the Bitcoin architecture". 

In the same year **Wei Dai**[\[11\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn11), a Chinese computer engineer, publishes the document "b-money, an anonymous, distributed electronic cash system". In the paper, Dai outlines the basic properties of all modern cryptocurrency systems: *"... a scheme for a bunch of untraceable digital aliases to pay each other with money and enforce contracts between them without external help "*.

 

**2009 - Bitcoin was officially born by Satoshi Nakamoto**, a pseudonym used by the person or group of people who developed Bitcoin. On January 12, 2009, Hal Finney, an American engineer and cryptographic activist, receives the first 10 BTC transaction from Satoshi Nakamoto (at the time of writing this book, about $ 400,000). From this moment on, the concept of Blockchain evolves hand in hand with the concept of Cryptocurrency, which we will discuss in more detail in chapter 4.

 

 

## 1.4 The Blockchain Trilemma

 

![](images/00002.jpeg) 

Figure 2: the Blockchain Trilemma

 

As defined by CoinMarketCap[\[12\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn12) *“The blockchain trilemma is a concept coined by Vitalik Buterin that proposes a set of three main issues - decentralization, security and scalability – that developers encounter when building Blockchains, ultimately forcing them to sacrifice one "aspect" as a compromise to satisfy the other two”.*

 

This question actually arises with Bitcoin and with the problems that arose when an attempt was made to extend its Blockchain. In fact, the low scalability of its Blockchain is due to the way it was conceived: the cryptocurrency consensus algorithm (Proof-of-Work) does not allow it to scale exponentially like others (e.g., Proof-of- Stake). On the other hand, it certainly has the safety and durability that make it the safest from attacks of any kind. We will talk about these concepts in the next chapter. To solve the trilemma, and thus become an ideal Blockchain in effect, the protocol created by Satoshi Nakamoto necessarily had to scale. Consequently, most of the Blockchains and therefore of the Cryptocurrencies that were born later, the so-called Altcoins, aim to solve in the best way the trilemma presented by Buterin, co-founder of Ethereum, today the second largest Blockchain project.

 

 

## 1.5 Types of Blockchain

 

Basically, we can divide the types of Blockchains according to whether they are public, private, or hybrid[\[13\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn13).

 

### 1.5.1 Public Blockchain

Public Blockchain refers to Blockchains that are publicly accessible from an Internet network. An example of this type of Blockchain are the aforementioned Bitcoin and Ethereum. A public Blockchain keeps its data, software and development open to the public, so that anyone can review, verify, develop or improve it. However, public Blockchains have some security measures that guarantee that no attacker can easily alter the functioning of the chain.

The characteristics of this type of network are the following:

 

·             **full participation**, i.e., they allow anyone to be part of it, whether as a user, miner or administrator of a node, without any restrictions

·             **full transparency**. The data of the Blockchain is available to everyone since its inception without restrictions and anyone can examine or control the functioning of the network and its software

·             **decentralization**. Public networks are completely decentralized and there is no central authority that regulates their operation

·             the **economic maintenance** of the Blockchain depends on the system integrated in it. Generally, this economic system depends on mining and collecting commissions for every transaction made within the network.

 

### 1.5.2 Private or Authorized Blockchain

Subsequently, with the evolution of Blockchain technology and its expansion, many companies have become interested in it, leading to the development of private or authorized solutions. This type of Blockchain generally has the same elements as a public Blockchain, but they depend on a central unit that controls all the actions within it. The central body guarantees access to users and controls their functions and authorizations within the Blockchain.

The main characteristics of this type of network are:

 

·    **access to the network** is limited to items that can only be authorized from the central control unit

·    **access to the transaction book** or any other means of information generated by the blockchain is private

·    **the economic maintenance** of the Blockchain generally depends on the company that supports the project. Private blockchains often don't use a reward system like cryptocurrencies.

 

### 1.5.3 Hybrid or Federated Blockchain

This type of Blockchain is a fusion of public and private Blockchains and represents an attempt to exploit the best of both solutions. In these Blockchains, participation in the network is private, so access to network resources is controlled by one or more entities. However, the ledger is publicly accessible - this means anyone can explore everything that happens on that blockchain block by block.

For example, these Blockchain networks are very useful for governments or business organizations that want to store or share data securely. A perfect use case is occurring in the pharmaceutical industry, where the Blockchain is used to store the data of its drug production lines. The stored data can be examined by the competent authority in order to check its quality, both at the corporate and governmental level. The goal of applying this blockchain model is to maintain a high level of transparency and trust. The characteristics of this type of network are:

·             **access to the network is limited** to items that can only be authorized by a central body

·             **access to the transaction book** or any other means of information generated by the blockchain is public

·             **there are no mines or cryptocurrencies**. The consent of the network is given by other means which ensure that the data is correct

·             **it is partially decentralized**, which leads to a better level of security and transparency

In the next chapter we will see in more detail how the blockchain works and we will clarify some doubts about some concepts previously encountered but not yet fully clarified.

## 2.1 Peer-to-Peer (P2P) network

 

In the field of computer network, 3 types of network architectures can be identified:

 

**1.              Centralized networks**

**2.              Decentralized networks**

**3.              Distributed networks**

 

 

**![](images/00003.jpeg)**

 

Figure 3: A comparison of the topography of centralized, decentralized and distributed networks (Source: Baran, 1964)

 

### 2.1.1 Centralized network

A centralized network architecture is built around a single server that manages all major processing. Less powerful workstations connect to the central server and send their requests instead of executing them directly. Having a central server that controls the entire network means less management time and fewer administrators to update the entire network. Also, all data on a centralized network has to go through a single location, so it is very easy to track and collect data across the network.

However, centralized networks have their downsides: by definition, the server represents a single point of failure, that is, its malfunction puts the entire network at risk. As a result, if the server goes down, the individual connected "client" machines are unable to process user requests. Another disadvantage of centralized networking is that it offers limited scalability: since all applications are hosted on a single server, the only way to scale the network is to add more storage, bandwidth, or processing power to the server. This may not prove to be a cost-effective long-term solution.

 

### 2.1.2 Decentralized network

As opposed to a centralized network, a decentralized network architecture distributes workloads across multiple machines, rather than relying on a single central server. A decentralized network offers a wide range of advantages over the more conventional centralized network, including greater system reliability, scalability and privacy.

One of the most important benefits of decentralized network management is the fact that there is no true single point of failure like in the centralized network, because individual users' machines do not depend on a single central server to manage all processes. Decentralized networks are also much easier to scale, as you can simply add more machines to the network for more computing power. In addition to this, a decentralized network architecture allows for greater privacy, as information does not pass through a single point but passes through several different points. However, the downside is that decentralized networks require more machines, which means more maintenance and potential problems, which in turn places an additional burden on IT resources.

 

### 2.1.3 Distributed network

An evolution of the architecture of decentralized network systems is represented by the distributed network, or peer-to-peer (P2P) on which the Blockchain is based. A P2P network is an architectural model in which the nodes are not hierarchized only in the form of fixed clients or servers, but also in the form of equivalent or 'peer' nodes, being able to act at the same time as clients and servers towards the other terminals (Hosts) of the network. This means that through this configuration any node is able to initiate or complete a transaction. Equivalent nodes may differ in local configuration, processing speed, bandwidth, and amount of stored data. Among the main features of decentralized networks, we list the followings:

 

·             each user shares his own resources locally and he is the administrator of his own Client-Server. On the one hand this can be positive for major "independence", but on the other it requires skills from each user, especially as regards protection

·             the average data transmission speed is much higher than a classic centralized network, since the information requested by a Client can be found by numerous Clients connected in an equal way (i.e., "peer"), rather than by a single server

·             the access security to clients is managed locally on each machine and it is not centralized

·             high scalability and "self-regeneration" capability. In the event that a node stops working - or simply disconnects from the network - the network will continue to function without major problems: the exchange of data or the search for resources will continue along alternative routes to the one that passed through the node that was not more available. In this way, the bottleneck that, in some cases, is represented by relying on the functioning of a single server is bypassed

The most popular examples of peer-to-peer (P2P) networks are the various file sharing protocols that have sprung up over the years:

·             **Napster**: launched in 1999 by Shawn Fanning, a computer programmer and Sean Parker (who would later become Facebook's first president) as a pioneering file sharing software service with an emphasis on distributing digital audio files. It was closed in 2002 due to legal problems related to copyright

·             **Bit Torrent**: born in 2001 by Bram Cohen, a computer science student at the University of Buffalo in the United States

·             **eMule**: the eMule project was started in 2002 by Hendrik Breitkreuz, dissatisfied with the original eDonkey2000 client

These protocols, although legal (data exchange is not an illegal activity), are nevertheless mostly used for the trafficking of works that infringe copyright, such as movies, music, books and video games. Only in the United States, more than 200,000 lawsuits have been filed for copyright infringement on Bit Torrent since 2010.

## 2.3 Blocks

 

Blocks are the bricks of the Blockchain. Each block of the chain basically contains 3 elements:

·             **the data** stored in the block. The type of data stored depends on the type of Blockchain. For example, the Bitcoin Blockchain stores the details of one or more transactions, such as user, recipient and the amount of Bitcoin traded

·             **the hash**, a string of numbers and letters that identifies that block and its content and that is always unique, like a kind of fingerprint. Each time a new block is created, a new, unique and specific hash for that block is calculated. If any data inside the block changes, the hash will also change accordingly

·             **the hash of the previous block**, thanks to which the connection to the previous block is generated, making the blockchain particularly secure. 

![](images/00004.jpeg)

 

Figure 4: graphic representation of the constituent elements of a block of the Bitcoin blockchain

 

On average, a Bitcoin block is created every ten minutes, while on Ethereum blocks are set every 17 seconds.

In summary: each block contains its own hash and the hash of the previous block. All the blocks of the main chain are numbered, starting with the number 0, then 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and so on. Taking as an example a Blockchain made up of 3 blocks, block number 2 will refer to block number 1 and block number 1 will refer to block number 0. Block number 0 is different from the previous ones: it cannot point to any previous block, in fact, is called in the jargon "genesis block". If for some reason block #1 was tampered with by a hacker, that block's hash would automatically be changed. Consequently, block #2 would no longer be valid as it would no longer contain the hash of the previous block, effectively compromising the validity of all subsequent blocks.

## 2.4 Hash and cryptography

 

In his book “Ultimate Step by Step Guide to Understanding Blockchain Technology, Bitcoin Creation, and the future of Money”[\[14\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn14), Keizer Söze clearly describes what a hash function is and how it works. 

A hash is basically a fixed-sized string of numbers generated on the basis of a series of algorithms. A hash function allows to reduce any text string, regardless of its length, into a new string with precise characteristics including a predefined number of characters.

One such algorithm is the so-called Message Digest 5, or MD5 (where 5 is the version number). For example, trying to type the word "blockchain". The resulting md5 hash value will be:

 

5510a843bc1b7acb9507a5f71de51b98

 

If we tried to perform the same md5 hashing on the word "blockchain1", the result would be completely different:

 

1150228f14788047028d774b7c83c5a6

 

Let's try a number now, and for simplicity, let's use very few digits. Let's try to hash md5 on a string of numbers of 123 and then 124 and see if there is any difference. For string 123, the hashing value md5 is this:

 

202cb962ac59075b964b07152d234b70

 

For string 124, on the other hand:

 

C8ffe9a587b126f152ed3d89a146b445

 

As we can see, once again, it is a completely different result. The md5 algorithm was born in 1992, however it has been compromised several times due to its vulnerabilities. 

There are several hashing algorithms: in 2001, the SHA-2 algorithm (Secure Hash Algorithm 2) designed by the National Security Agency (NSA) was published. The SHA-2 family consists of six hash functions with hash (or digest) values that are 224, 256, 384 or 512 bits, respectively SHA-224, SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-512. Specifically, SHA-256 is the hash algorithm used by Bitcoin. In 2012, the scrypt algorithm was released, designed specifically to perform large-scale custom hardware attacks that required a large amount of memory. This algorithm was later used as part of the Proof-of-Work mechanism by some cryptocurrencies such as Litecoin and Dogecoin.

 We can summarize the general characteristics of a hash algorithm in the following fundamental points:

·             **constancy: **the same output always and inevitably corresponds to each input;

·             **irreversibility: **while it is always possible to reproduce the output knowing the original input with which the output was generated, it is not possible to do the reverse path, therefore it is not possible starting from an alphanumeric string to go back to the initial content of the input that generated it;

·             **determinism: **regardless of how long the input is, the hash function will always return an alphanumeric string of a given number of characters; if I took the divine comedy, for example, and subjected it to a hash function, the result would always be a string of 32, 64 (etc) characters (the length of the output depends on the hashing function used);

·             **avalanche effect: **no matter how long and complex the input is, an infinitesimal variation of the input is sufficient to generate a completely different output; if the divine comedy were subjected to a hash function and then subsequently repeated the same operation by removing a single space from the original text, the output that would be generated in this second case would be radically different from that generated in the first.

To conclude, it should be emphasized that hash is used as an identification mechanism, but not as a confidentiality mechanism, for which more complex cryptographic processes are used. However, hash functions also play an essential role in the cryptography itself: they are useful for verifying the integrity of a message, since the execution of the algorithm on even minimally modified text provides a completely different message digest than the one calculated on the original text, revealing the attempted modification. 

 

### 2.4.1 Cryptography

Cryptography is one of the fundamental pillars on which Blockchain technology is based. Before explaining how cryptography is used in the Blockchain, we need to make a brief digression and provide some theoretical concepts. Encryption is a defined process by which data is converted into a certain form available only to the recipient, thus inaccessible to an unauthorized end user. What the encryption process does is simple: it turns a particular piece of data into an unreadable form, commonly known as Encrypted Text. To ensure that no unauthorized person can access the data, public and private key are used: 

·             **The private key **is a randomly generated secret number known only to the person who generated it. The private key is similar to a PIN or password that we use to log into different web pages, but in this case, it is encrypted, which adds much more security. In Bitcoin, for example, the private key is the code that guarantees access to funds on the Blockchain and is a 256-bit (32-byte) unsigned integer. In practice, a private key looks like this:

 

**![](images/00006.jpeg)**

 

Figure 6: example of private key

 

·    **The public key**, on the other hand, is a number that corresponds to a private key, but don’t have to be kept secret. A public key can be computed from a private key, but not the other way around. That is, the private key cannot be obtained from the public key. The public key, after subsequent changes, is the address that we can share with all members of the community so that they can send us money, or that of other users of the community that we will use if necessary to make a payment in their favor. There is no risk of theft, as the funds are only accessible via the private key.

 

Now let's add another piece of information. There are two types of keys used to encrypt and decrypt data:

·             **Symmetric key**: the encryption and de-encryption processes use the same key. We can imagine a symmetrical key as a key to open the door of the house: when we go out or enter, in fact, we use the same key. The symmetric key algorithm is thousands of times faster than asymmetric keys, however the problem here is that both the sender and the recipient must use the same key for both encryption and decryption as well. Of course, this isn't an advantage when it comes to security, and the Blockchain certainly doesn't use symmetric key algorithms

·             **Asymmetric key**: the Blockchain uses asymmetric key algorithms. To implement this type of algorithm, you need to have two different keys. One of the keys will be responsible for encrypting the information to turn it into ciphertext, and the other key will be used to decrypt the information to make it plaintext. The asymmetric key algorithm is much slower than a symmetric key algorithm; however, security is more complex and it is more difficult to be hacked. The Blockchain uses an asymmetric key algorithm called Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA)

 

The ECDSA algorithm is considered a secure standard for the implementation of digital signature systems and today it’s use is so varied that it finds application in almost all fields of information technology. For example, the Internet's SSL and TLS security certificate infrastructure makes heavy use of the ECDSA algorithm. 

 

**![](images/00007.jpeg)**

 

Figure 7: example of using public key and private key in the encryption process

 

One of the main problems that Satoshi Nakamoto had to face with Bitcoin was the distribution of public keys, necessary to guarantee the security of the tokens in the relative Wallets (we will see them in chapter 6). Nakamoto's vision was to enable the sharing of short, secure, low-cost, easy-to-use public keys, characteristics thanks to elliptic curve cryptography. For this Nakamoto decided to use the ECDSA algorithm for his system. Furthermore, it is guaranteed that:

·             the system is extremely safe. Satoshi decided to use the elliptic curve standard secp256k1 for Bitcoin;

·             the computational cost of key generation and signature validation is very low;

·             allows the generation of infinite public keys.

However, the 256-bit ECDSA keys are very long. For this, it was decided to use hash functions such as SHA-256 and RIPEMD-160, to reduce the size of the public keys and create the Bitcoin address file.

 

 

## 2.5 Consensus Algorithm

 

In paragraph 2.3 we mentioned that the generation of hashes does not guarantee the full security of the Blockchain, but that it is necessary to implement other mechanisms. A consensus algorithm is the set of rules that allows the finalization of transactions and the functioning of the system and has the purpose of keeping the network usable, safe and decentralized. We will focus on this aspect as one of the main differences between the different cryptocurrencies that are emerging is also based on the consensus mechanism used.

 We can divide the consensus mechanisms into two main macro categories:

·            Without authorization (open technology)

·            With authorization (closed technology)

 In the without authorization mechanism, the technology is open to anyone and there is no central body that can decide the rules of the protocol and the transactions to be carried out. In the second case, however, the validator nodes are known entities that must be trusted for the validation of transactions.

The two main types of open technology consensus algorithms will be described below, namely Proof-of-Work (PoW) and Proof-of-Stake (PoS).

 

### 2.5.1 Proof-of-work (PoW) 

In paragraph 1.3 we have already addressed the topic of Proof-of-Work (PoW), when in 1997 Adam Back developed an algorithm to limit email spam. The innovation introduced by the Bitcoin Blockchain was to use different cryptographic tools, including the hash function, digital signatures and PoW, combining them in such a way as to obtain a database that kept the history of transactions in a decentralized way. In the case of Bitcoin, in fact, the PoW serves to make it difficult to create new identities that are authorized to receive the rewards of the new Bitcoins as a certain computational power is required. The computational power required to mine new Bitcoins is expressed through the number of hashes that a computer can calculate in a certain time interval (H/s). More concretely, greater computational power equates to higher costs linked to the associated higher energy consumption.

In fact, without going into too much detail, we learned that the only way to find a hash is by trial and error, or "brute force", whereby machines constantly perform competing calculations, until a miner reaches the target. The first to find the solution of the block has the right to add this block to the chain and obtain a reward. As soon as a block is added, the miners immediately start looking for a new block of transactions to validate. By doing so, the greater the use of dedicated mining machines, the more secure and resilient the Blockchain will become. The majority of computational power will determine the longest chain, the valid one.

In the case of the Bitcoin Blockchain, the difficulty of the target is adjusted periodically, in such a way as to keep the emission interval of the new Bitcoins constant on average every 10 minutes. The rewards for these blocks are halved every 4 years in a process called Halving, forming a geometric series with decreasing inflation.

 

**![](images/00008.jpeg)**

Anyone can contribute to the system with their computational power; however, this activity requires huge investments in terms of hardware to have a better chance of solving these puzzles and therefore to have an economic return. In this regard, some companies have specialized in the production of special chips that have the sole function of calculating hashes.

In summary, the Proof-of-Work mechanism is necessary to have a certain commitment spent. The more miners devote energy to block solving and validating transactions, the harder it will be to perform a chain attack.

The amount of electricity consumption of Bitcoin has reached extraordinary levels. For this reason, over time, new consensus mechanisms have been created, which could be able to limit energy expenditure while maintaining a decent level of security and decentralization. For example, Ethereum, the second largest Cryptocurrency by capitalization, has been implementing the transition from a PoW to a Proof-of-Stake (PoS) for some years.

 Another problem with PoW is scalability. For comparison alone, while Visa can approve up to 4,000 transactions per second, Bitcoin can only process seven. A solution to this problem is called "Lightning Network". The Lightning Network protocol was designed to improve the scalability of Bitcoin due to the fact that the Lightning Network functions as a second layer (Layer 2) network on top of Bitcoin. This allows to do things that normally couldn't be done, such as making instant transactions with very low fees. Without deep dive into too many technicalities, a network is created that models’ payment channels using Smart Contract (we will talk about this in the next chapter) to ensure that the network can operate in a decentralized way. This approach offers one of the most powerful options for improving Bitcoin's scalability: in fact, it can lead Bitcoin to reach levels close to 1 million transactions per second, many more than those managed by payment systems such as VISA or MasterCard.

### 2.5.2 Proof-of-Stake (PoS)

Proof-of-Stake was devised in 2011 as a less expensive alternative to PoW. The first working use of PoS for cryptocurrency was Peercoin in 2012. The difference between the two consensus mechanisms is that, while in PoW miners use energy to validate transactions, in PoS blocks are validated by "validators" in proportion to the share of token held (stake). Validators are those who bind a certain amount of token as a guarantee for the right to verify transactions. The validators are chosen among the nodes of the network through an automatic selection process that takes into account a number of different factors. These factors may be different depending on the project; however, generally the following are taken into consideration:

1.              the amount of stake deposited

2.              the longevity of the stake (also called coin age, i.e., how long has the deposit been made)

3.              a randomization factor

Of course, the higher the amount and the longer the time of stake deposited are, the higher the probability of being selected as validators, as higher and historically solid stakes have a greater degree of reliability and integrity. Furthermore, when a node is selected by the algorithm and coins a new block, its coin age is reset and a certain period of time will have to pass before it can be re-selected. This system prevents nodes with large stakes from dominating the Blockchain.

Once a node is selected as the validator of the next block, it will have to check if the transactions it contains are valid, sign the block and add it to the Blockchain. This validation is known as an attestation. Validators get rewards for proposing new blocks and for certifying the ones they viewed. If a validator validates malicious blocks, it loses its stake.

Unlike PoW systems, where the miners' work is rewarded with the creation of a new currency, in PoS systems the reward consists of a fee withheld on the validated transaction. Before being able to withdraw your deposited share and collect your reward, the network verifies the validator's work, checking that no fraudulent blocks have been added.

The most important concept in support of the PoS consensus mechanism is that, if a validator attempted to hack the network or process malicious transactions, he would lose the entire tied stake.

 A variation of the PoS is the so-called Pure-Proof-of-Stake (PPoS), a mechanism created by Algorand. What changes is that the validators are also chosen through a random selection, regardless of the number of tokens owned. This automatic selection is based on a Verifiable Random Function (VRF). VRF behaves similar to a lottery: it is as if each ALGO in a participant's account had a lottery ticket.

 

### 2.5.3 Difference between PoW and PoS

What are the differences between Proof-of-Work and Proof-of-Stake? Below we list the main ones:

 

·    Proof-of-Stake requires less electricity than Proof-of-Work. As we have seen, PoW does not require the computational power of PoW as there is no activity on the part of the miners. Proof-of-Stake is consequently considered a more ecological consensus mechanism

·    Proof-of-Stake is able to process a greater number of transactions than Proof-of-Work, for the same amount of time. It is therefore more scalable

·    Both can have centralization problems, albeit for different reasons. PoW harnesses computational power to mine money; this means that power can be centralized by the main mining farms (before the famous ban by the Chinese government, some estimates reported that about 70% of world mining took place in China). On the other hand, the PoS mechanism encourages the concentration of capital over time on a few individuals by encouraging a mechanism whereby the rich become richer and richer. Since the amount of cryptocurrency made available (stake) heavily affects the probability of validating a block, and consequently the winning of the reward, the more people put a large stake at their disposal, the more the probability increases that they will become validators. In this way they get some sort of priority over the other participants

·    In a PoS mechanism, an attack attempt of 51% is less likely than in a PoW mechanism as it is not financially convenient. In fact, to achieve this goal, the attacker would have to bet at least 51% of the total amount of cryptocurrency in circulation. The only way to do this is to buy the coins on the market, increasing the real value of the coin over time. As a result, he would end up spending far more than he could earn from the attack. Not only that, but once the rest of the network realized what had happened, the malicious actor would have lost all of his stakes!

 

### 2.5.4 Other consensus mechanism

While PoW and PoS are the most widespread consensus mechanisms in the Blockchain environment, there are other consensus algorithms such as:

 

·    **Proof-of-Capacity (PoC)**, used by Burstcoin, it allows the sharing of the memory space of the contributing nodes on the Blockchain network. The more memory or hard disk space a node has, the more rights it is granted to maintain the public ledger

·    **Proof-of-Activity (PoA)**, used on the Decred Blockchain, is a hybrid that exploits aspects of both PoW and PoS;

·    **Proof-of-Burn (PoB)** is another that requires traders to send small amounts of coin to inaccessible wallet addresses, then "burning" them;

·    **Proof-of-History (PoH)**, developed by Solana, cryptographically encodes the passage of time itself to obtain consent.

 

 

## 2.6 The fork

 

In the context of the Blockchain, a fork occurs when a block is linked to 2 successive blocks. Forks are related to the fact that the parties must use common rules to keep the history of the Blockchain. Indeed, when the parties disagree, alternative chains can emerge. While most forks are short-lived, others are nevertheless permanent: short-lived forks are due to the difficulty of reaching rapid consensus in a distributed system, while permanent forks (i.e., due to protocol changes) are used for add new features to a Blockchain, or they can also be used to reverse the effects of hacking (as in the case of Ethereum and Ethereum Classic), or to avoid catastrophic bugs on a Blockchain as in the case of the Bitcoin fork of August 6, 2010. 

 

**![](images/00011.jpeg)**

 

Figure 11: example of a fork

 

Forks can be classified as:

1.              **accidental**

**2.              intentional**

Accidental forking occurs when two or more miners find a block almost simultaneously. The fork is resolved when subsequent blocks are added and one of the chains becomes longer than the alternative. The network consequently leaves the blocks of the shorter chain, because the miners' goal is to always work on the longest chain. Abandoned blocks are then called orphan blocks.

 

Intentional forks that change the rules of a Blockchain can be classified as follows:

 

·             **Hard fork**: a hard fork is a rule change such that software validating under the old rules will see blocks produced under the new rules as invalid. In the event of a hard fork, all nodes intended to operate under the new rules must update their software. If a group of nodes continues to use the old software while the other nodes are using the new software, permanent division can occur

·             **Soft Fork**: a soft fork is an update of the program code that manages a Blockchain file to add a change, usually a new feature or a bug fix. But to be a soft fork it must remain compatible with the old Blockchain. For example, in Bitcoin an update is first disclosed in a document known as BIP (Bitcoin Improvement Proposals). Once approved, further implementation is up to the developers of the Bitcoin clients. There are two types of soft forks that occur when:

o      Most miners upgrade to apply the new rules. This is called Miner Activated Soft Fork (MASF);

o      Nodes are coordinated to enforce the new rules, without the support of miners. This is called User Activated Soft Fork (UASF).

 

### 2.6.1 Hard fork: the cases of Ethereum and Bitcoin

Ethereum Classic maintains the original and unaltered history of the Ethereum network. The main Ethereum project network was in fact initially released through July 30, 2015. However, due to a hack of a third party project, on July 20 2016 the Ethereum Foundation created a new version of the Ethereum main network with a change implemented of irregular state that erased the theft of millions of ether from the history of the Ethereum Blockchain, applying its own brand to the new modified version of the Ethereum Blockchain (code: ETH), i.e. the one in which the stolen funds were returned to the owners, while the previous and unaltered version of Ethereum was renamed Ethereum Classic (code: ETC), i.e. the one in which the funds were not returned.

On August 6, 2010, a serious vulnerability was detected in Bitocoin protocol, effectively allowing users to circumvent the economic restrictions of the Blockchain and create an indefinite number of Bitcoins. As a result, on August 15, over 184 billion Bitcoins were generated in one transaction and sent to two addresses on the network. Within hours, that transaction was detected and cleared from the transaction log after the bug was fixed and the network switched to an updated version of the Bitcoin protocol. This was the only major security flaw found and exploited in Bitcoin's history.

However, Bitcoin has been subject to other forks for different reasons. The hard forks that divide Bitcoin were created by changing the rules of the Blockchain and sharing a history of transactions with Bitcoin up to a certain time and date. The first hard fork dividing bitcoin occurred on August 1, 2017, with the creation of Bitcoin Cash.

Below is a list of important hard forks that break down bitcoin by date and / or block:

·    **Bitcoin Cash**: fork occurred at block 478558, 1 August 2017, for each bitcoin (BTC), one owner obtained 1 Bitcoin Cash (BCH);

·    **Bitcoin SV**: fork occurred at block 556766, November 15, 2018, for each Bitcoin Cash (BCH), one owner obtained 1 Bitcoin SV (BSV);

·    **Bitcoin Gold**: fork occurred at block 491407, 24 October 2017, for each bitcoin (BTC), one owner has obtained 1 Bitcoin Gold (BTG).

## 3.1 Main benefits

 

Below we describe the main advantages related to Blockchain technology: transparency, costs, security, speed and scalability.

 

### 3.1.1 Transparency

To improve transparency in the exchange of information, organizations have tried to implement more and more rules; however, there is one thing that does not make any system 100% transparent, which is the centralization of information. In a centralized network, every decision-making power is in the hands of a central body that controls the network. For example, in the case of a central banking system, every decision goes through the banks that will implement the banking policy most in line with their strategy. Transparency therefore becomes arbitrary and not structural: this means that we will have to somehow "trust" the protection mechanisms that the bank puts in place towards savers. One of the main applications of the Blockchain is the so-called decentralized finance, also known as DeFi, which will be presented in more detail in Chapter 6.

Another application example concerns the voting process of democratic governments. In order for this process to take place as transparently as possible, each state implements a series of measures during the counting to avoid electoral fraud. If, on the other hand, the process was carried out in a completely computerized way through electronic voting, it would be enough to manipulate or tamper with the central server that processes the information to pilot the outcome of the votes as desired.

With the Blockchain, this kind of problem would find a robust solution: as we have seen, a Blockchain is made up of nodes responsible for executing transactions and their validation. Each transaction is also sent to the members of the network who keep an exact copy of it in their register, while any potential attempt to tamper with the outcome of the vote should be validated according to the consent mechanism adopted. All this would happen with a significant reduction in costs and time.

 

### 3.1.2 No intermediary cost

During transactions that involve the signing of official documents, the exchange of money or the sale of goods, the figure of the intermediary very often acts only as a guarantor of the transaction itself. This comes at a cost, which is sometimes called financial friction in the jargon. We have just seen that this figure in a distributed network context loses its meaning, as the validity of the transaction is guaranteed by the architecture of the Blockchain network itself. Consequently, the transaction does not require any intermediary, therefore no brokerage costs. The most common example is that of electronic payments: when we pay with a debit or credit card or make a bank transfer, the transaction is taken over by the bank which in some cases asks for a commission. In addition, the credit card often also requires an annual fee. In the case of electronic payments via Blockchain, such fees would not be necessary.

 

### 3.1.3 Security

Blockchain technology uses an advanced level of security compared to other platforms or registration systems. In the previous chapter we mentioned the use of hashing and cryptography, algorithms on which the Blockchain is based. Furthermore, the consensus mechanism and the decentralization of the distributed system make the Blockchain probably the safest information technology out there.

 

### 3.1.4 Transaction speed 

Centralized transactions take some time to process. Take for example the case of a national or international bank transfer between 2 different banks: this process can take several working days to resolve. However, in a decentralized process such as the Blockchain this mechanism is no longer necessary, as the transaction, not requiring an intermediary, can take place, if we take as reference the time of an average Bitcoin transaction, about 10 minutes.

 

### 3.1.5 Scalability

The scalability of a system generally denotes its ability to scale up or down according to need and availability. Referring to the Blockchain, scalability is its ability to manage an increasing number of transactions without affecting performance. The Blockchain has the advantage of being an extremely scalable technology; however, as evidenced by the Blockchain trilemma, this could happen to the detriment of security, for example. In fact, Bitcoin with its PoW mechanism has a scalability problem largely linked to the high energy consumption required as the growth of the nodes increases, however it guarantees high decentralization and security. For this reason, the altcoins that were born after Bitcoin aim to solve its intrinsic scalability problems. 

 

 

## 3.2 Main challenges

 

After having illustrated the main advantages, let's now see what are the main challenges that Blockchain technology will have to face to be adopted on a global scale: lack of knowledge, privacy, 51% risk of attack, energy consumption, deregulation, financial risks and risks related to the loss of cryptographic keys.

 

### 3.2.1 Little knowledge of Technology

This limit actually refers to human nature and more generally to any innovation process. As mentioned in the introduction of this book, every innovative process is associated with a series of opportunities but also a series of risks. By their nature, people are inclined not to risk excessively, unless they have implemented a series of countermeasures to reduce and manage these risks. However, these countermeasures require some effort. The first step consists in reducing the uncertainty associated with the lack of knowledge of a technology, in this case the Blockchain. It is essential to create a method for reducing the knowledge gap with respect to technology, a gap that is gradually growing due to the speed with which new technologies evolve and the inertia of people to adapt to new future scenarios.

 

### 3.2.2 Privacy 

If on the one hand a distributed architecture is one of the main elements that guarantees the security and transparency we talked about in the previous paragraph, on the other hand this can generate a problem related to privacy. Specifically, in Blockchain technology each node necessarily has access to the data of the Blockchain transaction itself. This means that in the case of a public Blockchain, every transaction that is public can be traced back to the first block of genesis.

In practice, this transparency and traceability cancels the benefit of anonymity, that is, of the fact that there is apparently no link between the individual and the Wallet to which he belongs. A public Blockchain actually allows for identification opportunities. An article on the Bitcoin investigations of the Journal of Forensic Research[\[16\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn16), for example, illustrates several: one way is to monitor communications between Blockchain nodes, which can associate Internet protocol addresses and transactions. Furthermore, while it is not public, the cryptocurrency wallet software can be forensically analyzed even without the necessary keys to use it.

 The higher-profile use of these techniques cost Ross Ulbricht's arrest for running the 'Silk Road' site, a black market for illegal drugs. Even more interesting is that a special agent from the IRS was also able to track Bitcoin transactions to determine that a U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration agent involved in the investigation, Carl Force, was laundering Silk Road related Bitcoins. As it is easy to guess, a Blockchain like Bitcoin provides limited privacy protection. Individuals who rely on exchanges such as Coinbase must be aware that such platforms must comply with international privacy laws and are therefore subject to controls. For example, the IRS also asked Coinbase to hand over certain records involving cryptocurrency transactions [\[17\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn17). 

Therefore, it is necessary to be aware of the privacy aspects of public Blockchains, which while on the one hand guarantee a certain anonymity of user transactions, on the other hand they still offer identification opportunities through the implementation of a series of specific techniques.

 

### 3.2.3 Risk of a 51% attack

In a PoW mechanism a 51% attack refers to a hypothetical attack on a Blockchain by a group of miners who control more than 50% of the hash rate or computing power of the network (hash rate is a parameter used to measure total computational power to process transactions and mine new coins). In fact, one of the characteristics that guarantees the immutability of the blocks is the fact that the transactions are distributed on the network, which must approve these transactions according to the consensus mechanism. This means that if most users agree to change the blocks, this change could be implemented. On the practical side, if a transaction already carried out had foreseen the exchange of 1 BTC, the network could decide to modify it, for example, by spending 2 times the same coins. This risk is known as "double-spending", which is the risk that a digital currency could be spent twice. This is a potential problem unique to digital currencies because digital information can be reproduced relatively easily by experienced individuals who understand the Blockchain network and the computing power required to manipulate it. An attack of this type is only hypothetical, however if it happened it would irreparably undermine the foundations of the Blockchain and cryptocurrencies, especially those based on a PoW consensus mechanism.

For this reason, the main mining farms are located in countries with low energy costs.

### 3.2.7 Financial risks

Decentralized finance exposes the investor to very high and unknown risks in the context of classical financial services. First of all, there is a general lack of depositor protection: users can also lose the entire investment due to sudden falls in the value of cryptocurrencies, fraud, and lost private keys. Furthermore, the irreversibility of transactions in digital currencies and the difficulty in establishing responsibility towards users due to decentralized management pose additional risks. Finally, it should be noted that the data strings representing the public key addresses of the holders in a Crypto transaction can, with a significant effort, be linked to the identifiers, thus compromising the identity of users and their privacy.

 

### 3.2.8 Loss of cryptographic keys

The possession of the private cryptographic keys in a Crypto transaction allows you to have only the value associated with it. Therefore, the digital currencies deposited in the Exchange are not really Bitcoin but rather a contractual right on the underlying Bitcoins. On the contrary, the user can decide to keep their keys personally using specific software or hardware in the proprietary Wallets (at most, paper and pen) bypassing the exchange. In any case, the loss of the cryptographic key cannot in any way be recovered.

## 4.1 Coin and Token, a subtle difference

 

In the previous chapters we have described what Blockchain technology consists of and what are its advantages and disadvantages.

Trying to summarize once again, Blockchain technology is a transparent, secure, reliable and affordable solution for carrying out transactions, whether they are of an economic, contractual or commercial nature. To do this, over the years solutions have been developed which take the name of Blockchain in their current form and which are essentially based on the use of:

·             a distributed network

·             a hash and cryptographic coding system

·             a chain architecture

·             a consent mechanism

These elements contribute to achieving the set objectives. The absence of one of these would invalidate the final result: without a distributed network there would be no complete transparency and without a cryptographic coding system there would be no security. However, due to the increasing computing power provided by modern computers, capable of generating thousands of strings per second, this system alone would not be sufficient to guarantee the necessary security. Linking blocks in sequence reduces the likelihood of block modification as the chain becomes more extensive (remember? To modify an upstream block, all the hashes downstream must be recalculated). If even this mechanism were not enough, the consensus algorithms set a series of rules so that each block is validated by the network. In the case of PoW, the computational effort in deciphering the blocks is made by the so-called miners, whose role is fundamental in an ecosystem of this type. To reward miners for the work done (who would work for free?) they receive tokens associated with the Blockchain the miners are working on. Such Tokens are also called Coins.

Each Cryptocurrency must have its own transaction log. Once this register has been created, the Tokens must be issued and whose exchanges will be transactions stored on the distributed register. For example, about 17 million tokens (i.e., Bitcoin) have already been issued, and more than 21 million will never be issued. In addition, the electronic tokens are divisible, so it is also possible to exchange fractions of tokens. For example, a Bitcoin (or 1 BTC token) can be split up to one hundred millionth, called "Satoshi", so that, if desired, even very small amounts can be exchanged.

In this sense, we can say that a Coin is a Token with its own register, while Tokens that do not have their own register (thus exploiting the register of an existing Blockchain) are simply called Token.

In fact, anyone can create and issue Tokens thanks to the so-called Initial Coin Offer (ICO), which we will discuss in paragraph 4.4. So, Bitcoin is properly a Coin that uses the BTC Token as a trading currency, just as Ethereum uses the ETH Token. Most tokens are issued on the Ethereum Blockchain, with the ERC-20 format (see Glossary), and although they are stored on the Ethereum Blockchain they have nothing to do with the native Coin of this network, i.e., Ether (ETH). In fact, it is necessary to distinguish between Coin, Ether, and the Ethereum network, which are not the same thing. Issuing a new Token nowadays is not very complicated, because in theory it is enough to write a special Smart Contract (we will talk about it in paragraph 7.1), for example on the Ethereum network. Creating a new Coin, on the other hand, is much more complex.

Tokens are therefore the representation of a digital asset and we could think of them as the chips of a casino or as tokens of the gaming rooms: they have the value of currency only in the context in which they are used.

In a PoW Blockchain like that of Bitcoin, the Coin or BTC Token was born as a reward to miners for coding the blocks by virtue of the energy consumption spent by their hardware, adding them to the chain and making it more and more reliable and secure. However, with the emergence of other applications and consensus mechanisms, Tokens represent a much more versatile asset. In fact, we can identify different types of tokens according to their economic use:

1.              **Utility Token**: used to help capitalize or finance projects for startups, companies or project development groups. Very often cryptocurrency projects use ICOs to offer utility tokens to those interested in the project by announcing the issue of a certain number of tokens. These tokens represent a means (such as coupons) to access a product or good / service of a company: they are exchanged for products or services of the issuing company;

2.              **Security Token**: Security Tokens are considered as real financial investments for which the holder holds part of the value of the underlying but does not imply a share of ownership of the "issuing" company;

3.              **Equity Token**: it is a type of Security Token but which implies, for the owner, a share of ownership. It works more like a traditional equity asset. In other words, those who hold stock tokens have some form of ownership in their investments.

We realize how the sometimes-subtle distinction between Coin and Token may have generated some doubts and perplexities in the reader. However, for a purely practical purpose and for those who intend to try their hand at investing these assets from a financial point of view, they can both be considered simply as cryptocurrencies, or Altcoins for all those other than Bitcoin. For this reason, in the continuation of the discussion of this book, we will never make this distinction, but we will speak exclusively of Cryptocurrencies.

## 4.3. Bitcoin, a new digital currency

 

Giacomo Zucco[\[27\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn27), a Bitcoin expert and consultant, as well as founder of Bcademy, is considered what in the jargon is defined as a "Bitcoin maximalist". Bitcoin maximalists believe that Bitcoin, which is the most popular Cryptocurrency in the world, is the only digital asset that will be needed in the future and that all other digital currencies are inferior to Bitcoin. There's more: many think that Altcoins are even real scams. It is not up to us to verify whether or not they are, however Zucco expresses interesting and in some cases even strong points of view, so it is advisable to see his videos on Youtube. Zucco describes Bitcoin as *"the attempt to climb into the information age the currency that man has used for millennia before the last few centuries, which is basically gold"*. In fact, gold was the most salable commodity on the market for a series of advantages:

·             it was not perishable, it was malleable and divisible, therefore transportable in different forms (e.g., coins, nuggets and ingots)

·             it was not subject to central control by a state, i.e., its value was recognized regardless of where you were;

·             it was not manipulated by governments by altering its quantity

·             was immeasurable, meaning third parties could not block the gold transaction once the two parties had reached an agreement

However, gold also had some disadvantages:

·             it was difficult to transport in large quantities

·             it was falsifiable in the eyes of non-experts

·             it was not virtualizable, like fiat currencies

On the other hand, from the point of view of central governments, exchange currencies such as fiat offer several advantages:

 

·    they can be controlled and manipulated by the monetary policies of central governments

·    are virtualizable. It’s worth to say that 97% of the money supply exists only as a record on a computer database, the remainder is circulating money

 

 Fiat currencies are essentially virtual currencies and, since 1971, not constrained by gold / currency convertibility. Indeed, the sudden decision of the American president, announced on TV on the evening of August 15, 1971, marked the end of the post-war international monetary system, created at the Bretton Woods conference.

 

Bitcoin aims to become a digital currency by taking the main benefits of gold and fiat currency, namely:

 

·             the **non-manipulability of the monetary offer** by central entities. Bitcoin is based on Blockchain, a database that is decentralized by nature. Governments cannot have control over it, for example by creating hyperinflation. Let's think for example of the case of Zimbabwe[\[28\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn28) or Venezuela, with decreases in purchasing power on savings of up to 30% per week[\[29\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn29). However, the West is not exempt from inflation: 1 US dollar in 1970 had the same purchasing power of 6.59 dollars in 2019, with an inflation rate of change over this period of 558.63%[\[30\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn30)

·             **privacy** on the use of currency (think of countries with limited freedom, such as Afghanistan, where women cannot even open a bank account)

·             **it can be virtualized**, as it can be exchanged in electronic form, therefore easily transportable and usable

·             **it is safe**, as it is subject to the immutability and security rules of the Blockchain

 

Bitcoin adds another solution to the problems of the digital age: the centralization of data by entities that monitor the exchange of money. In fact, payments are among the most sensitive information ever, however the new forms of digital payment that have emerged in recent years such as Apple Pay, PayPal, Google Pay and Stripe, just to name the most famous, collect sensitive data that can then be resold. This information can become a form of control if not properly managed. An example? On June 14, 2011, WikiLeaks decided to accept donations in Bitcoin after the boycott of Amazon, PayPal, Visa, MasterCard, Bank of America and Western Union, which suddenly decided to cut off any possibility of donating to WikiLeaks under pressure from the United States government, such as reported by Stefania Maurizi in her book "Il potere segreto "[\[31\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn31) (“The secret power”).

 

### 4.3.1 How does Bitcoin be worth thousands of Dollars?

In the introductory chapter we mentioned the fact of Laszlo Hanyecz who, on May 22, 2010, bought two pizzas worth $ 30 in a small town in Florida for 10,000 Bitcoins. **This was the first price given to Bitcoin in history, with an exchange rate of 1 BTC = $ 0.003.** Today, almost 12 years later, those two pizzas are earning an equivalent value of around 400 million dollars to the lucky pizzeria. But what is the mechanism by which a virtual asset, initially without any value, suddenly becomes a store of value?

 

**In other words, who determines the price of Bitcoin?**

The price is determined by all those who want to participate in the buying and selling process, essentially its users. It sounds trivial, but that's the way it is, although multiple factors play a role. In fact, miners can propose a price when they sell their Bitcoins to interested parties: when in a negotiation between two people, one proposes a price and the other is willing to accept it, then the transaction is made. What we have just described is the same process by which all existing goods are exchanged. And it happens tens, hundreds or thousands of times a day, often with great variations.

The value of Bitcoin has changed over time and is essentially based on the trust of its users, its usefulness and its popularity. There is no official or single price for Bitcoin, as there is no single platform to trade, the so-called Exchanges, which we will discuss in Chapter 6.

Certainly, a fundamental component in the price of Bitcoin is its intrinsic scarcity. In fact, its Blockchain was designed to have a finite number of coins that can be mined, or 21 million. Consequently, the greater the demand, the greater the cost. 

 

## 4.4 Ethereum, the # 1 Altcoin

 

After describing the reasons, the implications of the birth of Bitcoin, as well as some of the main reasons why it has accumulated value over the years, we now introduce Ethereum, the second Cryptocurrency in terms of market cap. Why is there a need for Ethereum, and more generally, for Altcoins?

Ethereum was conceived in 2013 by programmer Vitalik Buterin together with co-founders Gavin Wood (later creator of Polkadot), Charles Hoskinson (later founder of Cardano), Anthony Di Iorio and Joseph Lubin. The network went live on July 30, 2015 with the aim of allowing anyone to deploy permanent and immutable decentralized applications that users could interact with. This is the main difference compared to Bitcoin, according to the founders: Bitcoin generates tokens that essentially become a store of value or trading currency, while Ethereum thinks that thanks to the Blockchain it is possible to create Smart Contracts capable of making every safe and decentralized transactions, not just those of money.

 

As reported by an article by Vincenzo Cacioppoli on the Cryptonomist[\[32\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn32):

*"* *Many think that Ethereum is better than Bitcoin, from the perspective of its adoption in the real world. Bitcoin, as a payment tool, still struggles to find space in finance, if not in limited cases. Ethereum represented a real revolution for the blockchain world, and its example was followed by other projects, such as Solana, Cardano and Polkadot".* According to the author: *"Recent research carried out by the Sydney University of Technology has shown that Ethereum is better than Bitcoin, as a store of value, despite the fact that Bitcoin's coins in circulation are limited, unlike Ether. According to Australian researchers, Ethereum is a much more effective anti-inflation tool than Bitcoin."*

However, there are many skeptics, including the aforementioned Bitcoin maximalists, who express various criticisms regarding the Ethereum project, especially as regards the technical aspects.

Many of the altcoins on the market today are based on Ethereum, such as Layer 2 (see Glossary). Among these we mention:

 

·    **Chainlink[**\[33\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn33) (LINK)**: a decentralized network that allows you to bridge the gap between Blockchain and the real world in a secure way. In most use cases, dApps (decentralized applications, see Glossary) need real-world data to work, so they rely on intermediaries (called oracles), who provide a reliable source of information. Considering that some dApps manage hundreds of millions of dollars, the security of these external communication bridges is of vital importance. Chainlink minimizes the potential risks associated with oracles through the use of a highly decentralized infrastructure, innovative hardware and data sources protected from manipulation

 

·    **Compound[**\[34\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn34) (COMP)**: the decentralized equivalent of traditional credit markets. This protocol is basically a liquidity pool that allows users to obtain and grant loans, as well as earn interest on their cryptocurrencies

 

·    **MakerDAO[**\[35\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn35) (MKR)**: a service that, through Smart Contracts, makes it possible to obtain and grant loans without the need for intermediaries. It is one of the first projects developed in the DeFi ecosystem, as well as one of the most used

 

·    **0x[**\[36\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn36) (ZRX)**: a standard that facilitates interactions between platforms and introduces the concept of "interconnected liquidity": operators using 0x share trading orders on individual platforms to obtain better liquidity

 

·    **Kyber Network[**\[37\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn37) (KNC)**: a protocol that allows users to exchange ETH and Token in a totally decentralized way, without having to use Exchange

 

·    **Aave[**\[38\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn38) (LEND)**: the protocol allows to lend and borrow a wide range of Cryptocurrencies. The accrued interest is distributed in the form of this native token.

 

·    **Synthetix (SNX)**[\[39\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn39): Synthetix is a protocol for issuing synthetic assets, called Synth, which use the native SNX token as collateral. Users can convert Synth directly through a Smart Contract, without having to use Exchange. These synthetic assets represent cryptocurrencies, stocks and fiat currencies.

 

We have seen how Ethereum has basically given way to the birth of a series of Cryptocurrencies based on the creation of Smart Contracts. What value do these cryptocurrencies have? The initial value is assigned thanks to an initial token offer, or ICO.

 

 

## 4.5 Initial Coin Offering (ICO)

 

In the wake of Bitcoin, a series of different Blockchains have been born over the years, and consequently also a series of different cryptocurrencies. A digital currency market parallel and analogous to that of fiat currencies has therefore been created, i.e., currencies issued by the government not supported by physical commodities, such as gold or silver, but rather by the government that issued them (for example the US dollar, euro and other major global currencies) and that of the stock markets. In fact, thanks to the Foreign Exchange market, more commonly called forex, or currency market, it is possible to exchange different currencies, just as it is possible to trade shares in the stock market. In a completely similar way, it has become possible to exchange Cryptocurrencies through the Exchanges.

The question we ask ourselves now is: **what value to assign to newly born cryptocurrencies?**

 The answer still lies in the analogy with the financial markets. When a company decides to go public, it must go through a so-called initial public offering. An initial public offering or IPO, is an offer to the public of the securities of a company that intends to go public for the first time on a regulated market. It is a complex phase that requires notions that are beyond our scope. However, it is worth underlining that it is necessary to carry out an evaluation of the company being listed.

Similarly, in the Cryptocurrency market we proceed through an initial coin offering or ICO. However, the process is completely different from the IPO: it often takes place in the form of crowdfunding, although a private ICO that does not seek public investments is also possible. In an ICO, a quantity of Cryptocurrency is sold in the form of tokens to speculators or investors, in exchange for fiat currency or other Cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin or Ether. ICOs can allow startups to avoid regulations that prevent them from seeking investments directly from the public and intermediaries such as venture capitalists, banks and stock exchanges, which may require greater control and a certain percentage of future profits or joint ownership.

Furthermore, ICOs may fall outside existing regulations, depending on the nature of the project, or be banned altogether in some jurisdictions, such as China and South Korea. In fact, due to the lack of regulation and enforcement of securities law, ICOs have in the past been a vehicle for scams and fraud.

Less than half of all ICOs survive four months after offering, while nearly half of ICOs sold in 2017 fail by February 2018. Despite their record failures and falling cryptocurrency prices, January to June 2018 was raised a record $ 7 billion via ICO.

However, ICOs aren't the only way to raise money in the cryptocurrency industry[\[40\]](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn40).

 

 

## 4.6 Initial Exchange Offering (IEO) and Initial DEX Offering (IDO)

In return, the start-up has to pay listing fees and a percentage of the tokens sold through IEO. The relationship between Exchange and start-ups becomes symbiotic, as the former are incentivized to help the latter with marketing and promotions.

An Initial DEX Offering (IDO) is similar to an IEO, except that IDOs are conducted on decentralized exchanges (DEX), while IEOs are conducted on centralized platforms. ### 4.6.1 Bitcoin[**\[42\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn42)

Bitcoin is first presented in a 2008 Whitepaper by one person, or a group of people, using the alias Satoshi Nakamoto and launched shortly thereafter, in January 2009. Bitcoin was created, in Nakamoto's own words, for allow *"to send online payments directly from one party to another without going through a financial institution"*. Some concepts for a similar type of decentralized electronic currency precede BTC, but Bitcoin has the distinction of being the first Coin to actually be used. Nakamoto has managed to create a global community and give birth to a whole new industry of millions of enthusiasts who create, invest, trade and use Bitcoin and other Cryptocurrencies in their daily lives. The emergence of Bitcoin created a conceptual and technological foundation that subsequently inspired the development of thousands of competing projects.

The entire cryptocurrency market - which is now worth more than $ 2 trillion - is based on the idea realized by Bitcoin: money that can be sent and received by anyone, anywhere in the world, without relying on trusted intermediaries, such as banks. and financial services companies.

Due to its pioneering nature, BTC remains at the top of this market after more than a decade of existence. Even after Bitcoin lost its undisputed dominance, it remains the largest cryptocurrency, with a market cap that passed the $ 1 trillion mark in 2021.

 

### 4.6.2 Ethereum[**\[43\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn43)

The first alternative to Bitcoin in terms of market capitalization is Ethereum, a decentralized platform that allows you to create and run smart contracts and decentralized applications (dApps) without downtime, fraud, control or interference from third parties. The goal behind Ethereum is to create a decentralized suite of financial products that anyone in the world can access freely. Its Ether Token is used as a vehicle to move around the platform and is mainly sought after by developers who wish to develop and run applications within Ethereum, or by investors who wish to make purchases of other digital currencies using Ether. Ethereum launched a presale for Ether in 2014, receiving overwhelming response and helping usher in the era of the Initial Coin Offering (ICO).

According to Ethereum, the platform can be used to *"code, decentralize, secure and trade just about anything".*

Ethereum plans to move its consensus algorithm from Proof-of-Work (PoW) to Proof-of-Stake (PoS) by mid-2022, an update expected as Ethereum 2.0. This move is intended to allow the Ethereum network to function autonomously with much less power and better transaction speed.

 

### 4.6.3 Binance Coin[**\[44\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn44)

Binance Coin operates as a payment method for fees associated with trading on the Binance Exchange and is currently the third largest Cryptocurrency by market capitalization. Those who use the Token as a means of payment for the exchange can also negotiate at a discount.

The Binance Exchange was founded by Chinese Changpeng Zhao and is one of the most used exchanges in the world based on trading volumes. One of Binance's biggest competitive advantages is its drive to develop services. Since the launch of Binance Coin, the Exchange has also benefited from increased investor interest in the token. ### 4.6.4 Tether[**\[45\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn45)

Tether was one of the earliest and most popular of a group of so-called Stablecoins, or Cryptocurrencies that aim to anchor their market value to a currency or other external benchmark to reduce volatility. As most digital currencies, even major ones like Bitcoin, have experienced frequent periods of extreme volatility, Tether and other Stablecoins attempt to smooth out price fluctuations to attract users who might otherwise be cautious. By virtue of this, the price of Tether is directly linked to the price of the US dollar. The system allows users to more easily make transfers from other cryptocurrencies to US dollars in a more timely manner than actually converting to regular currency. Launched in 2014, Tether describes itself as *"a blockchain-enabled platform designed to facilitate the use of fiat currencies digitally."* Indeed, this Token allows people to use a Blockchain network and related technologies to transact in traditional currencies while minimizing the volatility and complexity often associated with cryptocurrencies.

 

### 4.6.5 Solana[**\[46\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn46)

Solana is designed to host decentralized and scalable applications. Founded in 2017, Solana is an open-source project currently run by the Geneva-based Solana Foundation, while the Blockchain was built by San Francisco-based Solana Labs. Solana is much faster in terms of the number of transactions it can process and has significantly lower transaction fees than rival Blockchains like Ethereum.

The protocol is designed to facilitate the creation of decentralized apps (DApps) and aims to improve scalability by introducing a Proof-of-History (PoH) consensus developed by Anatoly Yakovenko, combined with the underlying Proof-of-Stake (PoS) consensus of the Blockchain. In fact, Solana's hybrid protocol allows significantly reduced validation times for both the transaction and the execution of the smart contract. 

 

### 4.6.6 Cardano[**\[47\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn47)

Cardano has been created with a scientific approach based on the research of teams of engineers, mathematicians and cryptographic experts. The project was co-founded by Charles Hoskinson, one of the initial five founding members of Ethereum, following some disagreements with the other co-founders. Cardano's team created its Blockchain through extensive peer-reviewed experimentation and research. In fact, the researchers of the project have written over 90 articles on Blockchain technology on a wide range of topics and such research forms the backbone of Cardano today, which means that all of its innovative principles can be questioned by the network of researchers. before being validated. According to Cardano's team, this academic rigor helps the Blockchain to be durable and stable, increasing the chances that potential pitfalls can be anticipated. For this reason, Cardano seems to distinguish itself from other large cryptocurrencies, so much so that it even earned the nickname of "Ethereum killer". Cardano is one of the largest Blockchains to successfully use a PoS consensus mechanism.

The Token used for the Cryptocurrency uses the XRP symbol.

### 4.6.10 Polkadot[**\[51\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn51)

This interoperability aims to establish a completely decentralized and private web, controlled by its users, and to simplify the creation of new applications and services. Polkadot's native DOT token aims to provide governance and network operations and create parachains, the so-called parallel chains.

 

### 4.6.11 Dogecoin[**\[52\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn52)

Dogecoin was created in 2013 by two American computer engineers, Billy Markus and Jackson Palmer. Markus and Palmer reportedly created the coin as a joke, commenting on the wild speculation of the Cryptocurrency market. Dogecoin was primarily used as a tipping system on Reddit and Twitter to reward the creation or sharing of quality content. You can get a Dogecoin tip by joining a community that uses digital currency. Dogecoin caused a stir in 2021 as the coin's price skyrocketed. ### 4.6.12 Litecoin[**\[53\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn53)

Litecoin, launched in 2011, was among the first Cryptocurrencies to follow in Bitcoin's footsteps and was created by Charlie Lee, an MIT graduate and former Google engineer. Litecoin is based on an open-source global payment network which is not controlled by any central authority and uses "scrypt" as a Proof-of-Work. Although Litecoin is very similar to Bitcoin, it has a faster block generation rate and therefore offers a faster transaction confirmation time.

Its main advantage derives precisely from its speed and economy. Litecoin transactions are generally confirmed within minutes and the transaction fees are almost negligible. This makes it an attractive alternative to Bitcoin in developing countries, where transaction fees can be the deciding factor as to which cryptocurrency to support.

As of January 2021, Litecoin is one of the most widely accepted Cryptocurrencies and more than 2,000 merchants and shops now accept LTC worldwide.

As of November 2021, Litecoin has a market cap of $ 14 billion and a value per token of around $ 200, making it the 17th largest cryptocurrency in the world. 

 

### 4.6.13 Algorand[**\[54\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn54)

Algorand is a Blockchain-based platform which, basically like most of them, aims to be secure, scalable and decentralized. The development of the Algorand platform is overseen by Algorand, Inc., a privately held company based in Boston and was founded in 2017 by Silvio Micali, a professor at MIT. What makes this Blockchain different from the others is its consensus mechanism, the so-called Pure-Proof-of-Stake (PPoS) in which the creators of blocks are not chosen on the basis of their wealth, but are selected randomly. With PPoS, the likelihood of being chosen is proportional, but not limited, to the total amount of "wagered" ALGOs. This consensus mechanism is intended to increase decentralization, as low "stakes" requirements favor participation from anywhere in the world. Furthermore, the security is high because random selection represents a big obstacle for hackers or malicious users.

Another noteworthy feature is that Algorand declares itself "carbon negative": not only does it not produce CO[2] emissions, but it induces a permanent removal of CO[2] from the ecosystem

Algorand's native Token is called ALGO.

 

### 4.6.14 IOTA[**\[55\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn55)

IOTA currently represents a sort of unicum in the Cryptocurrency landscape and that is why, although it is not currently among the main ones in terms of capitalization, we think it is useful to mention it.

IOTA is an open-source distributed ledger designed for the Internet of Things (IoT), i.e., the exchange of data between systems such as industrial, such as robots, machine tools, processing plants and so on. IOTA is not based on a Blockchain mechanism, but on a mechanism called Tangle, motivated by potentially higher scalability than Blockchain-based distributed ledgers. IOTA does not use miners to validate transactions, but nodes that issue a new transaction on the network must approve two previous transactions. Transactions can then be issued commission-free, facilitating microtransactions.

This mechanism has come under criticism due to its unusual design, of which it is unclear whether it will work in practice. For this reason, IOTA was rewritten from scratch for a network update called Chrysalis, or IOTA 1.5, launched on April 28, 2021. However, despite this skepticism, the project enjoys first-rate investors, especially in the industrial field. For example, concepts based on IOTA technology have been developed in the automotive and IoT sectors by companies such as Jaguar Land Rover and Bosch. IOTA also participates in projects to develop smart cities, to establish digital identity, waste management and local energy trade.

IOTA is traded in megaIOTA units (1,000,000 IOTA) and is listed under the MIOTA symbol.

## 5.2 Broker

 

A Cryptocurrency broker is a company or individual that acts as an intermediary between the buyer and the different Cryptocurrency markets, to facilitate the buying and selling of Cryptocurrencies through financial instruments. 

Cryptocurrency brokers differ from exchanges in several characteristics, the main one of which is the impossibility of buying cryptocurrencies, as they deal with investments on their value. The investment in Cryptocurrencies through a broker can essentially take place through 3 financial instruments:

 

1.  **CFD**: it stands for contract for difference and is a financial instrument whose price is based on the difference between the entry price and the exit price of an underlying asset. Simply put, a CFD reflects the value states of an asset, both at the beginning and at the end of a day. Thanks to these features, traders have the ability to trade different types of assets such as indices, stocks, futures, or even Cryptocurrencies, without owning them. For example, when you buy a CFD on a stock, you are not the owner of the stock, but simply have a contract with the broker

2.  **ETF**: Exchange Traded Funds are special passively managed investment funds. As with all funds, buying an ETF is like buying a basket of stocks. Investing in a fund is equivalent to putting your savings together with that of other investors, the fund manager will then buy the instruments on which you will invest with this money. The investment performance will be given by the result of all the individual instruments in which the fund invests. Within the ETFs we can find investments in companies that, for example, operate in the branch of hardware production or mining, up to financial companies that hold real Exchanges on which to exchange Crypto, passing through banks and payment services in favor of Bitcoin adoption. To date, there are several indices that invest in companies operating in the Blockchain and Cryptocurrency sector, directly or indirectly. Some of them are:

1.  Elwood Blockchain Global Equity, which replicates the performance of companies around the world, developed and non-developed countries, which deal or will deal with the Blockchain sector;

2.  Indxx Blockchain, which replicates the performance of companies, globally, that are already using or creating products and services related to the Blockchain, or alternatively that are investing in this sector;

3.  MVIS Global Digital Assets Equity, which replicates the global trend of companies engaged in the sector of digital assets or similar, offering Exchange services, payment systems, mining operations, software, technologies or infrastructures related to this issue. 

3.  **ETN**: these are Exchange-Traded Notes, or financial instruments that differ from ETFs in that they are not funds and are issued against a direct investment in the underlying by the issuing company, replicating the trend. Who made these ETNs available is the company VanEck, which has decided to add the following ETNs to its ETF on digital assets (which we have analyzed before):

a.  VanEck Vectors Ethereum ETN

b.  VanEck Vectors Solana ETN

c.   VanEck Vectors Polkadot ETN

d.  VanEck Vectors Tron ETN

 

The advantage of investing in these ETNs is the fact of being able to expose yourself on these Cryptocurrencies without having to buy them directly through an Exchange, with all the risks associated with the counterparty. ## 5.3 The Wallets

 

There are many ways to store and protect your Cryptocurrencies and, in this section, we will take a look at each of these possibilities and their advantages and disadvantages. In reality, a clarification must be made immediately: Cryptocurrencies are actually stored on the Blockchain, or on a public address for each of which there is a private key necessary to be able to use them. Anyone who knows the private key of a public address can freely use all the tokens stored on it, therefore it becomes essential to keep the private keys of one's addresses properly.

Wallets or digital wallets are the place where public and private keys are stored, in a manner similar to physical wallets where coins, banknotes and credit cards are stored. 

The Wallet is a software or hardware designed exclusively to store and manage public keys and private keys of our Cryptocurrencies and uses advanced Blockchain encryption techniques to offer and guarantee total security to users. In this way, the Wallet can save, send and receive Cryptocurrencies without increased risk. Before operating with a Wallet, it is necessary to know the types of Wallets available on the market and to study the advantages and disadvantages offered by each of them to determine which is the one that best suits the needs of each. As we will see, the user has several means of protecting their private key.

 

### 5.3.1 Cold Wallet 

The so-called “cold Wallet” is any type of Wallet in which the private key is stored offline, without an Internet connection. **Paper Wallets** fall under this category: it consists of writing the private key on a piece of paper and is probably the safest way to keep keys but at the same time also the most inconvenient.

 

### 5.3.2 Hot Wallet 

These are Wallets that are saved on a device with an Internet connection. Hot Wallets are not insecure by definition, but it is essential to understand the risks because today it is necessary to expect that every device can be hacked. Basically, it is software that acts as a safe for the private key and a password is used to protect it. However, it is important to point out that theoretically the PC on which the software is installed can be tampered with, so the software Wallet password should not be saved anywhere on the computer. Furthermore, it is strongly advisable to carry out periodic backups in case of damage to the PC, in order to avoid that the password, and therefore consequently the coins, are lost.

### 5.3.3 Hard Wallet 

A Hard Wallet consists of a combination of a Cold Wallet and a Hot Wallet. This type of Wallet was created by companies such as Ledger or Trezor and consists of a USB stick, in which you need to enter a PIN code to access all the private keys stored on it. Ledger, for example, keeps a backup of all data saved on the Wallet; therefore, it can be restored even if it is destroyed. Trezor instead requires you to use your fingerprint and enter your PIN to access resources.

 

### 5.3.4 Exchange 

Exchanges are probably the most popular way to store Coins. Most Exchanges also provide a Wallet thanks to which Coins are stored before and after an exchange. The main advantage here lies in the convenience of use; however, you need to rely on secure Exchange that you can trust.

 

### 5.3.5 Wallet HD

HD Wallets (Hierarchical Deterministic Wallet) use a single seed (i.e., a list of words in a specific order that store all the information necessary to retrieve a Wallet) of 12, 18 or 24 keywords, with which they can generate an unlimited number of addresses. HD wallets are available as both hard and soft (hot) Wallets; also, some of them are free and others for a fee. The most popular are provided by Trezor, KeepKey, Ledger Nano S, Mycelium, Jaxx, Eidoo and Electrum. These Wallets require a single backup so that you can fully restore your data at any time. This is due to the use of the BIP32 transfer protocol which allows parent keys to create child keys in a hierarchy. The BIP32 specifies how to derive key pairs from the main seed in a so-called tree structure and how to build a wallet that uses this structure. The second advantage of Wallets HD is the ability to create a sequence of public keys without having access to the corresponding private keys. This allows the Wallet HD to be used on an insecure server or with only reception capability, with the issuance of a different public key for each transaction.

## 6.1 Smart Contracts

 

A Smart Contract represents an agreement between two people that is validated in the absence of an intermediary, such as a notary, through a verification procedure done directly by the computer system, in a decentralized and secure form, through the Blockchain. This means that if a party violates the contract, the Blockchain automatically executes the agreements that have been previously agreed or applies any other obligations that have been added to the contract. Making Smart Contracts also supports each counterpart equally, without favoring users who can afford legal offices with more expensive bills. Vitalik Buterin, the founder of Ethereum, explained the concept like this:

* "In a smart contract approach, an asset or currency is transferred into a program and the program runs this code and at some point, automatically validates a condition and automatically determines whether the asset should go to one person or return to the other person. or whether it should be immediately refunded to the person who sent it or a combination of these. Meanwhile, the decentralized ledger also stores and replicates the document which gives it a certain security and immutability."[**\[74\]**](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn74)*

The Ethereum network was developed primarily to allow users to develop this type of contract. The advantages are obvious:

 

·    smart contracts can be applied to any type of transaction, not just financial. We are therefore talking about exchanges of goods, services, agreements, etc.

·    absence of intermediaries to guarantee the validity of the contract. Less costs, faster execution

·    security that the contract is executed, as its execution is delegated to the algorithm

## 6.11 Ride sharing

 

The use of ride sharing (or car sharing) apps such as Uber is gaining considerable popularity around the world. However, the industry is also full of challenges:

Let's clarify the meaning of fungible and non-fungible.

A banknote or Cryptocurrency is said to be fungible as it can be replaced without any problem with another banknote or cryptocurrency of equal value. A Non-Fungible Token, on the other hand, represents a unique piece: that is, it cannot be replicated or replaced.

 

An NFT can be any object in digital format, for example a video, a photo, a GIF, a text, an article, an audio. Any of these digital objects, when certified with an NFT, is as if there was the author's signature on it and no one can say that it is not original or that there are other copies if not signed with an NFT. Let's take another example: a copy of a book, by itself, has some commercial value. Each copy is substantially the same as the other, and can for example be replaced with another completely identical. However, if this copy is signed by the author, for example by adding a dedication and date, this copy would become unique and unrepeatable. Furthermore, it would acquire a much greater value, so it would not make sense to exchange it for a different copy of the book. Here, this special copy would become non-fungible, an NFT. NFTs couldn't exist without the Blockchain. Which Blockchain? Ethereum was the first to support NFTs with its ERC-721 standard and is currently the most widely used. ## 7.3 How NFTs Work

 

Nowadays, NFTs are considered a sort of evolution of the world of art in digital format. Whoever buys a work linked to an NFT does not purchase the work in the strict sense. Rather, the possibility of claiming a right to that work is guaranteed, through a Smart Contract.

But how is this work produced? Basically, the artist saves a photo or video in digital format, which will correspond to a string of numbers. This sequence is then compressed into a shorter one, a hash. This hash is then stored in a Blockchain, complete with an associated timestamp. The NFT keeps track of hash sales internally, right up to the creator who changed hands the first time. With this reverse path, you can therefore have proof of the authenticity and ownership of the Token, and the owner of the work will not need to contact intermediaries, at least as long as the Blockchain is active.

At this point it is necessary to make a clarification regarding Intellectual Property rights: the ownership of an NFT does not intrinsically guarantee Copyright or Intellectual Property rights to any digital resource that represents the Token. While someone may sell an NFT that represents their work, the buyer will not necessarily receive Copyright privileges when ownership of the NFT is changed and thus the original owner is allowed to create multiple NFTs of the same work. In this sense, an NFT is simply a separate proof of ownership from a Copyright.

The technology underlying Meta's vision is virtual reality, a technology that is not really new (as we will see) but in which the company already has considerable know-how thanks to the acquisition in 2014 of Oculus VR (now renamed Horizon), a Californian company founded just 2 years earlier, for $ 2 billion. In this paragraph we will describe in more detail what virtual reality is and how it works.

Virtual Reality can be described as an interactive computer-generated experience that takes place within a simulated environment and mainly incorporates auditory and visual feedback, but can also allow for other types of sensory feedback such as tactile feedback. Furthermore, this immersive environment can be similar to the real world or it can be fantastic.

The first concepts of virtual reality were born in reality as early as 1838, when Sir Charles Wheatstone invented the stereoscope. In the mid-1950s, the filmmaker Morton Heilig developed the Sensorama (patented in 1962), an arcade-style theater furniture that was able to stimulate all the senses, not just sight and hearing. It featured stereo speakers, a stereoscopic 3D display, fans, odor generators, and a vibrating chair. In 1961 two engineers from the Philco Corporation (Comeau and Bryan) developed the first precursor to a head-mounted display as we know it today: the Headsight. It incorporated a video screen for each eye and a magnetic motion detection system, connected to a CCTV camera. In 1968 Ivan Sutherland invents "the Sword of Damocles", capable of tracing the movement of the user's head by superimposing synthetic graphics of primitive 3D computer-generated objects. With the age of the internet, virtual reality makes its way especially in the world of video games, with experiments and products that are sometimes not very successful (for example by Nintendo). To date, the most popular VR Headset devices are probably Meta's Oculus and HTC's HTC Vive.

 

#### 8.4.1.1 How VR works, such as hardware and software

 

There are basically 2 types of technologies to use Virtual Reality:

·             **Cave Automatic Virtual Environment (CAVE)**

·             **Headset technology**

 

CAVE technology consists of a video theater located inside a room, whose CAVE walls can consist of:

·             rear-projection screens, or

·             flat screen display

Projection systems are very high resolution due to close range viewing which requires very small pixel sizes to maintain the illusion of reality. The user wears the 3D glasses inside the CAVE to see the generated 3D graphics. People using CAVE can see objects floating in the air and can walk around them, getting a correct view of what they would look like in reality. This is made possible by the use of infrared cameras. The user's movements in the CAVE are tracked by sensors typically attached to the 3D glasses and the video continuously adapts to maintain the viewers perspective. Computers control both this aspect of the CAVE and the audio aspect. There are typically multiple speakers placed at multiple angles in the CAVE, which provide stereo sound.

 

![HMD vs CAVE in the world of VR. Virtual reality is a fascinating… | by Reghill J Manuelraj | XRPractices | Medium](images/00030.jpeg)

Figure 30: graphic representation of CAVE technology

 

Headset technology is more familiar to the general public as it provides a cheaper solution for experiencing Virtual Reality. The headset includes an eye-mounted stereoscopic display (providing separate images for each eye), stereo sound, and head motion detection sensors (which can include gyroscopes, accelerometers, structured light systems, etc.). Based on their tracking method, Headset technologies can be divided into 2 main categories:

·             **3 DoF**: monitoring tracks how the headset is rotated, so it will know where we are looking. Most mobile VR platforms use the 3 DoF monitoring system. Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) are used to track rotation. IMUs use a gyroscope, accelerometer, and magnetometer to detect rotations. All modern 3 DoF tracking uses an IMU

 

·             **6 DoF**: monitoring keeps track of position and rotation. The system knows where we are looking and where we are positioned. This is how high-end VR systems, like HTC Vive and Oculus Rift, let you move around the room. There are many different ways to do 6 DoF monitoring. The Oculus Rift uses a number of LEDs and external cameras that build a model and then try to fit a 3D model of the headset with the 2D model it sees of the camera. It then uses information from an internal IMU to calculate the position of the head. The HTC Vive instead uses infrared lasers to measure the time it takes to sweep a visual sensor vertically and horizontally. It uses the data it gets and an internal IMU to determine where the head and hands are

 

Another classification of VR Headset-based technologies is:

·             **Mobile VR: **Mobile VR uses IMU tracking, so it only has 3 DoF tracking. It can only monitor the rotation of the head, not the position. It is also "free" which means there are no wires connecting the headset to a computer. You can use it anywhere. It has less powerful graphics and is powered by a battery

 

·             **Desktop VR:** VR Desktop technology uses 6 DoF monitoring and is typically a wired solution. The device is connected to a computer and uses the computer to perform graphic work and to obtain its power. Graphics are generally better. Most desktop VR devices also come with manual controllers that allow for greater interactivity and a more immersive experience.

 

Generally, development for Virtual Reality requires writing code, using an editor, or a combination of both. An editor is a software development framework that simplifies game creation. Editors typically provide 3D rendering, physics, sound, scripting, animation, resource management, and more.

#### 8.4.2.1 How Augmented Reality works

Augmented Reality basically works according to one of the following approaches:

1.              **SLAM:** **Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM)** it is the computational problem of building or updating a map of an unknown environment while keeping track of the position of an agent within it. There are several known algorithms to solve this. Published approaches are employed for the development of self-driving cars, unmanned aerial vehicles, autonomous underwater vehicles, planetary rovers, new home robots, and even within the human body.

2.              **Recognition based:** systems based on recognition (or markers) using Augmented Reality use a camera to identify markers or objects, such as a QR / 2D code or natural feature tracking markers (NFT), to display an overlay of information or images only when the marker is detected by the device. This technology essentially depends on the device's camera to distinguish a marker from other real-world objects. It is not only possible to identify the marker image, but also to calculate its position and orientation. Once recognized, the on-screen marker is replaced with a virtual 3D version of the corresponding object. This is done to allow the user to observe the object in more detail and from various angles. Rotating the marker also rotates the virtual replica.

3.              **Position based:** Unlike recognition-based systems, location-based augmented reality relies on GPS systems, digital compasses, speed sensors or accelerometers to provide location data, while augmented reality information overlay is activated based on these inputs. It is also known as markerless augmented reality. The location tracking capabilities in smartphones make it easy to take advantage of this type of augmented reality technology, making it quite popular. Some common uses of location-based AR include mapping directions, searching for nearby services, and other location-centric mobile apps. A popular example is provided by Pokémon Go - the game uses your smartphone's GPS, camera, and clock to generate a virtual, brightly colored version of the real world on your phone. Just like Google Maps, "Pokémon Go" tracks your phone's location using GPS, supplementing this information with a game map.

### 8.4.3 Problems related to the use of VR and AR

Virtual Reality and Augmented Reality are however not without problems. Some of these are:

 

·             **User interface:** one of the main challenges of AR is to improve the field of view (FOV), brightness, display quality, latency, etc. In the case of the FOV, for example, even the best AR devices can only offer up to 90 degrees. Comparing this to the 190-degree horizontal and 120-degree vertical FOVs for normal human vision, the gap between where the technology is now and where it needs to go becomes apparent.

·             **Health problems:** Heavy use of AR and VR devices (described in the next chapter) could cause some health problems such as:

o      **Virtual reality sickness**: VR headsets contain 2 small LCD monitors, each projected onto an eye, creating a stereoscopic effect that gives users the illusion of an artificial environment created around you. And a longer screen focusing time increases the feeling of nausea and distortion, a phenomenon called Virtual Reality Sickness;

o      **Eye strain**: using AR/VR viewers we are confined to a limited area by our environment, we see and focus on details for a long time due to which it is correct to tend to blink less than normal frequency. This results in dryness of the front surface of our eyes and eye strain

o      **Dizziness**: individuals with amblyopia (an imbalance in visual strength between the 2 eyes) or other conditions that inhibit focus, depth perception or normal 3D vision may not experience the 3D effects of VR devices. Individuals with these disorders may be more likely to experience headaches and eye strain when using such devices

## 8.6 Some current technological limitations

In any case, Intel has recently stated that it is working in this direction in particular in the development of new graphics processors designed for the Metaverse. ## 9.2 Adopt new technologies

 

Are we facing utopian or dystopian scenarios? In recent years, more and more authors and film producers are focusing their attention on future scenarios allowed by the use of these technologies. The real problem is the one revealed at the beginning: technology advances but our condition as "creatures of habit" does not help us to adapt to the consequences of their diffusion, consequently creating an ever-greater gap between what is possible and what is possible. communities actually adopt.

This problem is actually not recent: in fact, as early as 1959 the German philosopher Martin Heidegger was writing[***\[141\]***](#part0000_split_077.html#_ftn141):

 

*“What is truly disturbing is not that the world turns into a complete domain of technology.*

*Far more disturbing is that man is not at all prepared for this radical change in the world.*

*Far more disturbing is that we are not yet able to reach, through a meditating thought, an adequate comparison with what is really emerging in our age.”*

 

 The large-scale adoption of one technology rather than another can take place on the basis of different mechanisms: it can be stimulated by the so-called network effect (see Glossary), it can be encouraged at a centralized level, for example by laws of state governments (see the case of El Salvador for Bitcoin), or it can even be discouraged at the community level, as in the case of the Amish or some indigenous peoples, who deliberately choose not to use some modern forms of technology.

 

Furthermore, a technology can spread regardless of whether it is the best on the market, as evidenced by some practical examples. An example is that of the layout of QWERTY keyboards, which became a standard at the time of analog typewriters instead of the Dvorak layout, designed to speed up typing in digital systems. In this case we enter the field of epistemic uncertainty: it is known that the Dvorak is better than the QWERTY, however it was decided to use the latter.

 

A similar thing could happen with the Blockchain, in which one standard (for example Bitcoin) could be preferred to another (for example Ethereum) although it may be technically inferior. Among other things, this is what happened with the TCP / IP protocol at the basis of the Internet: certainly not the best, but now a standard in all respects.

 

 

## 9.3 A true decentralization?

 

Of course, not all that glitters is gold and scenarios like the ones described also carry significant risks. One of the main ones would be the lack of centralized control bodies to ensure compliance with laws globally. But what if it is the controller who does not comply with the laws? This, as we saw in the introductory chapter in reference to the Snowden affair, has actually already occurred but it is only the tip of the iceberg.

 

To date, in fact, there is no certainty that this is not repeating itself or cannot occur again, regardless of the government that takes control of the information.

 

**The question we ask ourselves at this point is: better a centralized or a decentralized society?**

 

A decentralized corporate structure, meaning that it does not have central regulators such as a government (if not possibly DAOs) could help criminal organizations escape scrutiny. A concrete example to date is provided by the P2P file sharing network through which it is possible to exchange files subject to Copyright with very minimal risks. We have also seen that in the case of Second Life, scenarios of this type could also lead to the uncontrolled proliferation of inappropriate content, linked for example to the world of child pornography.

 

As pointed out by Silvio Micali, professor at MIT and founder of Algorand, in an interview with Marco Montemagno, the real problem in solving the centralization of Blockchains lies in the consensus algorithm. In fact, we have seen that the main PoW and PoS mechanisms actually centralize power, once again, in the hands of those who have more economic resources. In fact, in the PoW, the validation of the blocks is in the hands of the miners, to a greater extent than their available computing power, or the hardware in their possession. Similarly, in the PoS the power to validate blocks tends to focus on who has the largest stakes.

 

A true decentralized Blockchain will therefore have to guarantee a mechanism that gives the same possibility to anyone, regardless of their economic power, to add blocks to the chain, while guaranteeing the necessary security. Consequently, the system can only be considered secure if most of the users involved, or at least the users responsible for validating the blocks, are honest.

 

**The question can therefore be rephrased as follows: we would have greater confidence in being governed by a decentralized system, assuming that most of the people involved act honestly, or by a centralized society, in which a group of people take control?**

We could try accessories directly from home with a completely reliable experience, with no significant differences from the "real" reality.

 

We leave the reader to think about the countless scenarios that can arise and develop on the basis of these technologies.

It is difficult to predict what will happen and which directions we will take. What is certain is that we will see radical changes in the years to come that will also bring with them some great risks that must be managed, and at the same time great opportunities that must be grasped.

 

# Glossary

 

This section contains the definitions of some terms specific to the world of the Web3. It is therefore considered useful to provide the reader with this Glossary to allow them to approach the subject with greater ease.

 

**Algorithm**: strategy used to solve a problem and consists of a finite sequence of operations (also called instructions). An algorithm must be finite (consisting of a finite number of instructions), deterministic (starting from the same input data, the same output results are obtained), unambiguous (all must interpret the operations in the same way) and general (solution must be the same for all problems of the same class). The Blockchain, for example, is essentially based on cryptographic algorithms.

 

**Altcoin**: any type of cryptocurrency other than Bitcoin

 

**Fundamental analysis**: in finance, fundamental analysis evaluates the capital strength and profitability of a company, determining the intrinsic value (or fair value) of the company. It is often applied to companies listed on the stock exchange or in the process of listing, to underestimate the convenience or otherwise of a given investment. Fundamental analysis studies all the micro and macroeconomic events that have some impact on the society under consideration. It is therefore necessary to be able to have an overview of the markets, the sector in which the company operates, its business plan and its management, but above all it is necessary to have an in-depth knowledge of its financial statements, which is the primary tool valuation used in fundamental analysis.

 

**Technical Analysis (TA)**: in finance, technical analysis is the study of the price trend of financial markets over time, with the aim of predicting future trends, mainly using graphical and statistical methods. In a broad sense, it is that theory of analysis (or set of principles and tools) according to which it is possible to predict the future trend of the price of a listed asset (real or financial), by studying its past history. It is used, together with fundamental analysis, for the definition of financial operational decisions.

 

**Bear:** A bear is an investor who believes that a particular stock, or the broader market, is going down and may be attempting to profit from a decline in stock prices. Bears are typically pessimistic about the state of a particular underlying market or economy.

 

**Bitcoin**: first Cryptocurrency, created in 2009 by Satoshi Nakamoto.

 

**Bitcoin Maximalist**: Bitcoin maximalists believe that Bitcoin, which is the most popular Cryptocurrency in the world, is the only digital asset that will be needed in the future. Maximalists believe that all other digital currencies are inferior to Bitcoin.

 

**Block**: basic element of a Blockchain chain. Each block consists of three main elements: the data, the hash that identifies the block and the hash of the previous block.

 

**Blockchain**: a distributed database shared between the nodes of a computer network. As a database, a Blockchain stores information in digital format. The main features of a Blockchain are security, decentralization and scalability (see Blockchain trilemma).

 

**Bull**: a bull is an investor who thinks that the market, a specific stock or an industry is ready to grow. Investors taking a bullish approach buy stocks on the assumption that they can sell them later at a higher price.

 

**Bull run**: it means that there is an upward trend in the Cryptocurrency market (or shares or precious metals) for a prolonged period of time, which can also be defined as (bull market) or bull market.

 

**CFD**: a Contract For Difference (CFD) is a financial instrument, essentially a contract between two parties, typically described as "buyer" and "seller", which stipulates that the buyer will pay the seller the difference between the current value of an asset and the its value at the time of the contract.

 

**Coin:** see Cryptocurrency.

**Consensus (mechanism)**: it is a mechanism that is used in the Blockchain to obtain the necessary agreement on a single data value or a single state of the network between distributed processes. On the Bitcoin Blockchain, for example, the consensus mechanism is known as Proof-of-Work (PoW), which requires the exercise of computing power to solve a difficult but arbitrary puzzle in order to keep all network nodes honest.

 

**Corda**: it is an open-source project that allows you to design and develop Blockchains in an open development environment. With Blockchain technology and smart contracts, Corda can reduce registration costs by simplifying business operations and helping companies create interoperable blockchain networks or applications that transact directly.

 

**Cryptocurrency**: a digital or virtual currency protected by cryptography, which makes counterfeiting or double spending nearly impossible. Many cryptocurrencies are decentralized networks based on Blockchain technology. A distinctive feature of cryptocurrencies is that they are generally not issued by any central authority, which theoretically makes them immune to government interference or manipulation.

 

**Cryptography**: it is the study of secure communication techniques that allow only the sender and intended recipient of a message to view its content. In encryption, the data is encrypted using a secret key, then both the encrypted message and the secret key are sent to the recipient for decryption. Cryptographic algorithms are the basis of the blockchain.

 

**Cypherpunk**: it is any individual who advocates the widespread use of advanced cryptography and privacy-enhancing technologies as a gateway to social and political change. Cypherpunks have been engaged in an active movement since at least the late 1980s.

 

**DAO**: it stands for Decentralized Autonomous Organization and refers to a way of organizing and managing organizations, making use of smart contracts and Blockchain technology to provide them with transparency, immutability, autonomy and security. In a DAO, thousands of people autonomously decide the decisions to be made regarding a specific project, for which the organization was set up.

 

**DApp**: a decentralized application (DApp, dApp, Dapp or dapp) is a computer application that runs on a decentralized computer system. DApps have been popularized by distributed ledger technologies (DLT) such as the Ethereum through the use of smart contracts.

 

**Decentralized Finance**: see DeFi

 

**DeFi**: decentralized finance, or DeFi, is a system by which financial products become available on a public decentralized blockchain network. This makes them open for anyone to use, rather than going through intermediaries such as banks.

 

**Destructive innovation**: it is an innovation that creates a new market and a network of values and ends up killing an existing market and network of values. The term was first used in 1995 by the American scholar Clayton M. Christensen.

 

**Digital Wallet**: see Wallet.

 

**Distributed Ledger**: see DLT.

 

**DLT**: it stands for Distributed Ledger Technology. DLT is a system based on a distributed ledger, i.e., systems in which all the nodes of a network have the same copy of a database that can be read and modified independently by the individual nodes.

 

**Double-spending**: in the context of the PoW consensus mechanism, it represents the risk that a digital currency could be spent twice as a result of a 51% attack.

 

**ECDSA**: stands for Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm and is a cryptographic algorithm. The ECDSA algorithm is considered a secure standard for the implementation of digital signature systems. Their use today is so varied that they find application in almost all fields of information technology. For example, the Internet's SSL and TLS security certificate infrastructure makes heavy use of ECDSA.

 

**EIP-20:** the acronym EIP stands for Ethereum Improvement Proposal, which is a design document that provides information to the Ethereum community or that describes a new functionality for Ethereum or its processes or environment, while 20 is the progressive number of the document.

 

**Epistemic uncertainty**: the uncertainty that the information is unknown to the decision maker, although the facts are already established and knowable. This is in contrast to random uncertainty, where the facts are not yet established and are subject to a random stochastic process that will take place in the future.

**ERC-20**: an ERC-20 token is a Smart Contract that has a predetermined data structure. This facility is designed to facilitate the implementation of various functionalities on the Ethereum blockchain, making it easier for developers to create work. The acronym ERC stands for Ethereum Requests for Comments, while the number 20 comes from the EIP (Ethereum Improvement Proposal) in which it is described.

 

**Ether**: Ethereum token

 

**Ethereum**: it is a Blockchain platform with its own cryptocurrency, called Ether (ETH) and its own programming language, called Solidity. As a Blockchain network, Ethereum is a decentralized public ledger for verifying and recording transactions. Network users can create, publish, monetize and use applications on the platform and use its Ether cryptocurrency as a payment. Insiders call decentralized applications on the network "dApps".

 

**ETF**: it stands for English Exchange Traded Funds, they are special passively managed investment funds. Buying an ETF is like buying a basket of stocks. Investing in a fund is equivalent to putting your savings together with that of other investors, the fund manager will then buy the instruments on which you will invest with this money. The investment performance will be given by the result of all the individual instruments in which the fund invests.

 

**ETN**: it stands for Exchange-Traded Notes, these are financial instruments that differ from ETFs in that they are not funds and are issued against a direct investment by the issuing company, replicating the trend.

 

**Exchange**: platform where you can buy and trade cryptocurrencies and NFTs.

 

**Fear & Greed Index**: translated into Italian as an index of fear and greed, it was developed by CNNMoney to measure two of the primary emotions that influence how much investors are willing to pay for shares. The Fear and Greed Index is measured on a daily, weekly, monthly and yearly basis. In theory, the index can be used to assess whether the stock market is fairly priced. This is based on the logic that excessive fear tends to drive stock prices down, and too much greed tends to have the opposite effect.

 

**Fiat (currency)**: a fiat currency is a national currency not anchored to the price of a commodity such as gold or silver. The value of a fiat currency is largely linked to trust in the authority that issues it, usually a state or a central bank.

 

**Fork**: a fork occurs when a block is connected to 2 successive blocks. Forks are related to the fact that the parties must use common rules to keep the history of the Blockchain.

 

**Gas**: it refers to the commission, or price value, required to successfully conduct a transaction or execute a contract on the Ethereum Blockchain platform. Priced in small fractions of the cryptocurrency Ether (ETH), commonly referred to as gwei and sometimes also called nanoeth, gas is used to allocate the resources of the Ethereum virtual machine (EVM) so that decentralized applications such as smart contracts can self - to be carried out in a secure and decentralized manner. The exact price of gas is determined by supply and demand among network miners, who can refuse to process a transaction if the gas price does not reach their threshold, and network users looking for processing power.

 

**Halving**: it is an automated process of halving the Bitcoins that miners receive as a reward for creating a block. It was created to incentivize mining through Proof-of-Work. By doing this for every certain number of blocks the emission should be halved.

 

**Hash (function)**: the hash function produces a sequence of bits, called digest (or string) strictly correlated with the incoming data. The hash is used as a kind of fingerprint of each block of a Blockchain. In Bitcoin, identifying the hash of each block is an operation relegated to so-called miners.

 

**Hash rate**: is the speed with which a mathematical problem is solved. It is measured by the hash per second (H/s) parameter.

 

**Hype**: The English term hype means a great advertising launch or a sensationalist advertisement, accompanied therefore by great expectations, sometimes excessive. Hype often accompanies the rapid growth of new technology as a new opportunity. The Blockchain and in particular Cryptocurrencies are often subject to this phenomenon.

 

**ICO**: it stands for English Initial Coin Offering, it takes place during the launch of a new cryptocurrency and that often takes place in the form of crowdfunding, although a private ICO that does not seek public investments is also possible. In an ICO, a quantity of cryptocurrency is sold in the form of "tokens" ("coins") to speculators or investors, in exchange for legal tender (fiat) or other (generally established and more stable) cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin or Ether.

 

**IDO**: Initial DEX Offerings, or IDOs, are Tokens that represent any type of asset hosted on a decentralized exchange platform (DEX): an IDO occurs when a project launches a token through a decentralized liquidity exchange. IDOs offer companies a tool to engage their communities in an economy that enriches their products and services by enabling them to make smart business decisions about their assets.

 

**IEO**: An Initial Exchange Offering (IEO) involves the use of a centralized cryptocurrency exchange to raise funds for a new project.

 

**IPO**: from the English Initial Public Offering, is an offer to the public of the securities of a company that intends to be listed for the first time on a regulated market. It is the mechanism by which a company is listed on the stock exchange.

 

**Layer 2**: refers to a protocol built on the basis of an existing Blockchain, with the aim of solving some structural problems to the blockchain of origin. The main example for Bitcoin is the Bitcoin Lightning Network.

 

 **Market Cap**: stands for market capitalization and refers to the total dollar market value of a company's outstanding shares. It is calculated by multiplying the total number of outstanding shares of a company by the current market price of a share.

 

**Meme Coin**: a cryptocurrency that originates from an internet meme or has some humorous characteristics, with no apparent practical application. The most popular Meme Coins are probably "Doge Coin" and "Shiba Inu".

 

**Miners**: In the PoW mechanism, miners try to find the hashes of blocks in a chain by harnessing the computational power of their computer equipment and ultimately consuming electricity in exchange for Bitcoin (for example) as a reward. Ultimately, miners validate, authenticate, certify and finalize transactions through specific processes. Once miners have created a new block that is accepted by the community, the transaction record cannot be changed, making it permanent and irreversible information.

 

**Network effect**: it is the phenomenon whereby the value or utility that a user derives from a good or service depends on the number of users of compatible products. When there is a network effect, the value of a product or service increases with the number of other users.

 

**NFT**: Non-Fungible Token, in Italian non-fungible token. An NFT can be any object in digital format, such as a video, photo, GIF, text, article, or audio.

 

**Proof-of-stake (PoS)**: they are a class of consensus mechanisms for Blockchains that work by selecting validators in proportion to their number of holdings in the associated cryptocurrency.

 

**Proof-of-Work (PoW)**: it is a form of cryptographic proof in which one party (the prover) proves to the others (the verifiers) that a certain amount of a specific computational effort has been spent. Verifiers can subsequently confirm this expense with minimal effort.

 

**Pump and Dump**: is a stock scam that usually involves stocks or cryptocurrencies. Scammers create a false hype about a stock or new cryptocurrency to generate interest. Once investors start buying a new cryptocurrency, for example, its value increases. When the value reaches a certain point, the scammers behind the fake hype sell all their tokens, causing the price to plummet, leaving new investors with a worthless cryptocurrency (Shitcoin).

 

**Satoshi**: it is the smallest unit of Bitcoin. It is named after Satoshi Nakamoto, the creator of the protocol used in blockchains and bitcoin cryptocurrency. The ratio of satoshi to bitcoin is 100 million satoshi for one bitcoin (10 ^ -8 Bitcoin).

 

**Satoshi Nakamoto**: it is the name used by the alleged person or people who developed Bitcoin.

 

**Scalability**: it generally denotes the ability of a system to increase or decrease in scale according to needs and availability. Referring to the Blockchain, scalability is the ability of a blockchain to manage an increasing number of transactions without affecting performance (see Blockchain trilemma).

**Sentiment Indicator**: a sentiment indicator is designed to represent how a group feels about the market or the economy. These market psychology-based indicators attempt to quantify sentiment, in the form of figures or graphically, to predict how current beliefs and positions may affect future market behavior. Sentiment indicators look at how market players are bullish or bearish and what they think and feel, which can help predict future investor behavior. For example, when investors are extremely bearish, this is often a counter sign to sentiment indicator traders that market prices may start to rise soon.

 

**SHA-256**: SHA-2, Secure Hash Algorithm 2, is a set of cryptographic hash functions designed by the US National Security Agency (NSA) and first published in 2001. SHA-256 is a 256-bit hash function.

 

**Shitcoin**: in jargon, it is a cryptocurrency with little or no value or digital currency that has no immediate and recognizable purpose.

 

**Smart contract**: it is a transaction program or protocol that is intended to automatically execute, control or document legally relevant events and actions under the terms of a contract or agreement.

 

**Solidity**: is an object-oriented programming language for writing smart contracts. It was developed by Christian Reitwiessner, Alex Beregszaszi, and several former Ethereum key contributors to enable writing of smart contracts on blockchain platforms such as Ethereum. Programs compiled by Solidity are meant to run on the Ethereum Virtual Machine.

 

**Stablecoin**: identifies a particular type of cryptocurrency linked to a real financial product developed in order to compensate for the wide volatility typical of digital currencies, thereby providing greater stability. An example of a related financial asset is a fiat currency (for example the US dollar) or commodities with gold.

 

**Tangle**: used by the IOTA token, Tangle is a distributed ledger architecture (DLT) designed specifically for the Internet of Things (IoT) environment and which is based on an apparently similar approach to the blockchain.

 

**Token**: cryptographic tokens, or simply tokens, are a type of cryptocurrency that represents a specific asset or use and resides on its own blockchain. Tokens can be used for investment purposes, to store value or to make purchases.

 

**Tokenization**: process which consists in the transformation of a resource or an object within a Blockchain file. This transformation process consists in digitizing said object and bringing all its information on a block of a Blockchain. To be later exchanged or archived. 

 

**Trading**: it is a speculation activity on financial instruments with the aim of making a profit. Trading is mainly based on technical analysis, fundamental analysis and the application of a specific strategy to trading.

 

**Trading volumes**: Trading Volumes or Exchange Volumes are the number of transactions carried out during a certain period of time on a given financial instrument.

 

**Trilemma (Blockchain)**: The Blockchain Trilemma is a concept coined by Vitalik Buterin that proposes a set of three main issues - decentralization, security and scalability - that developers encounter when building blockchains, ultimately forcing them to sacrifice one "aspect" as a compromise to satisfy the other two.

 

**UX**: it stands for User Experience, or user experience, and defines the way in which a user interacts and experiences a product, system or service. It includes a person's perceptions of usefulness, ease of use, and efficiency.

 

**Volatility**: it is a measure of the percentage change in the price of a financial instrument over time. Cryptocurrencies are a highly volatile financial product, as the price change can vary significantly, especially in the short term. For this reason, these products are considered risky for those who want to trade.

 

**Wallet**: it is software or hardware designed exclusively to store and manage public keys and private keys of our cryptocurrencies and uses advanced blockchain encryption techniques to offer and guarantee total security to users. In this way, the wallet can save, send and receive cryptocurrencies without increased risk

**Whale (Bitcoin)**: A bitcoin whale refers to individuals or entities who hold large amounts of bitcoin. Whales hold enough cryptocurrency that they have the potential to manipulate currency valuations.
