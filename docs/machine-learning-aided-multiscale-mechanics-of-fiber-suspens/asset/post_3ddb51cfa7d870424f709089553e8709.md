
- The mapping of a first order tensor by a second order tensor \(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{a} = B_{ij}a_j \boldsymbol{e}_i\).
- The mapping of a second order tensor by a fourth order tensor \(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{B}] = C_{ijkl}B_{kl} \boldsymbol{e}_i \otimes \boldsymbol{e}_j\).
- The composition of two second order tensors \(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B} = A_{ij}B_{jk} \boldsymbol{e}_i \otimes \boldsymbol{e}_k\)
- The composition of two fourth tensors \(\mathbb{A}\mathbb{C} = A_{ijkl}C_{klmn} \boldsymbol{e}_i \otimes \boldsymbol{e}_j \otimes \boldsymbol{e}_m \otimes \boldsymbol{e}_n\).

Furthermore, we denote the transposition of a quantity with \((\cdot)^\mathsf{T}\), the tensor product with \(\otimes\), and the direct product with \(\times\). The operator \((\cdot)^{\otimes a}\) constructs a tensor space of \(a\)-th order, e.g., \(\mathbb{R}^{(\otimes 5)}\), and we define \((\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes 0} \equiv \mathbb{R}\) for all dimensions \(d \in \mathbb{N}_{>0}\). Additionally, we define the tensor product of a scalar \(a\) with a first order tensor \(\boldsymbol{v}\), such that \(a \otimes \boldsymbol{v} \equiv av_i \boldsymbol{e}_i\). Finally, we denote vectors and matrices via under-scores, e.g., \(\underline{a}\) and \(\underline{\underline{A}}\), respectively.

### 2.2 Kinematics

For a $d$-dimensional material body occupying the volume $Y_0 \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, let $\mathbf{X} \in Y_0$ denote the reference placement of the material points of the body for $d \in \{2, 3\}$ (Steinmann, 2015). Then, the continuously differentiable and invertible function

$$
\chi : Y_0 \times [0, T] \to \mathbb{R}^d, \quad (\mathbf{X}, t) \mapsto \chi(\mathbf{X}, t) \tag{2.1}
$$

describes the motion of the body using the reference placement \(\boldsymbol{X}\) and the time \(t \in [0, T]\) (Steinmann, 2015). Here, the time \(t\) and the furthest considered point in time \(T \in (0, \infty]\) live on the affinely extended real number line. The current placement \(\boldsymbol{x}\) is then defined as

$$
\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{\chi}(\boldsymbol{X}, t), \quad (2.2)
$$

from which the current placement \(Y_t\) of the body follows as (Steinmann, 2015)

$$
Y_t = \{ \boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{\chi}(\boldsymbol{X}, t) \mid \boldsymbol{X} \in Y_0 \text{ and } t \in [0, T] \}. \quad (2.3)
$$

Consequently, a tensor field \(\Lambda\) of order \(n \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge 0}\) on the material body can be described in terms of the reference placement \(Y_0\) (Haupt, 2002), such that

$$
\Lambda_{\mathrm{L}} : Y_0 \times [0, T] \rightarrow (\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes n}, \quad (2.4)
$$

as well as in terms of the current placement \(Y_t\), such that

$$
\Lambda_{\mathrm{E}} : Y_t \times [0, T] \rightarrow (\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes n}. \quad (2.5)
$$

Here, the parameterizations \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\) and \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{E}}\) represent the Lagrangian and the Eulerian description of the tensor field \(\Lambda\), respectively. Also, using the relations (Steinmann, 2015)

$$
\Lambda_{\mathrm{E}}(\boldsymbol{x}, t) = \Lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(\boldsymbol{\chi}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}, t), t) \quad \text{and} \quad \Lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(\boldsymbol{X}, t) = \Lambda_{\mathrm{E}}(\boldsymbol{\chi}(\boldsymbol{X}, t), t), \quad (2.6)
$$

one parameterization may be converted into the other. The temporal change of a tensor \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(\boldsymbol{X}, t)\) parameterized by the reference placement \(\boldsymbol{X}\) is defined through the material time derivative (Haupt, 2002)

$$
\dot{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{L}}(\boldsymbol{X}, t) = \frac{\partial \Lambda_{\mathrm{L}}}{\partial t}(\boldsymbol{X}, t). \quad (2.7)
$$

In terms of the current placement \(\boldsymbol{x}\), the material time derivative of a tensor \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{E}}(\boldsymbol{x}, t)\) is given by (Haupt, 2002)

\(\dot{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{E}}(\boldsymbol{x}, t) = \frac{\partial \Lambda_{\mathrm{E}}}{\partial t}(\boldsymbol{x}, t) + \frac{\partial \Lambda_{\mathrm{E}}}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{x}, t) \cdot \boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{E}}(\boldsymbol{x}, t),\quad (2.8)\)

where the Eulerian velocity \(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{E}}(\boldsymbol{x}, t)\) is related to the Lagrangian velocity

\(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{L}}(\boldsymbol{X}, t) = \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\chi}}{\partial t}(\boldsymbol{X}, t)\quad (2.9)\)

through equations (2.6). In the following, we omit the explicit declaration of Eulerian and Lagrangian quantities, and instead define the parameterization through the arguments of the discussed quantities. Eulerian quantities are defined through the argument \(\boldsymbol{x}\) and Lagrangian quantities are defined through the argument \(\boldsymbol{X}\). To capture local changes in the motion of the material body's points, we use the first spatial derivative of the motion \(\boldsymbol{\chi}\), defined as the deformation gradient field \(\boldsymbol{F}\) (Steinmann, 2015)

\(\boldsymbol{F}: Y_0 \times [0, T] \rightarrow (\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes 2},\quad (\boldsymbol{X}, t) \mapsto \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\chi}}{\partial \boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{X}, t).\quad (2.10)\)

Abusing notation and setting \(\boldsymbol{F} = \boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{X}, t)\), the relations (Steinmann, 2015)

\(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{F} \, \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{X},\quad \mathrm{d}a = \det(\boldsymbol{F})\boldsymbol{F}^{-\top} \mathrm{d}A,\quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{d}v = \det(\boldsymbol{F}) \, \mathrm{d}V,\quad (2.11)\)

describe the mapping of the infinitesimal line \(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{X}\), the infinitesimal area \(\mathrm{d}A\), and the infinitesimal volume \(\mathrm{d}V\) from the reference placement \(Y_0\) to the current placement \(Y_t\). We may split a deformation gradient \(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{X}, t)\) into the symmetric positive definite tensors \(\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{X}, t) \in \text{Sym}^+(d)\) and \(\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}, t) \in \text{Sym}^+(d)\), as well as a proper orthogonal tensor \(\boldsymbol{R}(\boldsymbol{X}, t) \in SO(d)\), such that (Steinmann, 2015)

\(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{X}, t) = \boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{X}, t)\boldsymbol{R}(\boldsymbol{X}, t) = \boldsymbol{R}(\boldsymbol{X}, t)\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}, t).\quad (2.12)\)

Here Sym$^+(d)$ denotes the space of symmetric and positive definite second order tensors of dimension $d$, and $SO(d)$ stands for the special orthogonal group of dimension $d$ (Steinmann, 2015). The left and right stretch tensors $\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{X}, t)$ and $\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X}, t)$ encode the local stretch, and the tensor $\boldsymbol{R}(\boldsymbol{X}, t)$ encodes the local rotation. For improved readability, we omit the dependence of the tensors $\boldsymbol{F}, \boldsymbol{U}, \boldsymbol{V}$, and $\boldsymbol{R}$ on space and time in the following. The deformation gradient $\boldsymbol{F}$ may be used to express the velocity gradient $\boldsymbol{L} \in (\mathbb{R}^d)^{\otimes 2}$ as (Haupt, 2002)

$$
\boldsymbol{L} = \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x}, t)}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}} = \dot{\boldsymbol{F}} \boldsymbol{F}^{-1} \tag{2.13}
$$

which may be decomposed into the symmetric part $\boldsymbol{D} = (\boldsymbol{L} + \boldsymbol{L}^\mathsf{T})/2$ and the skew-symmetric part $\boldsymbol{W} = (\boldsymbol{L} - \boldsymbol{L}^\mathsf{T})/2$, such that

$$
\boldsymbol{L} = \boldsymbol{D} + \boldsymbol{W}, \quad \text{and} \quad \boldsymbol{D} \cdot \boldsymbol{W} = 0 \tag{2.14}
$$

The strain rate tensor $\boldsymbol{D}$ encodes the rate of shearing and stretching, and the spin tensor $\boldsymbol{W}$ encodes the rate of rotation.
