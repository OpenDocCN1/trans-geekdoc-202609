The elongational and dilatational viscosity then contain the information of 15 and 6 components of the suspension viscosity \(\underline{\underline{V}}(\dot{\gamma}, c_F)\), respectively. However, due to incompressibility, all information contained in the suspension viscosity \(\underline{\underline{V}}(\dot{\gamma}, c_F)\) is completely and uniquely encoded via the elongational viscosity \(\eta_{app}(\dot{\gamma}, c_F, \boldsymbol{d})\) (He and Curnier, 1995, Sec. 4.3). This holds in both the Newtonian and Cross-type case. Thus, in the following, we use the elongational viscosity \(\eta_{app}(\dot{\gamma}, c_F)\) as a tool to completely visualize and qualitatively understand the suspension viscosity \(\underline{\underline{V}}(\dot{\gamma}, c_F)\).

In Figures 3.7 and 3.8, we observe that for both the Newtonian and Cross-type case, the elongational viscosity \(\eta_\text{app}\) is large when the direction \(\boldsymbol{d}\) is aligned with the principal fiber orientation axis. This is expected, since flow resistance along the cylindrical fibers is higher than in other flow directions. Furthermore, the elongational viscosity \(\eta_\text{app}\) for both cases increases with growing fiber volume fraction \(c_\text{F}\) in all directions \(\boldsymbol{d}\). ### 3.4.2 Polar representation and material anisotropy

While spatial representations of the elongational viscosity \(\eta_\text{app}\) provide intuition and qualitative understanding of the underlying viscosity matrix \(\overline{\underline{V}}\), cuts through the viscosity bodies shown in the previous section allow for a more focused discussion of the occurring effects. As noted in the previous section, the fiber volume fraction \(c_\text{F}\) has a strong influence on the suspension viscosity \(\overline{\underline{V}}\). Because the matrix \(\overline{\underline{V}}\) should be transversely isotropic, see section 3.2.5, we will discuss the effect of the fiber volume fraction \(c_\text{F}\) and the shear rate \(\dot{\gamma}\) on the suspension viscosity \(\overline{\underline{V}}\) by focusing on the elongational viscosities \(\eta_\text{app}^x\) and \(\eta_\text{app}^y\) in \(x\) and \(y\) direction. In section 3.4.1 we noted that the elongational viscosity \(\eta_\text{app}\) increases with the fiber volume fraction \(c_\text{F}\) in all directions \(\boldsymbol{d}\). Additionally, as shown in Figures 3.9 and 3.10, the changes of the elongational viscosities \(\eta_\text{app}^y\) and \(\eta_\text{app}^x\) with the fiber volume fraction \(c_\text{F}\) increase with the fiber volume fraction \(c_\text{F}\) itself. In the Newtonian case, the viscosity \(\eta_{app}^y\) increases from 530 Pa s to 1581 Pa s as the fiber volume fraction \(c_\mathrm{F}\) changes from 5% to 25%. In contrast, the elongational viscosity \(\eta_{app}^x\) increases from 769 Pa s to 3468 Pa s as the fiber volume fraction \(c_\mathrm{F}\) increases from 5% to 25%. Thus, the elongational viscosities \(\eta_{app}^y\) and \(\eta_{app}^x\) increase by 198% and 351%, respectively.

In contrast, the elongational viscosity \(\eta_{app}^x\) decreases from 759 Pa s to 43 Pa s as the shear rate \(\dot{\gamma}\) changes from \(10 s^{-1}\) to \(10^5 s^{-1}\). Hence, both elongational viscosities \(\eta_{app}^y\) and \(\eta_{app}^x\) decrease by the same factor of around 94%. However, for a lower shear rate \(\dot{\gamma} = 10^3 s^{-1}\), the elongational viscosities \(\eta_{app}^y\) and \(\eta_{app}^x\) decrease differently by the factors 57% and 62%, respectively. We observe a similar change for the higher fiber volume fraction \(c_F = 25\%\). In this case, the elongational viscosities \(\eta_{app}^y\) and \(\eta_{app}^x\) also decrease by around 94% as the shear rate \(\dot{\gamma}\) changes from \(10 s^{-1}\) to \(10^5 s^{-1}\). Again, for a lower shear rate \(\dot{\gamma} = 10^3 s^{-1}\), the elongational viscosities \(\eta_{app}^y\) and \(\eta_{app}^x\) decrease differently by the factors 75% and 78%, respectively. As a result, we conclude that for shear rates \(\dot{\gamma}\) where the matrix behavior is non-Newtonian the elongational viscosities \(\eta_{app}^x\) and \(\eta_{app}^y\) vary stronger with the shear rate for higher fiber volume fraction \(c_F\). Also, since the elongational viscosities \(\eta_{app}^y\) and \(\eta_{app}^x\) change by different factors in both the Newtonian and Cross-type case for a shear rate \(\dot{\gamma} = 10^3 s^{-1}\), the material anisotropy changes with varying fiber volume fraction \(c_F\) and shear rate \(\dot{\gamma}\), see Figure 3.11. We discuss these changes in the following.

For the transversely isotropic symmetry of the suspension viscosity \(\overline{\underline{V}}\), its anisotropy is characterized by the elongational viscosities \(\eta_{app}^x\) and \(\eta_{app}^y\). For further investigation we also use a third elongational viscosity \(\eta_{app}^z\) in \(z\) direction. Each parameter represents the value of the elongational viscosity \(\eta_{app}\) for an elongation direction \(\boldsymbol{d}\) in \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-direction, respectively. Thus, the fractions

\(f_1 = \frac{\eta_{app}^x}{\eta_{app}^y}, \quad \text{and} \quad f_2 = \frac{\eta_{app}^x}{\eta_{app}^z} \tag{3.55}\)

serve as a measure of directional anisotropy. For a theoretical, perfectly transversely isotropic suspension viscosity, both fractions \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) assume the same value.
