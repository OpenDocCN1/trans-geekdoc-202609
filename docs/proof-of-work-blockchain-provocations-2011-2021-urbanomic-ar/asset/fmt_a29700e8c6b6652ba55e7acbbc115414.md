
## RM

In my early writings and art, I don’t think I started out with that, I started with very simple little things which did one thing that art did, or that the art world wanted or did. They were all very much following this strategy of disobedience-in-obedience. You know, Artists need a catalogue raisonné. Here you go, here’s one in the blockchain. No, not like that! I didn’t move on to the decoding/recoding side of it until NFTs became a thing, at which point I could contrast what I had been imagining as a blockchain art world in 2014 with what the actually existing blockchain art world of 2017 had become. And it did not go in the direction I had imagined, but it was a really interesting direction. The reason I didn’t anticipate that direction was precisely because I was being anti-commercial for political reasons.

The point at which the delamination becomes entirely explicit in my work is when I get to [Certificate of Inauthenticity](#82-Certificate-of-inauthenticity.xhtml) (2020), which is obviously a negative assertion: This is not an authentic artwork by me. As Michael Jackson said, The kid is not my son. But I only really went for the delamination idea when genius Twitter users started pointing out that you can just right-click on and save the JPEG associated with an NFT, and that this means you don’t own the NFT. It’s absolutely wrong, in a really interesting way. Let’s just imagine for a moment that we destroyed the artwork as property, by placing it into the copyright public domain—and we can do that with `CC0` at this point—then you have delaminated possession from ownership, patronage, and reputation from exploitation; you have delaminated use and exchange value. Anyone can use the image, nobody can sell it. You can sell the thing which represents its social cachet or its reputational proxy.

## RM

Authenticity is tied to the problem of the fake. A fake is a cuckoo in the nest, it’s a double-spending of something that should only exist once. And it’s absolutely key to the commodity property regime, because if we take the story of the history of art, what we understand as the history of art at face value, you either look at a nice aesthetic work and it is good or you look at a nice aesthetic work and you don’t like it. Notice the difference. But if a fake has that effect on me, then we’ve introduced something from outside the canvas—and then the happy world of either aesthetics if it’s the 1950s, or signification if we’re in the postmodern and contemporary era, starts to look like a just-so story. The fact that whether something is fake or authentic can change its value from nothing to millions…it tends to focus the attention.

I think the Warhol authentication committee, for example, have stopped stamping works because you can’t authenticate a Warhol. He got his friends to make them, he gave them out as party favours, he would happily write ‘This is not an authentic Warhol’ on the back if you asked him to while he was signing your copy of Interview. It’s a category error in the case of Warhols. And people have these double-denied stamps on the back of these screen prints that Warhol gave them. As I already mentioned, this investment, this sort of fatherly ownership of offspring that can suddenly not be who you think they are, this anxiety of ownership, this anxiety of authenticity, maps from the double-spending problem in blockchain to the problem of the fake in art to the problem of patriarchal lineage in anthropology to the problem of wage labour in capitalist society—because you are doing this thing and you’re not getting the benefit of it, the benefit is going to someone else. You’re not getting what you think you should be getting.

The fake is a modern problem which belongs to a certain property regime. If you were a priest commissioning a panel for your church, someone turned up, they painted it, you were perfectly happy with it (unless they were a heretic, in which case you’re in big trouble). It’s only once you get the market operation of art that you know, and need to know, where the art has come from: you have paid for the thing, and if you find out that you didn’t get what you paid for then you are perturbed; if you find out that you are not selling what you thought you were selling, that’s even worse; and the promise of the surplus value affordance of the public ledger is that you know where the art came from, you know who’s had it, you know how much it was sold for, you know where it is.

Now, anyone within crypto discourse can immediately tell you all the problems with that—like the fact that you can register the Mona Lisa as an artwork that you have made on the blockchain. That’s simply illustrating what crypto theorists could have told you long ago had you been listening. It’s not ‘owning the crypto bros’, it’s being late to the party. It’s the problem of getting offchain truth onchain that undoes the dream of a more transparent fake-free market for art as an absolute.

There are two potential ways to approach this: firstly you can go for the oracle problem, the problem of getting offchain truth onchain in various ways that allow you to establish, with however much certainty you desire, that the person who made the artwork is the person who made the artwork and the person who bought it is the person who bought it, and that it’s not anywhere else. The NFT then becomes like a useful publication medium and a useful medium of representation and exchange but it becomes sort of a means to an end for these lofty ideals for blockchain art. And there’s nothing wrong with that.

But the other way you can go is to lean into not knowing who your father is, and just say: look, the idea that you look at something and like it or not is absolutely correct. We’re going for pure cypherpunk anonymity. Purely onchain work. If you like this bunch of pixels and want to pay me £10 in Bitcoin for it, that’s great; if you want to give me £1m that’s great; you don’t know who I am, you don’t know who made it, it’s entitled Turd at Sunrise or whatever, you find that amusing. You’re paying for the work not the meatspace provenance, so if you overpay then that’s on you. In theory at least.

## MBK

These are the effects on art of turning the dials on Satoshi’s assemblage, tuning the parameters to produce different situations. The interesting thing is that a certain contingent of people get angry about whatever model you suggest. They get angry about people buying NFTs just because they saw something they like, but they also get angry about the idea that NFTs can be real carriers of value. One way of making NFTs interesting is to ensure that they activate the fears that produce that anger.

## RM

People do get upset when artists just mint something. If it’s bad art then that’s perfectly valid. I’m going to grit my teeth here and say that what Damien Hirst attempted recently was good: he did an NFT drop, you could own the first Damien Hirst NFTs; he then said that, after a certain amount of time, you can burn the token and he will send you an actual little drawing or print or whatever. That’s a use of the technology to get people to work through the game theory of value. Which is far more interesting to me than anything that was hefted off of the wreck of the Unbelievable, it’s the most interesting thing he’s done since he positioned a shark carefully, because it leans into the game-theoretic and value-questioning underpinnings of technology in a way that needs someone with that reputation to give it weight, to make it a serious proposition.

You do get people who just go to content honeypots like DeviantArt, grab a load of pictures and use free minting on rare art platforms to see who will buy it. That is bad, but it’s an attack on both the NFT community and the illustration art community, it’s not the NFT community attacking the illustration art community.
