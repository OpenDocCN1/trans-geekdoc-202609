
## 对话记录

**RM**  Yes, I forget who said it, but if you point your finger at the moon, the dog just looks at your finger. There is a lot of this in the reception of Conceptual Art. And with institutional critique, once institutions worked out that they could have the aesthetic of critique with an ethic of affirmation—because institutional critique affirms these sites as powerful—Yeah, tell me more about how awful and powerful I am, it’s terrible, stop, no stop, no go on…—that historical moment gives way to a candy shell of critique with no actual payload.

But Conceptual Art does also critique the ideology of art criticism at the time of the emergence of information technology. Upon arriving in Vancouver in 2013 I discovered N.E. Thing Co., some Vancouverites who had a teletype in the late 1960s and would spam companies with artworks via the telex network. They sent out the first networked call for a networked art show over the teletype network around 1970. This engagement with what now we don’t see as information technology but which absolutely was at the time—the typewriter, the teletype, the photostat, the photocopier, microfiches, hot metal type printing, whatever—sought to problematise both that information technology and the idea of a purely visual unproblematic aesthetic art of pure sensation which you can consume without remainder or problem upon walking into the white cube.

Of course, institutions love site-specific institutional critique now. The horror of that kind of recuperation is something I priced in from the start. But if we think back to that landmark Haacke show [Shapolsky et al., 1971], I’m absolutely ripping off that technique of saying to the art world, You may actually want to think about where your money comes from or about your aesthetic of democracy and the problems underlying it, while also saying to the people producing blockchain technology, What if this works? What if this works and it has consequences, should we maybe look at those in an arena which doesn’t risk starving or freezing people or preventing them from getting their meds? Can we make use of the realm of ideas, of art, play there a while, and learn what we should not do before it has an actual impact? Treating it as a plastic medium for an art that is meant to be interrogative and uncomfortable both for the art world and for the blockchain technology world, that’s a strategy taken very directly from Conceptual Art.

So yeah, it’s focusing on an emerging technology which provides an attentional figure to the ideological and political and economic grounds that people are then able to see it against.

With Conceptualism, you had the first generation of artists who had modern art-school, degree-style training. It was the Cold War, it was consumer society, Greenberg had run out of steam but no one was really replacing him. Smearing some colours on a canvas just didn’t seem up to it. The work of art could no longer do the work of art. So either you stop doing the work of art but continue making art, or you stop making art and continue doing the work of art. Art & Language and a few others chose to just keep going without a map, but with the intuition that it was politically or socially important to keep going. This led to either intentionally or unintentionally market-resistant forms of art. Art & Language talk with great amusement about galleries that would say they wanted to show their work, and would then end up sticking a copy of Art-Language up in a frame. An understandable mistake, but no. And then for their own internal pressures, needing to organise the conversations into the indexing projects, and then later on in the eighties needing to feed their families and having to come up with a sustained project of painting. But that initial ‘We will not be constrained by the history of art because we wish to continue the history of art’ is a fascinating move, and it is very much a move of freedom however one looks at it. This exit from the rump of the post-Greenbergian art world was the creation of an imaginative world in which they could keep alive the possibility of resistance. That’s different from the dreamwork that I criticise, which is that of people simply resolving the psychological tensions of working against what they claim to work for every hour of every day in their day job.

[Certificate of Inauthenticity](#82-Certificate-of-inauthenticity.xhtml) (2020) was originally a Sol LeWitt certificate of authenticity and some Tipp-Ex. LeWitt’s certificates took unsaleable wall drawings and turned them into very saleable contracts and signed certificates. I took this aesthetic and conceptual reference and used it to capture what I felt was going on with the anxiety of authenticity in the actually existing blockchain art world at that time. That piece and [Tokens Equals Text](#76-Tokens-equals-text.xhtml) I’m fantastically happy with in terms of the way they draw in their references and compose them in order to be usefully attention-directing and critical, not in the sense of self-aggrandising gestures of dismissal but in the sense of, we need to understand x, extend y, and head off z. The ability to do that, to the extent that I can, comes from working through that model of exit, of not being constrained by the expectation of exhibitability or saleability or any of the other things which I would be much richer had I been concerned with from day one.

**MBK**  Ultimately, then, are you happy with being described as a conceptual artist?

**RM**  Kind of, yes. As Art & Language say, ‘Whatever the destiny of Conceptual Art was, it wasn’t Conceptual Art’. I feel quite awkward when people say that I’m a ‘blockchain conceptual artist’. I needed this sort of visual and technical and art-historical and cultural resource of signification and association in order to communicate something which, if I’d have come up with a novel form of representation for it, I’d have had three strikes against the resulting art: a technology that nobody really needs to understand, an ideology that nobody needs to understand, and a critique or consideration of these in a representational media that nobody understands. At that point I’m just screaming into the void. So what I do does look like extremely late postmodernism or extremely late conceptualism or, in the worst case, extremely late pop, but it’s more to do with a sort of cultural smog of the internet-era shift from successive movements to parallel genres, where everyone has the history of culture available at their fingertips, and therefore I can get a leg up by using people’s knowledge to put out signals that will make them look at something. It isn’t first-order Conceptual Art, just as Art & Language’s work already wasn’t. It does the Conceptual Art thing as part of its operation because that is the most effective way of engaging with what I wanted to engage with.
