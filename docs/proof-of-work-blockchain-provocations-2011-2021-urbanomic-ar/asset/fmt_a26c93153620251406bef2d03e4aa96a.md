
And if we look at those levers and dials again, we can get rid of free or just-in-time minting but that reduces accessibility and inclusion, so it has a social justice effect. Or we can maintain free minting, which includes more people who otherwise would not be able to afford to start with this stuff, but reduces the cost of double-spending—it precisely works against Satoshi’s original use of the hashcash algorithm, which was designed to stop email spam, to make it too expensive to be worthwhile. So once again, this is the fine tuning of the parameters to achieve different goals which people regard as just but which, if you change them without due care and attention and hope to have a nice subtle effect, may disappoint you or overexcite things in the wrong way.

**MBK** In relation to the actually existing world of NFT art, it seems as if there are two possible levels of appreciation, which are slightly at odds with one another, and both of which you bring into play in your work. There’s an appreciation of individual works that have a critical purchase on the medium, but then, in ['Welcome to the Dessert of the Real'](#44-Welcome-to-the.xhtml), you argue that the aesthetics of the majority of what I’m going to uncharitably call landfill NFTs can be seen as the product of the affordances of the collision between human perceptual response patterns and the technological platform. In that case, from a population-scale point of view, the NFT phenomenon as a whole tells us something critical about the platform and about art; it becomes its own critique, demanding to be read and interpreted.

**RM** Yes. My kink is obviously critical art. That is not for everyone, and I came to that position from liking pop art, graphic design, airbrushed painting, whatever. So I am desperate not to be a snob, or a ‘gatekeeper’, in current crypto art terms, and not to miss what is interesting and new and valuable in actually existing NFTs by sitting around pontificating about the ideals of mid-twentieth-century critical theory, now some seventy years old. I have had some very long conversations with very competent art historians about what could be interesting about 10k PFP projects, for example, and for me there are three things: firstly they’re all very anti-aesthetic; they all have anti-aesthetic aesthetics. Secondly, there’s a sort of all-overness to the collection and this is enabled by the way that you see them on their sites. And finally, they get used as ritual masks. These are interesting aesthetic and anthropological and art-historical things which my distaste for apes looking to one side must not prevent me from being able to examine. I’m not going to be a snob about this, I’m going to look at those things. It ties back to creativity theory: if you retain a certain naivety, you will look at things that people who have to be terribly secure in their sophistication will not pay attention to, and because of that they will not be able to jump between the tracks of ideas that they need to in order to get to the very critique they claim to be interested in.

There were all the NFTs in which lots of people were just stealing motion graphic demos off YouTube, which is bad, but at the same time, a lot of very talented illustration artists and 3d modelling artists and motion graphics artists suddenly had a source of revenue because they could represent their work as NFTs. What did the audience for these go for? It’s difficult to categorise it but in ['Dessert of the Real'](#44-Welcome-to-the.xhtml) I try to. I’d tie it to the idea of something ownable, something that, like a bower bird seeking a mate, signals that it has what you want. And what the buyers want is digitally expanded ownability. There’s a trivial sense in which any NFT is ownable, but it needs to look like it, like an expensive watch that has lots of jewels on it because the jewels tell you it’s an expensive watch, despite the fact that the actual expensiveness is in the very finely tuned mechanism. By symbolising or communicating or depicting quality, whether gold or heft and solidity or skill or unusualness, this art does this. And I think that if you ran some sort of cultural analytics software you would find that the art that people have gone to has this kind of overt ownability to it.

**MBK** It’s a mimetic lure that tacitly responds to the problems of value that the platform creates?

**RM** Absolutely, yes. And not just the problems of value that it creates, the problem of how to hook yourself up to those signals, of understanding or being able to see that value.

**MBK** And we’re back to property regimes being generative.

**RM** The other thing that interested me was that they all tended to be short animated loops, which for me irresistibly reflects blockchain time, but I think in reality it gives the buyer more to own, because it adds duration and therefore extra substance to the things you’re looking at. It renders it, not kitsch necessarily, but certainly unproblematic, by smoothly looping it. So you can consume it unproblematically, but the thing it does do in a critical way is to signify this value. It’s a peak shift stimulus, it’s a aesthetic value sex toy or fishing lure, it takes it all to 11.

**MBK** You could probably talk about that in quasi-Darwinian terms, because I assume the first rash of NFTs were more varied, before makers homed in on how to put the ‘right’ signals out there.

**RM** There’s definitely a fitness function at work.

**MBK** In the approach to aesthetics you present in many of the essays in this book, and which is embodied in some of your work, you lean right into this: Why don’t we just produce a market for aesthetic elements or qualities, and see what wins? Like a techno-anarcho-capitalist Koons: not only allow people to like what they like and celebrate it, but use technology and the market to reveal what they really like rather than believe their stated preferences.

**RM** If we accept the idea that markets are epistemological environments, that they are truth procedures but in which the truth is that of price, then yeah, the truth has to be extracted from a population. Something which has never been sold is difficult to price, something which is singular is difficult to price, something which is sold infrequently is difficult to price. Price is meaning, it’s one-dimensional meaning, it’s meaning collapsed into one dimension in a dark forest strike. But it’s all the more substantial for it.

**MBK** And is that compatible with critique?

**RM** For critique, there cannot be, there should not be, and there is not any secure external position from which we can point at the object of critique. If we believe we occupy such a position, that way lies very bad things. It’s very much that we have to build the plane while we’re flying it, or we are jumping between icebergs as they head towards the waterfall or whatever. There is no fixed point from which we can move the world by virtue of adopting an aesthetic or even an ethic of critique. Critique is not a solved problem. And the worst pseudocritic is the person who has an answer for everything, and it’s the last idea they ever need to know. What’s this? I don’t know but it’s fascism.
