
# Art and Money

Art and money have always been involved in each other’s production. This Greek Drachma features a relief depiction of a sea turtle on one side. For many people this would have been the artwork, or at least the image, that they saw most frequently in their everyday lives.

![](image/drachma.png)

*Greek Drachma, 600BC.*

![](image/3524-3508.jpg)

*Damien Hirst, For the Love of God, 2007. Platinum, diamonds and human teeth. © Damien Hirst and Science Ltd. All rights reserved, DACS / Artimage 2022. Photo: Prudence Cuming Associates Ltd.*

In the present day, high art and high finance (or big art and big finance) go hand in hand. Blue chip artworks produced by brand-name artists such as Jeff Koons are collected by hedge fund managers and oil oligarchs as investments and as signifiers of socioeconomic position (while stolen Old Master paintings are used as signifiers of value in transactions between criminal gangs…). This tendency has reached its logical conclusion (for now) with Damien Hirst’s *For the Love of God* (2007), a diamond-encrusted platinum cast of an actual human skull complete with the original teeth. It was sold for fifty million pounds sterling. But looking inside the sale of *For the Love of God* makes its narrative less straightforward. It was sold to a group including the artist and their dealer, making the actual figure and its ownership less straightforward than a simple sale would suggest.

![](image/141015_myers-Conceptual_art_cryptocurrency_and_beyond-04.jpg)

*Nanex HFT Visualization.*

Nanex’s High Frequency Trading visualisations (example shown here from 2010) look inside transactions in electronic stocks and shares markets, finding aesthetic forms in the activity of share-trading bots. What the sawtooth waves of this particular bot’s activity represent is unknown: a glitch, a strategy, a side-effect. But without making these forms visible, we would not be able to ask these questions or reflect on this economic activity.

## Art, Particularly Conceptual Art, Critiques this Relationship

It is part of the value of art, particularly Conceptual Art, that it can afford us these opportunities for reflection and critique. Cildo Meireles’s *Insertions into Ideological Circuits 2* (1970) overwrites the contemporary equivalent of the Drachma’s turtle with a rubberstamped message on a banknote, intruding into the everyday use and circulation of currency in order to give its audience pause for critical reflection.

Lynn Hershman’s *Roberta Breitmore Blank Check* (1974) is signed by her artistic alter ego Roberta Britmore, using financial transactions and their attendant contracts as a producer and guarantor of identity, literally underwriting it.

![](image/Cildo_Meireles_Insertions_into_Ideological_Circuits.jpg)

*Cildo Meireles, Insertions into Ideological Circuits: Banknote Project, 1970, ink on banknote, 2.6 x 6.25 inches (6.7 x 15.9 cm) © Cildo Meireles; courtesy Galerie Lelong & Co., New York.*

![](image/hershman.png)

*Lynn Hershman, Roberta Breitmore Blank Check, 1974. Offset lithography and letterpress with black marker on paper, 3 x 6 inches (7.62 x 15.24 cm). © Lynn Hershman; courtesy the artist.*

Douglas Huebler’s *Variable Piece no. 44* (1971) incorporates into itself an image of its owner each year for its first decade, in an analogue precedent for Bruce Sterling’s idea of ‘spimes’.[^1] When the artwork is sold, a new owner appears, making the artwork’s contingent economics its aesthetic subject.

![](image/P07234_H_1.png)

*Douglas Huebler, Variable Piece No. 44, 1971. Photographs and printed text on board, 457 × 613 mm. Purchased from the artist by Tate (Grant-in-Aid), 1974. © Douglas Huebler. Photo: Tate.*

The initial critique of the ontology and economics of art that Conceptual Art represented in its ‘dematerialisation’ phase represented as much of a challenge to the livelihoods of artists as it did to its chosen targets. One solution found early on was to produce certificates of authenticity or ownership for otherwise un-ownable art. This reappropriates Conceptual Art for scarcity economics and as property, returning it to the market. Sol LeWitt’s certificates for two wall drawings (1980) demonstrate how this works. If you own such a certificate and I do not, and we both follow the instructions on the certificate, you produce an authentic LeWitt and I at best produce a forgery.

![](image/T01766_10.jpg)

*Sol LeWitt, A Wall Divided Vertically into Fifteen Equal Parts, Each with a Different Line Direction and Colour, and All Combinations. Diagram and certificate, 1970. © ARS, NY and DACS, London 2022. Photo: Tate.*

![](image/141015_myers-Conceptual_art_cryptocurrency_and_beyond-09.jpg)

*Art & Language, Guaranteed Painting, 1967–68. Acrylic on canvas and photostat, 2 parts, canvas 31 × 31 cm, framed text 55 × 44 cm. © ART & LANGUAGE; Courtesy the Artists and Mulier Mulier Gallery, Belgium.*

This strategy was criticised (and parodied) within the Conceptual Art movement itself. An early Art & Language artwork, *Guaranteed Painting* (1967) contains a printed certificate guaranteeing that the painting accompanying it contains particular content and addressing the curator of the show it appears in as someone who can possibly intervene in art-world economic relationships.

![](image/141015_myers-Conceptual_art_cryptocurrency_and_beyond-10.png)

*Carey Young, Declared Void, 2005. Vinyl drawing and text, dimensions variable, 133 × 133 inches (337.8 × 337.8 cm) as installed. Vinyl band: 6 inches (15.2 cm.); letters: 6 1/4 inches (15.8 cm) high. Installation view: Carey Young, Consideration at Midway Contemporary Art, Minneapolis, 17 March–5 May 2007. © Carey Young; Courtesy Paula Cooper Gallery, New York. Photo: Cameron Wittig.*

Carey Young’s *Declared Void* (2005) is a wall drawing that creates a space in which its audience enters into a contract agreeing that the constitution of the United States does not apply while they are within its bounds. Legal form as sculptural form, this is no longer about the relationship between art and money, but rather about the relationship between the individual, contract law, and the state. This is the kind of relationship that produces money, or at least fiat currency, and serves as a broader context for considering the more specific relationship between art and money.

![](image/141015_myers-Conceptual_art_cryptocurrency_and_beyond-11.png)

*Monochrom and Ian Grigg, Flower Currency, 2006.*

The flower currency produced by a group of Viennese artists in 2005 is both a LETS-style complementary currency and a use of the aesthetics of pressed flowers to allegorise and aestheticise the relationship between nature, production, and value in economies.

![](image/Phelps-FINALArtwork_05.png)

*Danica Phelps, #210 from Income’s Outcome, 2013. Pencil, watercolour and gouache on paper on wood.*

#210 from Danica Phelps’s series *Income’s Outcome* (2013) shows the artist’s expenditure on grocery shopping. If it depicted income rather than outcome, the stripes would be green rather than red. Phelps’s work combines the ledger of their economic existence with the artistic record of their social presence.

## Bitcoin as Critique

Bitcoin emerged as a critique of state-issued ‘fiat’ currency following the financial crisis of 2008. Bitcoin is a cryptocurrency, a piece of software that runs on computers (‘nodes’) spread across the network that communicate with each other to reach a shared consensus on the current state of a cryptographically-secured ledger. Every ten minutes or so these computers bundle up transactions into ‘blocks’, each of which refers to the previous block. This is the ‘blockchain’.
