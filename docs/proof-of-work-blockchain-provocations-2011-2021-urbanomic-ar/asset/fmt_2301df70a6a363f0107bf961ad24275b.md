
# 对话记录

I spent a few years on some unrealised projects that involved me trying to avoid being the kind of performance art shaman that I thought I didn’t want to be. I did get a tri-field electromagnetic field meter and go to a gallery searching for the aura of art—I thought I’d found it but a statistical regression indicates that I was just kidding myself. In the end I couldn’t genuinely be enthusiastic about doing this sort of thing. No, it’s got to be visual, I’m not being paid for this so I should at least get some visual joy from it.

After Surgical Strike there were a couple of years at the end of the nineties when I pretty much stopped making art. My creative output consisted in porting the `Gwydion Dylan` compiler to classic MacOS as a historical intervention in the history of computing after Apple cancelled their Dylan project. It got to the point where I was at Apple’s developer conference in California for Douglas Adams’s The Digital Village game company, and I was fielding emails from the company that did the one remaining commercial Dylan compiler, trying to open-source their user interface toolkit. So I went pretty deep into that community. I do tend to—I don’t know what the politically correct anthropological term for ‘going native’ is—I do tend to dance with wolves.

**MBK** But presumably it would have been already evident to you at that point that, if you were looking to have an appreciable impact on the way in which people went about their lives and saw the world, intervening in software would be a more reliable means than making art?

**RM** Well, there was no hope that everyone was going to start using Dylan. But yes, I had already realised that, and that was where `PostScript Virus` came from, I wanted to intervene in everyone’s everyday experience of aesthetics critically.

It’s tricky though, because there’s such a gap between what software developers see and what everyone else sees. One of the good things about Richard Stallman’s ideology is his absolutely resolute refusal to differentiate between users of computers who are producers and those who are consumers. That was hopelessly idealistic even when he was doing it, and it’s become no less so since, but yes, we experience so much through computers that changing what computers do is a surefire way of getting things out into the world.

**MBK** You’ve talked about how you always felt it was important to understand, to know the recipes: as an artist, if software of some kind is the medium, that requires an engagement with and preferably a mastery of that plastic medium. The vast majority of people engage with the screen without understanding what’s going on beneath the surface, though. Would those that know be justified in holding that consumerist passivity in contempt?

**RM** Nerd supremacism slips into honest-to-goodness fascism far too easily. I’m someone who knows how to program a computer, and I’m deeply relaxed about you not needing to know how to do that. Honestly, if you want to engage critically with the technology, what you need is just some degree of technical and cultural literacy, whether it’s from training or street smarts. I don’t care where it comes from, just as long as you know what you’re talking about. (I may have had different opinions about this in the past….)

The sense of superiority can go the other way too. All of the concerned calls to ‘teach nerds ethics’—why not teach venture capitalists ethics, teach managers ethics?! Why are you, my status-anxious humanities bro, so obsessed with the idea that if only software developers understood that killing people is bad, software would be different? It’s almost like you’re aiming for the wrong part of the body technologic, for reasons that are at least as interesting sociologically and psychologically as the aims you claim to be trying to achieve by doing so….

And in fact, even though I’ve accidentally become a very good smart contract programmer, I didn’t mean to do that. It was an accidental side effect—but I always do this, I always learn an obscure language for history of ideas or cultural history of computing reasons and then it turns out to be the next big thing. I learned `Objective C` in the nineties because I was fascinated by `NeXT`, Steve Jobs’s big failed project after Apple. Then we got to 2001 and he tells us that they programmed MacOS in `Objective C`, and then the iPhone comes out, and suddenly everyone wants `Objective C` programmers. With programming `Ethereum`, I thought, this will be interesting to do a couple of projects in, and then suddenly everyone wants `Ethereum` programmers. There were a couple of other examples in-between. I’ve had the privilege, obviously, to be able to do that, but as someone who is not going to outcompete the world’s best `C++` programmer, I’m very lucky that that’s not my ambition. I’m drawn to languages more for how amusing they are than for any claims of efficiency they might make.

As with absolutely everything I do, I don’t suddenly have genius original ideas out of nowhere, it’s always that I remember something from earlier in my life, or that I didn’t understand something the last time I saw it, or that I want to do this thing so I’ll try and learn how to do it and maybe I’ll be able to use it later. It’s all fantastically contingent.

**MBK** You mentioned wanting your works to touch on or draw in something from outside of themselves. A part of what I see in your works is a continuation of the question of the artwork’s autonomy that was central to site-specific and institutional critique: the enterprise of making artworks that refuse to be autonomous self-contained objects transportable from one gallery to another, and instead manifest the conditions and environment of their production and exhibition—an idea allegorised physically in Hans Haacke’s Condensation Cubes (1963–1968).

**RM** In the late sixties cybernetics was being received in a popular way by the same public who would later become interested in environmental concerns, precisely because they were primed to understand feedback systems. I’m not trying to say that RAND were actually hippies, I’m saying that after cybernetics escaped the lab, it was received by political movements in a way that we cannot imagine today. But once opposition to the Vietnam war became the key focus for many of them, cybernetics became unimaginable to them as well, which is why information-based and cybernetic art disappears like a vampire in sunlight about a year before I was born. But Haacke is very much in this milieu, and the thing about Haacke’s institutional critique is that it is genuinely uncomfortable for the institution. You do not want a study of the slumlords of New York in your gallery as the nice polite property-owning class file in. There has to be that degree of discomfort to achieve successful institutional critique, but it can’t be so much of a discomfort that it won’t get shown. Haacke had the historical advantage of institutions not yet having worked this out. The end of that sort of institutional critique was when museums realised that they didn’t have to put up with this, that artists can actually be told, yeah you’re a genius but we don’t have to show this work. You can always aestheticise any ethical move of resistance: ‘They’re selling hippie wigs in Woolworths, man.’

**MBK** Just as Conceptual Art was wrongfooted by its documents being aestheticised as exhibitable works.
