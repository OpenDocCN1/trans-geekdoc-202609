During this period I also almost invented the Word macro virus. I didn’t, because we didn’t have the Word macro manuals, but I wanted to make a virus that would replace the word ‘postmodernism’ with something random. I did make a virus written in Adobe PostScript, a language designed to describe page layouts for print (PostScript Virus [1993]). The virus would have infected PostScript printers and made all of the lovely graphic design sent to the printer just a bit more wobbly. I had the payload and an attempt at the replicator…but I thought, I probably don’t want to do this, I don’t know if it’s actually technically possible or not but if it is I don’t want to find out…. It worked by redefining how the PostScript interpreter itself works. And the fact that code can interpret things and can do things visually was interesting to me. At the time people were obsessed with the idea of software as text, but for me, the design of the Macintosh, that visual encounter with software, with a standardized GUI, was very compelling.

Rhea Myers, ‘Arching’, 1994, from Blobs. Digital vector image.

Eventually, when I got fed up with spending twelve hours a day in the computer room, I fell in love, had my first serious relationship, and the joy from that went into the more graphically wibbly-wobbly works that make up Blobs (1994). Again, these were all done using vector graphics. Each of them required a very keen visual eye, but none of it required great hand-eye coordination. What I was looking for in vector graphics was a medium, meaning something with both affordances that direct you, and limitations that you have to work around, and which comes with a theory and a history. That’s what’s happening in Blobs: the history I got from graphic design, from The Designers Republic, from the cereal packets and candy wrappers and so on. And there’s a lot of play with the form going on, to the extent that these are just about sensuality and joy, but they’re about that sensuality and joy in a way that’s technically mediated and is very contemporary to that era. There’s nothing spiritual to it, and there’s nothing political except to the extent that just doing this is political.

**MBK** You mentioned Julian Opie, whose work is often interested in how much you can graphically pare down aesthetic means while retaining some kind of seductive or sensual allure.

**RM** Yes, it’s curious because on the internet I play someone who hates dialectics, but all the work I like is dialectical, and my own work—don’t tell anyone—is also generally deeply dialectical. In Blobs you have a cold commercial technology being made to use warm, sexy (in my conception of it at the time) art. The Blobs are bodies, they’re ecstatic maps of making out, and they are free objects—each has its own shadow. There is no God. The light of the fact. But they exist within an indexical environment of certain technical problems within the production and history of art and carry these very impersonal references.

Opie is fascinating because the painted steel sculptures he started with are a sort of ironisation of turn-of-the-80s Frank Stella. Where Stella painted abstract sculptures, Opie used the same technique with representational imagery in a retro clip-art style. I simply adore Opie’s concrete Scalextric sets (Imagine You Are Driving [1993]), toy-like in their scale and reduction of complexity and in their educational, imaginative reflective purpose. They give you something to look at. They’re not a text stapled to the wall or a blank canvas, they are something that has presence, virtuality. His air conditioners from the late 80s, I believe, although I have no evidence for this, come from a comment David Hockney made about minimal art, which was that you might as well look at the air conditioning unit.

**MBK** Didn’t he steal that from Columbo? In the episode ‘Playback’ (1975), Columbo’s in a gallery in LA sniffing around. In the line of duty, he’s trying to enter into the modern art ethos, and mistakenly starts ‘appreciating’ the air conditioning unit. ‘Um, now, this here…I see it doesn’t have a title…’!

**RM** Those Opie works put the visual to work in the way that—not to say that art should, but that art tends to, whether it’s persuading you to love Christ, to join the revolution, or to really enjoy getting on the train out of Paris for the weekend. The essentially propagandising function of art is a result of its visual seductiveness. Adorno says that to depict violence is to aestheticise it, which is entirely correct, but doesn’t go far enough, because to aestheticise something can be morally obscene, and the deployment of moral obscenity merely symbolically within art can be a very powerful move. Sometimes the only way you can get to sympathy is through disgust. I’m all about these lateral moves. But yeah, what is the point of visual art you don’t want to look at?

**MBK** So although you were already engaged with Conceptualism in its most stringent form, you didn’t have the hostility or suspicion of the visual that can imply.

**RM** No, and I still think design has a better claim to the craft of imagemaking qua imagemaking than contemporary art does. If you want a well-painted, well-drawn, well-proportioned image, go to design or comics or illustration or movies—anywhere other than the gallery, where the zombie formalisms are skulking in the hope that they can gather enough presence to be considered significant despite not signifying.

There’s a lovely Art & Language quote they’ve been trying to walk back for decades, which goes something like, ‘Some of the paintings seemed better than others, it seemed to us that this was somehow a product of the making of them’. This is about a series of their own paintings, and they tried to clarify that, of course, they were not saying that any skill or imagination went into them…no, too late!

In grappling with this kind of thing, I decided that you can’t do either reason or emotion alone. I’d seen a documentary featuring someone who had a brain injury which left them incapable of emotion, and as a result they couldn’t make a decision because they would never get bored of reasoning about it. Where should we go for dinner? Well, we could go to the fish restaurant but I like the wine at the Italian place but then we could…. They had no way of culling the search tree, because they had a faculty of pure logical search unconstrained by affect. So I realised that it’s ineffective to pretend not to rely on visual impact and the affect it creates. Often it’s ‘logical’ to use affect and indeed to be illogical, because sometimes you need to jump to a different line of flight from any you can think of directly. I wanted to make posters, things for people to have in their mental space. I didn’t have the kind of residual valuing of high culture that you find in the historical-materialist project-based discursive-but-not-illustrative work of Art & Language and Opie. I found something very interesting in the more productive and playful and, to be honest, queer art of Warhol and Koons.
