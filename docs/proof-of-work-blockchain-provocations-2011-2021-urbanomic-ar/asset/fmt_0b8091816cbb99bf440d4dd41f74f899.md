
## 对话记录

**RM**  Oh, absolutely. An institution I have described as throwing humanities students at computer programming for a year, and having the survivors put on a show. I got there in ’95 and there was the feeling of twenty years of history to the place already. There was a dead old Silicon Graphics box in the hall, there was a PICASO software manual next to a Macintosh attached to a video dumping device. We had lectures from people who’d founded the Computer Arts Society, people in the then-current new media art scene, and people who actually knew how to teach people like me maths and programming. So it was an absolutely amazing marriage between the technical knowledge that you needed to make a computer beep the way you wanted it to, and a sense of history that led you to feel that this was something that could be informed by and respond to precedent. And you were also situated within contemporary culture because you had people explaining the early social effects of the internet, or how virtual reality related to feminism, or whatever. So yeah, it was Camelot.

**MBK**  Did you have access to whatever constituted cutting-edge hardware and software at the time?

**RM**  No, we didn’t have access to the best computers, we had some little pre-PowerMacs. But if you were very nice to the Music Department they’d let you use their NeXT boxes, and if you were very nice to the Interactive Multimedia Department they’d let you use one of their ageing Silicon Graphics boxes or their PCs. I got into an ongoing battle with some Interactive Media students, getting there earlier and earlier in the morning to use the PCs.

**MBK**  This would have been during the period of the explosion of cyberculture too, and a growing general awareness of computing technology and the internet. How aware were you of that, and of the differences between the mainstream US Wired version of cyberculture and the more idiosyncratic and abrasive UK take on cyberculture by groups like Mute Magazine, Ccru, Orphan Drift, and so on?

**RM**  During this period, between 1995 and 2000, to be honest I encountered cyberculture and didn’t understand it. I encountered net art and didn’t understand it. I encountered the web and didn’t understand it (you’ll notice a theme here). I’d got over my printmaking obsession, but I thought interactive multimedia was the future. PostScript Virus was only the first in a long history of me not taking the bit as far as it could go…but it was the closest I got to cyberculture at the time. For me, cyberpunk was the eighties, I thought that was all done with, passé. Cyberpunk? You might as well be reading westerns, what are you doing?! My partner at the time was the creative director at a virtual reality startup in Shoreditch, you literally could not be more mid-nineties. So I got to see VRML and virtual communities emerge, and I also went to a show [Ccru, Syzygy, Beaconsfield, 2000] which had some strange drawings on the wall, featuring this sort of bracket notation for numbers. I looked at it and I was like Just use hex! So I very much didn’t know what the next big thing was at the time. If I had a time machine it would be a toss-up between whether I go back to teenage me and go, Hey, there’s a reason why all of your RPG characters are female, or go back to twentysomething me and go, Walk on the earth, engage with what is actually going on, take a risk!

**MBK**  How advanced were you as a programmer? Was your knowledge of coding drawn purely from having to learn in order to make designs and prints?

**RM**  I had no formal programming education until I got to Middlesex. I’d learned BASIC from the Usborne books, I’d learned Forth and some Pascal purely out of interest. I was obsessed with LOGO but couldn’t get a copy of it to use. I knew that LISP was a thing, probably thanks to Usborne books too, but couldn’t make head nor tail of it. I learned a bit of C, I had a C compiler for my Mac. But the language that I went all in on was PostScript, because it was the language that graphic design was made in. As a language it’s most like Forth, it’s an absolutely fascinating language, and the environment in which it operates is so amazing, this platonic mathematical realm of lines and curves that maps into very real ink being squeezed between metal plates onto paper. I later got a job working at a prepress company programming Adobe Illustrator plug-ins based on the knowledge that I had gained of the PostScript rendering model.

**MBK**  PostScript is a language that defines the available range of vector and typographical objects, the relations between them, and the transforms that can be applied to them, all in the most economical form. So it lays out a kind of graphic ontology, right?

**RM**  Yes, it’s curious because it’s not like having a pattern book for textiles or a Vasari study of Roman architecture; it’s not really a set of models, it’s not quite a tool box, it’s not quite a palette, it’s this combination of knowledge and tool and affordance that’s sort of the technical equivalent of a community of practice. If UNIX is the religious literature of computer science, then PostScript is its illuminated manuscripts.

**MBK**  It seems like, even for this period, you went a lot deeper into programming than you strictly needed to in order to do digital printmaking.

**RM**  At one point in art school I was given the task of doing what in the 2000s was called a ‘mashup’. I was given photocopies of a Futurist cyclist and a Jackson Pollock drip painting and told to put those together. I looked at the small grey reproduction of the cyclist and I just couldn’t work it out. I had been given a material task that I could not perform owing to my lack of experience, knowledge, and resources. So I went to the library and there was a book of preparatory sketches by the artist: a drawing of someone on a bicycle, a drawing of someone on the bicycle with lines of force, because it’s Futurism, then that was abstracted a bit, then that abstraction reduced to a circle and a couple of triangles…and I then understood the forms I was working with. And in the same way, I learned programming because I wanted to understand. I knew that I wanted to make computer art, whatever that was, so I needed to know the structures underpinning it. That interest in access to knowledge and source material for very practical reasons fed directly into my interest in copyleft and free culture and Creative Commons.

**MBK**  Because you had direct experience of the fact that it mattered whether people had access not just to the end product, but to the knowledge of how things are made? And you were able to see how that need translated from art to programming—in both cases, to really understand and be productive you need access to the ‘source code’?

**RM**  Yes, in my case I would not have competently been able to do the task without access to those resources. The funny thing is that, after doing a Marinetti in the style of Jackson Pollock, I found out about Art & Language’s Portrait of V.I. Lenin with Cap, in the Style of Jackson Pollock (1980).
