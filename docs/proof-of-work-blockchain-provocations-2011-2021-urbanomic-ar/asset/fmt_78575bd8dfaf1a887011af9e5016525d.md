
Ultimately, what I want more than anything is for someone to say No, you are wrong, here is a better alternative. I have a genuine anger for imaginary solutions that are not critical or even prefigurative, that are nothing more than affordable hobbies for those who use them to resolve the merely psychological contradictions of their own existence as agents of the social reproduction of the ideology of capitalism. That anger and heartbreak and sadness goes into making this stuff and just hoping that someone who is kinder and smarter and better than me has an answer that is not merely confected, that is not merely a necessary cog in the clockwork machinery that neutralises revolutionary potential by redirecting it into dreamwork.

**MBK** So operationalising Schelling Point convergence in order to optimise aesthetics and art criticism ([‘The ABC of Accelerationist Blockchain Critique’](#13-The-abc.xhtml)) isn’t an entirely serious proposition?

**RM** More of a modest proposal, I’d say, although it’s a functional one and one that would change things in interesting ways if it actually happened. The epitome of this is [‘Maximum Transaction Density’](#48-Maximum-transaction-density.xhtml), which is as Janus-faced as everything else I write, but if as a reader you get to the end of it and treat it as simply a first-order call to turn ourselves into blockchain clickworkers or whatever, then it has failed miserably. It is precisely the most intense attempt I have made to horrify the imagined reader into either accepting that, yes, this will achieve our ends, horrific though it is, and we should pursue this, or protesting in horror, No, that’s not right because…. [‘Maximum Transaction Density’](#48-Maximum-transaction-density.xhtml) is about the horror of getting what you want by means that you don’t want. If anyone has a better idea for how to do what they want than what’s in there, I’m all ears.

Just to try and wrap up my apology for my good leftist soul: some of this is simply Edward-de-Bono-style creative thinking. At times you have to make a sideways leap or lateral move to avoid being stuck in a rut. Working through these ideas, some of it is provocation, some of it is aspiration, some of it is education and…it’s not demanding that others do the work for me, it’s doing the work that I can do and hoping that others will be able to respond in a way that I haven’t already heard and been disappointed by.

**MBK** Your career in art eventually collided with computing, cyberculture, and cypherpunk, but how did it begin?

**RM** Both of my parents worked in arts education at various levels throughout their lives. When I was little I would go to my father’s basement office at Central Saint Martins and smell photo developer and be very impressed by all the big machines. So I had that background. And then we were members of a militant offshoot of the scouting movement, although by that point it was just a bunch of families doing cheap camping holidays somewhere in England each summer. The kids who were one high-school generation up from me there, the cool kids, were all into art and were going off to art school, so that was a major influence.

There were a couple of books in the art room at high school that were important for me, books the new art teacher had brought in (thank you Miss Hamer!) There was the Warhol retrospective catalogue from 1987, which I sat and read front to back many times; and another which was the catalogue from an, at least in part, conceptual retrospective. The two works I can remember from it are one of the Conceptual Art projects where someone introduces two strangers to each other to have a conversation, and an early example of lifelogging, of the quantified self—an artist who kept track of their activities over a week or a month or something.

This was around 1989, I was reading this at age sixteen to eighteen, so obviously I liked H.R. Giger, I liked graphic design, album covers, and all that kind of thing, very much in line with what kids of that generation were into on the visual culture front. But from the get-go I wasn’t into trying to copy heroic Boris Vallejo-style art like some of my best friends were. Apart from anything else, I knew I couldn’t draw that well. I also lived through the home computing boom in the UK, and one of the books I got from book club was the Usborne Guide to Computers, which featured Harold Cohen and his AARON drawing robot, and Ken Knowlton’s BEFLIX programming language. So from the age of about ten or twelve I had the idea that, even if I myself couldn’t do it, there were people out there making art with computers. And when I began an art foundation course in 1991, I made friends with the technicians, which meant that I could squeeze past them in their converted corridor office to use one of the two Macintoshes in the building. I can still remember seeing Photoshop boot for the first time. It was something very different: going from a very this-is-technical self-taught command-line programmer world to the very positively commodified design of the Macintosh.

![](image/bdog.png)

Rhea Myers, ‘Balloon Dog’, 2011, from Shareable Readymades. 3D digital model.

During that period I did a lot of screen printing and dye-transfer work. But I had this desire to make a sort of remix- or sampling-based art where I was taking things and chopping them up digitally and rearranging them. And by the time I got to art school proper, I was deeply in love with the sample-based musics that were going mainstream at that moment, and I was sure that was something I wanted to embrace. I had Andy Warhol as a reference, and I became an instant and lifelong Jeff Koons fan (sorry haters), between the pink Taschen book, which I still have on my bookcase, and the little red handbook which has that great quote: ‘If you put art in the hands of a monarch, it will reflect his ego and eventually become decorative. If you put it in the hands of the masses, it will reflect mass ego and eventually become decorative. If you put art in the hands of Jeff Koons, it will reflect my ego and eventually become decorative.’ And Koons’s comment about Warhol being about distribution, and his work being about the penetration of consciousness.

Later in the decade I found things like No Logo, and could conceptualise that sort of very Nike-style branding and Adbusters-inflected art, which you can follow through much later into Shareable Readymades (2011–2013), where I tried to take iconic art-historical objects and return them to the cultural commons by publishing them as 3d-printable digital models.

**MBK** When did Conceptual Art begin to play a significant role in what you were doing?

**RM** I’d encountered some Conceptual Art in that catalogue when I was at school, and on my foundation course we went on a field trip to the Serpentine for the exhibition Objects for the Ideal Home: The Legacy of Pop Art (1991), which included work by Koons and by Julian Opie, who was also a massive influence. I found out about the Lisson Gallery via Opie, and it was at Lisson that I first encountered Art & Language and had my mind blown by their writing and art.
