
Tokens Equal Text consists of an edition of 32 Ethereum `ERC-998` composable tokens each of which owns four child `ERC-721` non-fungible tokens. The `ERC-721` tokens have short descriptive texts encoded as their ID numbers. This makes the `ERC-998` tokens compositions of those text fragments. The content of that composition can be recovered if it is known that the token IDs can be (mis)read as text rather than numbers.

These text fragments describe key visual tropes of the Vaporwave movement. Vaporwave is a 2010s internet-based musical and cultural genre devoted to the ironisation of muzak and digital kitsch aesthetics from the long 1980s. It is named for ‘Vaporware’, future software that is announced but never released. Vaporwave has an inherent melancholy and a sense of lost future potential; in its ironising mashing together of time and desire it is inherently queer and, in leading examples such as Macintosh Plus’s genre-defining Floral Shoppe (2012), trans.

The question of whether a given trope fits the aesthetic of Vaporwave is key to the genre’s discourse, with canonical tropes establishing the content of that aesthetic. A composition of references to canonical elements of that aesthetic at least admits of the question ‘Is this aesthetic?’

Using Ethereum Web3 browser app code to display the texts of tokens in visual compositions using ‘aesthetic’ (in the Vaporwave sense) typography and colours nudges that answer along. It also prevents the content from becoming a trivial puzzle. The encoded information is placed, not hidden. Its easy recovery foregrounds the presence of compositional and indexical structure that rewards reflection.

The ‘Rare Art’ market demands something aesthetic to own. Nothing is more desirable to own than something that cannot be owned. Vaporwave is built on sampled and appropriated audio recordings, video, images, and 3D models. It is unownable, making it ideal common property for an internet cultural scene. This does however make it very difficult to truly claim ownership of most of its products without attracting the ire of corporate copyright holders.

By encrypting recoverable and imaginable references to this unownable aesthetic, Tokens Equal Text loops desire for ownership back through a complexly unownable genre of aesthetics to both satisfy and frustrate this demand and to place blockchain property and the history of art into a state of mutually critical interrogation.

(I was listening to Floral Shoppe pretty much on repeat while I made it.)

# Staking Ratio

## 2019 Ethereum DApp

![](image/staking-ratio.jpg)

A ratio of two amounts of Ether cryptocurrency that is staked within a smart contract by its users. Value determines value, further destroying the information that money destroys in order to represent it.

This is the last work in the series of resonant pairings of aesthetic properties and governance or economic allocation methods that began with Democratic Palette. It is the first to deal directly in cryptocurrency value.

Use at your own risk.

# Token Grid

## 2019 Ethereum DApp

![](image/token-grid.jpg)

The grid is a Modernist icon and the underlying organisational form of many aspects of our daily lives. Whether a North American city street plan, a power grid, the face of International Style architecture, page layout in print or onscreen, or the pixels of that screen, grids pervasively organise our activities and experiences.

This one exists on the Ethereum blockchain with a graphical user interface accessible via the world wide web. In contrast to the fixed grids of city streets and architectural structures, anyone can change it, but only if they are willing to procure and then burn one of the grid smart contract’s `ERC-20` digital tokens in order to do so.

# Proof of Work Bitmap

## 2019 Ethereum DApp

![](image/Proof-of-Work_Bitmap.png)

A 16×16 pixel monochrome bitmap that lives on the Ethereum blockchain. You can change it if you run a simple proof-of-work calculation and send the result in the update transaction.

Proof of Work Bitmap is the latest in a series of pieces that pair aesthetic properties with methods of economic allocation or social governance (in no particular order, Democratic Palette, Proof of Work Bitmap, Staking Ratio, Hack Line Properties, Pay Previous Path, Lottery Symbol, Token Grid). In computer art and digital culture, a monochrome bitmap is the simplest representation of a discrete image. In blockchain-based systems a ‘proof of work’ is a time-consuming computational puzzle that is impossible to cheat on but simple to check the result of. The result of solving that puzzle in Bitcoin, Ethereum, and Proof of Work Bitmap, is a 256-bit number with a specific number of zeros at the end. This is the same size as a 16×16 bitmap and as a memory cell in Ethereum. There’s a resonance here, as there is in each piece in the series.

# Pay Previous Path

## 2019 Ethereum DApp

![](image/Pay_Previous_Path.png)

Pay Previous Path allows anyone to store a vector drawing (encoded in a style similar to the `SVG` path line-art format) on the Ethereum blockchain in return for paying a small fee to the previous person who did so.

You may notice that the payment creates a path or line between accounts. This is the penultimate piece in the series of artworks pairing aesthetic objects with economic allocation methods.

# Hack Line Properties

## 2019 Ethereum DApp

![](image/Secure_Line_Properties.png)

Hack Line Properties is a supposedly secure blockchain smart contract designed to allow only its owner to update the line stroke properties that it stores. But a common Ethereum code bug allows anyone who finds it to ‘hack’ the contract and set the line properties themselves.

If code is law in the sense promoted by early Ethereum idealism, any action we can take using code is legal. But if that code is a bug, it embodies no intention and may break the intended operation of the software. Sometimes code has to be rescued by exploiting a bug (the first moon landing is a good example of this). Is this still a form of governance?

# Certificate of Inauthenticity

![](image/BalloonDog04.png)

Between 2011 and 2012, for the Shareable Readymades project I commissioned the awesome artists Chris Webber and Bassam Kurdali to create three 3D printable models of commonplace objects—a urinal, a balloon dog, and a pipe—that had become part of canonical art history. The artists retained copyright on their work and the results were placed under the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike (`CC-BY-SA`) licence with the attribution to myself as the person that commissioned the work. I did this in order to critique the production of neo-conceptual art by artists such as Jeff Koons and the processes by which art history cordons off models of commonplace objects as signature works by Jeff Koons, Marcel Duchamp, and others.

Furtherfield gallery in London (who meta-commissioned the Balloon Dog model) exhibited the works with ‘certificates of inauthenticity’ that I détourned from Sol LeWitt’s certificates for their wall drawings. Then at the #BlockchainLive18 show, also in London, Ruth Catlow and Dr. Charlotte Frost of DECAL and Futherfield produced gold castings of the Balloon Dog model and registered the resulting ‘Cryptopuppers’ on Arteia’s blockchain art registry.

I hadn’t planned these developments, but they diagonalised the project in interesting ways. The former recapitulated Conceptual Art’s capture by the very systems of sale and ownership that it had initially sought to escape. The latter mapped this onto the constant development of technosocial phenomena within successive waves of Andy Cameron and Richard Barbrook’s ‘Californian Ideology’ by taking a 3D printing project that was meant to be free-as-in-freedom and making an expression of it exclusively ownable with cryptographic certainty on the blockchain.
