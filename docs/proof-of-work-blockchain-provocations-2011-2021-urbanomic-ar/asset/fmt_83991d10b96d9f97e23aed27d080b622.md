
I have to admit that there’s some bad conscience to my continued individualistic art production, because I should really have made a `DAO` or some sort of platform or standard where I could bring in the wisdom and madness of crowds to make art that could not be made any other way and that would therefore have a historical necessity to it. I haven’t done that, partly owing to lack of social skills, partly owing to a very heavily internalised conception of the modernist artist, and partly because this is how I have fun! Having meetings is not how I have fun. But if there’s a failing to my own performance relative to any reading of what I’m doing, it’s that I’m still doing it as an individual cryptographic identity rather than at least socialising myself through a `DAO`.

**MBK** Maybe something for the future…! Having looked back in this conversation to where you started with all of this, how do you feel about the point it has brought you to—in art and life—today? I get the impression that you toiled for a long time in fairly cheerful obscurity, and that your sudden notoriety in recent years has come as more of a surprise to you than to anyone else. But you also seem to be engaged in a constant reinterpretation of what it is that you were doing in those earlier works. Has the art done what you needed it to do?

**RM** Where I started was the feeling of not belonging in the world and not understanding how the world worked, so there’s this emotional or psychological impetus in the work which I recognise now very clearly as dysphoria and dissociation—the feeling that the world is somehow behind glass and that everyone else has a rule book that you don’t have, and trying to explain the world to myself. With my art I’m trying to collapse the distance between myself and the world through understanding. I was aiming to change the world through my art, which is unlikely, or to change myself through my art and rehabilitate myself as a social subject. But I didn’t have the knowledge that I needed to fix my epistemology in order to really attempt that task because the part of my brain that should have worked out I was transgender when I was eighteen at the latest was being overridden by the part of my brain that was spending more and more of the day making very sure that this was not something I would ever consider as the remotest possibility. But by the time I was in my forties it was like having a stage magician following you around and whenever you’re like Hang on, this doesn’t make sense, they pop up with a card trick, Pick a card! It was just distraction and denial and disavowal and dissociation, not as a conscious project but as a background process in the operating system of my brain, taking up more and more of the runtime.

But this meant that, on the way through a succession of incorrect understandings of the world, I did learn how to learn about different aspects of the world, how to criticise myself and these aspects and their relations, and I managed to create this sort of spider web of associations and critiques. I came to enjoy the exercise of my critical and creative faculties upon something which other people couldn’t tell me was wrong and which I couldn’t tell me was wrong, and which often had an irresistible quality to it, something like the Shareable Readymades or [Tokens Equals Text](#76-Tokens-equals-text.xhtml). These are ideas that assembled themselves in my mind from what I was doing at the time, and I learned over time to get out of the way of that: to just make it, and then afterwards find out how it relates to everything I’m doing at the moment. So, much like Conan being chained to a wheel for his entire adolescence, I built up my critical faculties by not knowing what the hell was going on.

**MBK** Do those realisations mean that you now have a different view of the work, of its interrogation of secrecy and privacy and identity? And does the fact that the urgency of avoidance has abated make you less inclined to make more work, even though your work is now getting more attention than ever before?

**RM** In a sense, my egg cracking was the fulfilment of the projects I was doing when I was twenty.

**MBK** We’re entering into a retrochronic temporality here?

**RM** Yes, you can see why the work of the `Ccru` continues to resonate for terminally online trans women: we are our own hyperstitions, terminators sent back from the future to assemble ourselves from the resources we have now: our bodies and what we understand about culture, having not really understood it for however long.

But my constant theme is disobedience-in-obedience. What I had asked was How does the world work? and the answer Here is why you don’t understand how the world works does not answer my original question! It has the effect that I wanted, which is to remove the barrier between myself and the world, to collapse me into the prime material plane, to make me mortal. But it only raises more questions, so I am absolutely no less motivated to make the work. And I’m deeply flattered to have an audience that has invested psychologically and financially in the work that I have made, so I have a strong sense of obligation to them not to fuck it up.

Transition does not solve all your problems, it may not even make you less anxious, it may not make you less depressed: what it does do is it makes it possible for you to face the reality of those things. And this can be awful because actually dissociation can be very useful when you are dealing, or in fact not dealing, with them. Being checked out can help you survive things much better; if you’re no longer checked out it doesn’t make things easier to cope with, but it may give you the knowledge and impetus to deal with them. When Morpheus gives Neo the red pill he says, Remember, all I’m offering you is the truth. Being unplugged is not paradise. I always remember the line from *Neuromancer* where Case is injected with neurotoxins and he’s lying in a hotel room for three days feeling his ability to connect to cyberspace basically dribble out of his ears, and then he ‘crashes into the meat’. Hackers have disdain for ‘the meat’, and you’ll notice that lots of trans people are software engineers, and if that’s not the effect of a revenge hex by one of the women who was cast out of the software development in the sixties when it became professionalised, perhaps it’s because dissociation is a superpower: if you’re checked out of your body and want a world that makes sense when the world around you doesn’t, and if you do not care about how you look, feel, or smell, then you’re prime material for software development. But in whatever field it gets expressed, this displacement, this attempt to master a body of knowledge rather than knowledge of your body, it’s a coping technique which is completely understandable and deserves love and a big hug.

**MBK** But reality finally seeps in and the work then becomes about something other than what you thought it was about? Is that what you mean when you mention an anxiety around people who have invested in your work?

**RM** As with any body horror story where someone begins a transformation, the thing that has to start the process of change will be changed by the process of change. This is the general problem of self-knowledge, which is diagonalisation. Once you know that you are not who you should be, that doesn’t mean that the thing that you become will want to be the thing that you think you want to be…if the book tries to rewrite itself then you end up in the realm of recursion.

A meaningful existence was something that was inconceivable to me—I was proud of being a parent, I enjoyed being in love and making art, but each decade I lived I was amazed by it, and I could not imagine a future beyond. Crashing into my body just in time to watch the end of the world is the sort of cruel irony that would make you feel a Marvel universe movie is a bit underwritten.
