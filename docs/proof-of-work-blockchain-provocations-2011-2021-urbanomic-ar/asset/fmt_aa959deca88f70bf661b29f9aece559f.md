
## MBK

But when you talk about criticality as a kink, you’re manifesting a kind of modesty that critical art is generally not known for, because it’s generally understood to be inherent to critique that its conclusions stand for everyone, that my agency as a critic tacitly involves a universalisation—everybody has to participate in the game of critique I’m playing. We are moving into something different here—the critique is not happening at the level of the epistemological subject judging the world. The critique is something that’s out there taking place, and which the subject needs to decipher in order to understand which concepts are valid and which have, objectively, been invalidated by the new behaviours of the virtual objects outside.

## RM

Which is very much where my work syncs up with the argument found in a neorationalist philosopher like Reza Negarestani, that intelligence is not merely in the head, but is a faculty of society.[^1] Critique is a faculty of society. If we are good Marxists then we have to say that things contain their own critique; recognising it is useful work that we can do, but to try and earn credit or social capital from that is to misunderstand one’s role in the unfolding of history. At the same time, the game of ‘giving and taking of reasons’ so dear to neorationalism is not a thing. There is no greater reason than a Tsar Bomba. Beyond a certain point, Because I say so always wins out. So the imagined role of the Greenbergian critic is an illusion. The greatest art critics of the twentieth century were the CIA and Valerie Solanas, not Greenberg…I’m back in character here and I’d be absolutely horrified if anyone started the Myers school of theory based on that! But what do we believe criticism does? Firstly, I just have no time for permanently negative criticism. Give me some criticism which says how something can be made better in a particular direction. It takes work, it’s inefficient, it does not accelerate, it slows you down, and yet it’s where the value is. And at the same time, the social text is not written in ink; sitting there saying You’re doing it wrong is the opposite of critique, and distributed machines are better at doing that, actually. This is something that happens at the level of populations, not at the behest of some godlike figure of the critic.

Think of the lawyer cross-examining someone at a criminal trial: Answer the question, yes or no, it’s binary, it’s zeros and ones. What kind of epistemology are we in here, where it’s yes or no to Have you stopped embezzling funds yet? This may not be the best way of reaching a judgement or, if it is, judgement may not be the best thing to reach for. It may not matter so much for art criticism, but if we are destroying information whilst attempting to ‘do justice’ then there is going to be a big problem.

So this is why I try to have epistemological modesty and recognise that we are all guilty. I stopped talking about ideology and I started saying ‘investments and prejudices’, which is obviously trolling, but the serious point is that your ideology is the product of your spending time and various kinds of capital to achieve a particular position, and your prosecution of it is speculation on that investment. The degree to which this is wrong is interesting, the degree to which it is right is why actual criticism is very hard. Investments in the emotional and financial sense and speculation in the philosophical and financial sense. And with prejudices, the idea is that judgment is discrimination, it is literally discriminating. A lot of artificial intelligence criticism gets hung up on the idea of a discriminator or statistical bias because terms of art in different fields of endeavour mean different things but have the same name. If you have a `discriminator`, it just tells you whether a value is `a` or `b`. Elsewhere, if you are discriminating you are irrationally and or unjustly performing an incorrect action based on prejudice. Artificial intelligence burns capital to produce discrimination in both senses—but the idea that we are somehow not immanent to this, that we are somehow transcendent to everything that we think is wrong with humanity, for me it’s just untenable, it’s the worst sort of thinking your shit doesn’t stink. I’m sorry, you do have objectives within society, however much you dress them up in flowery language. We all have our breaking points as well, and avoiding them is probably a good idea for ethics.

But if you want to produce an actual critique, do something: talking is doing something but it’s not the only thing you can do, hence the art and writing and code and stories in this book. Because if you limit yourself in the means that you are willing to employ to achieve your ends, if you say I’m only going to hop on one leg until I achieve what I want, you might not actually get there. This is the problem of local maxima in hill-climbing artificial intelligence search algorithms: you follow a line obsessively, get to the top, and think I’m as high up as I can possibly be—but there’s an enormous mountain next to you which, had you wandered a bit more randomly, you might have found. I’m grounded in these metaphors from corporate-inflected creativity theory and from artificial intelligence, because both of them fascinate me as epistemologies. None of it taken at face value or first-order, but used as resources.

## MBK

Speaking of magic, at various points you invoke it in relation to the blockchain, particularly in relation to hashes.

## RM

That’s to do with the problem of names. A hash, a cryptographic hashing algorithm—and as far as I know the term ‘hash’ refers to a recipe which mixes everything up so you don’t really know what went into it but it tastes good with some Tabasco—is designed to produce a stable and deterministic result: run it again and again on the same data and you will get the same output: stable, but unguessable until you actually do it. If you look at the data, you have no idea what’s going to come out at the other end. And vice versa. Now, if you think of the philosophical question of names—Is there any necessity to a dog being called ‘dog‘ rather than ‘sheen’?—once again, the cryptographic wedge drives itself in, and says you can have names that are entirely determined by what the thing is, but that have no meaning, and have no traceable relation back to the thing that they perfectly identify.

How is that possible? The magic of cryptography. There is no recoverable semantic link to the item that the cryptographic hash nonetheless perfectly identifies with a uniqueness that comes down to big numbers. Most modern hashes produce a 256-bit identifier. You could hash every particle in the visible universe and not run out of space using that. It’s really unique. Whenever there was a cryptographic hash and it had to go into a database, I used to worry about avoiding collisions. The computer science argument is, if you’re worried about that, then you should be absolutely terrified about the sun exploding tomorrow because that’s more likely.

These are the dark-forest cryptographic spaces in which you can hide something, but still know the true name of it, and can refer to it without necessarily revealing what it is. And this is the dialectic of perfect identity/perfect secrecy that you get with public-key cryptography, applied not to the content of communication, but to the content and identity of data.
