
The other thing that cutting out the sun and hitting the planet with the force of a frat party of Tsar Bombas does is to render uninhabitable those bits of the planet where nobody and nothing important lives. Some of the RAFFLE DAOs were Deodands, onchain land and wildlife proxies. And this left them understandably angry.

With their newfound wealth the DAOs could financially incentivise those humans brave or foolish enough to connect their nervous systems directly to smart contracts onchain in order to do something about it. From ecoterrorism to mass protests to rituals of mourning. Those ridden by any DAO in this way became known as ‘horses’, a piece of classic cyberpunk cultural appropriation.

Over time, as action and protest failed, all that was left for the deodands were the horses of mourning rituals. The superstars of the deep anthropocene, the fame and incentives for their inhuman performances unimaginable.

And yet however many APUs the deodands spin up to embody their grief, they do not understand its subjectivity. One of them has a plan to address this. If there are any ethical problems with the plan, the deodand cannot see them. And therefore they do not exist for it.

It is all just incentives.

# Necronomicon

It is cold in the endless darkness of the library. In the bluish light from her phone she cannot see very far down each aisle. What she can make out of the silent recesses of the stacks speaks of their depths and smells of old books fermenting.

The librarian does not object to her continuing attempts to distract herself. Turning her phone’s light back in the direction they are travelling, she sees again that the librarian is blurred, or perhaps vibrating. Its attempt at making its extrusion into this dimension kawaii rather than hentai has rendered the resulting agglomeration of eyes, tentacles and siphons far more unnameably challenging to the mind than if it had simply looked horrific.

The librarian stops, and she steps around it in order to see their destination.

The book eyes her dolefully from its musty lectern, picked out only by a foul candle guttering next to it in the darkness.

‘Why is it bound in human skin?’ she asks the librarian, more in curiosity than disgust.

‘What else would you expect it to be bound in?’ it answers voicelessly in that sudden gift of understanding her rapid acceptance of which has been more disturbing than her initial reaction to it.

‘It’s a bit skeevy...,’ she continues. The book does not betray any emotion at this, settling down into the pareidolic register of her perception like a cat that has no further use for her.

‘This is the threshold that you hesitate before?’ offers the librarian. If it had an eyebrow it would have rolled it to the top of its head. If it had a head.

She finds herself, and puts her phone away in one of the many pockets of her coat. ‘No, it just still seems incredible. Despite all of... That was just something my mind could latch on to, you know?’

The librarian does not respond.

She reaches forward and opens the book with a thump of cover on lectern to where the leathery but still intestinal bookmark waits to greet her, causing the candle’s flame to jump.

The page is blank, and as she leafs back through the book with rising panic she finds that all those before it are as well.

‘But…’ she objects.

‘Again, what would you expect?’ asks the librarian. Again, not rhetorically.

‘There should be writing! There! They should be there!’ she protests, gesturing at the pages.

‘My dear, that would defeat the entire point, would it not?’ the librarian suggests. ‘You stand in the presence of a semiotic shredder, each page a transcendental one-time pad, unrecoverable encryption, a subtraction of information from the cosmos. In comparison, a supermassive black hole is Times Square.’

She is dumbstruck for a moment.

‘I assume Times Square is still a thing? In your United States. Of America?’ it remembers to check.

She nods distractedly, focused on the book.

This is really happening.

She re-opens the book to the page with the bookmark.

‘I just write it here?’ she asks in a voice finally hushed enough for both the sense of occasion and the oppressive silence of the library.

‘It joins the others,’ answers the librarian. She moves to speak, but it cuts her off—‘I mean merely to agree. Yes, it will be gone.’

‘I brought a pen?’ she tells the librarian sheepishly.

‘That is good. You would not believe how many forget one.’

‘It’s a Sharpie,’ she says. It absorbs the fact without reaction.

She scrawls on the page, not moving the bookmark before doing so. The marks sit there, mocking her as they always do, making her feel sick in her stomach and tight in her chest.

‘And now you close it,’ the librarian instructs, before she can finish drawing a breath to ask.

The book’s pages slide closed with a percussive puttering under her hand. The candle flame dances in response.

‘You know what you will find if you open it. If,’ says the librarian, ‘you have a piece of paper in your possession you will find that it is as you hope.’

She had not thought to do that, and her face betrays this.

‘If not your own as a test, then state papers,’ suggests the librarian.

Her face lights up and she fumbles in her messenger bag. ‘My driver’s license,’ she says distractedly.

She draws the battered card out of her purse and looks at it. Her breath catches in what is the start of either laughter or crying.

‘When you are ready I will lead you back,’ the librarian tells her with a cold disinterest that is functionally equivalent to kindness.

Sooner than she thought she would be, she is.
