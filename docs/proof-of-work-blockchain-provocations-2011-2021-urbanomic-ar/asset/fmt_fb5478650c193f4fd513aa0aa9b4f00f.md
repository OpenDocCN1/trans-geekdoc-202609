
This relates to magic because cryptography and magic go together at liminal moments in history. In the early modern era cryptography had to separate itself out from witchcraft. A lot of the early cryptography manuals in Europe served a double purpose as magical summoning guides—serious talking-to-angels-summoning-demons-creating-magic-squares kind of thing. Now, it may be the case that this was simply to conceal the cryptography, or it may be the case that the government agents of the time were concealing their cryptographic knowledge by pretending to be wizards. This would be fairly high risk given that you’d be burnt for being a wizard, but lower risk than the King or Queen being horribly displeased with you. Take John Dee, bumming around Europe trying to turn lead into gold for various monarchs and sending back magical writings to England—it makes a lot more sense as the activity of a secret agent than that of someone who was really easily taken in by anyone with a black mirror.

So at liminal moments the two are confused, and then they separate as things settle down. We see this again in the Victorian era: Edgar Allan Poe was big both in cryptography and in spiritualism. After Poe died, for a while one of his most popular poems was one that was written posthumously and channelled by a spiritualist medium. There’s the idea that the book of nature is encrypted and that to access it we have to break the encryption on it—a veil of ignorance that separates us from the spirit world and the afterlife.

Today, cryptographers are still mediating figures between the known and the unknown. If you take a sociological view, they do have this priest-class role in terms of their responsibility for communing with the unknown to underwrite the social order. This is why they appear to be such morally fraught figures; this is why the question of access to cryptography is so morally fraught for people who want trusted third parties in our communication that can stop us doing Bad Things as conceived of by Good and Nice People who of course never overreach, misunderstand, or have false positives.

So magic and cryptography look like one another, they both have these weird symbols and systems, apparent nonsense comes out of the other end of both, and yet it is claimed that communication is happening. They are both regulated by the state up to the modern era in various oppressive ways, and they are both concerned with access to an outside or an underneath.

To be more demonic about it, the dark forest of cryptography, this cryptographic hyperspace through which we send messages to be read by the recipient but no one else, embodies the same human desire as magic, the same masculine design on secrecy and power, but in a very efficient way.

Over time, the legal and social rhetoric around the threat and promise of cryptography is remarkably stable. Since the early modern era the same arguments have been given each time, with the same players and the same outcomes, and this rhetoric always comes back when you have these liminal moments where cryptography and magic, however conceived, are tangled together again. This rhetoric goes back to the early modern era, when the state was concerned with being able to decrypt communications between individuals and individuals were concerned with being tried by the state and the secret communications that they had produced being decoded by the state. The questions of privacy, of secrecy, of disclosure, and of methods of cryptography as popular entertainment and as a means of securing state commercial and personal communications, all of these go back to the late sixteenth century and recur in the Victorian era and then again in the nineties with the cypherpunk era. Each time the rhetoric is the same: the rhetoric of big numbers, of saving people with a timely secret communication, of harmful secrets. The cypherpunks certainly made the correct and winning latest move in this ongoing struggle, but we’re seeing it again now, just twenty, thirty years later with the state insisting that they should be able to read all of your communications: Trust us. No. We’ve been doing this for hundreds of years and if you had any historical literacy whatsoever you’d know that you’re being assholes: don’t do it, just for once learn from history.

With public key cryptography, we’re again dealing with a kind of delamination. There is something curious about it since it gives you both absolute secrecy and absolute identity, because the two don’t go together in the popular imagination. They’re not a mathematical product of cypherpunk ideology, they were deduced first by `GCHQ`, but the British can keep secrets so they didn’t tell anyone—and about two minutes later by some American researchers. The technology was liberated in the early nineties. This access to technology made it possible to imagine a society where you didn’t need the state to guarantee your private communications or your identification to others—you don’t need to show your papers, you don’t need a mailing address on the voters’ registration list. And, taking that and running with it, money is communication: money, as the American courts found if you’re a corporation trying to corrupt the political process, is speech. This is correct and interesting in ways that individuals have yet to fully benefit from. Cryptographic private speech—not private language in the Wittgensteinian sense, more like twinspeak but between an infinite set of twinned correspondents—allows you to imagine that you can be secure and private and identified against all areas of state overreach and interference.

Once again, Satoshi’s assemblage of different technologies just so happens to enable the completion of this part of the cypherpunk project. But it has this massive surplus value that we are still working through today. And for me, this technique, the combination of technology in these ways that speak to these anxieties and fit into this history of ideas and history of rhetoric, it seems like a useful irritant.

Then there’s obviously the problem of where religion goes when we’re all terribly smart post-enlightenment materialist thinkers. If you accept the theory that Satoshi was Hal Finney, this is someone who was terminally ill and is now cryogenically frozen in a transhumanist afterlife with the promise of resurrection. And Bitcoin happens to be a good way of paying for that because it’s a futureproof currency…. So the American spiritual landscape may feed into Bitcoin, or at least Bitcoin comes from that milieu—a milieu that is not just cypherpunk but where spiritual engagement takes the form of transhumanism.
