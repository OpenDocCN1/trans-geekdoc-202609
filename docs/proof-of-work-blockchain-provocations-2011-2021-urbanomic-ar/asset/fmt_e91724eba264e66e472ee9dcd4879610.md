
# Interview with Rhea Myers

In early crypto art, seeing the retreat from the art market and the attempt to create an alternative economy and life world, I recognised that from Conceptualism. And my other reference was the net art era, where artists had got into this media of communication exchange and exploitation first: the net artist got there before massive corporate exploitation and made it so that, from the get-go, you couldn’t just go on the internet and unproblematically consume ‘content’ without an alternative. They had a shared interest in the use of communications technologies. Glueing those two things together and recognising the idealism from the cyberculture moment in London, I was ready to, and had a desire to, demonstrate to the art world that there was something of critical and possibly organisational interest to the blockchain, and to try to explain to the blockchain people that there was something of organisational and possibly technological interest in the art world. So I’ve been trying to rub these two groups together to produce weird mutant offspring, to generate comprehension. What I’m doing is this double-A-side of critique of the institution of art and critique of the institution of technology and of the ideology of both.

**MBK** As it develops and intervenes in or substitutes itself for social relations, technology disassembles, analyses, and in effect critiques the social fabric, in the sense of disabusing it of claims to transcendence and reproducing its operations immanently as machine parts. In doing so, at the same time it also unbundles concepts, and your art and writing often hinges on demonstrating how blockchain technologies and crypto separate elements of social relationships that we thought belonged together by definition: property, ownership, unique reference to something and disclosure of its content, authenticity and authentication, social trust and the social construction of value, provenance and third-party authorities. All of these are things that you use the work to drive a wedge in between, or to show how blockchain technology is in fact already doing so.

**RM** This is what I refer to as ’delamination’. You can’t say that these things definitely go together now. Blockchain technology does accelerate the process of decoding, disrupting how these things work and how we assume they cluster together. And you can emphasise different bits of the cluster to get different takes on the technology, which make strange the phenomena we choose to try and capture or represent.

The interesting thing for the blockchain ‘event’ (pace Badiou), the publication of the Bitcoin White Paper, the creation of the Bitcoin software, is that it is very much an assemblage—it’s a copying and pasting together of existing technologies, which it places together in a unique new way to solve the problem in the cypherpunk political project. A lot of what we talk about when we talk about Bitcoin is surplus value of that code: the White Paper doesn’t say blockchain, it doesn’t say cryptocurrency. The White Paper talks about making a ‘timestamp server’, that is its technical contribution.

But as I argue in [’Blockchain Poetics’](#27-Blockchain-poetics.xhtml), what the White Paper specifies is a combination of very finely tuned different capabilities. If you emphasise one or the other of these capabilities more or less, you get something different: for instance, you just get distributed ledger technology because you don’t care about the ability to enforce the rarity of the currency. Or, if you emphasise the solution to the double-spending problem, which is the problem of the fake in art, if you lean into that, then you get NFTs. If you read the White Paper, you can see how each paragraph that Satoshi writes on the way to this timestamp server has so much surplus value, it weaves in so much possibility, so many affordances.

**MBK** Like an engine that you can tune in different ways to get it to perform differently?

**RM** Yes, and my only argument to the Left really is, for God’s sake, understand that engine, otherwise you will get played.

Nick Land talks about it in terms of subtraction. Bitcoin subtracts the need for a trusted third party. Money as previously understood is something issued by a trusted party that we can then exchange, to the degree that they allow us to. If we subtract that trusted third party then in theory we shouldn’t have money. The fact that Satoshi, to a greater or lesser degree, successfully does so, performs the operation of critique.[8](#1-interview.xhtml#footnote-224)

**MBK** In the sense that it demonstrates that the immanent operation of money is not necessarily dependent on a transcendent instance, on something that stands above it and is claimed to be the condition for its functioning.

**RM** Land’s relating of the technology of Bitcoin to such philosophical problems, and to his own political projects, and the fact that you can do that, shows what an interesting object Bitcoin is. It is a screen for the projection of hopes and fears, it does fit into the history of ideas, it does have a design aesthetic, and the problems that it tackles have their analogues in the problems that the market introduces into art, problems which ultimately are simply a projection of the patriarchal anxiety of primogeniture: the double-spending problem is simply Is this my child? Just as, in the art market, the fake is simply: Have I continued my line or have I been cuckolded? These are the anxieties attached to patriarchal society, a society which tries to find answers to them through vast amounts of money and/or power and/or violence, in order to reassure the male reproductive subject that actually, yes, he is secure: this security system for humanity conceived of as Man will look after you and make sure that what you are entitled to has not been stolen from you, that your offspring have not been right-clicked.

**MBK** But isn’t the timestamp server a way of securing provenance?

**RM** Absolutely, it operationalises post hoc ergo propter hoc, it produces a universe in which one thing follows another and you know that is true. You cannot spend something twice, which means you cannot spend it out of order, which means that if you are receiving currency then, over time, your certainty as to the security of your possession of it tends to grow. Now, to be clear, if the Vogons arrived tomorrow and had 278 trillion hashes of power, they could very quickly rework the order of transactions and exclude blocks. But they still couldn’t spend anyone else’s coins—unless they have a solution to the discrete log problem, something which we currently have our fingers crossed doesn’t exist.

**MBK** How about property, what happens to the concept of property here?

**RM** Land makes the argument that property didn’t truly exist before public key encryption: public key encryption is the thing that makes it possible for there to even be private ownership or control proper, with control of resources conceived as ownership in the absence of a state guarantor that can revoke that right.

**MBK** That’s the move you make at the end of [‘Recanting Quasiproperty’](#46-Recanting-quasi.xhtml), saying that whereas in earlier texts you had protested against the idea that Bitcoin is property, in fact what we used to call property was a kind of contaminated form of property, which Bitcoin distils into its pure form. But this does bring us back to the question of how this absolutisation of property could be some kind of a leftist project.

**RM** There are two things here: firstly, if you want to have any kind of critical purchase on society you need to understand what you are trying to critique. And the other thing is that it’s here, so let’s at least understand what can be done with it. And how we can ironise it or extend it or judo-throw it into something we can make use of.
