
This is implemented by applying CCV’s JavaScript face detection algorithm to SHA-256 digests represented as greyscale pixel maps. An industrial-strength version would use OpenCV. Owing to the limitations of face detection as implemented by these libraries, the digest pixel map must be upscaled and blurred to produce images of the size and kind that they can find faces in. The difficulty can be varied by altering the size and blur of the pixmap. Or by only allowing particular detected face bounds rectangles to be used a set number of times.

If Facecoin was turned into an actual cryptocurrency and widely adopted, the two value identity systems of cryptocurrency and art would be trivially but critically mapped onto each other by millions of machines churning out imaginary portraits across Facecoin’s peer-to-peer communication network as part of its financial network, and vice versa.

If you understand Facecoin you understand proof-of-work cryptocurrency’s block production and some of its critiques at a reasonable level of abstraction. I made it to understand this, so hopefully it can help you understand as well.

# Monkeycoin

## 2014 HTML5 and JavaScript

![](image/monkeycoin.jpg)

Monkeycoin is the follow-up to Facecoin. It implements a web-page version of a proof-of-work algorithm for Bitcoin-like cryptocurrency that uses trying to write the complete works of Shakespeare as its puzzle.

Literature and drama are proof of aesthetic work.

Monkeycoin uses attempting to write the complete works of Shakespeare as its proof-of-work function. This is implemented by converting Bitcoin-style SHA-256 cryptographic hash digests of block data to ASCII and checking the results against the first few characters of the First Folio (after dedications and credits), which is the start of The Tempest.

The difficulty of finding new blocks is increased by requiring successively more characters from the text of the complete works of Shakespeare to be matched.

# Blank Canvas

## 2016 Ethereum DApp

![blankcanvas](image/blankcanvas.jpg)

A blank canvas that anyone can set the colour of on the Ethereum blockchain.

This was a sketch for Democratic Palette, and a borrowing of the history of the blank canvas in modernist art. It is more complex than Is Art despite appearing to contain a similar amount of information. A single colour is a single piece of information, but in computer science terms it is usually more than a single bit of information—one bit can represent black and white, for example, but to get close to the millions of colours that modern computer graphics use takes twenty-four bits.

# The Legend of Satoshi Nakamoto: TORCHED H34R7S aka 1Flamen6

## 2015

## (Collaboration with Marguerite deCourcelle)

![](image/1FLAMEN6.png)

Marguerite deCourcelle, The Legend of Satoshi Nakamoto, 2015. Oil pastel on canvas.

As a child I loved Kit Thompson’s book Masquerade (1979), even though the solution was a mystery to me. I also had the Usborne book The Knowhow Book of Spycraft (1975), which included sections on encoding secret messages in the visual environment. So when I discovered Marguerite deCourcelle’s ‘crypto puzzle trail’ paintings via Twitter I found them both a very new solution to the problem of making art and assembling an audience—particularly for painting—in the crypto space, and a way of engaging that audience using methods I recognised from my childhood.

Marguerite invented and popularised the crypto puzzle trail, a cultural event in which a community follows a sequence of puzzles, competing and/or collaborating to solve them. The puzzles may be contained in artworks, games, encrypted messages, or some combination of the three. The reward for solving each puzzle may be reputation, cryptocurrency, or (in modern crypto puzzle trails) NFTs. Crypto puzzle trails were an important early means of engaging audiences with cryptocurrency beyond its immediate use as a medium of exchange or speculation.

I became one of Marguerite’s collaborators on her crypto puzzle trail ‘The Legend of Satoshi Nakamoto’, helping to encrypt some of its chain of clues in accordance with her vision. For the last puzzle in the series, Marguerite knew the iconography she was going to use, and we had been surprised by how quickly her community had cracked the last couple of puzzles, so we wanted to come up with something slightly more complex encoded in it.

We knew that people were looking for one-bit binary encoded information, so we decided instead on a six-bit encoding that used variations in the colour and shape of the painted flames that bordered the canvas. To add a very obvious clue, we placed into the painting a six-bit key which the data had to be XOR-ed against, encoded as ribbons tied to a key. The key is a key, it’s six bits, and it’s encoded in wavy red forms that are a bit like flames. We thought that transcribing the information from each edge of the painting and arranging it in the right order would provide more of a challenge.

The encoded solution to the puzzle was a Bitcoin cryptographic private key, chosen to produce a Bitcoin ‘vanity address’ starting with ‘1Flame…’ to fit the theme of the painting. This was prefixed with a short message in English to help the reader establish that they were on the right track. I wrote some JavaScript code to turn the solution into a list of written painting instructions, which Marguerite then followed, painting just under two hundred flames without error. Marguerite placed the prize, an amount of Bitcoin worth around 1200 US dollars at the time, into the Bitcoin address generated from the private key. She announced the puzzle on Twitter, and we settled in for what we thought would be a couple of weeks of people trying to crack the puzzle.
