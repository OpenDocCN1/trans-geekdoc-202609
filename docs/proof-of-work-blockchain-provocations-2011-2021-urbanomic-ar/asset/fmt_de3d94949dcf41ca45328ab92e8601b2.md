
Early NFT art that was not simply existing illustration work registered onchain became steeped in blockchain promotional imagery—project logos and figureheads. These were soon joined by easily consumed attentional lures appealing to the preexisting sensibilities of viewers/potential purchasers, such as naked human bodies and technological glitches. In the rare cases where the problem of their technical production and content intruded, it was when an artist or a buyer did not understand the source of a library image or of a filter’s stylistic flourish. This was a problem because of the concept of ownership as transcendental possession and control that blockchains embody. Blockchains exist to establish ownership of digital assets with mathematical certainty. This means that to own a piece of NFT art is to possess something unique with cryptographic certainty backed by a sizeable percentage of the Earth’s computing power. It is to possess it more securely than if it were stored in a vault (en-crypt-ed) at a maximum-security freeport facility, and for the object of that possession to be a more uniquely identified object than the Mona Lisa. Where the thing that you possess is a copy, a fake, or misrepresents its production or its materials in some way either intentionally or unintentionally, the unspoken contract of ‘true digital ownership’ is broken offchain. Or at least ironised and degraded in the hodler’s eyes.

Discussions of authenticity (as opposed to authentication) in ‘authentic digital art’ emerge as a panicked response to this encounter between onchain mathematical certainty and the messy nature of human understanding offchain. To respond to this by securing authenticity offchain is to destroy the value of onchain security entirely—you may as well have a paper certificate of authenticity. A cypherpunk dark forest of aesthetics, completely disjoint from meatspace concerns of identity, reputation, and career, would firewall artistic value from authenticity—a liberation that would upset art historians as much as any existing market. But security thinking can be a cold, lonely, corrosive lifestyle and is not for everyone. Or even for most.

A different approach is required in relation to the slow recuperation of the cypherpunk imaginary that is wider society’s encounter with it, an approach more engaged with the affordances and resonances of blockchain technology. This is not an ideological prescription, it is a recognition of the factors that drive every artist and collector/purchaser’s attraction to NFT art and which must show returns if NFT art is to be coherent. As transaction fees rise ever higher, neither preestablished iconography nor discrete stylistic flourishes sufficiently establish aesthetic value worth securing and owning on a blockchain as blockchain art. Art is proof of work, whether it’s Whistler’s Nocturne or an Abstract Expressionist act of heroism. There must be something to own, a parsable product of labour that represents the potential to exponentially multiply the value of an investment of time by the artist and of cryptocurrency by the collector. Strategically, the artwork has very few moves at its disposal to achieve this. One such move (a modernist/postmodernist one) is to dramatise and reflexively intensify this experience of the possession of something that lives in a world of discrete technological time. The more vivid this experience of ownership can be made—the more parsable, although absolutely not the more easily digestible—the better.

This has led to the accumulation of significant form in various registers (aesthetic, technical, economic, social) for excess/surfeit, materiality/presence, and presentation/encounter in NFT art. The in-frame agglomeration of this is reminiscent of the bricolage of Post-Internet Art, its careful leading of the viewer reminiscent of motion graphics in advertisements, cut scenes, and other liminal moments of guided significance. Where assets and shaders being from libraries would be an impediment to true ownership when presented in a two-dimensional artwork consisting of the mere conjugation of one or two of them, their composition and animation in time—the work that they do and represent—within the current wave of NFT art performs the requisite alchemy to make it clear that it makes the unownable ownable. Materials, interactions, choreography, are assembled into brief microworlds boasting an aesthetic surfeit that makes very little sense as anything other than an answer to the problem of NFT art at this point in its development, but makes a great deal of sense as that.

There are limits to this (and any) strategy, but we are not there yet. Intensification of or finding lines of flight from the elements of this strategy, in sum or individually, can take us very far beyond the current state of the market now that we have broken this resistance level. Capitalism loves challenges to its property regime, they are how it ensures its further expansion. True digital ownership requires there to be something to own. Unproblematic commodities, consisting either of artistic originality or of commercially manufactured aesthetic swatches, do not provide a sufficient challenge or object upon which to hang the predicates of desire for an ever-intensifying experience of possession. The fact of the digital aside, this dynamic was as true of paintings of fruit or of music and dance based on peasant forms as it is of GPU-accelerated aesthetic flights of value.

High culture is a strategy of enclosure, of proprietisation, of the transubstantiation of kitsch/low forms in an affirmation of the wider economy’s transubstantiation of resources into value. It is also a heatsink—symbolic resolution or closure for the untenable claims or other moments of a given (property) regime. You can see far more of the former than of the latter in the current state of NFT art, but the latter is present precisely in that art’s engagement with the psychological requirements of ownership. Just how far the latter goes will depend upon how blockchain technology engages with wider society over the next few years.

True digital ownership as a memeplex that collapses itself into aesthetics by assembling materials and competences to be deployed in an intensive experience that can be owned onchain has produced an experience of moments of post-digital surfeit as an answer to the demands of artificial digital scarcity.

This is the world as it exists today.

# Blockchain Temporalities

## 2021

Every ten minutes on average, Bitcoin’s miners add a new block of transactions to a chain that stretches back to the ‘genesis block’. Other blockchains may have faster heartbeats, their pulsions targeting just a couple of minutes or even a single second. But they all move forward only, from zero blocks in the direction of time’s arrow.

Block formation time is probabilistic, part of a cybernetic process that converges on certainty over time as more and more blocks build on top of an existing chain. This process is irreversible, owing not just to its use of probability but also to the trapdoor-function-based proof of work system that secures the Bitcoin blockchain. Its products, the blocks, can be walked trivially via the chain of hash values between them. But only backwards. The angel of history has very little to do onchain, but a general-purpose time machine is useless. Block history needs and can have no theory internal to itself. To observe that this isn’t what history or a theory of history is, is to identify one limit of historical thought.
