
## RM

One of the arguments I make is that property regimes are generative. It's not the case that certain entities come into being and we then create a property regime to describe them. We create a property regime and then this generates, in a very real way, the object to which it applies. It may be that there are a few initial exemplary or liminal objects that it's intended to cover for some special interest or other. But once it becomes possible to describe things that were not previously describable as property, people will track that singularity and it will start to generate these objects—like a market Stalinist executive demanding that you measure particular metrics….

## MBK

I think that's what Deleuze and Guattari, reading Marx through the psychotic Judge Schreber, call 'miraculation':[^1] in every type of society there's a recording surface upon which social acts and the organisation of bodies and the earth are inscribed (for us, money), and eventually it seems as if everything is emanating from that recording surface, and its markings become the 'quasi-cause' rather than the effect of everything that happens in the social world—it naturalises itself.

## RM

Yeah. Depending on how you rotate the time crystal, this is autopoesis. The hyperstition of property creating itself. Here you have a blockchain timestamp server producing this Deleuzian empty repetition of time—every block is the same, block after block, every ten minutes. You have festivals, you have halvings of block rewards for example, and hard forks. But at the block level there are no seasons, there are no birthdays, there are no jubilees. This sort of very capitalist linear clocktime feeds very nicely into Anna Greenspan's idea of capitalism's transcendental time machine,[^2] because this quantisation, this meting out of time, once again, is generative.

## MBK

It is linear and empty time in one sense, but it's also not, because every block 'contains' every other block that's gone before it, so there's a kind of continual intensification.

## RM

Yes, the being of blockchain is intensified with each block, each block makes it more of a thing, it would take more work to undo it, to rewrite its history or to destroy it or to reallocate the value within it, everything becomes more difficult to rework over time. This is the advantage of proof of work: it would take an appreciable chunk of the earth's computing power to rewrite even a fraction of Bitcoin's blockchain and you still wouldn't be able to spend other people's coins. You could prevent them ever having existed but you couldn't send them to someone else and pretend that they sent them. So the existence of the blockchain intensifies, and certainly the amount of value within each block can be said to be more or less intense if we treat monetary value here as a pure intensive quantity. We are however in a smooth space: there's nothing within the Merkle tree of the block which says, this block has this hash, because that's impossible. There is an inside and an outside, an onchain and an offchain.

Certainly, in one sense all the information that you need to understand the blockchain is onchain, but then to interact with it you need the private keys, which are offchain and must not be onchain otherwise you can break the system. Thinking about on- and offchain in terms of natural/supernatural or phenomenon/noumenon is fascinating—it's trivial, you know, it's a card trick in philosophical terms, but it's kind of evocative and it's one of those things which, if you press on it, helps to unpack assumptions. You have this inside world of transactions with an ontology enforced mathematically, and then offchain you have this wild domain that nothing onchain can know of, except as cryptographic signatures transmitted from it.

And again this can be a useful irritant, part of disobedience-in-obedience. We must have a realm of pure mathematical understanding and reason, and this can then bring justice to our messy society. Hey, here's a blockchain! No, not like that! Okay, but then in what way? Just...not like that!

## MBK

Two elements that blockchain brings together in a somewhat paradoxical manner are fungible currency and the concept of a ledger in which information is retained forever—they seem to run counter to one another, because the key thing about fungible money is that, once you've done whatever you do and received payment for it, that money is just as good as anybody else's, no one knows where it came from and it doesn't carry any information beyond its exchange value. *Pecunia non olet*. In a sense, money, or cash at least, is precisely a machine to wash information away, so how does that fit with a ledger that is transparent, public, and perpetual?

## RM

Right—to quote Barbara Kruger, 'Money is an information-destroying medium', which is the point of it. In the third part of Liu Cixin's Dark Forest trilogy, where the solar system is being collapsed into the second dimension—which freaked me out for days after reading it—that dimensional reduction, the image of Jupiter leaching out into the second dimension like an ice cream on a pavement on a hot day, that's money.

Firstly, Bitcoin is not anonymous money, so it doesn't quite solve the cypherpunk requirement. It is pseudonymous money: you can tell which address has carried out which transaction. Satoshi said to use one address for each transaction, do not reuse addresses. There are good cryptographic key safety reasons for doing so. But also, doing so allows us to hold on to the early internet pseudonymity of not having a tied-down singular identity. So if we were using bitcoin as Satoshi told us to, then you would have this sort of pseudonymous thicket of transactions which you can absolutely see, but which would not be tied to singular addresses, and you would just need to make sure that you do not leak that this address is actually you.

The public ledger is a means to an end of enforcing transaction order; the point of Bitcoin is to produce the transaction server, not the ledger as such. Satoshi said that for efficiency of space reasons, once you have spent all of a transaction's outputs you don't need it anymore, you don't need to validate past that point, and you can dispose of that information. So keeping this information and making it available in block explorers is absolutely something that Bitcoin as a technology doesn't prevent, but it's not an intended outcome of it.
