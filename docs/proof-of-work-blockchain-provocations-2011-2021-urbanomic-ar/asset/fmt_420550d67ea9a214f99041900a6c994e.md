**RM** Again, it’s all about names. Hashing gives you a true name for some information that you can use to summon it, but which you can use to refer to it without revealing its content—you can share it with others without them knowing what it is. As I say in [‘Crypto Sigils’](#8-Crypto-sigils.xhtml), this is like a residual sigil as an information-destroying representation for an idea or an intent. [‘Crypto Sigils’](#8-Crypto-sigils.xhtml) describes an entirely serious bit of chaos magic, because it’s saying, take this popular technique from contemporary culture and apply it to the actualisation of your intent, to your coincidence intensification, which is what magic is and also what money is. But the reason it’s an interesting exercise is that it helps to understand how these things work, and it’s attempting to understand how they work in a way that isn’t going to melt down a nuclear reactor or lead to anyone being out of work or anything. It’s just this pocket universe where you can go into that realm, mix everything up, come back, and it’s concretised in different ways.

In [Blockchain Aesthetics](#66-Blockchain-aesthetics.xhtml) (2014–2015, 2019) I rendered transaction hashes as modern and postmodern aesthetic bromides. As I originally realised with the YBA generators I made, you can use those kinds of things because people will recognise them as signalling art. So we’ve got the crunchy candy shell, the reassuring protein shell of recognisable and recoverable aesthetics. And then we have the experience of the complexity of this system which, at the time I made it, was this unseen network which was understood by the people making it, but I felt there wasn’t an appreciation of it as a thing in the world that was really doing something.

Hashes are produced all the time, everyone pushing github is producing a hash, everyone using Bitcoin is producing a hash, Bitcoin miners produce trillions of hashes a day. But this means that hashing is something which has being, which has reality, through repetition, through the intensification of its application. And so rather than saying that the application of a hash is creative, I make the move of saying that the application of a hash is culturally resonant. It’s the same as using a pack of cards in the pack-of-cards era, or using a newspaper for bibliomancy in the mass-media era. Here we are in the cryptographic era, and what is the way that everything is touched? By hashing. And that gives me what I regard as a critically or genetically or projectally defensible thing that I am doing that is not simply cargo-culting the technology. I had to convince myself that it was legitimate to use this purely mechanical procedure as a link in the chain of production of meaning.

If you can get a hash which, like a vanity address, has curiously ordered properties that grab human attention—for example, if you represent your hash as any kind of numbers, if it has a lot of zeros at the start—that’s unlikely. Something unlikely is perceptually interesting, something perceptually interesting can be worked with creatively or reflectively and, because you have something unlikely, you have already started from the position of coincidence intensification. And this comes directly back to Bitcoin, because its proof of work algorithm is precisely modifying the inputs to a cryptographic hash algorithm until the output starts with a long and unlikely series of zeroes. Bitcoin mining is literally coincidence intensification, and as I say coincidence intensification is one definition of magic, particularly within chaos magic: polling chaos repeatedly until it produces something that’s perceptible.

So on one hand these gestures map very clearly onto the technology of Bitcoin, but on the other hand they take the work that Bitcoin does, in various senses of the word including the physics sense, and extract surplus value from what is already a coincidence intensification machine.

I’m not suggesting that I can take an external point of view on any of this, I’m involved and there are limits to rational investments in it and logical reasoning about it. We don’t have a clear view or a perfect theory for this because we don’t know how it turns out.

**MBK** Before we leave the question of magic, the other spook you invite in is the possibility of artwork-DAOs that have a kind of agency and are able to operate in the art world on their own behalf without human intervention. **RM** That’s very tricky, because the quasi-agentic model of art is in some ways key to the idea of art’s autonomy. The idea that artworks can somehow speak or enter into a dialogue with the viewer is very powerful, but it threatens to mislead our understanding of society. If we make this horribly literal via bot art—and I was working on bot art before blockchain—a DAO can absolutely be a quasi-agentic collection of rules, of embodied intent, and people lose their shit about those, even though they have no problem with charitable trusts or legal personhood for rivers. This representation and embodiment of intent by a particular model of code and a particular set of incentives is merely something that the blockchain makes cheaper for anyone to be able to do.

There are various different models for how DAOs could produce artworks which literalise or operationalise the quasi-agentic model of art. You then come back to the idea of embedded purpose that you get in chaos magic with servitors, and, back in computer software, with daemons, which run in the background and do things without you always asking them to. Again we’re going back and forth through the rhizome of the history of ideas from computer science to demonology to chaos magic to anarchist co-ops and the market anarchist idea that if you give people property rights over the sky then they’ll be economically incentivised to not burn it up. That all comes together and, again, doing this in art gives us a dry run for doing it for real, but with lower stakes.

LeWitt said: the idea becomes a machine that makes the art. DAOs are ideas made into machines, and they can produce something. Let’s take each of those steps, juice the surplus value from it, take it very literally, and see what we can do.

Why people who are interested in human organisation in all forms are not fascinated by a public plastic medium for making explicit organisational principles, I’m not sure. Sure, we can try and evolve bigger bladders so that we can win any given cooperative meeting, but, you know, the telegram was invented some time ago, the average conception of organisation in contemporary organisational circles is less sophisticated than in Ancient Greece, not philosophically but literally, technologically, in terms of the technology they use.
