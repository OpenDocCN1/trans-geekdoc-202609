# Satisfying Political Needs

“That's my, so I want it on my servers!” Because information has been the most important asset of many firms, occasionally parts of management have been reluctant to yield control of that to a central IT group. And that is not irrational: if you were in charge of maintaining a five-million mailing list and if that generated half of your firm's sales, then you might well want to see that data housed on machines by people who report directly to you.

Of course, on the other side of the story, there is the IT director who wants control of all the servers, and her reason is just as valid as you. If a busy server goes down and that affects the rest of the network, it’s her head on the block.

So, the department head or VP wants to control the iron and silicon that happens to be his data lives, but the IT director concerned with making sure that all data is safe and everything on the network works. Wins?—and that’s the “politics” part.

What does Active Directory do to help with the political problems? Well, not as much as would be preferred-there is “no” make the vice presidents get well “wizard”—AD’s variety of options for domain design gives the network designers the flexibility to build whatever kind of network structure they need. Got a small organization that would fit nicely into a single domain but one with server ownership? No problem, get her OU of her own domain. Got a firm with two moderately large offices with independent IT shops? Two domains and a trust relationship may be the answer. Because Windows Server 2012 R AD controllers don’t need much WAN bandwidth in comparison with earlier versions, you might find that a single domain makes sense because it’s easier to administer than two domains, and it’s not impossible from a network bandwidth point of view. And bandwidth utilization is our next consideration.

# Connectivity and Replication Issues

More and more companies don't live in one place. They’ve purchased another firm across the country, and what once two separate local area networks are now one with a WAN link. The design of Active Directory is affected by the available bandwidth across the WAN. The decision point on a single or multiple domains is determined by how fast the WAN link is. Fast is a relative term. Some designs can tolerate 10 Mbps bandwidth, while others can’t. This is primarily dependent on the size of the partition and how often it is modified.

If the WAN can handle it, then you can hook the two offices up and create one domain. Single-domain design is beneficial because it will be easier to administer. Each site will contain at least one controller that manages all the users and computers. If the WAN link is fast, the domain controller in each office can manage logins.

But those domain controllers must communicate with each other whenever something changes, as when a user’s password changes or an administrator creates a new account. This replication would occur across the WAN link.
