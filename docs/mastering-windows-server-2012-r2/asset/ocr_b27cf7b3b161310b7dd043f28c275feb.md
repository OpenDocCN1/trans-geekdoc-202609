Of course, if you're in one of these networks, you'll realize that your packets do it outside the router—what you may not realize is they do so by virtue of a NAT, which alters the source address to that the router will be willing to pass.

Originally, NATs would assume that only a certain number of internal users would be accessing the Internet at any time that number of external addresses was assigned to the NAT, and each time a user's traffic forwarded towards the Internet, that user's internal address was mapped to a free, publicly routable external address. For some organizations, this is still the way in internal systems become accessible to the outside Internet.

# SUBNETETING AND SUPERNETTING

Something that was realized early on the design of the IP class system that even a huge multinational corporation that might want a class A address would not actually have a physical Ethernet wire with 16 million devices authored. So, a scheme was developed whereby a larger network could be divided into several networks, or subnets.

The way to do this was to say that classes were no longer important; every host would have its own notion of how many of its addresses defined "network" and how many of its addresses defined "me on the network." Because this divided the binary address into two portions, not on an 8-bit octet boundary, they used a term from graphics processing and called it a mask. Specifically, this netmask. You can imagine it as a sheet of paper with holes cut in it (just like a mask you might make for your kids at Halloween), replacing those bits with.

In Figure 20.9, we are looking at the effect of the netmask 255.252.0.0 on the address 192.168.143.8. The netmask is equivalent to the binary string 1111 1111 1111 1100 0000 0000 0000 0000—0000—that's 14 bits set to 1 and 18 bits set to 0.
