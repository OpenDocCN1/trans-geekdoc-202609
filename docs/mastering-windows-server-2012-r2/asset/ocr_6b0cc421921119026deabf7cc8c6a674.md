# Real World Scenario

# THE CASE FOR PASSPHRASES

Microsoft has been talking about passwords for a few years now. There has always been debate about what the best approach is with passwords. People typically use seven or eight characters for their passwords. They've used complexity as capital letters, numbers, or symbols to strengthen the password. However, that very complexity makes passwords harder to remember and type. The result is that the desk has a fine time every Monday dealing with locked out users and password resets. This is made worse when you force people to change their password every 30 days, which is done you need the password to change before it could be cracked by an attacker.

The alternative is to use longer passwords, as 12 characters, with no enforced complexity. This lengthening makes the password mathematically stronger. You're already thinking that will never fly with management or with the users. Hold on a moment.

The key to solution is to advertise the concept of a passphrase. Up to now some people enter things like 'November1982-1' and increment every month when their password expires. How about telling people to enter something that means something to them or is easy to remember? For example, a person with a yellow Italian sports car might have 'my prancing pony is'. That's a long password, but it is easy for that person to remember, and it's easy to type. Here is the winner: the passphrase! You could advertise the concept of passphrases using posters, emails, settings, and so on if you decided to introduce the policy. 'Thanks to fine-grained password policies, you could bring it in gradually with sympathetic pilot users who could spread the good word on your behalf.
