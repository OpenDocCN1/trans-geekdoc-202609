# CHAPTER ONE -    
INTRODUCTION

 

## It is the Best of Times…

 

The subject of Artificial Intelligence (AI), or Machine Learning (ML), has been adequately researched since the 1960s, if not earlier, and it has recently received a lot of attention once again. While there could be several reasons for that (advances in computing, deep learning, and so on), what is most exciting for us is the disruptive nature of technology companies such as Google, Facebook, Coursera, etc.

 

Significant advances in technology, cutting-edge research, and discoveries daily are now readily available to anyone who cares to find out. The economy of sharing gives individuals and companies access to tools and information for research and product development, which would have only been available to a handful a few years ago.

 

The rising productivity and lowering barriers to entry have left many industries vulnerable to the threat of disruption. That, of course, is the opportunity; the challenge, however, is access to high-quality data.

 

In this chapter, we will take a look at some of the trading concepts that AI can handle.

 

Learning Objectives:

 

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

 

*   Explain how AI can be used in trading to quantify trading ideas and for improving existing quant strategies.

 

*   Identify inputs that can go into a machine trying to trade momentum.

 

*   Hypothesize and analyze to improve the performance of a carry portfolio.

 

## Trading

 

AI provides a generalized framework for quantifying our thoughts. While algorithmic trading has always used statistical tools, AI provides a set of tools that could quantify almost any trading idea. That, however, does not mean that the AI-based solution will necessarily be better or most optimal.

 

In our experience, AI is most suitable for problems where:

 

*   The volume of data is huge. How huge is huge? Empirically and speaking entirely from our experience, we saw benefits of using deep learning models as we increased our sample size from tens of thousands to 20–30 million.

 

*   The signal is weak, and the noise is high. We risk generalizing here but for most problems in trading, this is the case.

 

*   There is a structure to the data and there is information content to be learned. There is an old English adage, “if all you have is a hammer then everything looks like a nail”. AI is a very powerful tool, but when misused, it is fully capable of generating trading signals from purely random noise. We will discuss various potential pitfalls along the way. For one, we like to focus on data sets and trading strategies, which have an economic rationale to exist. We can use AI to quantify it or to improve an existing strategy.

 

Smaller data sets that have a much stronger signal and a lower noise content would probably be best tackled using traditional statistical tools. The huge benefit of using traditional statistical tools is that the models are interpretable, which is of immense value in trading.

 

## Momentum Trading: Buy High, Sell Higher

 

What is the economic rationale for momentum to exist? We believe it is deeply rooted in the process of price discovery. Fundamentals and news drive price action but we do not know with certainty the price level at which all fundamentals and news actions have been correctly and fully priced in. This, coupled with asymmetric information and understanding of markets among various investor types, has made it one of the best performing factors over several decades and in several asset classes.

 

Momentum is the first derivative of price. As a trading strategy, it is most prevalent, even today. The ambiguity lies in defining momentum, identifying the period where it is most prevalent, and identifying assets that will most likely benefit from it. As an insight, Figure 1.1 shows the astronomical rise of palladium futures in 2019 (60% in a matter of a few months). Figure 1.2 plots momentum defined as the one-day change in price. Figure 1.2 is much noisier than the seemingly linear/exponential price action in Figure 1.1. It is a challenge to fish for a signal in this noisy Figure 1.2 data set.

 

![A close up of a map Description automatically generated](images/00002.jpeg)

Figure 1. 1 is the futures price of palladium plotted against time.

Figure 1. 2  Momentum, which is defined as the day-on-day change in price.

 

The question is: what would we want to include to define momentum?

 

How should we define something as abstract as momentum?

 

### Price momentum

 

Price momentum can be defined in multiple ways. Who is to say that day-on-day change in price is the best way to define momentum? How about a week-on-week, a month-on-month, a year-on-year or for that matter any nonstandard period? Alternatively, how about using all the aforementioned and trying to fish for a signal in this extremely noisy data set? Essentially, we are implying that, since there is no known optimal way of defining momentum, we will accept all possible definitions to be legitimate, and use AI to learn a somewhat optimal definition.

 

### Momentum in related instruments

 

Palladium is a relatively small market and its price action is also affected by what is happening in gold, or what is happening to risky assets (S &P futures, for instance) in general or what is happening to the broad dollar index.

 

If you have spent some time at the trading desk (trading macro instruments), you probably only need to know what your one or two instruments of choice have done overnight and that will give you an indication of other instruments’ performance.

 

Therefore, to try and define momentum for palladium, we may also consider including momentum of related instruments, that is, momentum of gold, momentum of the broad dollar index, and, momentum of risky instruments (such as equity indices). So, if we originally had 100 ways of defining momentum, we now have another 300 (3 instruments × 100 definitions per instrument).

 

### Regime variables

 

The point to note is that this relation to other instruments may change with time.

On some occasions, a risk-on move may be associated with dollar weakness, which may be supportive of things such as palladium. However, the same risk-on environment being driven by a search for high yield/dividend bearing instruments may be bad for assets such as gold, which do not provide any yield/dividends. In addition, palladium, being a close ally of gold, may actually suffer in performance.

 

Therefore, we may want to add information about sentiment or information about the regime that the market is currently in and this further adds to our data set.

 

### Market microstructure

 

We may also consider adding information about the market microstructure to our definition of momentum. For example, Figure 1.3 gives a snapshot of what the level one order book looks like. Let us go through it step by step.

## Carry Trading

 

Carry trading is a strategy that involves borrowing at a low-interest rate and investing in an asset that provides a higher rate of return. The hypothesis we are trying to establish with carry trading is whether assets with a positive carry appreciate over time. We do that in the succeeding text using a single instrument and not AI. Ideally, we would like to test this hypothesis over several instruments and several regimes. We then briefly talk about how AI could be used to improve performance of this portfolio. The aim of this exercise is twofold:

 

*   To understand what “carry” is.

 

*   To protect against the hammer and nail syndrome, where we use AI to improve, fine tune, and quantify.

 

### What is carry?

 

When we talk about carry, we essentially mean we are paid for owning an asset. There are various ways by which one can earn carry in financial markets. Portfolios constructed with the aim of benefitting from carry could include stocks that pay a high dividend, bonds that pay high coupons, short option positions - thereby receiving premium, and short positions in futures with a steep upward slope (contango), long position in futures with a steep downward slope (backwardation).

 

These strategies have attracted a fair bit of capital over the last several years driven by low benchmark rates and investors’ desire to earn a higher yield. The strategies have done quite well too; however, that is not to suggest that the future performance is also expected to be as good.

 

### Drivers of carry

 

Let us look at the risks and profile of one of these strategies. It will give us some insights into what we can do to enhance the performance of these strategies. We will analyze a short position in VX-Cboe Volatility Index (VIX) futures, which for the most part has been in a contango (upward sloping futures curve).

We will begin by clearly defining two types of returns that futures trading exposes us to:

 

*   Spot return

 

*   Roll return

 

It is best understood through a toy example.

### Trade 3

When the commodity increased from $100 to $104, we made $4 in “spot returns”. We bought the contract on March 1, 2019 at $100 and sold it for $104 on May 31, 2019, which is the spot return of $4.However, we did a few more trades.

On April 30, 2019, we sold the April contract for $102 and on May 1, 2019, we bought the May contract for $104, incurring another loss of $2.

 

The total loss on these inter-period trades is $4, which we call the “roll return,” which exactly offsets what we made as “spot return.” The total PnL on this trade is zero.

Additionally, there are models such as logistic regression and artificial neural networks where the scale of the features has a very significant impact on the solution.

 

Let us discuss an example to understand the intuition behind scaling of features. Let us say we have only two features: x1 and x2. A decision tree solution would typically be interpreted as follows:

 

1.  All samples with x1 < a and x2 > b belong to the same compartment and have a predicted value of .35. Here a and b are the parameters which the algorithm has solved for. All samples meeting this condition are in region 1.

 

2.  All the other samples have a predicted value of .75. All samples meeting this condition are in region 2.

 

Changing the scale of x1 by say a factor of 3, and x2 by a factor of 2 is only going to change the values of a and b by similar scales. The samples that belong to region 1 and region 2 will not change. In this case, the solution does not change as we are interested in the regions that a specific sample belongs to. In particular, we are interested in the samples that are grouped together.

 

A regression (scale-dependent model), on the other hand, has a solution such as

 

Y = F (a\*x1 + b\*x2)

 

The a and b values here are again the output of the model. If the scale of x1 and x2 changes, the values of a and b change and the output Y changes.

# CHAPTER  THREE -   
VALIDATION STRATEGIES AND   
PERFORMANCE METRICS

 

In the previous chapter, we have discussed ways to deal with various types of data that you may come across in finance. We have also discussed various preprocessing techniques.

 

In this chapter, we introduce a broad classification of various Artificial Intelligence (AI) algorithms. We then discuss a general framework on how to organize the data for appropriate learning. We also highlight some pitfalls that need to be avoided - some obvious, some not so obvious.

 

Broadly speaking, there are two types of learning algorithms: supervised and unsupervised.

 

As the name suggests, supervised learning has a “supervisor,” which is the target or the dependent variable that is to be predicted. All decisions that we make to design the machine are taken with the sole purpose of being able to accurately predict the target. All tools and tricks that we present and have presented will be useful in trying to improve the quality of this prediction. Needless to say, whether this target prediction meets a business objective is outside the scope of the prediction algorithm. Therefore, you may have a machine that has a great accuracy because the machine has been designed to predict just that. Whether that makes money, or whether that makes enough money to meet costs, or whether it has the right risk profile, is outside the scope of what the machine is expected to do.

 

It may seem obvious, but it is important that we reflect on it before we jump in and invest precious time in designing the algorithm. Therefore, we may be designing an algorithm to predict the probability of prices breaking a trend line. The business objective, however, is to make money. Does the prediction help do that?

 

At the other end of the spectrum, we have unsupervised learning. As the name suggests, in this case we do not have a supervisor or a target. We present  data to the machine and expect the machine to return some intelligence. For instance, the machine could cluster  data into similar subgroups. Dimension reduction algorithms come under this as well. Our further discussions shall not be focusing on this.

 

## Organizing the Data

 

Some of the algorithms we present are very powerful. Give them a dataset and they are very capable of learning everything and producing almost a zero-error prediction. The problem is that the zero-error prediction is limited only to the data that the machine has been trained on, known as the training data. If we use this machine on unseen data such as incoming market data, the prediction error skyrockets, and you quite easily infer that the machine has zero intelligence. We call this second type of data, the test dataset.

 

Therefore, we want a machine that works well on data that it has seen (training data) and does reasonably well on test data as well. However, does that mean we keep iterating through various possibilities such that we eventually find a machine that works well on the training data and the test data? The answer is “no” because if we do infinite iterations on the training data to get good results on the training and the test data, we have effectively trained the machine on both datasets, thereby merging the two.

 

We therefore need another data set, known as the validation dataset. We now do this back and forth iteration between the training and validation data, and once we have a satisfactory model doing well on training and validation data, we bring in the test dataset only once to see performance.

 

## Characteristics of Validation Data

 

*   It should be very similar in quality and distribution to the test data we are expected to perform well on.

 

*   Zero leakage between training data and validation data, between validation data and test data.

 

*   The samples should be IID (Independent and Identically Distributed).

 

A useful question that we like to answer while designing the validation data set is: If the validation data were our test data, would we still get the same results? And would we be able to realistically implement the exact same process of validation? Would all the information of the validation dataset be available when required so as to make necessary predictions for the validation dataset?

 

These seemingly theoretical considerations are extremely important and can be the reason behind why an algorithm works and why it does not. Before we discuss these considerations in more detail, let us discuss which validation methods are generally adopted.

 

## Validation Strategies

 

###  Separate validation data set

 

We divide the entire universe of data into three unequal parts, namely train, validation, and test. The training data generally is 60–75% of the entire set; the balance is split equally between test and validation data. The more important consideration is the validation data, which should be similar in distribution and characteristics to test data. This method of validation is simple and robust. However, there are two limitations.

 

*   Expensive: The training is forced to happen on a smaller data set, as about 20% is being kept aside for validation.

 

*   Regime-specific model selection: Let us say you are designing a strategy to trade gold futures based on end of day data. The previous 20 years would give us about 5000 samples, 3000 of which we keep for training, 750 for validation, and 750 for test. Generally speaking, 3000 samples will never be enough for most AI algorithms and definitely not enough for deep learning algorithms. Furthermore, we are forced to keep 3 years of samples aside for validation. More importantly, if our validation data included 2009, 2010, 2011 and the test data included 2012, 2013, 2014, then the validation period for most part was a quiet upward trend (except for a short period in 2011) and the test data were primarily a downward trend. The problem with this kind of a setup is that the validation data is inherently different in characteristics to the test data, thereby models expected to perform well on the validation data may actually perform poorly on the test data.

 

k-fold cross validation helps us address both these concerns.

 

### k-fold cross validation

 

1.  We divide the universe of data into train and test. The validation data is derived from the training data in the following manner: We RANDOMLY divide the training data into K(=3) equal parts, and call them A, B, and C. Then we perform three operations.

 

*   Train the model on datasets A and B and validate on dataset C.

 

*   Train the model on datasets A and C and validate on dataset B.

 

*   Train the model on datasets B and C and validate on dataset A.

 

K can assume any value like 3,5, and 10. This is how most software packages would implement it. K-fold cross validation datasets can be created with few lines of code.

 

![A picture containing knife, table Description automatically generated](images/00018.jpeg)

 

This splits the original data into difference subsets.

 

It may be obvious to the reader that this process of validation does address the two limitations discussed in the previous section. For most financial data sets however, this process is wrong and fails at multiple levels.

 

We have done things in the aforementioned process on the validation data, which we would not be able to do if the validation data were to be treated as our test data. We have also exposed our self to allowing leakages to occur between the training and validation data.

 

Let us take a simple example to get the intuition.

 

In our very small training data of 2 months, spanning January 1, 2018 to February 28, 2018, let us say we are trying to predict what the market did 5 days later. The target is set to “1” if it went higher, “−1” if it went lower, else it is set to ”0”. As features, all that we are providing is the daily market return for the previous 5 days.

 

1.  By RANDOMLY assigning samples from our training into k = 3 folds, we may very often be using data from the future to predict the past. That is something we obviously cannot do if our validation data were to be treated like our test data, or data that we are to see in the future. Therefore, we want to avoid RANDOM selection of samples into each fold.

 

2.  Independence of data splits: To understand this better, let us look at the following example: 

 

| FOLD || TRAIN DATA  |
| --- | --- |
| A || 1-Jan-18 || 2-Jan-18 || 3-Jan-18 || 4-Jan-18 || 5-Jan-18 || 8-Jan-18 || 9-Jan-18 || 10-Jan-18 || 11-Jan-18 || 12-Jan-18 || 15-Jan-18 |
|   || VALIDATION DATA………… |
| B || 16-Jan-18 || 17-Jan-18 || 18-Jan-18 || 19-Jan-18 || 22-Jan-18 ||   ||   ||   ||   ||   ||   |
|  ||  ||  ||  ||  ||  ||  ||  ||  ||  ||  ||  |

Figure 3. 1 Example of training and validation dates, which violate the independent part of the IID criteria.

 

A visual representation of our training and validation dates for a particular fold is shown in Figure 3.1 for calendar year 2018. The target for January 15(last day of our training data) is known only on January 22. At this point, we need to ask ourselves the ephemeral question: Can we use training data up to January 15 and predict the validation data on January 16? The answer is an obvious ‘no’ because on January 16, we do not know the targets for samples from January 9 to January 15. The information between January 9 and January 15 cannot be used for learning to predict an outcome on January 16. This definitely needs to be accounted for when including January 16 in our validation data. However, as stated earlier while designing our validation data set, we should look at it as a test data set. If it can serve as a test data set, it can serve as a validation data set. 

 

So why is January 9 to January 15 not suitable for training if January 16 is included in our validation data? What we need to appreciate is that if there is a very strong relationship between the targets from January 9 to January 15 on one hand and January 16 on the other, our machine will focus and pick up this relationship. We may be impressed by the validation accuracy, but the test data accuracy shall be poor. Importantly, if there is some actual intelligence in the training data, which could have resulted in discovering meaningful alpha for a test data set, we have obstructed that process. 

 

Very often, the machine will pick up the easier relations faster and first. If these easier relations are wrong, we may not be able to reach a stage where we actually allow the machine to focus on less obvious but valid relationships, which actually do exist.

## Classification Metrics

 

Building on the momentum setup we spoke about in Chapter 1; we may be interested in predicting whether palladium prices are expected to rise or fall the next day. If they are expected to rise, we assign those samples a class label of 1. Similarly, if they are expected to fall, we assign those samples a class label of zero. The classification machines will help us put a probability against these occurrences.

 

The output of any classification algorithm is a conditional probability. For a two-class classification problem denoted by 1 and 0, the output is:

 

P(Y\=1 | Feature set)  : Probability of Y equal to 1, given the Feature set.

 

P(Y\=0 | Feature set) : Probability of Y\=0, given the Feature set. 

 

Once we have a probability measure, we need to make an assumption of a certain threshold value, which is the probability value above which samples are assigned to class 1 and below which samples are assigned to class 0.

 

That is to say, if:

 

P(Y = 1 | Feature set) > Threshold implies class 1 or

 

P(Y = 0 | Feature set) > threshold implies class 0

 

What follows is a list of metrics best suited for a classification problem. Before that we need to understand a confusion matrix.

In a binary classification problem with class labels 1 and 0, we enter the number of samples that belong to each quadrant. Each quadrant contains samples, which satisfy both conditions of the actual and predicted class values, for example, quadrant 2 in the succeeding text will contain all those samples, which were predicted to belong to class 1 and actually belonged to class 0.

How many of these buy signals made money for us? Or, how many of these predicted buy signals belong to Class 1? Either way, we are interested in knowing how many buy signals we are getting compared to the total number of buy signals the market gives us. That is recall. That would be computed as the number of samples in quadrant 4 divided by the combined number of samples in quadrant 3 and quadrant 4.

Specificity is the number of samples correctly predicted to belong to class 0 divided by the number of samples actually belonging to class 0.That would be computed as the number of samples in quadrant 1 divided by the combined number of samples in quadrant 1 and quadrant 2 of the confusion matrix.

## Construction of Decision Trees - Regression setting

 

The decision tree algorithm essentially takes as input our tabular data and constructs the decision tree.

We expect the algorithm to yield two outputs:

 

1.  The split points that help us take a decision at each node or define the boundaries of the rectangular regions in the earlier representation. So one of our nodes could be taking a decision such as x<=2%. This split point of 2% will be output by the algorithm.

 

2.  The correct hierarchy of features to do the split. Which feature comes at the root, which comes at the nodes below the root and so on.

 

Let us take a cursory look into the algorithm that gives us these outputs. For the time being, we stick to a regression setting. This level of detail is necessary for innovative application of the algorithm.

 

We start by considering all the features as a potential candidate to form the root. For each feature, we consider various possible split points. For each possible feature and split point combination, we calculate the value of the Equation 4.1 across all the samples of the training data set.

 

MSE = ∑(Yi\-Yavg)2                                          Equation 4.1

 

Where 

 

Yi: the target for each sample

 

Yavg: the average value of the targets for each of the samples

 

Let us go back to our rectangular region representation to understand this completely. We start with feature F1 and take a split point as 1%. We denote the (feature, split point) combination as (F1,1%). We have four samples (1,2,4,5) in the region to the left of F1=1% and one sample (3) to the right.

For samples 1,2,4, and 5, we calculate Equation 4.1

 

Yavg \= 0 and 

 

∑(Yi−Yavg)2 = .025%                                           Equation 4.2

 

Likewise, for sample 3, we calculate Equation 4.1 as well

 

∑(Yi−Yavg)2 = 0                                                   Equation 4.3

 

We add the findings in Equation 4.2 and Equation 4.3. This calculation is repeated for each feature, and split point combination. The combination that gives the lowest recording forms the root. For purposes of this example let us say (F1,1%) forms the root.

 

The process detailed earlier is repeated to find the feature/split point combination for all the nodes. The only modification is that we limit ourselves to the samples of the region that we are trying to further subdivide. So, in our toy example, samples 1,2,4 and 5 are now clubbed together to form region A (with F1<1%) and sample 3 forms region B (with F1 >3%). We now focus only on region A to find the node for the second level of the tree. Region B contains only one sample and cannot be split any further which is effectively a terminal node or a leaf. This process is repeated until stopping criteria are met.

2.  The metric used for finding the feature, split point combination is no longer the mean squared error (MSE). We use either the Gini index or cross entropy.

# CHAPTER FIVE -   
ENSEMBLE METHODS

 

In the previous chapter, we have discussed the algorithm for constructing a decision tree in detail. It became apparent that this algorithm could overfit the training data easily. In this chapter, we will discuss modifications that can help us deal with the problem of overfitting.

 

There are several ways of reducing overfit in decision trees. These include the following:

 

*   Early stopping

 

*   Cost-complexity pruning

 

*   Bagging

 

*   Random forest

 

*   Boosting trees

 

Bagging, random forest, and boosting trees are the more popular and powerful approaches.

 

## Early Stopping

 

As discussed in the previous chapter, while constructing a tree we need to start by specifying a metric that we intend to minimize. For a regression problem, it could be mean squared error (MSE) and for classification, it could be cross-entropy. With early stopping, in case the MSE/cross-entropy stops changing by a predetermined threshold, we could stop the compartmentalization of the feature space. Alternatively, we could stop the further splitting of our samples and our tree would be ready. This is a shortsighted approach. The eventual aim of the decision tree algorithm is to get leaves that contain samples with a very similar set of target values. Accordingly, the leaves should be as pure as possible. Simply stopping the compartmentalization process because the MSE/cross-entropy did not change adequately at a particular level obstructs a potential split in the future, which could have created a pure leaf. This method is rarely deployed.

 

## Cost-Complexity Pruning

 

Cost-complexity pruning improves the short-sighted nature of the early stopping criteria. It starts by building a complete tree. From this complete tree, it creates various sub trees. Any one of the generated trees could be the optimal solution. The optimal tree is chosen by cross-validation.

 

What is interesting and important is a little bit of insight into the cost-complexity-pruning algorithm by which we are able to generate the subset of trees. The cost function that helps in implementing the cost complexity pruning algorithm is in Equation 5.1. 

 

\[∑(Yi - Yavg)2 + α |T|\]                               Equation 5.1

 

The first part of this equation does not require any explanation and is the same as discussed in chapter 4 . The α |T| is the regularization term where |T| represents the number of terminal nodes or leaves in the tree and α is a parameter that we need to provide. So, if α =0, Equation 5.1 reduces to simply minimizing MSE. The tree that we get is the entire un-pruned tree. However, as we start increasing α, there is a price to be paid for having a large number of terminal nodes and therefore Equation 5.1 will tend to be minimized for a tree with a smaller number of terminal nodes. As α increases, we get smaller and smaller trees, minimizing the value of the cost function. For instance, for α equal to infinity we would need T to be zero, reducing the tree to a single node. This way we are able to map α to |T| and hence the particular sub-tree.

 

The tree we would use is the one that gives the best performance on a validation data set.

 

## Bagging

 

Another way of reducing the overfitting or high variance problem in decision trees is bagging. Bagging is a two-step process comprising first of “bootstrapping” and then of “averaging”.

 

As illustrated in Figure 5.1, we start by creating several samples of the original dataset and then build an ensemble of decision trees on each of the datasets. The final ensemble of trees is just the average of all the predictions coming from each of these decision trees. The idea is that this process creates a set of uncorrelated trees - each learning some information and inevitably some noise. The averaging over several such trees would reduce the noise component and retain the information.

 

![](images/00037.jpeg)

Figure 5. 1 The concept of bootstrapping illustrated.

 

The process by which we extract multiple datasets from the original dataset is called bootstrapping. Let us say the original data set has m samples. We select n(<m) samples randomly with replacement. We do this b number of times. Therefore, we now have b data sets with n samples each, rather than one data set with m samples. We create b trees, one on each newly created data set. The final machine is an aggregation of the results from each of these b trees.

 

Now if this is a regression problem, we will simply average the result of each b tree, and if it is a classification problem, we will take a majority vote on the predictions made by each of these classifiers.

 

### Out-of-bag samples

 

Each sample from the original data set has either been used to create a particular tree or has been left out in the bootstrapping process. Accordingly, each tree has its own training data on which it has been built. Each tree also has some data that it was not seen and that can be used as the validation data. Therefore, to get the validation performance for all the b trees that we have built, we will simply aggregate the results of each tree on its corresponding validation data set. This is great and really data efficient, as we do not really need a separate validation data set.

 

Only one important issue needs some attention - while bootstrapping, we randomly select m (<n) samples with replacement. As we have highlighted earlier in this book, we are not entirely comfortable with this approach when dealing with time series data. Furthermore, as we are using our validation data to be out-of-bag samples, we are hindering the process of learning by destroying the time series aspect of this data set (that is what random selection does).

The samples are not independent of each other. As a reminder while designing our validation strategy, we need to be able to answer the following question: If our validation data were our test data, would we have all the information in time to predict our test data?

 

If the answer to the aforementioned question is “no,” then we are using a suboptimal machine to make our prediction. There may be information in the data, which we have not attempted to learn yet.

To resolve this, each time we do bootstrapping, we need to ensure that the samples selected are in order. A scheme that could be used is as follows (also depicted in Figure 5.2):

 

1.  Randomly select a number i from 1 to (n − m)

 

2.  Select i to (i + m) samples as a bag

 

3.  Repeat b times

 

![](images/00038.jpeg)

Figure 5. 2 Ideal way to bootstrap time series data.

 

Bagging derives its benefits from the following two sources:

 

*   Averaging over multiple predictors reduces variance.

 

*   Each bag forces the tree to focus on fewer samples and not just on the prominent ones, thereby forcing the tree to work hard to learn information content that may be overlooked.

 

Let us see what else we can do to deal with the high variance problem by using Random Forest.

 

## Random Forest

 

As mentioned earlier, one of the drivers of the bagging algorithm was that each tree was focusing on a different set of samples and was therefore forced to learn less obvious intelligence. The random forest does all of that and a little more. Figure 5.3 shows the steps.

 

While forming the nodes of a tree, we need to identify which feature we place at the node and at what point we split it. Our current algorithm has the option to choose this split at each node from all available features. Random forest changes that. We randomly select a subset of m features from the original p set of features (m/p ≤ 1). It is from this subset that we identify the best feature at a particular node. We no longer have the luxury of selecting a feature from the original p set of features. We are limited to selecting the best splitting candidate from the subset of features.

 

![A screenshot of a cell phone Description automatically generated](images/00039.jpeg)

Figure 5. 3 Random forest schematic diagram.

 

Random forest derives its benefits from the following two sources:

 

*   Averaging over multiple predictors reduces variance.

 

*   Each bag forces the tree to focus on a fewer samples and not just on the prominent ones, thereby forcing the tree to work hard to learn information content that may be overlooked.

 

What happens very often in data sets is that only a few features are correlated. Moreover, sometimes it is only a subset of the entire feature space that contributes to the bulk of the predictive power of the model. By focusing only on a smaller subset of the features each time a split is being made, the random forest forces learning from even the less prominent features. This not only reduces the correlation between the various tree models of the ensemble thereby reducing the variance when averaging is done, but also learns more valuable information from the data set.

 

## Boosting

 

Let us get straight to the algorithm first and then work out the intuition later.

 

The steps for a boosting algorithm are as follows:

 

1.  Fit a tree (T1) to the training data set.

 

2.  Calculate the residuals/errors/misclassifications.

 

3.  Fit a tree T2 to the residuals of T1.

 

4.  Update Model as T1 + shrinkage\*T2.

 

5.  Fit a model T3 to the residuals of T2.

 

6.  Update Model as T1 + shrinkage\*T2 + shrinkage\*T3.

 

7.  Fit a model TN to the residuals of TN-1.

 

Before we jump into more details, let us quickly understand what shrinkage is. It is a hyper-parameter (i.e. a parameter that needs to be given by the user) which also goes by the name of the learning rate. It helps us control the amount of learning (reduce overfit) at each stage that we want to incorporate into the final model. If the shrinkage is too high, we risk overfitting to the training dataset; if it’s too low, we risk underfitting and not learning anything. Moving on, the first intuition we need to understand is that this is a stage wise additive model. It is additive in the same sense as bagging and random forest, where the final model is an aggregation of predictions from N models.

 

Where boosting is different from bagging and random forest is that it is a stage wise additive model. T2 can be built only after T1, T3 can be built only after T2, and so on. In bagging and random forests, T1, T2, and T3 can be built simultaneously and are independent of each other. The same cannot be said about boosting. We first need to build T1, calculate the errors on the training data, build T2 to fit these residuals, calculate the errors of T2 on the training data, and then build T3 to fit to the errors of T2’s prediction on the training data. Accordingly, T1, T2, T3, and so on are dependent on each other and cannot be created in a parallel way. They need to be created serially one after the other.

 

Therefore, unlike most other algorithms where we fit the model on the independent variable, here we fit several models. The first is fit on the independent variable and the rest are fit on the errors of the previous model. So, let us try to understand why we need to build models to fit the errors of the previous model.

The basic construct of a stagewise additive model is as follows:

 

Fm(X)=Fm−1(X) + bm\*Tm(X,Ym−1) Equation 5.2

 

Fm(X) is the ensemble after mth iteration.

 

Tmis the tree built on the mth iteration.

 

Ym−1 is the error using Tm−1 as the predictor for m\> 1.

 

Ym−1 = Y, for m = 1.

 

bm is the learning rate that controls the amount of learning we want to take forward from each stage.

 

All that Equation 5.2 is saying is that a model at any stage m (Fm) is equal to the model at the previous stage m (Fm−1) and a new model Tm.

 

Why fit models on residuals of previous stage?

 

Now our loss function is a simple MSE:

![](images/00040.jpeg)

 

Using equation 5.2 we modify the loss function as 

 

![](images/00041.jpeg)(y,Fm-1(X) + bm\*Tm(X,Ym-1) )![](images/00042.jpeg)(y-Fm-1(X)- bm\*Tm(X,Ym-1))2

           = (rm - bm\*Tm(X,Ym-1))2

 

Therefore, the tree at the mth stage has to be built to fit the residual r of the previous stage.

 

Before we look at the practical implementation, let us quickly understand regularization in trees.

 

### Regularization

 

Regularization is a way of reducing the high variance problem. The objective is achieved by modifying the loss function. So instead of simply minimizing a loss function L we minimize a loss function L + gamma\*|T|, where |T| represents the number of leaves. The complexity of the tree is directly proportional to |T| . The gamma is a parameter that needs to be supplied by the user. If gamma is zero, then gamma \*T = 0, which effectively means we are left with the original loss function and hence do not intend to reduce the high variance problem. As we increase the value of gamma to say infinity, the only way we will be able to reduce the loss term from exploding is by reducing the value of T, which effectively means we are reducing the complexity of the tree. Hence, by varying gamma in the aforementioned equation we are in a position to control the complexity of the tree and hence influence bias variance.

BIAS VARIANCE TRADE-OFF

 

This is a short chapter, but we would like to dedicate a full chapter to this topic simply based on the universal applicability of this concept. By the end of this chapter you should be able to understand the bias variance trade-off, identify a biased machine, identify a high variance machine, and be able to implement the bias variance trade-off in any machine.

 

What do you do if your machine is not giving desirable results? We have an entire set of options. We could:

 

1.  Change features

 

2.  Change target

 

3.  Change machine

 

4.  Change hyper parameters

 

5.  Train longer

 

6.  Change validation strategy

 

We could also use any combination of the points. Each of these and their combinations has several sub options that significantly increases the number of things we can do to fit our machine. An exhaustive search is practically impossible. Understanding the bias variance trade-off gives us a framework wherein our search is more focused. Generally speaking, understanding the bias variance trade-off is the first step in troubleshooting most machine learning problems.

 

## Bias

 

As the name suggests, a biased machine is one that has a biased view of the outcome. At an extreme, the outcome does not even change with varying inputs. A machine such as the one defined in Equation 6.1 for independent variable X taking values between \[0,1\] is

 

P(Y = 1 |X) = 0.6 +.00002 \* X                                           Equation 6. 1

 

Here X is the only feature. This is a highly biased machine as the coefficient of X (.00002) is very small and the outcome is almost always very close to 0.6. Some machines are more prone to give biased results than others. For instance, there is a greater chance of getting a biased machine using logistic regression rather than decision trees. Why does this happen? The simplicity of the feature and the lack of an easily discoverable relationship can create a biased machine. In the relationship, the probability and X may not have a linear relationship that the machine is set up to capture. If we create a feature X ^2, we may find the coefficient of X^2 is larger and thereby the probability value may start getting less biased. Some machines may get biased due to heavy regularization.

 

### Detecting a Biased Machine

 

The following two guidelines help us identify a biased machine

 

*   The error rate on the training data is high - essentially implying that the machine has not been able to learn any information.

 

*   The range of probabilities/values predicted by the machine are quite tight.

 

### Reducing Bias of a Machine

 

*   Add more nonlinearity in features.

 

*   Reduce regularization.

 

*   Add a more complex machine.

 

## Variance

 

A machine suffering from a high variance problem is one that has learnt the training data well but gives very poor prediction on unseen (validation/test) data. As an analogy, consider a student who has studied the textbook very well, but finds it very difficult to apply those learning’s in solving real-life problems.

Some algorithms are more prone to overfitting than others. As discussed, decision trees divide the feature space into rectangular regions and assign samples to each of these regions. If allowed to run long enough, they could divide the feature space to a very high level of granularity where there is only one sample per rectangular region, essentially creating zero-error on the training data. Very likely this machine will do quite poorly on validation data even though the features may only be slightly different.

 

If we increase the number of features and are not able to provide more samples, we may cause overfitting.

 

### Detecting a High Variance Machine

 

*   Performance is very good on training data but very poor on unseen data.

 

*   Predictions change a lot with a small change in feature values.

 

### How Do We Solve a High Variance Problem?

 

*   Feature selection, dimension reduction techniques, and regularization.

 

*   Reducing the run time.

 

*   Reducing the complexity of the machine.

There are several financial concepts that this approach could be applied to, which were discussed in Chapter 1. We chose to uncover “Carry Trading” here. Carry essentially refers to the yield that assets provide if you own them, after deducting the cost of funding their purchase. This net yield can be positive or negative, which means one could receive a net positive increment simply by owning the asset or may have to pay a net amount for owning the asset.

 

## Carry in Foreign Exchange 

 

Let us make this more concrete with a simple example from the foreign exchange markets. AUD versus USD. At the time of writing this chapter, AUD benchmark interest rate is at 1% and USD benchmark rate is at 2%. The AUD/USD spot exchange rate is 0.6771 USD per AUD.

 

The US central bank seems to be more hawkish on the US economy, while the Australian central bank holds a more dovish view on the Australian economy. Therefore, if we borrow AUD for a maturity of say a month and use the proceeds to buy USD, we need to pay 1% in AUD rates, as that is the currency, we have borrowed in. In return, we receive 2% on the USD holdings, as that is our asset. The yield on our asset (2%) is more than the cost of funding the purchase in AUD (1%). Therefore, the carry on this trade is positive.

 

Apparently, the market already prices this in. So, at that very instant, the 1-month forward rate for AUD/USD is .6777, which implies that the AUD spot rate 1 month from now is expected to be .6777. Therefore, the AUD is expected to strengthen from .6771 to .6777, which exactly offsets the net carry yield that we were expecting by buying USD and selling AUD. That is the way the market prices instrument as per efficient market hypothesis.

 

Effectively, if we borrow AUD and convert it to USD at a rate of .6771, we will earn 2% on the USD asset and pay 1% on our AUD liability. At the same time, we enter a commitment to repay our borrowing of AUD in one month’s time. We intend to do this by selling our asset (USD) and using the proceeds to buy AUD, which effectively means we buy AUD and sell USD for 1-month maturity. The forward price will be such that the net gain in yield of 1% in holding USD over AUD for 1 month gets exactly offset. The forward price therefore will be .6771\*(1+.01/12) = .6777.

 

The hypothesis we want to test here is that the forward price does not accurately represent the gain or loss that may be incurred in holding AUD/USD for a month. We would like to see if there is a benefit of holding instruments that are net carry positive against a set of instruments that are net carry negative. If we can make money by consistently holding assets that are net carry positive against a set of instruments that are net carry negative, then we can conclude that the forwards do not accurately price future outcomes. We obviously intend to use machine learning and some of the techniques we discussed earlier to prove/disprove this hypothesis. Before that, let us understand the concept of carry as applied to other asset classes. In foreign exchange markets, as discussed earlier, carry is simply the difference between the foreign interest rate and the domestic interest rate.

 

Assets that pay to be owned are positive carry. Assets that you need to pay to own, are negative carry. We want to see if owning positive carry instruments outperforms negative carry instruments.

 

One last insight that we would like to share is that interest rates are the “price” of money. If the price of a good is high, it means it is in demand and vice versa. We want to test if we systematically own assets, which are in demand, do we make money.

 

Let us see how carry is calculated in other assets.

 

## Carry in Commodities

 

Most liquid commodities have at least two futures, which reflect the supply and demand conditions for those 2 months.

 

The futures (F) contract can be priced as in Equation1

 

F = S0\*(1 + (rfunding – rcommodity) \*T/365)                                  Equation 1

 

Here T is the number of days to maturity, F is the futures price (first or second liquid contract), S0 is the spot price (spot or first futures contract, respectively), rfunding is the interest rate of the currency the commodity is denominated in, and rcommodity is the implied interest rate of the commodity (more commonly known as convenience yield). If rcommodity\>rfunding, F<S and the commodity is in backwardation. If rcommodity<rfunding, F\>S and the commodity is in contango. If rcommodity is high, then it implies demand for that commodity is high and one should expect it to outperform.

 

## Carry in Equity Indices

 

Most liquid indices are traded through futures contracts. Futures are priced similarly

 

F = S0\*(1 + (rfunding – rindex) \*T/365)                                         Equation 2

 

Here T is the number of days to maturity, F is the futures price (first or second liquid contract), S0 is the spot price (price of the cash index), rfunding is the interest rate of the currency the index is denominated in, and rindex is the implied dividend yield.

 

Quite often, there is difference between the closing time of the cash index and the futures, so we need to be careful if we are using end of day data.

 

More appropriately we can use the actual cash dividends paid out by the companies forming the index and weigh them by the weights used to construct the index. We can also imply the dividend yield from “put call parity” relationships in the option market for the index futures, assuming sufficient liquidity.

 

## Carry in Rates

 

The difference between short-term rates and long-term rates (2-year maturity vs. 10-year maturity) can be used as a measure of carry in the government bond markets.

## Maximal Margin Classifier

 

So how do we classify the sample (u) once we have the line? We simply take the sign of βTu + β0 . 

 

If greater than 0, then it belongs to class 1 with a target value T =1 . 

 

If less than 0, then it belongs to class -1 with a target value T = -1 .

 

Effectively T\*( βTX + β0) > 0 

 

What follows from here is a little more mathematical representation of the above  intuition and how to generalise the search to a higher dimensional problem.

We have P features and n samples.

 

The ith sample has xi1, xi2, xi3, …. xip  features 

 

The ith sample has yi, target 

 

The classifier is given by 

 

β0 \+ β1 xi1\+ β2 xi2\+ β3 xi3 …… βp xip  = 0

 

M ofcourse is the margin 

 

So the maximum margin classifier is obtained by 

 

Finding values of β0, β1, β2 …. β p

 

1)        For which we maximize M

 

2)        Such that yi(β0 \+ β1 xi1\+ β2 xi2\+ β3 xi3 …… βp xip  ) >= M for all the n samples  ----equation 2

 

3)        And β12  + β22 + β22 …….+ βp2 = 1 

 

The solution to the above set of equations gives us the maximal margin classifier 

 

The reason we have 2) and 3) is because 

 

(β0 \+ β1 xi1\+ β2 xi2\+ β3 xi3 …… βp xip  )/sqrt(β12  + β22 + β22 …….+ βp2) represent the perpendicular distance froma sample to a line,as discussed in the section on Linear Algebra.

 

We multiply by yi because we want it to be correctly classified.

 

So 2) and 3) together imply that each sample needs to be correctly classified and atleast M units away from the line. And then we select all the possible solutions that has the highest value of M. In other words, we want a linear classifer that perfectly separates all our samples. The best of bias and variance machines. Achieving this in reality though, is hard to satisfy. ### Allowing Misclassificatons

 

The reality of data sets may be a bit different. To begin with equation 2) above requires ALL samples to be correctly classified. That is too stringent. We may not be able to get a linear classifier that perfectly separates all samples. Figure 8.11 is an example of this:

![](images/00082.jpeg)

Either way, we may want to allow for some amount of misclassification of samples. 

 

The way we account for that is by modifying equation 2 to

 

yi(β0 \+ β1 xi1\+ β2 xi2\+ β3 xi3 …… βp xip  ) >= M(1-ei)

 

where ei\> 0 , and 

 

sum (ei) <= C for i= 1,2,3…n

 

If ei = 0, then sample is correctly classified 

 

If ei< 1, then sample is correctly classified but the margin condition is violated 

 

If ei\> 1, then sample is incorrectly classified  

 

As discussed earlier, the solution to the above set of equations is dependent only on the support vectors. When we solve the equations using optimization techniques (beyond the scope of this books and quite frankly us ), we get an equation of the form 

 

β0 + α1x.x1 \+ α2x.x2 ……+ αsx.xs

 

where there are s support vectors and x.x1 is the dot product of two p-dimensional vectors (where p is the number of features) and x1  is the first support vector, x2  is the second support vector and so on.

Finding values of β0, β1, β2 …. Βp, β12, β22 …. Βp2

 

1.  For which we maximize M

Minimize β02  + β12 + β22 …….+ βp2

 

St βTxi ≥ 1 if yi \= 1

This is essentially stating that we need to select a classifier that maximizes ǁxiǁcosθ, the perpendicular distance of the sample to the classifier.

 

We have now seen that one of the ways to introduce nonlinearity is to introduce nonlinear features. A more robust method is the kernel trick.

 

What we do in the kernel trick is to take each sample, pass it through a kernel transformation and get a new representation of the sample. We then apply the above discussed algorithm on these transformed features.

K(x,xi) = e\-(x-xi)( x-xi)/2(σ^2)

 

As x approaches xi, K(x,xi) approaches 1 

 

As x is very different to xi, K(x,xi) approaches 0

So xu,which was originally p-dimensional, is now n-dimensional in the feature space. We represent all the n samples from the original data set as these n-dimensional features. What was originally n x p is now n x n. We then apply the SVM algorithm to this feature space.

DEEP LEARNING

 

 

The use of machine learning is increasingly becoming synonymous with the use of neural network or more specifically, with deep learning. In this chapter, we will explore the workings of deep learning models in detail. To develop an intuitive understanding of the workings of a neural network, we will be exploring the use of neural networks for tackling supervised learning problems. We will break the network down to its components so that you, the reader, can play around and build your models to suit your specific requirements. In other words, we will try to demystify the black box, as these models are commonly referred to, layer by layer, component by component.

 

A deep learning architecture is a logical, structured, sometimes complex aggregation of some relatively simple building blocks. Let us open the structure and see why it has become the go-to-architecture for seemingly difficult problems and what makes it so powerful.

![](images/00087.jpeg)

Figure 9. 1 Neural network basic architecture.

 

A neural network, as the name rightfully suggests, is a network of neurons that operates as a two-pass architecture. An example of a very simple network is provided in Figure 9.1. The first pass that proceeds from input to output is known as the forward propagation. The second pass traces the reverse path from the output to the input and is known as the backward propagation.

 

The idea is to quantify the relationship between the input features and the target outputs. In the first pass that comprises a forward propagation, a nonlinear function is used to derive the value of target output(s), using the input values. A loss function is calculated to find the error between the actual and the predicted value of the output. The network aims to minimize this loss by finding the global minima of the loss. In the second pass, the backward propagation, the parameters (weights and biases) are tweaked in a systematic way to try and minimize this loss. The rate at which the biases and weights are changed is determined by a hyper parameter called the learning rate. The forward pass and the backward propagation are, often, repeated a few times for a step-by-step systematic move toward the global minima. The rate at which we can converge to the global minima is a function of the structure, data, and many other hyper parameters. This will be discussed in more detail in this and future chapters.

 

## Neuron

 

A neural network is, as the name rightfully suggests, a network of neurons. Each neuron is a specialized unit of computation that does a two-step operation during the forward pass phase to convert an input to a processed output. Depending on the specific architecture, each neuron passes its output to one or more sets of neurons as their inputs. These neurons, in turn, use these inputs and perform a “similar” two-step operation to pass the processed output along further in the network.

 

Let us say the input to the neuron is “X” and the output of the neuron is “Y\*.” Each neuron is associated with a few parameters (which the network learns during the process of training. More on that later). The parameters are called weights “W” and bias “b.” These parameters appear in step 1 of the two-step process undertaken by each neuron (Figure 9.2).

 

Step 1: Calculates a value, let us call it Z, such that

 

Z = Wt X + b

 

The superscript “t” essentially implies that we need to take the transpose of the W matrix. We multiply or rather perform a dot product of the transpose of the W matrix and the input X. We then add the bias to this value to generate our intermediate output, Z.

 

Step 2: It involves applying a nonlinear function to the intermediate output Z to get the output Y\*. The nonlinear functions are known as activation functions, A(Z).

 

![](images/00088.jpeg)

Figure 9. 2 Two steps, step 1 and step 2 that are performed in every neuron.

 

Why do we need these activation functions to be “nonlinear”? While we do have a linear activation function, as described ahead, it is rarely used exclusively in a network. The reason why we need to introduce nonlinearity is to be able to approximate all possible relations or, mathematically speaking, all possible functions that link our input to our final desired output. The ability to do this mapping from any input to output is called universal function approximation. In other words, it is this nonlinearity that helps create the universal function approximator - hence providing the ability to the neural learning system to predict variables in multiple domains.

## Layers

 

A group of neurons connected to another group of neurons, but not necessarily to each other. The types of layers that together form the framework of the neural network architecture are as follows:

 

### Input layer

 

As the name suggests, this is the layer that contains the input features from the dataset. This is the 0th layer of the neural network. It connects to the hidden layer(s).

 

### Hidden layer

 

These are the working horses of the neural network. They receive inputs from either the input layer or other hidden layers and provide its output to other hidden layers or output layer of the network.

 

### Output layer 

 

This is the final layer of the network and provides the output of the process. The output can be a probability, class, a real number, and so on based on whether we are dealing with a classification or regression problem.

 

A neural network is called an “L-layer network” if the total number of hidden layers and output layers is equal to “L”. Hence, an NN with an input layer, one output layer, and one hidden layer is called a two-layer network (input layer is the 0th layer). Example of such a network is illustrated in the Figure 9.8.The term “deep learning” essentially implies a large number of hidden layers in the network. In Figure 9.8 we only show the direction of the forward pass. Backward propagation goes in the opposite direction (not shown here).

![](images/00095.jpeg)

Figure 9. 8 Deep network.

 

A connection between two neurons in layers La and Lb simply implies that during the forward pass, the receiving neuron in layer Lb uses the output of the neuron in layer La as part of its input to calculate the activation function. During backward propagation, layer Lb’s gradient descent output goes as an input to layer La’s gradient.

 

## Fully Connected Network

 

In a neural network, if every neuron of a layer is connected to every neuron of the preceding and succeeding layer, it is known as a fully connected network. Why do deep networks work well? The initial layers can look at detecting low-level features of the inputs exploring simpler relations between inputs and outputs and the latter layers can work on more “complex” relations by using the simpler features detected in the earlier layers, hence exploring the entire gamut. This has contributed to the popularity of deep networks across domains even though they are computationally much more expensive than shallow networks.

 

### Epoch

 

Once all the layers and neurons have completed the two-step process, we come to the end of phase 1, known as forward pass. At this point, we have a “predicted”/calculated value of the output(s), known as Ypred. This value may not be the same as the actual output Yact. Depending on the kind of problem we are dealing with, we find an appropriate representation of this error. For example, in the case of a regression problem, we may represent the error as mean square error or mean absolute error. In the classification setup, we may choose to go with a metric such as log loss. The aim is to minimize this error to get a function that “best” approximates the relationship between the input and the output. Let us call this L(Yact, Ypred). To minimize this, we use partial derivatives of L(Yact, Ypred) with respect to the parameters, that is, weights and biases. Let us call these dWl’ and dbl’ for the weights and biases related to layer “l.” The parameter values of the layer “l” are updated as:

 

Wl = Wl – alpha \* dWl’

 

bl = bl – alpha \* dbl’

 

Similar steps are carried out starting from the final layer right up to the first layer. This is the backward propagation step. One such pass through the entire training set is known as an epoch.

 

## Parameters and Hyper Parameters

 

As described in the chapter so far, several variables affect the outcome of the neural network in various ways. Some of these variables, called parameters, are the ones that are learned during the process of training via forward pass and backward propagation. Weights and biases of the various layers fall under this category. On the other hand, there are variables such as learning rate that are provided by the user. Such variables are known as hyper parameters. These are user-chosen but affect the outcome and performance of the network. Other hyper parameters that we have come across so far are the number of layers, the number of neurons in each layer, and so on. It is very important to tune these hyper parameters to get the best possible performance from the network. There are a few ways to select the best possible hyper parameters.

 

### Grid search

 

Specify a range of values for all the hyper parameters.

 

1.  Train the neural network for each of the possible hyper-parameter value combinations.

 

2.  Select the values that construct the best performing neural network of all possible ones based on the hyper-parameter combinations.

 

3.  The biggest issue with this is the exponential increase in the number of possible hyper-parameter combinations.

 

### Random search

 

Specify a range of values for all the hyper parameters.

 

1.  Train the neural network for a few randomly selected possible hyper-parameter value combinations.

 

2.  Select the values that construct the best performing neural network out of all the possible ones from the randomly selected hyper-parameter combinations.

 

3.  The biggest issue with this is that, while the number of models created is much lower than grid search but because of random selection, you may miss the “best” possible hyper-parameter combination.

 

### Coarse to fine search

 

1.  Specify a range of values for the hyper parameters.

 

2.  Train the neural network on a comparatively widely spaced (or coarse) sample of the hyperparameter combinations.

 

3.  Select the range of values that get the highest performing neural network.

 

4.  Retrain neural networks for all possible values between the range of values selected earlier.

 

5.  Select the hyper-parameter combination that gives the highest performing neural network.

 

While selecting values within a given range, it is not necessary to search by uniformly selecting values. Values should be selected based on the range of values. For example, if the range one is looking at for a hyper parameter is 2–8, one can select uniform values of 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8. If the range is 32–1024, one can select values separated by a power of 2 so it will be 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, and 1024.It is important to note that, if it is allowed to train long enough or with a complex-enough network, the neural network architecture is capable of learning the exact outputs for the inputs provided in the training set. However, it will not necessarily be able to predict the outputs on unseen data. This is known as overfitting.

 

 

## Overfitting

 

Let us look at some of the important and common ways of reducing overfitting.

 

### More data 

 

If the neural network has overfitted on the existing training data, then the logical step to solve this issue is to add more training data. However, this is not always easy as this may be time consuming and/or expensive.

 

### Regularization 

 

Penalize the complexity of the network by adding a regularizing component to the backward propagation. This essentially helps to reduce the impact of lots of hidden units which, in effect, makes the network much simpler.

 

### Dropout 

 

For every example that the neural network is trained on, some neurons are dropped at random. The neurons chosen to be dropped, change with every example. This makes the network simpler and prevents any one neuron to be dominant in determining the output. Two things to remember while using dropout:

 

*   Dropout is used only for training and not at the time of prediction.

 

*   As the expected value of the activation is reduced by the proportion of the dropped neurons, the expected value of the activation is pumped up to compensate for this. This is known as an inverse dropout.

 

Both the aforementioned issues are handled by all standard implementations of neural networks, so you do not need to explicitly code for this.

 

### Early stopping

 

One of the visual tools to determine if overfitting is happening, is to plot the cost function against the number of iterations. If both training and development/validation set costs are decreasing with an increase in the number of iterations, we can continue training.

 

![](images/00096.jpeg)

Figure 9. 9 Identifying when overfitting is happening

 

The problem with early stopping is that it mixes two orthogonal (or independent) tasks of preventing overfitting and optimizing the cost function.

 

## Methods to improve optimization

 

This brings us to the next topic: How do we optimize the cost function, that is, how do we make the training faster and more efficient?

 

### Scale/Normalize the inputs

 

The scaling parameters are based on the training set only. Both validation and test set inputs or features need to be scaled according to the learnt scaling parameters of the training set. How does scaling help? Scaling ensures that, even if the actual value of the underlying inputs change, the mean and variance of it are known beforehand. Using scaling, one can thus build more optimal models.

 

### Batch normalization 

 

On the same lines of reasoning as the preceding text, it is helpful to normalize the activations going from one layer to another. It is open to debate whether one should normalize the intermediate value of Z \= wX + b or the activation value Act(Z).

 

Another point to note is that you may not want all your hidden units to have 0 mean and 1 variance. To avoid this, the normalized intermediate values are multiplied by a parameter “gamma” and the parameter “Beta” is added. These are both learnable parameters via gradient descent, or other methods such as RMSprop, Adam's optimizer as described in the succeeding text.

 

### Parameter initialization

 

The weights and biases linked to various layers and neurons need to be initialized before the training begins. Based on practical experience, it is clear that the initialization method affects the learnability of the network by reducing the impact of vanishing or exploding gradients. Various initializations such as He initialization, Xavier initialization (details beyond the scope of this book), and so on are provided in standard implementations of neural networks or you can use a self-defined initializer. Note that the weights matrix should not be initialized to 0 as that would negate the learning due to multiple layers.

 

### Mini-batch gradient descent 

 

Split the training set into smaller subsets and apply the gradient descent on one such smaller batch at a time. This is called mini-batch gradient descent. If the size of each subset = 1, it is known as stochastic gradient descent. Mini-batch size is also a hyper parameter.

 

### Gradient descent with momentum

 

This uses an exponential average of gradients to update the learnable parameters such as weights and biases.

 

### RMSprop(Root Mean Square Propagation) 

 

At every iteration, the exponentially weighted average of the square of the gradient is calculated. The weights and biases are updated by a scaled value of the current gradient (scaled by the square root of the exponentially weighted average of the square of the gradient).

 

### Adam’s optimization 

 

It uses a combination of both gradient descent with momentum as well as RMSprop. Most of the standard libraries come with built-in Adam optimization.

## Implementing Recurrent Function in Neural Networks

 

Neural networks are known as universal function approximators. That is given a set of inputs X we can model a function F to capture the relationship from X to Y. Let us say we are interested in modelling the price of a financial instrument at time t as Xt. We hypothesize that the instrument X is dependent on a set of variables at time t denoted by Vt. Needless to say, these set of variables are also observed at various point in time. We want to create a function F, such that

 

Xt = F (Vt−1, Vt−2, … Vt−n)Equation 11.1

 

Where n is the look back window. Vtis an array of dimension m. If m = 1, and Vi = Xi, then

 

Xt = F (Xt−1, Xt−2, Xt−3, … Xt −n)Equation 11.2

 

Essentially saying that the price of the instrument X at time t is dependent on the price of the instrument up to a lag to n. This simplification of m = 1 and Vi = Xi was only used to give an understanding of the notation. We revert to the more general setting of

 

Xt = F (Vt−1, Vt−2, … Vt−n)

 

Based on what we have discussed in Chapter 9, the neural network representation would look like Figure 11.1.

 

![](images/00153.jpeg)

Figure 11. 1 Neural network representation of Equation 11.1.

 

The input layer is of dimension n\*m. As the look back increases, the size of the input increases linearly and the neural network gets bulkier. There is a more efficient way of representing this. Let us try to understand how. From Equation 11.1 we have

 

Xt = F (Vt−1, Vt−2, … Vt−n)

 

Moving it one time step forward, we get

 

Xt+1 = F (Vt, Vt−1, … Vt−n+1)

 

There are a lot of common terms in the two equations that are used to predict Xt and Xt+1. We can represent all historical information as Ht. This Ht is something we will learn by training with data. Effectively,

 

Xt = F (Vt−1, Ht) and Xt+1 = F (Vt, Ht+1)

 

Ht represents the relevant historic information. Therefore, our network looks like Figure 11.2 for time step t and like Figure 11.3 for time step t+1.

At = g (W \[Vt−1, Ht\] + b) Equation 11.3

 

In addition, the output Xt as

 

Xt = g’ (W’ At + b’) Equation 11.4

 

Now, in a RNN (Recurrent Neural Network), we set At = Ht+1. This way the history is learnable and now the graphical representation looks like Figure 11.4. ## Dimensions of Various Vectors

 

In our notation so far, we have introduced several matrices and vectors, and it is important to get the dimensions of these right to improve our understanding of what is going on.

 

Vt = 1 ×m

 

Ht = 1 × h, h is a hyper parameter, how many neurons needed to effectively learn the information in the entire look back period. 

 

W is the weight matrix in Equation 11.3 and W’ is the weight matrix in Equation 11.4.

 

W = h × (m +h)

 

W’ = 1 × h, assuming Xt is price at time t so of dimension 1 × 1.

 

b = h × 1, where b is the bias term of Equation 11.3.

 

b’ = 1 × 1, where b’ is the bias term of Equation 11.4.

 

The important thing to note is that the parameters W, b, W’, and b’ do not have any subscript t. Which means it is the same set of parameters and hyper parameters that are used across time. In other words, only ONE MLP is used to predict the outcome at various time steps. The parameters are shared across time. This setup is the RNN.

## Back propagation through time

 

We simply do the forward propagation and get the prediction for each sample at each time step. We calculate the loss at each time step and sum it over all the time steps. We then sum it over all the samples, and it is this aggregated loss across all the time steps and all the samples (of the batch) that is back propagated, essentially using the chain rule of differentiation. Here we may encounter the problem of vanishing or exploding gradients. Since this loss has to be back propagated across all time steps, it is often known as back propagation through time. In addition, if the number of time steps is quite large, the learnings of the early stages of the time series may be difficult to implement as the gradients may vanish (approach zero) or explode (become very large).

 

We look to solve this problem using a slight modification to the RNN architecture. This is where the GRU (Gated Recurrent Units) and LSTM (Long Short-Term memory) come into play.

 

For each cell of the GRU, we need to specify the memory size. It is this memory that’s retained and passed through all the time steps. Each time step can read from this memory and make necessary predictions. Each time step is also allowed to write or update all or parts of this memory. That way any relevant information coming at any time step can be written to this so-called memory bus. Going forward, this information is available for use at any future time step. It can also be over written.

 

Let us look at how this is done. The general architecture looks like this: Across various time steps we have a memory bus running. The RNN can read data from this memory bus to make predictions and, based on new information, can overwrite the memory bus too.

 

![](images/00165.jpeg)

Figure 11. 13 GRU

 

The equations that enable us to do this are discussed next.

 

At = tanH (WA \[ C(t−1), X(t−1)\] + bA)

 

The proposed activation function is tanH. In addition, all that the aforementioned equation is stating is that the activation At is calculated based on what the memory has stored so far C(t−1), and the input X(t−1).

 

We define a new operation, which is called the “update gate”. The justification for this name will become clear in a moment.

 

GU = Sigmoid (WU \[ C(t−1), X(t−1)\] + bU)

 

Since GU is computed using an activation of sigmoid its value is between 0 and 1. It is used to update the memory as follows:

 

C(t) = GU\*At + (1−GU) \* C(t−1)

 

If GU = 0, C(t) = C(t−1) hence memory is not updated.

If GU\= 1, C(t) = At, hence memory is overwritten by At.

To summarize the equations that describe the behaviour of GRU is

 

At = tanH (WA \[C(t−1), X(t−1)\] + bA)

GU = Sigmoid (WU \[C(t−1), X(t−1)\] + bU)

C(t) = GU\*At + (1 − GU) \* C(t−1)

At = C(t)

 

These equations may seem overbearing but the implementation in Keras is relatively straightforward.

 

## LSTM

 

The concept behind the LSTM is very similar, it just has more gates. For one, the LSTM differentiates between the At and C(t).

 

It defines the output gate that bridges the gap between Atand C(t).

 

GO = Sigmoid (WO\[C(t−1), X(t−1)\] + bO)

 

At\= GO\* tanH(Ct)

LSTM retains the GU, which is the update gate. The role of (1 – GU) or the forget gate is modelled separately as

 

GF = Sigmoid (WF\[C(t−1), X(t−1)\] + bF)

 

Therefore, the update of the memory is written as

 

C(t) = GU\*At + (GF)\* C(t−1)

 

The equations that describe the behaviour of LSTM is as follows:

 

At = tanH (WA\[C(t−1), X(t−1)\] + bA)

 

GU = Sigmoid (WU\[C(t−1), X(t−1)\] + bU)

 

GF = Sigmoid (WF\[C(t−1), X(t−1)\] + bF)

 

GO = Sigmoid (WO\[C(t−1), X(t−1)\] + bO)

 

C(t) = GU\*At + (GF)\* C(t−1)

 

At = GO\*tanH(C(t))

 

All these equations and other points may seem overwhelming at first, but if you understand why we replace RNN with GRUs and LSTMs, it is a pretty decent start. Most APIs (Application Programming Interface) take care of this rigor for us and, to be totally honest, I hardly bother about the equations myself.

## KNN Algorithm

 

To understand the KNN algorithm, we need to first understand Minkowski Distance. Figure 12.1 gives a snapshot of our data set.

 

![](images/00168.jpeg)

Figure 12. 1 Dataset under consideration

 

Let us pick up two samples xa and xb from this dataset as shown in Figure 12.2 with n samples and P features.

 

![](images/00169.jpeg)

Figure 12. 2  xa and xb sampled from our dataset

 

The minkowski distance is defined as in Equation 12.1 below 

 

![](images/00170.jpeg)Equation 12.1

 

When q =1, it is known as the Manhattan distance and when q =2 we call it the Euclidean distance. It is therefore a hyper parameter the user needs to specify. 

 

All that we do in the KNN algorithm is that, for a test sample, we calculate the Minkowski distance to each sample of the training data and select the k samples that are the closest to this test sample. Closeness, of course, is measured by the Minkowski distance. It is these k samples that form the nearest neighbours.

 

The prediction is simply an aggregation or a vote of the target values of these k nearest neighbours. k itself is a hyper parameter and is found through the process of cross validation. We may also choose to weigh the target value of the k nearest neighbours by the distance to the test sample for purposes of prediction. Finally, there are two important considerations:

 

*   Since our distance measure is highly dependent on the scale of each feature, it is highly recommended to apply the standard scalars on the input features. Any of the standard scalars discussed in Chapter 2 could be used.

 

*   Computation can be slow. For each test sample we need to find the Minkowski distance to each sample of the training data set from which we then select the k nearest neighbours. This can be quite time consuming, and several modifications have been suggested. A k – dimensional tree or a k –d tree is one such modification, wherein in Minkowski, distance is computed only with a select number of samples. We refer the readers to the references for further reading.
