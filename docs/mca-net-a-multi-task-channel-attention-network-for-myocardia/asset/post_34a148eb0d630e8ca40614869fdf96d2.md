

MI detection and location tasks may have overlapping key features and additional information that can be complementary.

Multi-task frameworks have been shown to play a gainful role in similar tasks by learning shared and complementary information between multiple tasks to optimize the performance of all tasks [42–44]. And we use the most classical multi-task framework, a shared-bottom model [42]. This model achieves improved classification performance by sharing the bottom representation across multiple tasks and performing loss optimization within multiple tasks. Thus, MCA-net based on this multi-task framework can more effectively combine MI detection and location tasks. The framework can be expressed by :

```
y^n = F^n(\boldsymbol{C}(\boldsymbol{x}))
```

In this paper, we design three related tasks: (1) a two-category MI detection task (Detection), which aims to identify whether people are MI or HC; (2) a five-category MI location task (Location-5), whose goal is to distinguish AMI, ASMI, ALMI, other and HC populations; (3) a seven-category MI location task (Location-7), whose objective is to distinguish AMI, ASMI, ALMI, IMI, ILMI, other and HC populations. For the Location-5 task, we select it because a large proportion of patients with MI have Generalized Anterior MI (GAMI) [36], which is consisting of AMI, ASMI, and ALMI, so we first focus our task on differentiating GAMI and classifying the remaining MI labels into other categories. We then consider the task for most of the existing studies and the relevance of that task to the Location-5 task, as well as the distribution of labels on the dataset. Ultimately, the IMI and ILMI are additionally selected for classification based on the Location-5 task to form the Location-7 task.

Specifically, the multi-task framework shares parameters and feature representations among the three tasks in the channel attention network, which is represented as a function **C**. While _n_=1_,_2_,_3 for each task, respectively. Then we get each task output _y^n_ after passing through the corresponding fully connected layer _F^n_.

The losses of these tasks in the multi-task framework are combined in the form of a weighted sum for joint optimization, and the total loss can be expressed as :

```
L_{total} = aL_d + bL_{l-5} + cL_{l-7}
```

where L_d, L_{l-5}, L_{l-7} correspond to the Detection, Location-5 and Location-7 tasks, respectively, and a, b, c are the weights for the three related tasks.

## 5. Experiments and results

In this section, we fully evaluate MCA-net on the PTB and PTBXL datasets and compare it with state-of-the-art methods for automatic MI detection and location. In addition, we perform a significance test of the results of our experiments to show the improvements in our approach.

### 5.1. Comparison methods

For a comprehensive assessment, we conduct comparative experiments on our method and other three state-of-the-art MI classification methods, i.e., MFB-CBRNN [23], ML-ResNet [22], and ML-Net [24] on both the PTB and PTBXL datasets.

- **MFB-CBRNN**: MFB-CBRNN used a multi-lead framework for MI detection based on the 12-lead ECGs. To fully utilize the ECG waveform information contained in all 12 leads. 12 multiple-feature-branch was employed to extract features within each lead which was mainly composed of convolutional layers. For effective feature fusion, MFB-CBRNN also used a bidirectional LSTM to integrate all the features output from each lead branch.

- **ML-ResNet**: ML-ResNet also employed a multi-lead framework for MI detection and location, but it used a residual-based network structure in designing multi-feature branches. Each multi-feature branch mainly consisted of three stacked residual blocks, each of which included three convolutional layers and a skip connection. Thus, ML-ResNet can be layered to learn feature representations within the leads and perform feature fusion.

- **ML-Net**: ML-Net also had a novel channel branch structure based on a multi-lead framework. The branch structure is designed as three parallel convolutional networks which used setting different convolutional kernel sizes to integrate multi-scale features within the leads. However, unlike the above two approaches, ML-Net only used four leads ECG data to accommodate the lightweight needs of portable devices.

Unlike these methods, our approach combines 2-D and 1-D residual blocks for extracting early significant features and finer-grained features. And we also used the SE module and skip connections in the MCA-net for feature weighting and to prevent loss of valid information. Finally, by introducing a multi-task framework, the inter-task correlation and complementarity are fully exploited to extend the MCA-net to individual tasks and improve the overall classification performance. Because MFB-CBRNN, ML-ResNet, and ML-Net are all originally designed for different MI classification tasks, using different amounts of data and different means of data processing. We extend them to our task by unifying the data inputs and modifying their corresponding classification layers for a fair comparison.

### 5.2. Implementation details

All methods involved in this paper were developed based on Keras and the TensorFlow framework [45] which are trained and tested using python 3.6. The relevant experiments were performed on a server with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6230 CPU @ 2.10 GHz and 251 GB memory. We set the initial learning rate of the model to 0.001 and optimized it during the training process using the callback function ReduceLROnPlateau. The number of epochs and batch sizes were set to 100 and 64, respectively. Moreover, we also deploy the Adam and early stop strategies to speed up the learning process and prevent overfitting.

### 5.3. Evaluation metrics

In this work, the model performances on the PTB dataset are evaluated by three metrics, namely, sensitivity (Sen), specificity (Spe), and accuracy (Acc). The Sen, also called recall, represents the percentage of positive samples correctly predicted by the model over the overall positive samples. Conversely, Spe represents the percentage of negative samples correctly predicted by the model over the overall negative samples. While, the accuracy rate (Acc) is the percentage of all correctly identified samples over the total samples. These three evaluation metrics can be calculated by the following equation:

```
Sen = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
```

```
Spe = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}
```

```
Acc = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}
```

Where TP (True Positive) and TN (True Negative) refer to the number of samples where both the true categories and the predicted categories are positive or negative. While FP (False Positive) and FN (False Negative) refer to the number of samples where the true categories and the predicted categories are positive and negative, or negative and positive, respectively. For the multi-label dataset PTBXL, we use the same metrics to evaluate the model performances on the MI detection task. But for the MI location task, we select the accuracy and the area under the roc curve (AUROC) suggested by the dataset publishers [41] to evaluate the model performances. It refers to the expected value of a uniformly drawn random positive sample ranked ahead of a negative sample. In our work, we set each label in turn as a positive sample and set the remaining labels as negative samples to calculate their AUROCs and average them.

All evaluation metrics are shown as mean–variance in the cross validation experimental results to measure the classification performance and stability of involved methods.

### 5.4. Experimental results on the PTB dataset

Unlike the intra-patient schemes which are often used in previous studies, the inter-patient schemes are of greater clinical significance to prove the model generalization. Therefore, we perform 5-fold cross validation experiments on the PTB dataset under an inter-patient scheme to verify the effectiveness and generalizability of our approach, the mean sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of our model are shown in Table 5 where the optimal solution is highlighted. We can see that MCA-net achieves the best performance for all tasks. In the detection task, the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of MCA-net are improved compared with other comparative methods, reaching 98.01%, 89.01%, and 95.57%, respectively, while the improvement in specificity is extremely obvious. The classification performance of MCA-net is also improved in the Location-5 task, achieving 55.35%, 67.96%, and 68.90%, respectively. For the Location-7 task, the MCA-net is achieving 41.80%, 49.01%, and 49.18%, with an improvement of more than 6% on accuracy.

We can also observe that MFB-CBRNN performs slightly worse in all tasks compared to the other methods. And ML-ResNet achieves good classification accuracy on both detection and location tasks. And ML-Net performs a little better than ML-ResNet on both the Detection and Location-5 tasks, but slightly worse on the Location-7 task. This indicates that they lack adaptability to different tasks. In contrast, our method has not only superior performance but also high generalizability. This is mainly because we use multiple convolutional layers with different convolutional kernel sizes and SE module to learn important features at different granularities. On the other hand, it also benefits from the multi-task framework which can effectively share underlying parameters and complementary information between different tasks.

In addition to the mean result of the evaluation metrics, we also calculate their ranges of variance to compare the stability between methods. As can be seen in Table 5, the range of variance fluctuation of our method is smaller than that of the comparison method for most of the evaluation indicators, which proves that our method ensures reliable stability while maintaining superior performance. Moreover, a T-test at a 0.05 significance level was used to analyze whether the MCA-net was significantly better than other comparative methods for each evaluation metric. As can be seen, our method has some performance advantages on the Detection and Location-7 tasks, especially in terms of sensitivity. However, in the Location-5 task, which may be influenced by the label imbalance and distribution, a higher demand is placed on the model's ability to differentiate.

### 5.5. Experimental results on the PTBXL dataset

Different from the PTB dataset, the PTBXL dataset involves a larger number and wider range of patients, and each patient can provide one to more ECG records, allowing for richer sample information and longer ECG sequences. In addition, the PTBXL dataset is closer to the actual clinical situation than the PTB dataset because its ECG records usually contain multiple labels. We extract HC and MI records from the PTBXL dataset for MI detection and location experiments to validate the performance of the MCA-net. Longer duration ECG records are used as input units to validate the effectiveness of the MCA-net in learning long time series features. To more closely resemble the real-world data distribution, the PTBXL dataset is divided by its publisher into ten folds of the inter-patient paradigm [41] which represents that there is no overlap of patient origin for ECG records in all folds. Therefore, we perform 10-fold cross validation based on these sets to obtain more reliable results. As shown in Table 6, our method still outperforms other comparative methods, achieving a sensitivity of 88.59%, specificity of 96.55%, and accuracy of 93.65% on the detection task. Meanwhile, MCA-net also performs better than other methods on the Location-5 and Location-7 tasks, achieving 84.03% and 81.47% accuracy as well as 78.76% and 74.05% AUROC, respectively. This demonstrates the effectiveness and generalizability of our method across the different datasets.

We also can see that in contrast to the results on the PTB dataset, ML-Net performs the worst on the PTBXL dataset. The possible reason is that the length of data provided by the PTBXL dataset is much longer (typically more than several thousand sampling points), while the lightweight framework adopted by ML-Net cannot effectively learn long-term dependencies from such long ECG sequences. And MFB-CBRNN performs relatively well on both datasets, which demonstrates the stability of the convolutional layer. In addition, the average performance of ML-ResNet performs better than the other two comparison methods, which proves the effectiveness of the residual module. Our approach not only combines the stability of the convolutional layer with the effectiveness of the residual module but is also better able to learn the long-term dependence of ECG sequences thus achieving better performance.

Again, the reliable stability of the MCA-net is further verified by the range of variance fluctuations of the experimental results. In addition to this, we still use the T-test to verify the significance of MCA-net on the PTBXL dataset. As can be seen in Table 6, MCA-net achieves significant improvements in most tasks relative to the comparison method, especially the Location-5 and Location-7 tasks.
