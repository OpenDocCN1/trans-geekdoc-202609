
# Network Devices and Topologies

## Source Routing Bridges

In a [source routing bridge](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss473), routing occurs based on the use of a discovery frame that travels the network to determine all possible paths to the intended destination, and then it selects the best one to use. This causes additional network traffic but often results in a faster delivery of the packets. Over the course of a day, the traffic on a network can change drastically based on resources requested and the users accessing it. As the discovery frame is sent out right before the actual packets, the most optimal route is always selected.

## Brouters

A [brouter](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss60) is a combination of a `bridge` and a `router` and can be referred to as a bridging router. This device performs the functions of both a bridge and a router in that it can effectively route packets to the different segments of a network and work as a bridge to send those packets to a different network. Depending on the specific functions performed on the network, the device can work at either Layer 2 (Data Link Layer) or Layer 3 (Network Layer).

## Gateways

A [gateway](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss193) is a device that connects two networks that use dissimilar protocols for communication ([**FIGURE 3-8**](#12_chapter03_02.xhtml#fig3-8)). A gateway is similar to a bridge and can function as a bridge; however, a bridge cannot function as a gateway due to dissimilar protocols in use. This device essentially takes data packets from one network in a specific protocol and converts those packets to the protocol of the receiving network. Because of this action, the device is sometimes referred to as a [protocol converter](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss370).

### FYI

[Modem](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss303) is the name originally given to the device that translated digital signals from the computer into analog signals on traditional telephone lines so the information could be transmitted. The term is derived from the words `mod`ulate and `dem`odulate to relay the transformation the data undergoes in the process. Modems are used to connect to [Internet service providers (ISPs)](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss258) using cable wire, fiber optic wire, or telephone wire, as was traditionally used.

Historically, the router and modem were different devices that performed different tasks for the network environment. Today it is possible to purchase devices that offer the functionality of both. Although the telephone connection is rarely used in most settings, the term `modem` is still used to refer to the device offered by the cable company to bring television, computer, and video access to the home.

## What Differentiates Logical and Physical Topologies?

Computer networks are arranged in a manner that includes the way each is physically connected, which is the [physical topology](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss346) (wired or via the air if wireless), and the logical manner, which pertains to how resources are segregated and allocated. All computer networks have physical and logical topologies, and it is possible for the topologies to be similar. From the user perspective, neither topology matters unless the user is unable to access the resources needed to complete tasks.

Because it is easiest to consider a topology as the way computers and devices are connected, let's cover wired topologies first. [Wired topologies](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss552) have a physical wire between devices, allowing for communication among those devices. A [route](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss420) is the pathway that communication packets travel within the wired connections of a network. The route for network communication is not always easily identified or managed if there is not appropriate documentation to aid the network administrator or engineer. Network equipment, including routers, switches, and gateways, may alter what appears to be a straightforward path of communication to better balance the communication load or to restrict access to certain devices or parts of the network.

The [logical topology](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss286) is how the network appears from any device or user and is governed more by policy and access than by physical connectivity. Instead of how the computers and components are physically arranged, think of the logical topology as how those computers access and share data and resources. It may have little resemblance to the physical connectivity of the devices.

Whether identifying the physical or logical topology, some elements should be kept in mind. Due care should be exercised when planning a network installation or upgrade, or [scaling](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss429) (whether making it bigger or smaller) to ensure that the appropriate physical and logical topologies are selected to support the needs of the business. Both topologies should be documented clearly in both the security policy and in a standalone network document, so it is accessible for troubleshooting networking problems by the network administrators and engineers.

### Types of Physical Topologies

#### Bus Topology

A [bus topology](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss66) is considered a linear network because it has computers in a line (although not necessarily a straight line). See [**FIGURE 3-9**](#12_chapter03_03.xhtml#fig3-9). Each computer or device in the topology is connected to the computer or device that is next in line. The actual configuration could be wavy (weaving up and down rows of computers in an office space) or appear circular in formation, although it is not a ring or circle, as the ends never meet. A bus topology requires equipment called [terminators](#30_glossary.xhtml#gloss497) to be placed at each end of the physical cable. The terminators stop the signal on the line from bouncing back and forth down the line.

![FIGURE 3-9 A bus topology.](Images/9781284183658_CH03_FIGF09.png)

**FIGURE 3-9** A bus topology.

In this topology, a single cable is used as the backbone of the network. The computers and devices that are part of that network are connected to that cable. This network topology is easier to implement and troubleshoot in home office and small business settings, but it is often slower, especially when there are more than 10 devices on that network. When a system fails in a bus topology, the entire network goes down.

Connectivity issues in the network affect all other computers and devices connected to the network in a bus configuration. A network with a bus topology is often more susceptible to performance issues as the number of computers and devices on the network increases beyond 10. There are also length limitations on the cabling—the signal degrades the further from the origination point the packet gets. Repeaters often solve some of the problems with long distances, but if the desired quality of throughput is not achieved, wireless may be added to reach additional distances. However, wiring and installation is easier and usually less expensive than other configurations, which is why the bus topology is the most common network design in small office/home office installations. Another advantage of the bus network is the relative ease of scaling the network up to the maximum number of devices or removing devices to scale down.
