Now, to talk about the idea of "how sure" we are, we'll use the ideas of **surprise** and **entropy**. The numerical surprise of observing that a random variable $X$ takes on value $k$ is:

```
$\log \frac{1}{P(X = k)} = -log P(X = k)$
```

The entropy of a random variable $H(X)$ is:

```
$H(X) = E[surprise]
= E_X[\log \frac{1}{P(X = k)}]
= \sum_{k} P(X = k) \log \frac{1}{P(X = k)}$
```

Here's a graph of entropy vs. $P(X = k)$ for a **binary discrete random variable** $X$ (as in, $k \in \{0,1\}$):

![](bbox=[0.305, 0.41, 0.694, 0.565])

Observe that (1) it's convex and (2) it's maximized when the probability distribution is **even** with respect to the outcomes. To say that, a coin is completely fair $P(X = 0) = P(X = 1) = 0.5$ has more entropy than a coin that is biased. This is because we are less sure of the outcome of the fair coin than the biased coin *overall*. Even though we are surprised when the biased coin comes up as its unlikely outcome, the way that entropy gives a higher uncertainty score to the fair coin. In general, a random variable has more entropy when the distribution of its outcomes is closer to uniform and less entropy when the distribution is highly skewed to one outcome.
