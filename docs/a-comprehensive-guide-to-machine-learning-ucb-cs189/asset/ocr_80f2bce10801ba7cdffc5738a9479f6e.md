Finally we have an analytical solution, but it is in a different coordinate system than our original problem! In particular, $u_d$ and $v_d$ are the best weights in a coordinate system where the data has been whitened and correlated. To bring it to our original coordinate system and find the vectors we care about ($u$ and $v$), we must invert the changes of variables:

```
$u = W_u D_u u_d \quad\quad v = W_v D_v v_d$
```

More generally, to get the best $k$ directions, we

```
$U_d = \begin{bmatrix} I_k \ O_{p-k,k} \end{bmatrix} \in R^{p \times k} \quad\quad V_d = \begin{bmatrix} I_k \ O_{q-k,k} \end{bmatrix} \in R^{q \times k}$
```

where $I_k$ denotes the $k$-dimensional identity matrix. Then

```
$U = W_u D_u U_d \quad\quad V = W_v D_v V_d$
```

Note that $U_d$ and $V_d$ have orthogonal columns. The columns of $U$ and $V$, which are the directions we see, in general not be orthogonal, but they will be linearly independent (since they come from the application of invertible matrices to the columns of $U_d, V_d$).

# Comparison with PCA

An advantage of CCA over PCA is that it is invariant to scaling and affine transformations of $X$ and $Y$. Consider a simple scenario where two matrix-valued random variables $X,Y$ satisfy $Y=X+\epsilon$ where $\epsilon$ has large variance. What happens when we use PCA on $Y$? PCA maximizes variance, it will project $Y$ (largely) into the column space of $\epsilon$; however, we are interested in $Y$'s relationship to $X$, not its dependence on $\epsilon$. How can we fix this? It turns out, CCA solves this issue. Instead of maximizing variance of $Y$, we maximize correlation between $X$ and $Y$. In some sense, we maximize the “predictive power” of information we have.

# CCA regression

Once we computed the CCA coefficients, one application is to use them for regression tasks, predicting $Y$ from $X$ (or vice versa). Recall that the correlation attains a greater value when the two sets of data are more correlated. Thus, it makes us to find the $k \times k$ weight matrix $A$ that linearly relates $X$ and $YX$. We can accomplish this with ordinary least squares.
