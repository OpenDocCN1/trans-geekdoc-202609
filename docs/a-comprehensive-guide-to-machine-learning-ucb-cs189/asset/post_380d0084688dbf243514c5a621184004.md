$$
$= \arg\min_{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^*, \mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{N}(X)} ||\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{r}) - y|_2^2 + \lambda|\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{r}||_2^2$$
$$= \arg\min_{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^*, \mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{N}(X)} ||\mathbf{XX}^T\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{X}\mathbf{r} - y|_2^2 + \lambda|\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{r}||_2^2$$
$$= \arg\min_{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^*, \mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{N}(X)} (\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{XX}^T\mathbf{v} - 2\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{XX}\mathbf{r} - 2y^T\mathbf{v} + ...) + \lambda (\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{XX}^T\mathbf{v} + 2\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{r} + ||\mathbf{r}||^2))$$
$$= \arg\min_{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^*, \mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{N}(X)} (\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{XX}^T\mathbf{v} - 2\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{r} + \lambda ||\mathbf{r}||^2)
$$

We crossed out $2\mathbf{r}$ and $2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{r}$ because $\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{N}(X)$ and therefore $Xr=0$. Now we are optimizing $L(v,r)$, which is **jointly convex** in $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{r}$, because its Hessian is PSD. Let's show that this is the case:

$$
\nabla^2 L(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{r}) = 2I \ge 0
$$
$$\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}L(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{r}) = \nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}L(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{r}) = 0
$$
$$\nabla^2_{\mathbf{v}}L(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{r}) = 2\mathbf{XX}^T + \lambda\mathbf{XX}^T \ge 0
$$

Since the cross terms of the Hessian are **0**, it follows that $\nabla^2_{\mathbf{r}}L(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{r})$ and $\nabla^2_{\mathbf{v}}L(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{r})$ are PSD to establish joint convexity. With joint convexity established we can get the gradient to $\mathbf{0}$ w.r.t $\mathbf{r}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ and obtain the global minimum:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}L(\mathbf{v}^*) = 2\mathbf{r}^* = 0 \implies r^* = 0
$$

Note that $\mathbf{r}^* = 0$ just happens to be in $\mathcal{N}(X)$, so it is a feasible point.

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}L(\mathbf{v}^*, \mathbf{r}^*) = 2\mathbf{XX}^T\mathbf{v}^* - 2\mathbf{XX}^T\mathbf{r}^* + \lambda\mathbf{XX}^T\mathbf{v}^* = 0
$$
$$\implies \mathbf{XX}^T\mathbf{v}^* + \lambda\mathbf{v}^* = 0
$$
$$\implies \mathbf{v}^* = (\mathbf{XX}^T + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}y
$$

Note that $\mathbf{XX}^T + \lambda\mathbf{I}$ is positive definite and therefore invertible, so we can compute $(\mathbf{XX}^T + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}y$. Even though $(\mathbf{XX}^T + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}y$ is a critical point for which the gradient is $\mathbf{0}$, it must achieve the global minimum because the objective is jointly convex. We conclude

$$
\mathbf{w}^* = (\mathbf{XX}^T + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}y
$$

Having derived the kernel regression formulation for the data matrix $\mathbf{X}$, we can apply the same logic to the augmented matrix $\Phi$ and replace the optimal expression with

$$
\mathbf{w}^* = (\Phi\Phi^T + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}y
$$

Let's explore the $\Phi\Phi^T$ term in kernel regression in more detail: ![](img/99defe6aed215976e83d281581f33_0.png

Each entry $\Phi_{ij}$ is a dot product between $\phi(x_i)$ and $\phi(x_j)$ and can be interpreted as a similarity measure:

$$
Phi_{ij} = \langle\phi(x_i), \phi(x_j)\rangle = k(x_i, x_j)
$$

where $k(\cdot)$ is the **kernel** function. The kernel function takes raw-feature inputs and outputs their inner product in the augmented feature space. We define the matrix of $k(x_i, x_j)$ terms as the **Gram matrix** and denote it as $\mathbf{K}$:

$$
\mathbf{K} = \Phi^T\Phi = \begin{pmatrix} k(x_1, x_1) & k(x_1, x_2) & \dots \\ k(x_2, x_1) & k(x_2, x_2) & \dots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ k(x_n, x_1) & k(x_n, x_2) & \dots \end{pmatrix}
$$

Formally, $k(x_1, x_2)$ is defined to be a valid kernel if there exists a feature map $\phi(\cdot)$ such that $\forall x_2$,

$$
k(x_1, x_2) = \langle\phi(x_1), \phi(x_2)\rangle
$$

Equivalently, we can also say that for all sets $D=\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$, the Gram matrix $K(D)$ is PSD.

Computing the Gram matrix $k(x_i, x_j)$ can be done in a straightforward fashion if we apply the feature map to $x$ and $x$, and then take their dot product in the augmented feature space — this takes $O(d)$ time, where $d$ is the dimensionality of the problem in the augmented feature space. However, if we use the **kernel trick**, we can perform this operation in $O(l + \log p)$ time, where $l$ is the dimensionality of the problem in the raw feature space and $p$ is the degree of the polynomials in the augmented feature space.

### 1.7 Kernel Trick

Suppose that we compute $k(x, z)$ using a $p$-degree polynomial feature map that maps $l$ dimensional inputs to $d=O(p^l)$ dimensional outputs. Let's take $p=2$ and $l=2$ as an example. We have

$$
k(x, z) = \langle\phi(x), \phi(z)\rangle
$$

$$
= \begin{pmatrix} x_1^2 & \sqrt{2}x_1x_2 & \sqrt{2}x_1 \\ \sqrt{2}x_1x_2 & x_2^2 & \sqrt{2}x_2 \\ \sqrt{2}x_1 & \sqrt{2}x_2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} z_1^2 & \sqrt{2}z_1z_2 & \sqrt{2}z_1 \\ \sqrt{2}z_1z_2 & z_2^2 & \sqrt{2}z_2 \\ \sqrt{2}z_1 & \sqrt{2}z_2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}^T

### Computational Analysis

Back to the original question — in ridge regression, should we compute

$$
\mathbf{w}^* = (\Phi\Phi^T + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}y
$$

or

$$
\mathbf{w}^* = (\Phi\Phi^T + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}y
$$

Let's compare their computational complexities. Suppose are given an arbitrary test point $z \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and you would like to compute its predicted value $\hat{y}$. Let's see how these values are calculated in each case:

- 1. Kernelized

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \langle\phi(z), \mathbf{w}^*\rangle = \phi(z)^T(\Phi\Phi^T + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}y = [k(x_1, z) \quad \dots \quad k(x_n, z)]^T(\mathbf{K} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}y
$$

Computing the $\mathbf{K}$ term takes $O(n^2(l + \log p))$, and inverting the matrix takes $O(n^3)$. These two computations dominate, for a total computation time of $O(n^3 + n^2(l + \log p))$.

- 2. Non-kernelized

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \langle\phi(z), \mathbf{w}^*\rangle = \phi(z)^T(\Phi\Phi^T + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\Phi^T y
$$

Computing the $\Phi\Phi^T$ term takes $O(nd^2)$, and inverting the matrix takes $O(d^3)$. These two computations dominate, for a total computation time of $O(d^3 + d^2n)$.

Here is the takeaway: if $d \ll n$, the non-kernelized method is preferable. Otherwise if $n \ll d$, the kernelized method is preferable.
