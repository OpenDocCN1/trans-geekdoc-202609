# Solution

To solve the TLS problem, we develop another formulation that can be solved using the singular decomposition.

Assume we have $n$ data points, $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d, y_i \in \mathbb{R}$, and we stack them to get

```
$(\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{f} \quad (6)$
```

where $\mathbf{w}$ is the weight, and $\mathbf{f}$ are noise terms that we need to explain the error in the model. Our goal is to minimize the Frobenius norm of the matrix composed of these error vectors. Recall that from a probabilistic perspective, finding the most likely value of a Gaussian corresponds to minimizing the squared distance from the mean. Since we assume the noise is 0-centered, we want to minimize the sum of squares of each entry in the error matrix, which corresponds exactly to minimizing the Frobenius norm. Thus we arrive at the following constrained optimization problem:

```
$\min_{\mathbf{w}} ||\mathbf{E}||_F^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad (\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{y}+\mathbf{f}$
```

In order to separate the term being minimized, we rearrange the constraint equation

```
$[\mathbf{X} \quad \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{w}]^\top=0 \quad (7)$
```

In linear algebraic terms, this expression shows that the vector $\mathbf{w}^\top$ lies in the nullspace of the matrix on the left. Note that $[\mathbf{X} \quad \mathbf{y}]$ would not be full rank if we observe the data with no noise, since we would have $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}$, which implies that the columns are linearly dependent. But the observations we get have noise, which makes the training data matrix full rank. To compensate,

> Recall the Frobenius norm is the standard Euclidean norm applied to the elements of a matrix instead of a vector.

```
$||\mathbf{A}||_F^2 = \sum_i\sum_j A_{ij}^2$
```

```
$[X \quad \mathbf{y}] = [\mathbf{u}_1 \quad \dots \quad \mathbf{u}_{d+1} \quad U_{rest}] \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & & & 0 \\ & \vdots & \vdots & \\ & & \sigma_{d+1} & \mathbf{v}_1^T \\ & & & \mathbf{v}_{d+1}^T \\ 0 & & & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad (8)$
```

where $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_{d+1} > 0$, and $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ are orthogonal matrices. Recall that this implies that multiplication by $\mathbf{U}$ or $\mathbf{V}$ does not change the Frobenius norm, so minimizing $||\mathbf{E} \mathbf{f}||_F^2$ is equivalent to minimizing $||\mathbf{E}' \mathbf{f}'||_F^2$ where $\mathbf{E}', \mathbf{f}'$ are $\mathbf{E}, \mathbf{f}$ expressed in the SVD coordinates. Now our problem reduces to finding

```
$\begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & & & 0 \\ & \vdots & \vdots & \\ & & \sigma_{d+1} & [\mathbf{E}' \mathbf{f}'] \\ & & & \vdots \\ 0 & & & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad (9)$
```

is full rank and $||\mathbf{E}' \mathbf{f}'||_F^2$ is as small as possible. Since the matrix on the left is diagonal, we can reduce its size by simply zeroing out one of its diagonal elements. Therefore our perturbation $[\mathbf{E}' \mathbf{f}']$ will have $-\sigma_j$ in the $(j,j)$ position for some $j$, and zeros everywhere else. To minimize the size of the perturbation, we decide to eliminate the smallest $\sigma_j$ by taking

```
$[\mathbf{E}' \mathbf{f}'] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & & & 0 \\ & \vdots & \vdots & \\ & &-\sigma_{d+1} & \\ & & \vdots & \vdots \\ 0 & & & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
```

Such a perturbation in SVD coordinates corresponds to a perturbation of

```
$[\mathbf{E} \mathbf{f}] = [\mathbf{u}_1 \quad \dots \quad \mathbf{u}_{d+1} \quad U_{rest}] \begin{bmatrix} 0 & & & 0 \\ & \vdots & \vdots & \\ & &-\sigma_{d+1}\mathbf{v}_{d+1}^T & & &&\mathbf{v}_{d+1}^T \\ 0 & & & 0 \end{bmatrix} = -\sigma_{d+1}\mathbf{u}_{d+1}\mathbf{v}_{d+1}^T$
```

in the original coordinate system. It turns out that this choice is optimal, as guaranteed by the Eckart-Young theorem, which is stated at the end for reference. The nullspace of our resulting matrix is then

```
$null([X \quad \mathbf{y}] + [\mathbf{E} \mathbf{f}]) = null(\sum_{j=1}^d \sigma_j \mathbf{u}_j \mathbf{v}_j^T) = span(\mathbf{v}_{d+1})$
```

where the last equality holds because $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_{d+1}\}$ form an orthogonal basis for $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$. To get the weight $\mathbf{w}$, we find a scaling $\alpha$ such that $[\mathbf{w}]^\top$ is in the nullspace, i.e.

```
$\mathbf{w} = \alpha v_{d+1}$
```

Once we have $v_{d+1}$, or any scalar multiple of it, we simply rescale it that the second component is 1 and since $v_{d+1}$ is a right-singular vector of $[\mathbf{X} \quad \mathbf{y}]$, it is an eigenvector of the matrix

```
$\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{X} \\ \mathbf{y} \end{bmatrix}^\top \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{X} & \mathbf{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} & \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} \\ \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{X} & \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y} \end{bmatrix}$
```

To gain an extra perspective, ignore the bottom equation (we can do this because we have an extra degree of freedom) and consider the solution of the top:

```
$\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} = -\sigma_{d+1}^2\mathbf{w} \quad (13)$
```

which can be rewritten as

```
$(\mathbf{X} + \sigma_{d+1}^2 I)\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{y} \quad (14)$
```

This looks like ridge regression, with a negative regularization constant! Why does this make sense? One of the motivations of ridge regression was to ensure that the matrix being inverted is in fact nonsingular, and subtracting a scalar multiple of the identity seems like a step in the opposite direction. We can make sense of this by recalling our original model:

```
$\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{X}_{true} + \mathbf{Z}$
```

where $\mathbf{X}_{true}$ are the actual values before corruption, and $\mathbf{Z}$ is zero-mean noise term. Then

```
$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}] = \mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{X}_{true} + \mathbf{Z})] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{true}] + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}]$
```

Observe that the off-diagonal terms of $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}]$ are zero because the $i$-th and $j$-th rows of $\mathbf{Z}$ are independent for $i \neq j$, and the on-diagonal terms are essentially variance. Thus the $-\sigma_{j+1}^2$ term is there to compensate for the noise introduced by our assumptions regarding independent variables.

# Eckart-Young Theorem

The Eckart-Young theorem essentially says that the best-rank approximation (in terms of the Frobenius norm) is obtained by throwing away the smallest singular values.

## 3.2. PRINCIPAL COMPONENT ANALYSIS

**Theorem.** Suppose $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ has rank $r \le \min(m,n)$, and let $A = \mathbf{U}\Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^\top$ be its singular value decomposition. Then

```
$\mathbf{A}_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^\top = \mathbf{U} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & & & \dots & 0 \\ & \vdots & \ddots & & 0 \\ 0 & & \sigma_k & & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \\ 0 & & & & 0 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{V}^\top$
```

where $k \le r$, it is the best rank-$k$ approximation to $A$ in the sense that

> $||\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{A}_k||_F \le ||\mathbf{A} - \hat{\mathbf{A}}||_F$

for any $\hat{\mathbf{A}}$ such that $rank(\hat{\mathbf{A}}) \le k$.

## 3.2 Principal Component Analysis

In machine learning, the data we have are often very high-dimensional. There are a number of reasons why we might want to work with a lower-dimensional representation:

- Visualization (if we can get down to 2 or 3 dimensions), e.g. for exploratory data analysis
- Reduce computational load
- Reduce noise

**Principal Component Analysis (PCA)** is an unsupervised dimensionality reduction technique. Given a matrix of data points, it finds one or more orthogonal directions that capture the largest amount of variance in the data. Intuitively, the directions with less variance contain less information and may be discarded without introducing too much error.

If $u \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a unit vector, i.c. $||u|| = 1$, the projection of another vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ onto $u$ is given by $x^\top u$.
