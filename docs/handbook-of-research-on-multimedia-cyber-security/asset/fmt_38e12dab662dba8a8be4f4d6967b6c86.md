
# Digital Fundus Images in Medical Sciences

## Overview

Digital fundus images are a particular class of medical images. Ophthalmology is the branch of medicine dealing with diseases and surgery of the visual pathways, including the eye, brain, and surrounding areas such as the eyelids. In ophthalmology, the fundus is the interior surface of the eye, comprising the retina, optic disc, and macula. The fundus can be viewed with an ophthalmoscope, and fundus images are captured using a fundus camera.

A fundus camera is a specialized low-power microscope with an attached camera designed to photograph the interior surface of the eye, as shown in **Figure 4**. Its optical design is based on the principle of monocular indirect ophthalmoscopy, providing an upright, magnified view of the fundus. A typical camera views 30 to 50 degrees of retinal area with a magnification of 2.5x, allowing modification through zoom or auxiliary lenses from 15 degrees (5x magnification) to 140 degrees (wide-angle lens minimizing the image by half). The optics are similar to an indirect ophthalmoscope in that the observation and illumination systems follow dissimilar paths (Coatrieux, Lamard, Puentes & Roux, 2005).

The observation light is focused via a series of lenses through a doughnut-shaped aperture, then passes through a central aperture to form an annulus, before passing through the camera objective lens and cornea onto the retina. Light reflected from the retina passes through the un-illuminated hole in the doughnut formed by the illumination system. As the light paths of the two systems are independent, there are minimal reflections of the light source captured in the formed image. The image-forming rays continue towards the low-powered telescopic eyepiece. When the button is pressed to take a picture, a mirror interrupts the path of the illumination system to allow light from the flashbulb to pass into the eye. Simultaneously, a mirror falls in front of the observation telescope, redirecting light onto the capturing medium. Because of the eye’s tendency to accommodate while looking through a telescope, it is imperative that the exiting vergence is parallel for an in-focus image to be formed on the capturing medium. An example of fundus images is shown in **Figure 5**. Fundus photography usually uses a green filter to acquire images of retinal blood vessels. Green light is absorbed by blood and appears a darker color in the fundus photograph than the background and the retinal nerve fiber layer. Hence, the green channel of fundus images possesses more valuable information for diagnosis than other channels (Hu, GuoHu, & Chen, 2005).

## Importance of Digital Fundus Images in Medical Sciences

Fundus images are used by optometrists, ophthalmologists, and trained medical professionals for monitoring disease progression and diagnosis. They can also be combined with retinal angiography for mass screening, where these images are analyzed first. Retinal fundus images are useful for the early detection of a number of ocular diseases which, if left untreated, can lead to blindness. Examinations using retinal fundus images are cost-effective and suitable for mass screening. Consequently, retinal fundus images are obtained in many health care centers and medical facilities during medical checkups for ophthalmic examinations. The increase in ophthalmic examinations improves ocular health care but also increases the workload of ophthalmologists. Therefore, modern health care systems are developed with Computer-Aided Design (CAD) and networking for analyzing retinal fundus images, assisting in reducing the workload of ophthalmologists and improving screening accuracy. Such modern health care systems require highly secure communication techniques for exchanging information (Wu & Liu, 2003).

Developing user authentication is a challenging task for digital fundus images. When images are sent through a network for suggestions and judgments from medical experts in various geographical locations, they must be secured against any form of external attacks.

## Scope of the Research in Digital Fundus Images

Research on digital fundus images is an emerging area with good scope in medical sciences. Regular fundus examinations can detect the initial signs of various major diseases like diabetic retinopathy, thereby drastically reducing the actual treatment cost of such diseases in later stages. In the security of medical images, digital fundus images have not been studied extensively. Hence, the theme of developing a security scheme for digital fundus images has been undertaken here.

## Key Requirements for Medical Image Authentication Scheme

The following are key requirements used to design and develop an authentication scheme for any class of medical images. The same requirements have been considered for digital fundus images; however, the behavior of the requirements will differ from one class of images to another.

### R1: Reversibility

Reversibility refers to the lossless watermarking scheme that retains the original image after the extraction process. The scheme is said to be irreversible if it cannot produce the original image without loss of information after extraction. Since fundus images are used for diagnosis purposes, the watermarking schemes must be reversible. The loss of information in such cases may lead to an inaccurate diagnosis. Hence, fundus images use only reversible watermarking schemes (Bovik, 2000; Muharemagic & Furht, 2004; Poonkuntran, Rajesh, & Eswaran, 2008).

### R2: Tamper Detection

Tamper detection is an essential feature in authentication. It detects unauthorized modifications made to fundus images while communicated over a network. This requirement can only detect modifications on the fundus images, not localize them (Bovik, 2000; Gonzalez & Woods, 2002).

### R3: Tamper Localization

This is another feature in authentication. It localizes modifications in the fundus images. Based on this requirement (R3), the second requirement (R2) is measured. For a given fundus image, if there are no modifications, then the image is authentic; otherwise, it is not authentic (Bovik, 2000; Gonzalez & Woods, 2002).

### R4: Imperceptibility

Imperceptibility refers to the level of appearance of the watermark in the fundus images under normal observation. Since the investigation is mainly done for authentication, it is to be invisible. The hidden watermark should not create any external artifacts in the images. External artifacts may create rooms for suspicion to hackers (Bovik, 2000).

### R5: Capacity

Capacity is the amount of message to be embedded inside the fundus image, measured in terms of the number of bits. The capacity will be in tradeoff with imperceptibility and fragility as shown in **Figure 6**.
