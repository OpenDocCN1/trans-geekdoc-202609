
# IPRs refer to thoughts, creations, and artistic works that the public recognizes as proprietary

IPRs confer exclusive rights to owners, enabling them to monetize their inventions or reputations. A variety of laws, such as those about patents, copyrights, trademarks, and others, protect intellectual property as represented in [Fig. 9.1](#9783111573861_013307.xhtml#b_9783111574288-009_fig_001).

## 9.2.1 The patent

A product or technique that presents a novel solution to an old problem or a new technical approach is called an invention. An invention is entitled to an exclusive patent, which gives the patent holder protection for their invention. This protection is implemented for a limited duration. A patented innovation cannot be manufactured, utilized, sold, or disseminated for profit without the consent of the patent holder. The patent holder is responsible for determining who may utilize the innovation and under what conditions while it is protected by the patent. Patent owners may license third parties to utilize the invention under mutually acceptable conditions. The owner may transfer the rights to the invention to a third party, who will then assume ownership of the patent. Upon the expiration of a patent, an invention forfeits its exclusive rights and becomes available for economic exploitation by others, thus concluding its protection [[18](#9783111573861_013307.xhtml#b_9783111574288-009_ref_018)]. The phrase “invention entering the public domain” denotes this concept. The majority of nations have enacted domestic laws to safeguard their IPRs. The protection granted to inventors and creators in specific countries, such as India, or regions, such as the European Union, is localized and cannot be transferred to other countries or regions, with the exception of copyrights [[19](#9783111573861_013307.xhtml#b_9783111574288-009_ref_019)]. A patent granted in India is valid just within that jurisdiction and cannot be enforced in the USA. The primary motive for patenting an idea is to generate profit from its exclusivity. The resultant patent would confer exclusive market rights to the patent owner if the inventor or their assignee: (a) developed a significant invention after considering customer modifications; and (b) the invention was precisely articulated and claimed in the patent specification prepared by the patent agent. The patent holder may exercise their exclusive rights by selling the patented invention directly to consumers or by licensing it to another entity [[20](#9783111573861_013307.xhtml#b_9783111574288-009_ref_020)].

Furthermore, a multinational accord referred to as the Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) was enacted in 1978. By specifying the countries of interest in the PCT application, an inventor from a PCT contracting state may concurrently get priority for their invention in any or all member nations. It obviates the necessity for the inventor to file a distinct application in each member nation [[21](#9783111573861_013307.xhtml#b_9783111574288-009_ref_021)]. To protect an innovation internationally, a distinct patent application must be filed in each pertinent country; in some circumstances, it must occur within a designated timeframe to get priority in those jurisdictions. A substantial and prompt financial investment is necessary to address filing fees, translation costs, legal charges, and other related expenditures. Moreover, the limited timeframe may render conclusions regarding the necessity of filing a patent application in a country misguided [[22](#9783111573861_013307.xhtml#b_9783111574288-009_ref_022)].

## 9.2.2 The copyright

The term “copyright” refers to the legal protection afforded to published works, which prohibits anyone other than the author from publishing or selling them. A set of exclusive rights known as copyright governs how an idea or piece of information is used in a specific way. In its broadest sense, it refers to “the right to copy” an original work. Literary works such as plays, novels, poems, and reference books, as well as technical drawings, maps, and advertisements, as well as computer programs, databases, movies, musical compositions, photographs, sculptures, and architecture, are a few examples of works that are covered by copyright. Works that are the product of human intelligence and creativity are protected by copyright. Both published and unpublished works are included. Expressions of ideas are protected by copyright, not the ideas themselves. For a predetermined amount of time, it permits the copyright holder to prevent others from reproducing the work [[23](#9783111573861_013307.xhtml#b_9783111574288-009_ref_023)]. As it gives creators of content exclusive rights over their original works, copyright law is crucial. Among other things, it offers protection for works of art, music, literature, and film. Authors can manage how their works are reproduced, distributed, performed in public, and altered due to copyright laws. Content creators are better equipped to protect their works when they are aware of the extent of copyright protection, including the duration of rights, the requirements for copyright registration, and the notion of fair use [[24](#9783111573861_013307.xhtml#b_9783111574288-009_ref_024)].

A multitude of research initiatives have been conducted across different countries to examine copyright legislation from diverse perspectives. The study’s author focused on copyright and public domain legislation. The study indicates that the public domain is a fundamental component of copyright law, and its incorporation meets both historical and logical criteria. Without citing “public domain copyright,” one cannot justify their actions. The study goes on to say that copyright and public domain are similar in that they have similar evaluative values. According to a related study, the general public is a stakeholder in copyrights. According to the author, the US copyright law grants creators several exclusive rights, many of whom either knowingly or unknowingly give up these rights to business entities [[25](#9783111573861_013307.xhtml#b_9783111574288-009_ref_025)]. Citizens should support a copyright system that considers their interests since the general public is now considered to be a stakeholder in copyright. Traditionally vested interests must come first. The ethical and financial aspects of copyright were demonstrated by three additional studies on copyright policy. The term of protection was the subject of one study. It concluded that extending the protection would be detrimental and needless. According to the second study, there are two different schools of thought on copyright law: the first emphasizes the creators’ inherent rights to the money made from their works, while the second highlights the high cost of creating a framework for copyright that maximizes social welfare. The study’s findings ultimately demonstrated that the “natural rights view” had a stronger impact on Canada’s copyright laws than the economic perspective [[26](#9783111573861_013307.xhtml#b_9783111574288-009_ref_026)].
