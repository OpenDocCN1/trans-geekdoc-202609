
# AI in Pharmacovigilance and Drug Safety

To improve pharmacovigilance, statistical analysis and machine learning techniques have gained increasing traction in recent years. In a study, two machine learning techniques were used: decision trees as well as inductive logic programming, as shown in [Fig. 8.2](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_fig_002), to look into common side effect profiles depending on combined drug and target descriptors. Of these, the inductive-logic programming approach is the most effective in relational learning, outperforming decision trees and leveraging not only drug properties, but also past knowledge [[33]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_033). In a different study, single nucleotide mutations were analyzed using artificial neural networks; however, no significant findings were obtained. Several commonly used algorithms for drug toxicity predictions, including cancer risk, mutagenicity, hepatotoxicity, acute oral toxicities, as well as cardiovascular toxicity, include naive Bayesian, support vector machines, random forests, and back propagation neural networks. These techniques have a well-established theory and are simple to apply [[34]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_034).

Moreover, `Naive Bayesian (NB)` is a simple Bayesian probabilistic classifier. The feature vectors in the analysis are largely unaffected by the decision variables. Every piece of data needs to be categorized as input, and the output serves as the training sample and characteristic attribute. Each feature attribute for each class of conditional probability estimations is categorized during the classifier training stage, after which the frequency of occurrence of every group in the training sample is calculated. A later classifier is used to categorize the classification items. Using sources of drug information, the following approach has been studied for side-effect prediction [[35]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_035). It has also been researched for the preclinical safety evaluation of novel medications, as well as for the forecasting of the carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, and hepatotoxicity of various substances.

`ANNs` are inspired by biological neural networks, which use example-based learning to solve problems in the real world [[36]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_036). It is a paradigm for using highly networked neurons to process information. It is positioned as a scientific and engineering study specialist due to its remarkable capacity to handle imprecise datasets and discover intricate patterns from complex data sets. It has been researched to predict side effects from medication side information [[37]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_037). By extracting word subsequences that characterize ADRs, the technique has been used to classify them [[38]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_038). Potential applications have included the analysis of single nucleotide variation changes for drug safety, preclinical safety evaluations of new drugs, and modeling and simulation-based prediction of the carcinogenicity and mutagenicity of chemicals [[39]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_039), [[40]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_040), [[41]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_041), [[42]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_042).

Representational learning, which generates predictive features from data, is a basic concept in machine learning. The process lessens the need for the laborious, sometimes necessary, human feature engineering found in non-ML techniques. One method for representational learning that can be enhanced is regression. Non-ML AI expressly uses symbolic representations of human reasoning and coded domain knowledge. These analyses can be regarded as falling outside the purview of artificial intelligence in pharmacovigilance (`AIPV`) because they initially relied on static rules that were unable to self-correct or self-learn [[43]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_043). However, AI in general, and `AIPV` in particular, are still evolving, if unfortunately, sometimes in lockstep with hype cycles. The twenty-first century is seeing a resurgence of sorts for non-ML symbolic AI, so it’s important not to write it off entirely. There are two illustrative examples. First of all, description logic is a web-based system for knowledge representation that enables data to be analyzed and read by machines [[44]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_044). Better adverse event cases and queries, in addition to data set integration and inferencing, are made easier by the language. These elements matter in many different PV domains. Second, hybrid methods combine symbolic AI and deep learning to enhance each other. One example of this is neurosymbolic AI. These methods are currently being used in PV-related fields [[45]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_045).

## 8.3 Challenges and Opportunities of AI in Pharmacovigilance and Drug Safety

In LMICs, there is a dearth of PV healthcare professionals (HCPs) as a result of inadequate training and educational opportunities. Professionals in a traditional PV system operate in four domains: qualified persons for pharmacovigilance (`QPPV`), systems, operations, and surveillance. Traditional PV requires `QPPVs`, data/system security administrators, drug safety physicians, and supervisors of drug safety. But for AI-based PV, specialists in a variety of AI domains are also required. For example, machine learning, as well as natural language processing (`NLP`) algorithm development, requires engineers. It is yet another significant barrier for LMICs. Very few LMICs are among the very few nations that provide cross-specialty, even cross-language learning for AI-based PV. Although AI technologies have already been used in pharmacovigilance systems, there are currently very few dedicated training programs for AI-based pharmacovigilance systems available globally. The lack of funding for healthcare professionals (HCPs) from low- and middle-income countries may also restrict the opportunities for HCPs to attend these courses. Despite this, HCPs in developed nations could receive training in AI technologies like data mining, deep learning, and natural language processing. However, there aren’t many options for AI training in LMICs [[46]](#9783111573861_011894.xhtml#b_9783111574288-008_ref_046).
