
# AI in Pharmacovigilance and Post-Marketing Surveillance

In a study published by Celgene and IBM, deep-learning techniques to evaluate ICSR processing exceeded many accuracy rate thresholds, suggesting the potential of AI to improve data consistency and quality along with reporting efficiency [[20](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_020)]. AI in pharmacovigilance is favored by drug safety professionals, per a survey conducted by Celgene’s Global Drug Safety and Risk Management division. This is because it would free them up to concentrate on information evaluation and analysis as opposed to labor-intensive tasks [[21](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_021), [22](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_022), [23](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_023), [24](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_024)]. Similarly, artificial intelligence (AI) solutions have a lot to offer PMS practitioners as well as medical device manufacturers. They can accomplish this by lowering the time and cost associated with adhering to EU MDR/IVDR regulations and by establishing flexible, fact-based quality management systems that promote innovation and more deliberate decision-making. Understanding that AI can enhance and simplify adherence to QIAGEN, Huma, and EU MDR/IVDR, AI developed a state-of-the-art post-market intelligence platform to enhance signal detection and expedite PMS workflows linked to risk and performance evaluation through literature surveillance [[25](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_025)]. Keeping an eye on published research is essential to PMS because it can offer important insights into pathogen genetics, patient demographics, emerging technologies, the safety and efficacy of devices, and possible off-label use. Nonetheless, more than 35 million citations and abstracts are stored in biomedical databases like PubMed [[26](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_026)], and approximately 1.4 million new citations are added annually [[27](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_027), [28](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_028)].

## 11.2 Role of Machine Learning in Post-Marketing Surveillance

There has always been a need for trustworthy, effective, and cost-effective techniques to evaluate students’ written and spoken abilities. Project Essay Grader (PEG), created in 1966 by Ellis Page, is an application that generates computer-generated scores according to writing characteristics like grammar, essay length, and structure. With it, automatic essay scoring (AES) technological advances were introduced [[29](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_029), [30](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_030)]. AES systems, like PEG, are designed to make essay scoring faster and easier, with the goal of either supporting or replacing human scorers [[31](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_031)]. These kinds of systems use predictive models that analyze the text and, depending on how well the results correlate with scores generated by humans, generate standardized scores at varying degrees of accuracy in forecasting. Since the release of PEG, numerous other AES systems have been invented to improve predictive model accuracy and provide extra features like critical feedback and plagiarism checks. AES systems are currently divided into two main categories: those that use raw-text techniques and those that use hand-engineered essay features [[32](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_032)]. Hand-engineered features, which employ domain expert knowledge to produce numerical illustrations of the text that allow basic statistical modeling techniques to find relevant patterns for scoring, have long been the industry standard. In raw text approaches, the space of possible words is mapped to a higher multidimensional integrating space using statistical models, usually neural networks, encoding a deeper comprehension of the word [[33](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_033)].

Moreover, BERT turned out to be the most efficient technique for all the macrostructure components. The reason for BERT’s success could be traced back to a method known as transfer learning, which involves pre-training the system on a sizable corpus to give it a deep understanding of the English language. To help explain words with unclear semantics, the technique may be employed as well to train the model to understand each word’s embedding in the wider context of its sequence. Lastly, word embedding creates a semantically connected lower-dimensional space in which unknown phrases can still be portrayed despite their association with known words, which allows BERT and GVEL to handle unseen words in training [[34](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_034)]. Alternatively, without any prior training, the CMRF and TIRF methods were compelled to model the narrative structures to score these different elements from the bottom up. For instance, CMRF and TIRF had no structures to deal with the sequential nature of the text, so to score “Initiating Event,” they had to figure out the way an initiating incident could be stated from a small sample of multiple narratives. The inability of these models to handle words that were not included in the initial training set makes them even more limited in practice, and it may have additionally contributed to their subpar results on the tests that were not shown. However, GVEL uses some transfer learning principles and manages sequences naturally through the LSTM. Given the literature’s reports of LSTM performance, it’s plausible that an LSTM with sufficient training data could beat BERT. However, the experiments showed that training an LSTM from unknown weights is very difficult and ultimately not feasible, requiring many narratives to acquire these kinds of dependencies and patterns [[35](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_035), [36](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_036)].

While AES systems have many obvious applications, their main use in development has been to score high-stakes written assessments like the SAT and GRE. AES technology is very useful but not always readily available, although well-known systems like PEG, E-Rater, and IntelliMetric are exclusive technologies that are not accessible to the general public. Thus, if clinical practitioners in a variety of disciplines, including psychology, pathology, and education are to benefit from AES technology open-access technology must be developed. The fact that many AES systems rely on holistic scoring techniques, which aim to assess writing quality overall using a point system, is another disadvantage of these systems. Although it is the preferred method for grading standardized exams such as the GRE, scoring systems based on a rubric can provide more detailed feedback by highlighting specific areas of a writer’s work that are strong or weak. Educators, psychologists, and clinicians frequently use tools with a rubric to identify language deficit areas in children as well as track changes in the children’s language abilities over time [[37](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_037)]. It is especially recommended that medical professionals, like speech-language pathologists (SLPs), take language samples from their patients to identify their strengths and weaknesses and track improvement during the intervention. During the school-age years, narrative examples of language are the ideal elicitation context; however, due to time constraints, many clinicians do not use them [[38](#9783111573861_015802.xhtml#b_9783111574288-011_ref_038)].
