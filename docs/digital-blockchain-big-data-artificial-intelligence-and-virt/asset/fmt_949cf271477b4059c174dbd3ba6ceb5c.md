
# Pharmacokinetic and PK/PD Modeling with Machine Learning

Pharmacokinetic and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) modelings are essential methodologies in drug discovery, development, regulatory assessment, and approval. Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic modeling is crucial for the design of clinical trials, data analysis, and dosage determination.

Non-compartmental analysis, lacking structural model assumptions, produces macroscopic pharmacokinetic parameters (area, mean residence time, and variation of residence time) that define the temporal variations of concentration levels in the circulation [[95](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_095)]. Nonetheless, the methodology has constraints in extrapolating for applications such as including temporal pharmacodynamic data and fails to delineate the temporal evolution of pharmacokinetic data.

Furthermore, blood circulation and the anatomical composition of the organs are used in physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling (PBPK) to more precisely represent actual physiology. Nevertheless, the methodology continues to rely on structural models and requires rather extensive data for modeling. Furthermore, it is based on numerous difficult-to-validate assumptions [[96](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_096)].

A multitude of structure-based PK/PD models exist, each dependent on the drug’s mechanisms of action, the feasibility of doing PK/PD sampling, and additional factors. Deep learning is presently widely utilized in numerous fields, including pharmaceutical research, due to the development of increasingly robust deep learning neural networks. There has been an increasing interest in employing neural networks for modeling pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) [[97](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_097)].

The researchers demonstrated that the network proficiently captured the temporal correlations in the pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) relationship by utilizing a long short-term memory (LSTM) network to assess the PK/PD data of a hypothetical drug based on an indirect response model. The LSTM network was applied to examine the data used for the evaluation of PK/PD data [[98](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_098)].

## Novel Deep Learning Technique for PK/PD Models

A novel deep learning technique creates PK/PD models that can simulate the effects of unknown dosage regimens and forecast patient response time courses by directly learning the governing equations from data. The method is unique in two respects.

First, the deep learning model’s architecture guarantees the preservation of the pharmacological principle of the dose-concentration effect. This means that the model presupposes a causal relationship in which drug concentration is driven by dosage, and effect dynamics are subsequently driven by dosage.

Second, the network architecture is set up so that the dosing data enters the model through two different ports, guaranteeing that the model can simulate the patient’s dosing regimen in addition to predicting the treatment data that is currently available [[99](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_099)].

## ML-Based QSAR and PBPK Integration

Quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models, used to predict ADME properties and analyze complex pharmacokinetic profiles of drugs and nanoparticles, have been developed using ML techniques. Furthermore, researchers initially used support vector machines, a notable ML method, as a prospective tool in quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) studies to forecast the tissue/blood partition coefficients (Kp) of organic compounds in various tissues.

Subsequent research utilized ML techniques to predict many pharmacological parameters, including intestinal absorption in individuals. Researchers conducted multiple studies evaluating tissue and plasma concentrations, as well as various pharmacokinetic parameters (including AUC, Cmax, and t1/2) in both people and animals, by combining a ML-based QSAR model with a simplified PBPK model [[100](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_100), [101](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_101)].

Likewise, additional researchers computed the pharmacokinetic parameters, such as AUC, Cmax, Vd, and mean residence time, employing the ML/AI-based pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic modeling program BIOiSIM. BIOiSIM, an AI biosimulation program, utilizes the PBPK model, distinguished by numerous separate compartments that represent essential organs. The model was incorporated with existing in vivo datasets to forecast the first-order absorption rate constant (h1), Kp, and the blood-to-plasma ratio. The PBPK model was subsequently executed [[101](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_101)].

## XGBoost Applications in Transplant Pharmacokinetics

Researchers computed the interdose AUC of everolimus in recipients of renal transplants using XGBoost ML algorithms. Because the training dataset containing real patient data was smaller than that of other immunosuppressants, ML algorithms were taught on patient, simulated, and hybrid data.

The estimates were subsequently compared between MAP-BE AUC estimates, derived from trapezoidal AUCs obtained from an independent database of transplant recipients with kidneys, which had been compiled through extensive sampling and a three-point limited sampling strategy utilized by the ISBA expert system. Consequently, simulated training datasets of varying sizes were evaluated using many measures, with imprecision (i.e., RMSE) as the primary parameter [[102](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_102)].

The best outcomes were obtained by ML algorithms that were trained on numerous simulations without patient data. However, RMSE does not represent the exact error in a specific patient; rather, it represents imprecision within the dataset. Researchers trained a PK model on a limited set of PK profiles, and during that process, they generated a MAP-BE. The model was not, however, independently validated.

In a similar line, PK in whole blood and mononuclear cells in peripheral blood was explained by a different team of researchers using a PK model that did just a little bit better than the algorithm that was trained on patient data [[103](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_103), [104](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_104)].

Despite training on a large amount of data, the XGBoost algorithm’s performance was only slightly better than the previous MAP-BE. Because of the extremely high peak concentration at an hour, these unusual flat profiles either led to an underestimate of the atypical cases or an overestimation of the cases. The modeling approach, which intentionally anticipated a wide range of scenarios, presumably addressed these scenarios better.

To enhance the ML algorithms and attain an equivalent degree of precision and accuracy for a pair of drugs, it would take a long time to gather the same amount of real patient data [[105](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_105), [106](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_106)].

Researchers chose to enhance ML as a test that could be used for other drugs by combining patient as well as simulated profiles in the training dataset or by relying exclusively on a high number of simulations. The last strategy turned out to be the most successful since it yielded superior outcomes over the whole AUC value range of the external validation dataset [[107](#9783111573861_008368.xhtml#b_9783111574288-006_ref_107)].
