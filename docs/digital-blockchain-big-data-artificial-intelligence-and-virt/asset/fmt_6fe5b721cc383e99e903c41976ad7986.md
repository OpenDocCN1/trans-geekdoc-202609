
## 3.2.5 Collaborations with R&D industries

There are several inherent risks associated with forming a successful strategic partnership for R&D activities, particularly when considering the context of multinational enterprises’ (MNEs) knowledge misappropriation. According to resource-based theory, small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) nevertheless need to collaborate on R&D projects with large corporations despite the possibility of technology secrets being misappropriated. It requires strong intellectual property policies (IPPs) to protect the SMEs’ vital proprietary technology. Additionally, R&D outsourcing and collaboration are brought on by the growth of large-scale cooperative multi-technological products [49](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_049), [50](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_050). If SMEs don’t have enough resources, big businesses won’t be able to acquire the cutting-edge information and tools needed to add value. Trust in the ability to exchange sufficient information is one of the key success factors in the collaboration between large corporations and SMEs. Based on the types of new markets that are entered, knowledge management systems, and R&D systems, researchers propose significant distinctions between open and closed innovation. Certain researchers conduct analyses of open innovation cases based on factors such as industry, size, and the types of open innovation, which include technology exploration and exploitation. According to the research, medium-sized businesses engage in more open innovation than small businesses do, and they are motivated to collaborate in the market by factors like new market development and customer needs realization. The open innovation strategy and how firm size affects the degree to which the tactic is chosen are discussed by other researchers [51](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_051), [52](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_052).

## 3.3 Motivation for alliances in the pharma sector

Small biotech companies provide the pharmaceutical industry with “external” sources of research and development. A deficiency in internal development is a primary reason for larger companies to seek new product technology from external sources. However, numerous minor factors frequently encourage the use of external procurement to facilitate the development of new technological innovations in the industry: (1) the licensee firm’s functional capabilities have a high absorptive capacity for new technologies, (2) the top management believes that external sources will complement internal sources, (3) the environment is perceived as too hostile and hazardous for R&D investment due to rapid product changes and regulatory uncertainties, (4) the company’s management is risk-averse, (5) the company has extensive experience with external sourcing, (6) the management has a strong global orientation, and (7) there are substantial opportunities for external sourcing [53](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_053), [54](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_054). Firms can contribute substantial resources through alliances that are greater than what each individual cooperating firm could provide. These alliances frequently unite businesses that possess complementary core competencies, which facilitate the expansion of the companies into new markets, the surmounting of trade obstacles, and the development of new products [55](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_055). The following strategic justifications for alliances are provided: (1) to resolve technological gaps; (2) to mitigate the risks associated with entering new markets, including international ones; (3) to accelerate the introduction of new products into the market; (4) to achieve scale economies; (5) to reduce trade and legal barriers; (6) to broaden the scope of current operations; and (7) to reduce divestment costs [56](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_056).
