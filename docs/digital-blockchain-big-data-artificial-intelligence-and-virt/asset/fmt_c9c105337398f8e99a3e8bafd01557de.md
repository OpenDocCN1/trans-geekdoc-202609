
# 13.4 Future trends and innovations of AI in healthcare

Given the restrictions on the use of contemporary technology that affect the decisions that are made about people’s lives, it may be difficult to replace every antiquated or traditional scientific technique that is part of the healthcare infrastructure of today. These issues won’t be resolved until every element of the current infrastructure – including the medical personnel – supports improving the situation and the available resources. Furthermore, it is crucial to comprehend the potential benefits and drawbacks of utilizing the technology, which may require modifications to the current infrastructure. Ultimately, it would require a large amount of budgetary and financial support from the organization in charge of overseeing healthcare infrastructure. Even though there are certain limitations on the use of contemporary technology that would place those in charge of making crucial decisions about people’s lives in a difficult position, there might be difficulties in replacing all of the antiquated or traditional technical techniques that are employed in the current medical system [42]. In addition to their primary role in addressing current issues in their respective fields of study, AI and ML-based solutions can be implemented in several healthcare subfields. One such area, which contributes to the annual increase in global death rates, is the diagnosis of common illnesses and diseases. A significant portion of this issue can be resolved with the aid of deep neural networks and other AI-based technologies. With the rapid rate of population growth, doctors and other staff members would also find it challenging to complete their duties effectively due to excessive workloads and an absence of free time. As a result, hospitals can use ML and AI to help them solve the issue. These technologies can save critical time and resources by learning to predict every possible outcome at every stage with little room for error, which speeds up subsequent procedures. However, to carry out such solutions, hospitals might have to make investments in training physicians to use the technology, which requires the preparation of large datasets. Despite this, the existing infrastructure can incorporate virtual intelligence with minimal upfront costs for setting up internal diagnostic centers. It is anticipated that the long-term benefits will exceed those initial costs [43].

AI in health research and initiatives has surged in the last few years, combining various clinical data elements, establishing links between bio repositories and clinical data, and creating links between clinical data and pharmaceutical research and development. More importantly, knowledge and data must be combined to create AI algorithms for health that work. Unlike other computational fields like sight and speech evaluation, where enormous amounts of information can be obtained, patient data is often sparse and subject to substantial variability [44, 45]. Furthermore, health issues in the real world are frequently complicated. Rule-based systems, for example, are examples of conventional AI technologies with high interpretability. Even though deep-learning models and other recent AI technologies may achieve beneficial quantitative performance, they are largely disregarded. Interpretability of models is essential and has been the topic of many conversations lately. The model interpretability may not always be as important as it might be, especially in scenarios where AI algorithms can produce accurate results consistently and widely. However, it isn’t the medical case – at most not with computer science currently dominating healthcare analytics [46, 47, 48]. For instance, it demonstrated that when employing claims data and electronic health records for readmission to hospital tasks, deep learning models were able to match the effectiveness of logistic regression. While deep-learning algorithms have demonstrated cutting-edge efficacy for medical image analysis, their generalizability remains questionable. In other words, it is difficult to argue that a model that performs well on a medical image data set from one radiology center will perform well on a data set from another radiology center. Additionally, human clinicians typically make decisions in healthcare, with AI algorithms serving as tools to assist them. To reassure the clinician, it is crucial to present comprehensive explanations for the assumptions made by those AI algorithms. Additionally, integrating AI algorithms into routine clinical workflows will enhance their clinical utility [49, 50].

While there are numerous AI models being developed for healthcare, there is a potential risk regarding their security. Researchers found that autonomous driving systems can be easily tricked by pollution on transportation signs. Researchers found that even small changes to a patient’s electronic health record can significantly change the predictor’s mortality estimate, even if the predictor is otherwise well-trained [51, 52, 53, 54]. A more thorough explanation of the possible worries regarding the “incentives for developed adversarial assaults” in the healthcare sector is given by another researcher. According to the authors of the study, (i) medical practitioners must understand the possible risk; (ii) AI researchers create strong defenses against medical adversarial assaults; and (iii) it is recommended that policymakers consider the possibility of model security risks when developing new regulatory frameworks. All AI models require samples of training data [55]. Generally, patient shifts and the intricate nature of their health issues are not well captured by a training sample size that is derived from patient data. A model created with patients from a single facility frequently does not work with patients from another. One of the main problems AI still has in the healthcare industry is the problem, also known as bias in the data. The researcher claims that bias of the following kind can exacerbate health disparities. The collection of extensive and varied patient data sets is one strategy to lessen bias [56, 57, 58].
