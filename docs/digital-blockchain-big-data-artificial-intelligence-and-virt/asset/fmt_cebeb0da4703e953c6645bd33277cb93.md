
## 7.6 Conclusion

AI, ML, and data mining are being used by pharmaceutical companies more and more to improve model generalization, drug discovery, and decision support systems (DSS). There are two categories for these technologies: knowledge-based and non-knowledge-based. Empirical modeling is the preferred method for creating the right model in pharmaceutical formulations, eliminating the need for prior knowledge and assumptions. AI-powered CDSS can help doctors, nurses, and patients make informed decisions by examining patient data from the past, present, and future. Two types of decision support systems are ML systems and rule-based ES. AI systems have shown good accuracy in diagnosing *H. pylori* infections and gastric cancer, while deep learning models identify and classify esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinomas. Natural language processing, or NLP, is being used increasingly in the medical field to give clinicians a way to communicate with patients and to enhance the use of unstructured electronic health records, or EHRs. AI-CDSS implementation in medicine faces challenges due to human factors, social and organizational aspects, and a lack of trust among clinicians. AI technology in healthcare has the potential to become autonomous and threaten human autonomy, but it must be fully under human control and informed about its advantages and risks. Trustworthiness, data security, privacy, and frequent audits are crucial for AI-based diagnostic tools. In the future, patients will be the primary influencers in determining operational performance metrics and product specifications. Cost-effective technologies and advanced decision-support tools can lead to decreased production costs, lead times, and inventories. Collaboration between experts in social and behavioral science, mental health, and cognitive science is recommended for developing ML-based decision-support systems.

A multidisciplinary process should take into account the requirements of all stakeholders when developing an ML-based CDSS. This is particularly valid when these systems are integrated with XAI. Decision-makers who utilize the systems, people impacted by the decisions’ outcomes, and system designers should all be taken into account [[108](#9783111573861_010289.xhtml#b_9783111574288-007_ref_108), [109](#9783111573861_010289.xhtml#b_9783111574288-007_ref_109)]. It is recommended that experts in the domains of psychological and social behavioral psychology, mental health, and cognitive science collaborate when developing models [[110](#9783111573861_010289.xhtml#b_9783111574288-007_ref_110)]. Unstructured medical data continues to be a difficulty for the advancement of practical AI-based systems, despite the ability of XAI to aid in identifying data-related difficulties. It is necessary to consider domain-specific requirements, such as having a clear grasp of the system’s goal, evaluating the effectiveness and interpretability of the systems already in place, and determining the kind and volume of necessary explanations. Moreover, scientists [[108](#9783111573861_010289.xhtml#b_9783111574288-007_ref_108)] recommend against using those black-box simulations unless necessary. They recommend against using complicated algorithms that call for the post-mortem application of XAI methods whenever feasible. Rather, they suggest using transparent or interpretable by-design algorithms. The audience’s cognitive abilities and limitations, as well as implications for ethics, justice, and safety, must all be taken into account when determining the kind of explanations that should be given. More thorough user studies are required [[111](#9783111573861_010289.xhtml#b_9783111574288-007_ref_111)]. Some authors [[112](#9783111573861_010289.xhtml#b_9783111574288-007_ref_112)] claim that while giving doctors a more detailed explanation of the data used to generate the system’s recommended diagnosis boosted trust, it also led to issues with over-reliance. Less thorough explanations, on the other hand, had a reverse impact, leading participants to doubt the system’s dependability and develop problems with self-reliance [[113](#9783111573861_010289.xhtml#b_9783111574288-007_ref_113)]. Some researchers discovered through an investigation that inaccurate ML recommendations can have an impact on clinicians and reduce decision accuracy, and that explanations alone are insufficient to address an over-reliance on an algorithm that proposes incorrect decisions. It was discovered that the choice of explanation strategies should be based on the clinicians’ experience with ML and that the recommendations from ML were more useful to those who had prior experience.

However, generative chemistry, ML, and methods like Pareto optimization are increasingly being used in AI approaches for hit and lead discovery. Community-based platforms that provide resources and information for AI4DD technologies are strong supporters of the trend. Predictive toxicology is making significant strides, and regulatory bodies are positioning themselves to profit from AI [[114](#9783111573861_010289.xhtml#b_9783111574288-007_ref_114)]. AI technology can lower healthcare expenses by increasing productivity and decreasing mistakes. It is essential to acknowledge that there remain certain constraints and challenges associated with the utilization of AI in pharmacy that require resolution. For example, using AI-powered systems to track information about patients may raise security and privacy issues. Pharmacists might also require additional training to properly apply and incorporate artificial intelligence into their work. Future research may also focus on the ethical implications associated with the utilization of AI in pharmacy. As technology advances, it is essential to address concerns such as algorithmic bias, data security and confidentiality, and the possibility of AI systems supplanting human labor. By addressing these concerns judiciously and proactively, pharmacists can integrate AI in a manner that is both ethical and efficacious [[115](#9783111573861_010289.xhtml#b_9783111574288-007_ref_115)].
