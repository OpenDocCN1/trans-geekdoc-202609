## 10.2 Regulatory bodies and framework

The objectives of every national pharmaceutical legislations worldwide are to assess and address the global convergence and harmonization of pharmaceutical laws. The predominant basis for national pharmaceutical evaluations is the uniformity of efficacy, safety, and quality requirements. Nonetheless, national legislation and technical specifications have differed, at times considerably, and the execution of procedures and systems to attain these identical aims has been markedly dissimilar. Globally, numerous frameworks exist for the regulation of drugs [16]. These are determined by public health requirements, the magnitude of the pharmaceutical market, and the resources at hand. Various institutional pharmaceutical systems have been built as a result of the diverse histories, cultures, political experiences, and economic profiles of nations. Furthermore, there are still significant gaps in the regulatory capacities of industrialized and developing nations, even with some advancement in recent years. The availability of medications and the state of pharmaceutical systems and regulations vary greatly among global regions. ### 10.2.1 Food and Drug Administration (FDA)

The safety of prescription medications is a vital public health issue for millions of Americans dependent on these pharmaceuticals for disease management and treatment. The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FDC Act) designates the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) with the responsibility of ensuring the safety and efficacy of all prescription medications for patient use. The entity that developed the drug must submit an application to the FDA for assessment and approval, accompanied by sufficient data and information to demonstrate the drug’s efficacy and safety, before its authorized sale to American consumers. The FDA assesses the advantages and disadvantages when deciding on the approval or possible withdrawal of prescription medication by the FDC Act and its related regulations. The statute and regulations do not establish a quantitative threshold for drug safety findings or a specific method for drug safety analysis [17]. The FDA authorizes the application of a drug for specific indications within the regulatory framework based on the supporting data that is presented. The drug sponsor conducted numerous clinical trials throughout three phases to assess the medication’s efficacy and safety for its intended purpose. Typically, six comprehensive Phase III trials are randomized clinical trials that include treatment and control groups for comparative analysis. The FDA also authorizes the medication’s labeling, which includes exhaustive information about the drug’s chemistry, toxic effects, clinical data, evidence, and warnings, as well as usage characteristics. These clarifications are intended for healthcare professionals, including physicians and chemists. The labeling is present during the drug’s transfer from the sponsor to the dispenser [18].
