
# Big Data and AI in Drug Development

Big data, or an enormous collection of extensive data from many domains, is increasingly being used in drug development. To analyze the heterogeneous data – which ranges from clinical to bioassay – advanced computer strategies and processing capacity are required. Big data analysis is moving toward high-performance computers, cloud services, and GPUs due to the expanding volume of big data. The intricacy of the data must be resolved in part by AI, underscoring the necessity of sophisticated computing skills in the pharmaceutical sector [[56](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_056), [57](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_057)].

Drug research and discovery depend heavily on multidimensional data, such as target structures, chemical assays, chemical frameworks, and clinical data. There are now more opportunities for the successful use of AI in medication development due to the quick growth of these data sources. Advances in hardware have also boosted data availability and processing capability, opening new modeling methodologies such as large-scale networks. Because biocompatible nanomaterials, a kind of modern nanotechnology, offer flexible and dependable drug delivery systems for systemic disorders like cancer, they have had a substantial influence on drug development. The development of biocompatible nanomaterials, AI, and hardware is opening the door to a more successful and efficient drug discovery method [[58](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_058), [59](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_059)].

Molecular dynamic (MD) simulations served as the foundation for early attempts to apply AI to nano simulation for drug development. Later, a similar method was employed to assess the carbon nanotubes’ sensitivity to organic substances [[60](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_060), [61](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_061)].

AI algorithms, especially ML models, depend on data. The more data they are given, the more accurate, predictive, and adaptive they become. AI evolved as a tool for large data interpretation, analysis, and application in the drug development industry. Furthermore, big data offers a platform for comprehensive patient analysis. The increasing acceptance of personalized medicine underscores the importance of comprehending the impact of an individual’s genetic composition on their response to medication. The customization of drugs to match individual patient profiles, which improves treatment efficacy and reduces adverse effects, was made feasible by AI-driven analysis of large genetic data [[62](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_062), [63](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_063), [64](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_064)].

Big data produced during the drug discovery process and made available through open databases may prove to be very beneficial in lowering drug attrition throughout the pipeline. To determine the preferred order of ranking for drug candidates with specified medicinal actions, conventional machine learning techniques like QSAR modeling can be employed. The technique may be employed as well to weed out substances that are inappropriate or have known side effects. Advances in AI have enabled CADD research to leverage the robust architecture of big data studies, thereby taking the field to new heights. These advancements include the creation of several deep learning algorithms and notable boosts in processing power [[65](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_065), [66](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_066)].

An important connection between chemical frameworks and their characteristics and activities can still be made with the help of the effective method of QSAR. When QSAR modeling was first used in drug development, it was based on modest data sets and straightforward linear regression techniques. ML is used in drug research and discovery for several reasons other than QSAR. For example, the numerical outline for chemical pattern-matching investigations was used for de novo drug creation using generative models [[67](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_067), [68](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_068), [69](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_069), [70](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_070), [71](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_071)]. These propagative models can produce synthetic molecules with the right characteristics and functionality. As a result, by using ML modeling for a range of data types, including public databases, CADD may produce substantial value in the big data age [[72](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_072), [73](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_073)].

## 1.4 Role of Stakeholders

Stakeholder analysis, which identifies and ranks stakeholders according to the information obtained, is an essential step in the design of health innovations. Because stakeholder selection criteria in participatory research are sometimes ambiguous, stakeholder results must be taken into account while developing participatory planning procedures. Stakeholder analysis aids in understanding the setting in which new technologies are developed and put into use; it directs the planning process regarding individuals, organizations, or groups involved; and it produces plans that facilitate innovation in development and use while avoiding roadblocks that might impede system integration. Stakeholders are appropriately involved in health innovation planning through the process’s fairness and openness, which is ensured by integrating stakeholder analysis into participatory planning procedures [[74](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_074), [75](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_075), [76](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_076), [77](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_077), [78](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_078), [79](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_ref_079)].

The marketing approach known as customer relationship management (CRM) places a strong emphasis on the product’s final user, even speculating that the user may be a pharmaceutical marketing patient. Nevertheless, salespeople in the industry are forming connections with doctors, stockists, and retailers rather than with patients. Physicians issue prescriptions for drugs manufactured by a particular business, but merchants and stockists are an essential part of the supply chain and may increase sales by telling local physicians about promotions or providing alternatives to certain prescriptions. Salespeople never have a conversation with their final customer, the patient, yet the patient still purchases and uses the product. Determining the true participants in the pharmaceutical industry is therefore essential. As [Fig. 1.2](#9783111573861_000727.xhtml#b_9783111574288-001_fig_002) illustrates, several stakeholders are involved in the formulation as well as execution of a pharmaceutical policy framework. Regulatory agencies, patients, physicians, chemists, insurance companies, academic institutions, professional organizations, non-governmental organizations, nonprofit organizations, and consumer groups with no active participation belong to these stakeholders in addition to state and federal governments (all of which include all ministries like health, trade, commerce, and industry). National and multinational manufacturers of medicines are among them. Governments encompass state authorities, the federal government, and any associated ministries responsible for the manufacturing, importation, exportation, investment, licensing, pricing, research and development, and quality control of pharmaceuticals.
