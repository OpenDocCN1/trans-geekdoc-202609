
# Chapter 3 Partnership and collaboration in the pharmaceutical industry

## Abstract

A significant contributor to the creation of treatments and vaccines is the pharmaceutical industry. To develop vaccines or treatments (like COVID-19), relationships and open, cross-disciplinary collaboration among scientific and research communities, startups, and pharmaceutical firms have been critical success factors. The chapter looks at the evolving models of open innovation (OI) that pharmaceutical companies created, funded, and supported to interact with startups to accomplish sustainable corporate growth. The foundation of the open innovation model is the idea of an alliance between an established business and a startup. The public-private partnership (PPP) system is a startup-company asset-sharing arrangement that can be used to foster innovation in a variety of drug discovery and development contexts. The chapter primarily focuses on the various types of collaborations and their significance in the pharmaceutical industry. The conclusions of the chapter show that the pharmaceutical industry needs to work with startups, producers, and businesses from a variety of fields to collaborate and accelerate innovations when new pharmacological mechanisms, enabling techniques, and translational models are discovered.

**Keywords:** partnership, collaborations, pharmaceutical industries, public-private partnership, start-ups, challenges

## 3.1 Introduction

It is unambiguous that the pharmaceutical industry needs to supplement its traditional approach to innovation with a quick approach that focuses on getting acquainted with startups and innovators quickly and repurposing concepts, expertise, and technologies that are easily accessible. The key success factors in developing vaccines and discovering treatments (such as COVID-19) have been the relationships and open, cross-disciplinary collaboration among the scientific and research community, startups, and pharmaceutical companies. The role of corporate venture funds, partnership initiatives, and internal and external systems for sourcing and scouting was never as crucial in generating ideas that pharmaceutical companies could adopt or in coming up with novel approaches to employing already-existing facilities, services, and products as coronavirus crisis solutions. Large companies’ main strategic reasons to participate in open innovation are to forge new alliances, investigate emerging technologies, and spot new business prospects [1](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_001), [2](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_002). Academic, commercial, and nonprofit partnerships can give projects with low financial returns enough momentum to advance; this is especially true for the drug discovery industry. Public-private partnerships (PPPs) offer strategic guidance; industry groups are dominated by strongly product-focused processes and wide expertise; academics typically collaborate on high-risk projects alongside little financial return; and each partner brings a unique set of skills and technical know-how to the table that benefits the project as a whole [3](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_003). The need to sustain and grow shareholder value and successful manufacturing levels drives the pharmaceutical industry continuously. When it comes to advancing candidates through the development pipeline, small and medium-sized pharmaceutical and biotechnology businesses with stronger ties between research and development have outperformed their larger sisters. To address unmet market needs, they have become more adept at creating biological products, including monoclonal antibodies, vaccines, and peptides [4](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_004), [5](#9783111573861_003373.xhtml#b_9783111574288-003_ref_005).
