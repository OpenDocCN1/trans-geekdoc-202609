
Two novel procedures for pharmaceutical approval were instituted with the formation of the EMEA. Biotechnology and other high-tech items are required to undergo a centralized approval process, while other products are exempt from the following requirements. A single national entity is responsible for conducting the scientific evaluation, and the Committee on Proprietary Medicinal Products is responsible for overseeing the assessment process. At the national level, a secondary, decentralized procedure is implemented. Applications are submitted to the “Reference Member State,” which is the market in which the business intends to introduce its product [19](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_019), [20](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_020), [21](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_021). The agency thereafter assists other “Concerned Member States” in acknowledging the marketing authorization. The current national process is still in place for applicants who are only interested in one country. The new regulations have not altered the fundamental components of these procedures, but they have brought about certain modifications. Furthermore, the EMEA has a history of receiving criticism for its lack of transparency [22](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_022), [23](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_023), [24](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_024). To address this, multiple provisions are introduced in the new rules. National authorities are now mandated to disseminate meeting minutes and details in conjunction with their procedural regulations. Upon approval, the authorities will promptly issue the marketing authorization, assessment report, justification for the opinion, and Summary of Product Characteristics. Historically, national agencies were not mandated to reveal the results of their evaluations. The new rule pertains to European Public Assessment Reports (EPARs), which provide appraisals of new applications sanctioned by the EMEA. While not to the extent desired by many critics, they are now required to compose their information “in a manner that is intelligible to the public” and provide a clear delineation of the terms and conditions associated with the product’s use [25](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_025), [26](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_026).

### 10.2.3 Other regulatory bodies

The National Regulatory Authority of India is known as the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO). The Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), a division of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, operates under the Government of India. The Drugs and Cosmetics Act appoints the CDSCO as the principal regulatory authority for pharmaceuticals, tasked with executing the responsibilities assigned to the national government. The headquarters are located in New Delhi [27](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_027), [28](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_028). The authority to issue licenses for intravenous fluids, vaccinations, sera, and blood and blood products is shared between the CDSCO and state regulatory bodies. The Drug Controller General of India (DCGI) regulates medical devices and pharmaceutical items under the CDSCO. The DCGI solicits counsel from the Drug Consultative Committee (DCC) and the Drug Technical Advisory Board (DTAB). The Central Licensing Approval Authority (CLAA) oversees the categorization and licensing of medical equipment. It is responsible for formulating and enforcing safety standards, conducting post-market surveillance, alerting consumers, and recalling pharmaceutical products in instances of adverse effects [29](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_029). The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) is the Australian regulatory authority responsible for overseeing all operations associated with therapeutic products, including product development, production, and distribution. The TGA regulates prescription medications, over-the-counter drugs, complementary and alternative therapies, sunscreens, medical equipment, biological products, blood and its components, and other therapeutic items [30](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_030), [31](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_031), [32](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_032), [33](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_033), [34](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_034).

The UK Department of Health and Social Care’s Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) is another regulatory body that likely has a significant global impact. This might be due to a few major pharmaceutical companies that distribute their products worldwide and have their headquarters and manufacturing facilities in the UK, such as GlaxoSmithKline and AstraZeneca. Both regulatory bodies may have regulations regarding Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) that the market will implement [35](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_035), [36](#9783111573861_014526.xhtml#b_9783111574288-010_ref_036).
