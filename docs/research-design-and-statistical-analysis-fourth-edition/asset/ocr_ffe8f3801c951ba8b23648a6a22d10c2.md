# Box 8.1 Parameter Definitions and Assumptions

1. The parent population mean, $\mu$. This is the grand mean of the treatment populations selected for this study and a constant component of all scores in the $a$ populations. It is the average of the treatment population means:

```
$\mu = \sum_{j=1}^{a} \mu_j / a$
```

2. The effect of treatment $A_j$, $\alpha_j$. This equals $\mu-\mu$ and is a constant component of all scores obtained under $A_j$, but may vary over treatments (levels of $j$).

+   2.1 Because the deviation of all scores about their mean is zero, $\Sigma_j \alpha_j = 0$.
2.2 If the null hypothesis is true, all $\alpha_j = 0$.
2.3 The population variance of the treatment effects is $\sigma^2_A = \sum_{j=1}^{a} \varepsilon_{ij}^2 / a$.

3. The error, $\varepsilon_{ij}$. This is the deviation of the $i$th score in group $j$ from $\mu$ and reflects uncontrolled, or variance. It is the only source of variation within the $j$th group, and if the null hypothesis is true, the only source of variation within the entire data set are:

+   3.1 The $\varepsilon_{ij}$ are independently distributed; i.e., the probability of value of $\varepsilon_{ij}$ does not depend on other values of $\varepsilon_{ij}$ in the sample.
3.2 The $\varepsilon_{ij}$ are normally distributed in each of the $a$ treatment populations. Also, $\varepsilon_{ij} = Y_{ij} - \mu$ the mean of population errors is zero; i.e., $E(\varepsilon_{ij}) = 0$.
3.3 The distribution of the error has variance $\sigma^2_{\varepsilon}$ (error variance) in each of the $a$ treatment populations; i.e., $\sigma^2_{\varepsilon1} = \dots = \sigma^2 = \dots = \sigma^2_{\varepsilon a}$. This is the assumption of homogeneity of variance. The error variance is the average squared error; $\sigma^2_{\varepsilon} = E(\varepsilon_{ij}^2)$.

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_i = \dots = \mu_a = \mu$

or, equivalently,

$H_0: \alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \dots = \alpha_j = \dots\alpha_a = 0$

To understand the variance of the F test, we need to consider the relationship of the mean squares to the population variances. This requires us to consider the expected values of our two mean squares.

Suppose we draw $a$ samples of $n$ scores from their respective treatment populations, and calculate $MS_A$ and $MS_{Error}$. These MS values are sample statistics that would vary across replications of this experiment; this implies that each have a sampling distribution. The means of these two sampling distributions, one for $MS_A$ and one for $MS_{Error}$, are the expected values of the mean squares, or the expected mean squares (EMS).
