A simple experiment is a well-defined process that leads to a single outcome (Hays, 1981). All probabilities are defined with respect to an experiment. Randomly drawing a slip of paper from the box and observing the grade and major on the slip is an example of a simple experiment. Another example is recording whether or not Rachel's 20 responses is correct or wrong.

The possible outcomes of a simple experiment are called *elementary events*, and the complete set of elementary events are called the *sample space* for the experiment. In the statistics class example, the sample space consists of the 100 combinations of major and grade across all the students in the class. In the ESP, the sample space consists of every possible sequence of correct and incorrect guesses across trials.

# ### 3.2.2 Combining Elementary Events into Event Classes

We are not interested in the probability of a particular elementary event as we are in the probability of meaningful collections of events that are called *event classes* or *simply*, events. For example, a student in the statistics class might be interested in the probability of getting a grade of A or B. In the ESP experiment, we would likely be interested in a summary of performance (e.g., proportion of correct responses) across all the trials in the experiment.

Events can be combined in important ways. Events may be “joined” such that an elementary event may be simultaneously classified into two (or more) event classes. In our statistics class example, a person may be a linguistics major and have earned an A in the class. In our ESP experiment, Rachel might pick the correct card on the first and third... and twelfth trial. Thus, we can talk about *joint events* that are comprised of the intersections of two or more events. We will use the conjunction “and” to refer to the joining of two or more events, but we will understand the term to refer to the logical operation of intersection.

Events may *unite* to *form compound events*. In contrast to joint events such as “Linguistics and A,” the *union* of two events occurs if an elementary event may be classified as *either* or *or* of the events. In our statistics class example, the union of linguistics and A if we observe “Linguistics” or “A” or (i.e., a linguistics major who earned a grade of A). In a simplified version of the ESP example, the outcome of at least one correct guess in *two* trials is comprised of the events: “correct on the first trial only” or “correct on the second trial” or “correct on three trials” (i.e., correct on trial 1 and on trial 2). We will use the disjunction “or” to refer to the union of two or more events but understand the term to refer to the logical operation of union (i.e., either or both).
