
## 7.3 Method

### 7.3.1 Participants

The pharmaceutical researcher posted a request for volunteers on the bulletin board of a local center. Volunteers were assessed in individual sessions to ensure that they were not suffering from any form of dementia. The assessment included a brief test of memory, and questions about facts that were judged to be common knowledge (e.g., the name of the president). From the pool of potential volunteers, 50 were chosen, ranging in age from 65 to 75.

An important point is that volunteers may differ from people who choose not to. For example, self-selected volunteers may be younger, healthier, or simply have more available in their schedules. They may be better educated or concerned about memory. We must remember these biases because they limit the population to which our results generalize

### 7.3.2 Experimental Design

As discussed in Chapter 1, there are several design to consider. We could employ a within-subjects design, testing the participants prior to taking the pill and then again later after taking the pill. The researcher decided against it, feeling that prior exposure to the situation might raise scores in the subsequent test. Another possible problem was that age-related memory decline might accompany the period between test, potentially masking any benefit of the pill. Finally, just knowing they were given a pill might have motivated participants, producing unwanted reluctance to the pill itself. The simplest procedure was to use an independent-groups design, randomly assigning the volunteers to either an experimental condition or control (i.e., placebo) condition. The experimenters opted for a relatively simple design for their pilot study

### 7.3.3 Procedure

Each participant was given a bottle containing 28 pills. Half of the bottles contained the placebo and half the experimental pills. Each participant was assigned a different numbered bottle and only the researcher knew the relationship between the bottle and the experimental conditions. Participants were instructed to take one pill each day and return to the laboratory on day 29. They were told that no side effects were expected but, as a precaution, they should note any departures from their physical or emotional state.

On day 29, participants were seated, and the research assistant presented a list of words on a projection screen, one at a time. These 30 words were common English nouns of five to seven letters in length. Immediately after they were read, the participants independently wrote down many of the words as they could recall, in any order.

## 7.4 Exploring the Data

The score for each participant was the number of words correctly recalled out of 30. Minor spelling errors were ignored when the participant’s intended word was clear. For the present, we used only the column of scores labeled Y in the AI_memory file. We begin our analysis by obtaining descriptive statistics and data points that are illustrated in the summarise function in the (dplyr) package of R, details of which are available at the book's website.

Although we used R for this purpose, most statistical packages will provide similar output. The means are similar in each condition, suggesting that the distributions are symmetric. The drug resulted in numerically better recall scores than the placebo taken by participants in the control group. The difference of about 1.5 words is small and is not of practical or statistical significance. We will shortly calculate a standardized effect measure, Cohen's d, to obtain a better sense of the effect of the memory pill. Confidence intervals and significance tests will address the issue of statistical significance. First, however, we consider several of the other statistics in Table 7.1, and some data plots as well.

Before conducting any inferential tests, we should evaluate whether violations of test assumptions are a concern. We begin with an assessment of normality of the data, including skewness and kurtosis. Recall that the standard errors of skew and kurtosis are a function of sample size and can be calculated easily (Chapter 2). The SE is 0.46 in each group, which is roughly the magnitude of skewness in the data. In the control condition, the ratio of the skew statistic to its standard error is over 2, which suggests some skewness in that data. There is stronger evidence of kurtosis in the data of groups. The ratios of the transformed statistics to their standard errors (0.902) are greater than 2.5.
