# 6.3.1 The t Statistic

In Chapter 5, we considered statistics that had the general form $z = \{(V - E(V))/\sigma$, where V represents some statistic such as the sample mean, $ar{X}$, or the difference between sample means, $\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2$, E(V) is the expected value of V, also referred to as the population parameter. For example, if $\bar{X}$ is the sample mean, $E(V)$ is the population mean, $\mu$. The denominator of $z$ is the standard error of sampling V. In this chapter we consider a similar ratio, the $t$ statistic; it has the general definition

```
$t = \frac{V - E(V)}{s_V} \quad (6.1)$
```

Where V and $E(V)$ are defined as before. The statistic $s$, is the sample statistic that estimates $\sigma_s$, the SE of the sampling of V. Note that the $t$ statistic is identical to the $z$ statistic except that the denominator of Equation 6.1 is an estimate of the standard error of V rather than its actual value. An implication of this difference is that the denominator of the $z$ statistic is a constant over replications of the experiments whereas the denominator of the $t$ statistic will vary over samples. In the case of $z$, it is assumed that scores are independently and normally distributed.

The $t$ distribution is tabled in Appendix C.3. When we turn to that table, we find that the entries in the first column correspond to something labeled $df$, which stands for degrees of freedom. The concept of degrees of freedom is closely related to sample size but is not quite the same. Because degrees of freedom are an important concept, not only for the $t$ distribution but also for other distributions that play a role in data analysis, they deserve further discussion.

# 6.3.2 Degrees of freedom (df)

The degrees of freedom associated with any quantity is the number of independent, free to vary, observations upon which that quantity is mean. The meaning of “independent observations” is best illustrated by an example. Suppose we have 10 markers of different colors to pass out to a group of children. The first nine children would have choice in colors, but the last could not (i.e., no df remaining). Translating to a numerical example, suppose that we are asked to choose 10 numbers that sum to 50. We can freely choose any values, but the tenth number must be 50 the mean of the first nine. In this case, there are 10 scores but because only one can independently vary, there are only 9 $df$. The constraint on the values that they must sum to 50 costs as a “degree of freedom."

The same kind of constraint occurs when we calculate a standard deviation. Doing requires us to subtract the mean from each score. Recall from Chapter 2 that the sum of the deviations about their mean is always zero; that is, $\sum(Y-\bar{Y})=0$. Rewriting, we see that $\sum Y=nY$. If the mean is 5 and $n$ is 10, then the sum of the 10 scores must equal 50. Therefore, the sample standard deviation is based on $n-1$, or $9, $df$.
