The distributions at the top of the Figure 5.4 are helpful for understanding the power calculations. The left-hand distribution is the sampling distribution assuming the null hypothesis is true. It is centered on 0 because $H_{0:change} = 0$, and the standard deviation is 1 because we are working with z-scores, raw data. The distribution on the right is the sampling distribution assuming the specific alternative, $H_A = 1.429$, true. It has a mean of 1.429 and a standard deviation of 1. The rejection region is also shown. It is the shaded area labeled $\alpha$ that is under the distribution and the critical value of $z=1.645$, which is marked with a vertical line. Finally, the unshaded area under the alternative distribution above $z=1.645$. By eye, we can estimate that the power is a bit less than 0.5 because the critical value that defines the rejection region falls above the mean of the alternative distribution. This visual check is consistent with the rejected power of 0.41.

We can compute power using the distributional calculators described in Section 5.4.3. For example, using the `pnorm` function in $R$, `pnorm(1.645, mean=1.429, sd=1, lower=FALSE)` returns 0.414, which is the same as the power obtained in G* and visualized in Figure 5.4. This command calculates the area above the critical value $z=1.645$ in a normal distribution with a mean of 1.429 and a standard deviation of 1, as shown in the right-distribution in Figure 5.4. The analogous calculation in SPSS is $1 - Cdf:Norm(1.645, 1.429, 1)$.

Whatever calculation method we choose, the result is the same: if the mean change in the population is 0.557, the planned experiment with $n=100$ has only a .41 probability of resulting in a rejection of the null hypothesis that there is no change in distribution over the two seasons. The low power serves to remind us of the critical effect that variability has on our inferences. Despite what many laboratory scientists would consider to be a large sample, power is what appears to be a reasonable alternative (based on an actual study) and, accordingly, the Type 2 error rate is very high. The situation is considerably–though hardly greatly–if we replicate the study using the same sample, $n=215$ against the specific alternative ($\mu=5.57$) of $n$ of 215 instead is approximately .67. The variability in Beck Depression makes it difficult to achieve high power to test hypotheses, even with relatively large samples.

The preceding details detail the calculation of power using the z-statistic and the normal distribution. However, it is important to understand those aspects of our procedure that are general to calculating the power of all statistical tests. The point is that we do not need to know how to calculate power; computer software will usually do this for us. The point is that we should understand exactly what is meant by the power of an experiment, and the factors that affect it.

As we have illustrated, all the relevant test statistic and distribution vary across different situations, the steps involved in power calculations remain the same. Thus, the steps that we followed in computing power for a test based on the normal distribution were the same steps that we followed in computing power for a test based on the binomial distribution (see Box 4.3). The factors affecting power are the same regardless of the test.

# 5.6.2 Factors Affecting Power

In general, the power of a test depends upon several factors, all of which have effects unlike those noted in the discussion of power in Chapter 4.

- 1. Power increases as $\alpha$ increases because the increase in $\alpha$ requires an increase in a range of values included in the rejection region. If $\alpha=0.10$, the critical value in Figure 5.4 shifts from 1.645 to 1.28, increasing the rejection region, and consequently the area above.

- 2. Power is affected by the nature of $H_0$ and $H_1$. If the statement of $H_1$ is two-tailed, $\alpha = 0.05$, the decision is to reject $H_0$ if $z < -1.96$ or $z > 1.96$. Then there would be two critical values in Figure 5.4, -1.96 and 1.96, and power would correspond to the sum of the areas to the right of 1.96 and to the left of -1.96 under the $H_a$ distribution. We can see in the $H_a$ distribution, the probability of $z < -1.96$ if $H_a$ is essentially zero, and the probability that $z > 1.96$ is less than the probability that $z > 1.645$. Therefore, the one-tailed test is more powerful against the specific alternative, $\mu_a = .357$. On the other hand, this one-tailed test has virtually no power against specific alternatives of the form $\mu < \mu_{prev}$, whereas the two-tailed test has the same probability of rejecting $H_0$ against these alternatives as those of the $\mu > \mu_{prev}$.

- 3. Power is affected by the population variance and sample size. Increased $\sigma$ and increased $n$ result in smaller errors. As the $SE$ decreases, the sample mean is more likely to be close to the true parameter value. As we noted considering confidence intervals, a smaller $SE$ increases the probability of getting values of $\hat{Y}$ close to the true population mean. Accordingly, as $\sigma$ decreases or $n$ increases, we are more likely to reject the mean under the null hypothesis, if that hypothesis is wrong.

Most hypotheses are false. We should always consider whether $n$ is so large that the detected effect is of little practical or theoretical importance. This is one reason why confidence intervals are an important part of our analyses. Very large sample sizes sometimes result in rejection of a null hypothesis even if the effect is trivial, by providing a bounded estimate of the effect, enables us to assess its importance.
