# 7.9 Summary

All too often, researchers are concerned only with whether they have a significant effect. The current example illustrates the importance of considering both descriptive and inferential statistics. The exploratory stage of our analysis proved several pieces of important information:

+   - The memory pill had a numerical advantage in the sample. Using the sample means and variances, we estimated that the population effect was medium in magnitude by Cohen's (1988) guidelines.
- Comparison of group variances, and significance tests comparing these variances, indicated that the assumption of homogeneity of variance under infrequent analyses was not violated.
- A careful examination of the exploratory output indicated that the data departed significantly from the assumption of normal distribution under the test and confidence interval based on the t distribution. Box plots of the data revealed the presence of outliers that could contribute to an increase in variance and a resulting loss of power.

A t test on the full data failed to reject the null hypothesis of no difference, and the confidence interval for the difference between the population means was wide. However, the results of the exploratory stage suggested that tests and confidence intervals based on ranked or trimmed data might provide stronger support for the memory pill. While the rank-based analyses were only marginally significant, a reanalysis using the trimmed t statistic resulted in a highly significant effect and a narrow confidence interval.

These are just some of the potential benefits of examining before carrying out statistics. We urge exploration of any data set, whether intended as a pilot study or a more extensive study, and whether experimental or observational. As stated in Chapter 2 and in the overview to this chapter, such exploration may reveal patterns in the data that support our hypotheses or suggest others we have not considered; they may also reveal departures from assumptions underlying our inferential analyses; and they may reveal outliers, scores that depart markedly from the distribution of our data.

Our analysis also went further in other respects than is the case. We calculated confidence intervals on both the raw and standardized effect sizes. These emphasized our variable data and how wide the possible range of effect might be. The standardized effect size also helped us to decide on the effect size to input into a priori analysis to determine the sample size for a larger, more decisive study of the effects of the drug we studying.
