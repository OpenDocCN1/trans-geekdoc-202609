On the other hand, the one-tailed test has virtually no power to reject $H_0$ against these alternatives, whereas the two-tailed test has more power than a two-tailed test if—and only if—the direction of the hypothesis is correct.

3. Power increases as a increases. This is because increase in $\alpha$ means the size of the rejection region becomes larger. For example, when $n=20$ and the alternative is one-tailed, the rejection region enters from $Y \le 6$ to $Y \le 7$ as $\alpha$ increases from $.06$ to $.20$. Because power is also calculated for this larger set of $Y$ values, the relationship between power and $\alpha$ is evident in both panels of Figure 4.5: The power curves for $\alpha = .20$ above those for $\alpha = .06$.

4. Power increases as n increases. Comparing the two curves for $\alpha = .06$ (or for $\alpha = .20$) in either panel, we can see that $n=20$ rise more quickly than the $n=15$. This demonstrates the effect of sample size on power and it follows from our discussion of the fact that $Y/n$ is a consistent estimator of $\pi$. Recall that the property of consistency (Section 4.3.3 and Figure 4.2) means that as $n$ increases, $Y/n$ is more likely to be close to the true value of the parameter. Therefore, if $H_0$ is false, it is more likely that the data will demonstrate that as $n$ increases.

# 4.5.4 The Importance of A Priori Power Calculations

Power is important for researchers to understand and calculate *a priori* (in advance of collecting the data). This allows an informed choice among possible statistical tests of the hypothesis and of various experimental designs: Also being equal, the design and the test that can afford the greatest power for a particular sample size. Additionally, a researcher who calculates *a priori* power can assess the consequence of potential violations of the assumptions of the statistical test. Finally, and most importantly, the relationships between power, effect size, and sample size can be used to decide how much data should be collected. For example, suppose we want power of $.80 to reject $H_0$ with a one-tailed test if $\pi$ is at $.6$. We can define power functions for various values of $n$ like those depicted in Figure 4.5. The n we want for our study is that one rises to a power in which (the y-axis value) is at .80 when the specific alternative hypothesis value ($\pi$) is .6.

The last point provides further comment. If we collect enough data, we are likely to obtain statistically significant results. Whether these results will be of practical importance or theoretical significance is another matter. The effect may be trivially small or in a direction that makes no sense in terms of any theory or practical concern. Therefore, it makes sense before we collect data to ask the following: What is the smallest effect size that would be of interest and what power we want to do (i.e., find significance for) such an effect? The answer to these two questions will be major factors in determining the sample size for our research. Sometimes the required $n$ will be practically large and we will face the challenge of redesigning the research so that a smaller sample will achieve the desired power against the specific alternative hypothesis we had in mind.
