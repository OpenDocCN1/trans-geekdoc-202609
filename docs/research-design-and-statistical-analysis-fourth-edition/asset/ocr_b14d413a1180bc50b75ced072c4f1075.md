# 25.6 A Hypothetical Experimental Test of the Effects of Leisure Activity on Depression

To provide a test of the hypothesis that amount of leisure physical activity affects depression, consider a hypothetical experiment in adult men and women perform different amounts of exercise each day. The experiment is a 2 x 2 (Gender identity x Activity Level)-between-participants design. Random assignment to activity condition is used to control for other factors evaluated in the preceding study (e.g., household activity, occupational activity, BMI). It is also decided to collect information on participants' perceived health and education levels both variables were to correlate with depression scores in the Seasons study (see Tables 25.4 and 25.5). Scores on these two variables will be used as covariates to improve the power of the statistical test of the effect of exercise on depression scores.

To decide on the number of participants to include in the study, a priori power analyses were conducted (see Section 24.6). We will again conduct separate analyses on the data for the men, and the relevant design has one factor (activity, two levels) with two covariates (health and education). Convert Cohen's values for small, medium, and large effects of .1, .25, and .40 to the corresponding values for the ANCOVA, p² was estimated by regressing the BDI scores on the health and education scores in the Seasons data set; R² was .16 for men and .120 for women. The regression of the dependent variable on both covariates and using R² to estimate p² is an extension of the procedures presented in Section 24.7. To be conservative in estimating the required number of participants, the value of .120 was used. Using G*Power 3.1, the NS required for power = .80 are 702, 114, and 46, respectively. Because the planned procedure is expensive in both time and resources, it was decided that 114 men and 114 women would be recruited for the experiment.

Participants were solicited from the same lists of HMO units in the Seasons study. At the start of the experiment, all participants completed a questionnaire in which they reported how much education they had completed and their perceived level of health, as in the Seasons study. They then received instructions about the nature of the activity condition to which they were assigned. One condition involved engagement in regular, vigorous exercise; the other, an alternative activity with minimal physical exercise. To ensure compliance with condition assignment, every participant agreed to report to the campus gym at a scheduled time every weekday to engage in the appropriate amount of exercise. Participants in the experiment followed a regimen of the strenuous, one-hour workout on each weekday (rate of energy expenditure estimated at 6 METs). Participants assigned to the control condition participated in one of group discussion per day (estimated at 1 MET). All participants participated in the experiment for six months. At the end of the six-month period, all participants took the BDI. Note that the difference in the energy demands of the two activities is relatively large compared to the observed ability in exercise found for the correlational study. This permits a more definitive comparison of exercise levels than occurs naturally. This, in turn, will mean a more powerful test of the effects of exercise than was possible in the observational study.
