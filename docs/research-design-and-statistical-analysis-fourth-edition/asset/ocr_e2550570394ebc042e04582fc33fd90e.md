# 17.7 More About Interpreting the Pearson Coefficient

Now we have introduced linear regression and coefficients such as the standard error of the estimate, we are in a position to describe the interpretation of the correlation coefficient in more detail. It is important to understand the characteristics of correlation coefficients and the factors that influence them because correlations seem to be the statistics of choice in some research areas. Moreover, they often serve as the raw material for more complex procedures such as factor analysis.

# 17.7.1 Ten Things to Remember About Correlation

+   1. The Pearson correlation coefficient depends on the variabilities of X and Y. Because the correlation coefficient is a measure of the similarity of the standardized values of X and Y (see Figure 17.6), $s_x$ and $s_y$ affect $r$ because they influence the standardization of X and Y. From Equation 17.13, we have

```
$r = b_1 \frac{s_X}{s_Y}$
```

This means that for a given regression slope, different correlations will result from different deviations of X and Y. Even if for both the slope and standard error of estimate, $s_{y,x}$ and $s_{x,y}$ are constant, Equations 17.13, 17.17, and 17.18 can be used to show that different values of $r$ will result for different values of $s_x$ and $s_y$, for example,

+   - For a given $b_1$ and $s_{y,x}$ $r$ increases with $s_x$.
- For a given $s_{x,y}$ $r$ increases with $b_1$, $s_x$ and $s_y$.
- For a given $s_x$ and $s_{x,y}$ $r$ increases with $b_1$ and $s_y$.

Therefore, if we are concerned with Y changes with X, we should generally look at $b_1$, not $r$.

+   2. Because the correlation coefficient depends on $s_x$ and $s_y$, it is a sample-specific measure. Given a population in which X and Y are linearly related with a population correlation of $ho$, the value of $r$ obtained in a sample depends on how the sample is selected. If the sample is largely selected from only part of the distribution of X (say, the regions A, B, or C in Figure 17.9), $r$ will tend to be smaller than $ho$ because $s$ will tend to be smaller than $\sigma$, in the population. Note that this dependence on the variability of X in the sample does not hold for the unstandardized estimate of slope, $eta_1$.
