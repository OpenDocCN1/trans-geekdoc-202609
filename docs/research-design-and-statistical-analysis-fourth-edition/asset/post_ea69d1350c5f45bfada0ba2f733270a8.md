## 9.3 Two-Factor Designs: The Analysis of Variance

The analysis of the variability in the data for the two-factor design follows the same logic developed for the one-factor design presented in Chapter 8. The structural model (Equation 9.6) suggests a way to partition the deviation of a score from the grand mean, $\hat{Y}_{\cdot\cdot\}$, into several components. We derive these components in Section 9.3.1, and then develop formulas for the sum of squares based on them in Section 9.3.2. We construct mean squares and, subsequently, $F$ statistics to test null hypotheses about main and interaction effects.

### 9.3.1 Components of the Scores

In Section 9.2.1, we assumed the structural model

$Y_{ijk} = \mu + \alpha_j + \beta_k + (\alpha\beta)_{jk} + \varepsilon_{ijk}$

After subtracting $\mu$ from both sides and substituting treatment population means, we have

```
$Y_{ijk} - \mu = (\mu_j - \mu) + (\mu_k - \mu) + (\mu_{jk} - \mu_j - \mu) + \varepsilon_{ijk} \quad (9.7)$
```

Substituting the estimates of these parameters from Table 9.1, we have the basis for the ANOVA:

```
$$(Y_{ijk} - \tilde{Y}_{ij}) = (\tilde{Y}_j - \tilde{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}) + (\tilde{Y}_k - \tilde{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}) + (\tilde{Y}_{jk} - \tilde{Y}_j - \tilde{Y}_k + \tilde{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}) + (Y_{ijk} - \tilde{Y}_{ijk}) \quad (9.8)$$
```

In other words, an individual score may differ from the mean for four distinct reasons: (1) the effect of the level A to which they are exposed; (2) the effect of the level B to which they are exposed; (3) the effect of the combination of the levels of factors A and B; and (4) measurement error and other random noise. Put more simply,

```
score – grand mean = main effect of A+ main effect of B+ interaction effect
+ residual error
```

### 9.3.2 Sums of Squares

Equation 9.8 forms the basis for the sums of squares; these are important components in tests of null hypotheses. Squaring both sides of Equation 9.8 and summing yields the sums of squares (SS) formulas in panel a of Table 9.5. As a first step, we partition the total sum of squares into two components: between-cells sum of squares and a within-cell (SSAB) of squares. The between-cell variability is itself of interest as it has several sources. For example, in the Wiley-Voss data set, the eight cells may differ because they represent different formats, different instructions, or different combinations of formats. Although software packages do not include the between-participants variability, $SS_{cell}$ we include because it is involved in our conceptual and calculation of the $SS_{AB}$ and the $SS_{cellAB}$. The components of between-cell sum of squares in Table 9.5 correspond to the main and interaction sources of variance that we wish to.

The formulas presented in Table 9.5 define the sums of squares. Although statistical software usually will be available to perform the calculations, the formulas are presented to remind us that the sums of squares are indices of variability. For example, the $SS_{total}$ is $ab-1$ times the variance of all the scores, the $SS_A$ is $bn(a-1)$ times the variance of the A marg, and the $SS_{cell}$ is $n(ab-1)$ times the variance of the ab cell means. The tests of null hypotheses corresponding to the A, B, and AB sources of variance (SV) test whether those variances are greater than.

### 9.3.3 Degrees of Freedom

A formula for degrees of freedom is associated with each sources of variance. The $df_{total}$ are $ab-1$ because this SV represents the variability of all $ab$ scores about the grand mean. The $SS_{cell}$ is distributed on $ab-1$ because it introduces the variability of the ab cell means about the grand mean. The $df$ for the main effects has the same form as in Chapter 8; these SV reflect the variance of the $a$ (or $b$) means about the grand mean and one one is df. The selection of degrees of freedom is

```
$df_{AB} = (ab-1)-(a-1)-(b-1)=(a-1)(b-1)$
```

## 9.3.4 Mean Squares (MS), Expected Mean Squares (EMS), and F Ratios

The mean squares (MS) are obtained by dividing the sums of squares by their corresponding degrees of freedom. The expected mean squares (EMS) are the values of the mean squares under the null hypotheses. If the null hypothesis is true, the EMS for the numerator and denominator should be equal. The $F$ statistic is the ratio of the numerator mean square to the denominator mean square. If the null hypothesis is false, the $F$ statistic will be greater than 1.

The EMS are derived by assuming the structural model, independence of scores, and homogeneity of population variances. In practice, the ratio of follows that the numerator and denominator MS must have the same expectation. In addition, if the populations are normally distributed and the null hypothesis is true, the ratio of mean squares follows $F^4$.

We now have both a conceptual framework and formulas on which to base tests of hypotheses. We apply this framework to the analysis of the verification test (IVT) data in Table 9.6.
