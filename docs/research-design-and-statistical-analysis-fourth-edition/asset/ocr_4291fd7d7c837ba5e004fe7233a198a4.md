We have already witnessed an example demonstrating that different data sets, possibly arising from different populations, can give rise to similar regression statistics. In Chapter 17 we discussed the Anscombe data sets with identical bivariate regression statistics were obtained from very different data sets (see Figure 17.10). With many predictor variables, there is far more potential for different kinds of data structures to produce similar regression equations, and as for different combinations of predictors to account for similar amounts of variance.

Also, as we will discuss further in Chapter 22, we often work with relatively simple models that contain important variables as predictors. These models are extremely useful for focusing our thinking and developing further research, but they rarely include all the relevant predictor variables. As an example, the research question of how student performance is influenced by reductions in class size is largely inconclusive because many variables other than class size influence performance—examples of relevant variables characteristics of the student, background, and as well as teaching style and course content (see 22.7).

These extraneous variables may not present a problem if we can conduct a true experiment in which variables of interest are manipulated and use randomization and matching to control other variables. If so, a model such as that expressed in Equation 20.12 may be reasonable. The error component, $\varepsilon$, contains the effects of variables other than those are manipulated, but these other variables are not systematically related to the manipulated variables because of the controls present in an experiment.

However, suppose the data come from an observational study in which the variables are are related to other important variables. For example, smaller classes are more likely to occur in more affluent school districts that can afford to pay teachers better salaries and have parents with higher education levels themselves. Unless these other variables are included in the model, $\varepsilon$ is not really a random error component and may be correlated with the variables in the model. If important variables are omitted from the model, the $eta_s$ in Equation 20.11 will generally be biased estimators of the $eta$ in Equation 20.12.

When important variables are left out of the regression equation, the regression coefficients of variables in the equation may, in part, reflect the effects of these omitted variables. We consider this point further in the next section.
