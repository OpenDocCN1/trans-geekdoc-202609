# 17.6.3 Regression Toward the Mean in Predicting Y from X

We showed in 17.5.4 that whenever there is imperfect linear relationship between X and Y, the values of $z$, associated with any given value of $z$, will, on the average, be closer to zero than $z$ is. We can extend this result to row scores by rewriting Equation 17.10

```
$$\hat{Y} - \bar{Y} = r\frac{s_Y}{s_X}(X - \bar{X})$ (17.14)
```

Here we see that the prediction for Y, must be closer to $ar{Y}$ than to X, is to $ar{X}$ whenever $s_x = s_y$ and $r$ is less than 1.

Regression toward the mean must occur for predictions whenever two variables with equal variances are not perfectly linear. However, the finding that predicted scores are less extreme than those used to predict them often leading to faulty reasoning. Because people often ignore the fact that regression effects can be produced by random variability, measurement error, or any other factors that result in less-than-perfect correlation, they often felt compelled to provide more "interesting," elaborate, and often unwarranted explanations.

We must keep the possibility of regression toward the mean in mind when considering every everyday phenomenon. Suppose we are interested in assessing the effects of some kind of instruction. Say, a group of children make a multiple-test, and then are given a period of instruction, followed by a second. Usually, children who score the top of the distribution on the first test will show some decline in performance on a second test (just think what it takes to score at the top of the distribution), whereas children with extremely low scores on the first test will score relatively better on the second. This pattern seems to suggest that the intervening instruction is more effective for the students who scored lower on the first test than for those who scored higher. However, even if instruction was equally effective for all children, we would expect regression toward the mean if the two tests did not measure exactly the same thing or if there were random fluctuations in children's alertness and success in guessing. Therefore, if we want to assess the effects of instruction, we must include an appropriate control group and conduct an experiment.
