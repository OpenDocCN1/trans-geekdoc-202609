Beyond the reality of practical limitations, the purpose of the research should be an important factor in the sampling of participants. If the investigator wants to investigate the cognitive abilities of older adults, then that is the population to be represented in the study. This seems obvious, but matters are more complicated. For example, what range of older ages will be included in the study? The actual sample of participants may be a function of several considerations beyond the very general goal.

Theoretical considerations may influence the choice of participants. Suppose the investigator is studying cognitive functioning of older adults hypothesizes that speed and accuracy in memory and performance are a direct function of the degree to which people engage in intellectual pursuits. This depends on the definition of intellectual activities, and the researcher might administer a questionnaire to ensure that he or she had a range of individuals with respect to whether they participated in activities such as puzzles, reading and discussing these, learning new things as a foreign language.

The participants' previous histories were not an obvious constraint on the sample. If the investigator wants older individuals with no obvious cognitive impairments, individuals who may be suffering from such impairments should be excluded from the study. If part of the research goal is to generalize to a population of older individuals representing a broad spectrum of experience, some attempt should be made to include people from various social and economic classes, ethnic backgrounds, and work experiences.

Although less obvious than the preceding concerns, the control of error variance is an issue in selecting research participants. The investigator may want to include a wide range of attributes in the study so that there is a statistical basis for generalizing to a broad population. However, the more diversified the sample, the more variable the data will be, and therefore, the clearer will be the effects of independent variables on the behavior of interest. For example, if we want to evaluate some method of improving memory in a sample whose members are similar in attributes such as age, educational experience, and socioeconomic status than one with wide variation in these attributes.

The tradeoff in sampling from a more narrowly defined population is that, although the data are less variable, inferences about individuals outside the range are more specific. For example, we may assume that if a method of improving memory is less effective with older individuals than those in our sample, however that is a hypothesis that is only supported by our data. Ideally, investigators should think about the implications for the population to which they wish to generalize, consider to a decision about the attributes to be controlled in recruiting participants. We can compensate for a heterogeneous sample by having a larger sample (see Section 1.6), by the choice of the research design (Chapter 12), and by statistical means (Chapters 12 and 24).
