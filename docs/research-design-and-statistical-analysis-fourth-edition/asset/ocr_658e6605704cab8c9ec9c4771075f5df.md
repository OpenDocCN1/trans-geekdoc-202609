# 4.5.2 Computing Power

Power is a conditional probability: It is the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis that the null hypothesis is Wrong. Then, the first step in computing power is to determine the conditions under which the null hypothesis will be rejected (see Box 4.2). Once the rejection region for $H_0$ is found, we must compute the probability of observing a result in the rejection region assuming $H_a$ and that some alternative hypothesis, $H_A$, is correct. It is important to be clear about the distinction between $H_a$ and $H_A$, the class of alternative hypotheses determines where the rejection region is placed (right or left tail, or in tails). In contrast, $H_a$ is a specific alternative hypothesis; power is calculated for the test of $H_0$ assuming $H_A$ to be true. Course, the true value of the population parameter is never known. However, if some value of the parameter is assumed, the power of the test against alternative can be calculated.

Let's illustrate the calculation of power for our ESP experiment. Suppose we wish to test $H_0: \pi = .25$ against the alternative hypothesis $H_a: \pi > .25$. Further, let's assume that Rachel has rather weak ESP abilities; specifically, we will assume that her abilities permit her to correctly identify the identity of a card 35% of the time. In this example, this is our specific alternative hypothesis,

```
$H_a: \pi = .35$
```

The $H_0$ and $H_A$ specify different sampling distributions for our experiment, as illustrated in Figure 4.4. Our example assumes that $H_1$ correctly describes the probabilities of different outcomes for the experiment, the extensive overlap between the two distributions means that the two hypotheses will not be able to discriminate. Thus, we can expect that power will be low.

The general steps in computing power are all the statistical tests. These steps are summarized in Box 4.3. Applying this to our example:

+   1. Calculate the theoretical distribution of Y assuming $H_0$ to be true. In this example, the distribution is presented in the .25 column of Table 4.2.
2. Determine the rejection region. In this example ($n = 20, \alpha = .05; H_a: \pi > .25$), the rejection region is $Y \ge 9$. As we see in Figure 4.4, the outcomes are discrete, we choose the largest possible $\alpha$ that does not exceed 0.05; in this case, $\alpha = .0409$.
3. Calculate the probability distribution of Y assuming $H_a$ is true. In this example, $\pi = .35$ is replaced by presented in Table 4.2.
4. Sum the probabilities for $Y \ge 9$ (the rejection region) in the .35 column. This is the conditional probability $p(Y \ge 9 | H_A = .35)$ and is the power of the test of $H_0$ against $H_A$. In this case, power = .1158 + .0686 + .0336 + .0136 + .0045 + .0012 + .0003 = .2376.
