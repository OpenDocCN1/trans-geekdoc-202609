## 3.1 Overview

In Chapter 2, we discussed various descriptive statistics and their uses. However, because statistics vary across samples, our inferences may be incorrect. A sample is not a miniature version of the population, so the mean or variance may vary. How then can we justify statistics from a single sample? As we see, we use probability models, which describe the possible outcomes and their probabilities, to tell us how likely our observed statistic is.

Let's begin by considering an example. Imagine that we want to test extrasensory perception (ESP). We decide to conduct an experiment to test the ability of Rachel, who claims to have ESP. An experimenter is seated in a room with different cards. On each trial, the experimenter shuffles the cards, then randomly picks one, looks at it, and tries to transmit its contents mentally. Rachel sits in a room in different building, is familiar with the pack of cards, and knows when each trial of the experiment is to occur. For each trial, she tries to “perceive” and then record the card chosen by the experimenter.

How well Rachel perform before we decide that the evidence is sufficiently strong to support her claim? We first note that Rachel is guessing, the most likely outcome is that she will be correct on 25% of the trials. However, we recognize that Rachel may not better than 25% correct even if she guesses. Before we can conclude that Rachel is not simply guessing, convincing evidence... How more than 25% correct must Rachel do before we are convinced? Twenty correct responses (i.e., 100% correct) would be impressive support for Rachel's claim to have ESP because such a high level of performance is extremely unlikely if she was just guessing. But what about 19 correct in 20 trials? Or 12? Or 8? Those outcomes will seem very unlikely given guessing, but how much evidence against guessing do they provide?
