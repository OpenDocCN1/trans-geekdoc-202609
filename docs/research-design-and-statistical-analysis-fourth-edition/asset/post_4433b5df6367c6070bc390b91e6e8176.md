The results of the analysis of variance for the data are presented in Table 9.7. As the experimenters hypothesized, a significantly higher proportion of inferences were correctly verified by the participants in the web and text format condition. This means that the average of the four populations of IVT scores obtained under the web format differs from the average of the four populations of IVT scores obtained under the test format. In terms of Table 9.6, it is the marginal means, $\mu_{Web}$ (78.13) and $\mu_{Text}$ (71.56), that differ significantly. We can conclude that, averaging over levels of instruction, the effects of the two formats differ significantly. However, this does not provide information about the effects at any particular level of instruction.

The experimenters also interested in whether instructions would affect performance. They reported that the effect was "marginally significant" because the p-value was .07, short of the .05 level required for statistical significance. We will consider further the question of effect of instructions later in this chapter when we calculate values of effect size for the Wiley-Voss data.

The F test of the Format × Instructions tests the null hypothesis that the effects of one variable are the same under all levels of the other variable. One statement of the null hypothesis of no interaction is that the difference between the text and web population means is the same under all types of instructions. This may be represented as

> $H_0 : (\mu_{Text, N} - \mu_{Web, N}) = (\mu_{Text, S} - \mu_{Web, S}) = (\mu_{Text, E} - \mu_{Web, E}) = (\mu_{Text, A} - \mu_{Web, A})$

where, for example, $\mu_{Text,N}$ is the mean of scores obtained under the text format and the narrative instructions.

An equivalent of the null hypothesis of no interaction is that the effects of instructions are the same at the two format levels. We would state this null hypothesis as

> $H_0 : (\mu_{Text,N} - \mu_{Text,S}) = (\mu_{Web,N} - \mu_{Web,S})$

9.4 Three-Factor-Between-Participants Designs

9.4.1 The General Case

Extending the two-factor design to the three-factor design is straightforward; the only new concept is the three-factor interaction. Therefore, we will present the general case of the three-factor design concisely so that we reinforce the basic concepts already developed for the simpler one- and two-factor designs.

The general case of the three-factor between-participants design involves $a$ levels of $A$, $b$ levels of $B$, $c$ and $n$ scores in each of the $abc$ cells. The relevant indices are $i = 1, 2,...,n; j = 1, 2,...,a; k = 1, 2,...,b$; and $m = 1, 2,...,c$

The structural model looks like that for the two-factor experiment except that there are now three two-factor interactions (AB, AC, and BC) and there is the three-factor interaction:

$Y_{ijkm} = \mu + \alpha_j + \beta_k + \gamma_{im} + (\alpha\gamma)_{jm} + (\beta\gamma)_{km} + (\alpha\beta)_{jk} + \varepsilon_{ijkm}$ (9.9)
