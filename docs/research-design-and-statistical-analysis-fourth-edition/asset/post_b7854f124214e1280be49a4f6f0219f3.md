
## Research Design and Statistical Analysis

## 12.3 The Treatments × Blocks Design

### 12.3.1 The Design

Blocking refers to a design in which the participants are matched with respect to a measure that is correlated with the dependent variable. For example, in an experiment comparing three methods of teaching fractions to elementary students, we might take steps to match the distributions of arithmetic skills of the children assigned to the three methods. One way to this would be to administer a prestest to measure basic arithmetic skills of 36 children, and then divide the children into four blocks based on their prestest scores. The nine children scoring highest would then be randomly assigned to one of the three instructional methods, the same done with the next highest scoring students, and so on until we had four blocks based on prestest scores.

Although this is a common type of blocking design, blocking could be done on other variables that we assume be related to performance on the dependent variable. If two experimenters are involved in testing participants, and if there is reason to believe that responses may be influenced by the person administering the test, half of each experimental group could be tested by each experimenter. Similarly, if there is reason to believe that the time of day at testing takes place can affect performance, we can either test all participants at the time of day of, or if that is impractical, we can establish time of day as a blocking variable. In that case, if we had testing formats, 1/4 of each treatment group would be tested at each time.

Regardless of how it is accomplished, the primary purpose of blocking is to reduce error variance, increasing the power of the hypothesis test and decreasing the width of confidence intervals. We consider how this occurs.

### 12.3.2 Blocking vs Completely Random Assignment

Suppose two investigators independently run the experiment we described in which 36 students were divided into three treatment groups, each group taught by one of three instructional methods. Experimenter CR randomly assigns each of the 36 participants to one of the three groups; the only restriction is that there are 12 students taught by each method. Experimenter TB follows the blocking procedure described in the preceding section, using prestest scores to divide the students into four blocks with equal n and then randomly assigning each of the nine students within a block to one of the three instructional methods, with the constraint that there are three students in a block by each method. The first design is a one-factor completely randomized design, with the data analyzed as described in Chapter 8. The data from blocking design are analyzed as a two-factor-participants design, as in Chapter 9.

The blocking design should result in a smaller error variance than the one-factor CR design based on the same total number of participants. This statistical benefit occurs because the three participants within each treatment × block (A x B) cell will vary less in arithmetic knowledge than the 12 participants in a level of A in the CR design, so we can expect that their scores on the posttest will also be variable. For example, consider the following of 12 posttest scores, grouped into three levels of ability as measured on a prestest:

However, if the 12 scores were not grouped by pretext level, their variance would be 12.63 (i.e., $MS_{S(A)}$). Thus, the error variance will be smaller in a treatments × blocks design than in a completely randomized design, provided that the pretest scores are correlated with the posttest scores.

The results in the table were obtained by using a simulation to draw 5,000 samples of 36 pretext and posttest scores in a focus with which the correlation between the two measures was 0.4. In the CR design, the 36 posttest scores were then divided randomly among the three treatment levels; in the blocking design, the posttest scores were divided among four blocks in order of their pretext scores, and then randomly among the treatments within each block. To simulate treatment effects, a constant was added to all posttest scores at a given treatment with a different constant for each level. An ANOVA was then performed for the data in each design and the proportion of the 5,000 samples that resulted in rejection at the .05 level was considered. The results presented in Table 12.1 are averages over the 5,000 replications of the simulated experiment. There are several points to note about the results:

- 1. The average $SS_{total}$ is virtually identical for the two designs, the very small difference due to chance variability involving a finite number of samples.
- 2. The treatment variance, $SS_A$, is smaller in the blocking design than in the CR design. To see why, consider the expected mean square for treatments, we saw in Chapter 8, with 12 scores at each level $E(MS_A) = \sigma^2 + 12\sigma_A^2$; as the preceding numerical example demonstrated, we expected the error variance, $\sigma^2$, to be smaller in the T × B design than in the completely randomized design. Therefore, we expect $MS_A$ to be smaller in the blocking design.
- 3. The $T \times B$ design partitions both the $df$ and $SS$ for error in the CR design ($df_{S/A}$, $SS_{S/A}$) into variance portions for the $B$, $AB$, and $S/AB$ terms. The reduction is proportionally larger than the reduction in $df$, resulting in a smaller MS for error in the $T \times B$ design ($MS_{S/AB} < MS_{S/A}$).
- 4. Power of significant results is greater in the $T \times B$ ANOVA, despite the fact that the $MS_A$ and the error $df$ – affect that power – are smaller in that design.

Power is greater in the $T \times B$ design than the CR design because $MS_{S/AB}$ is smaller than $MS_{S/A}$. To understand why the two error terms differ in magnitude, consider the sources.

### 12.3.4 How Many Blocks?

If a blocking design is chosen, the researcher must decide how many blocks to use. As the number of blocks increases, the scores in the cells representing the combinations of treatments and blocks become less variable, yielding a smaller error term. However, the increase in blocks involves a loss of degrees of freedom.
