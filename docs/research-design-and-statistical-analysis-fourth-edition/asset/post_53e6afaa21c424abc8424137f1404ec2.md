## 14.5.2 Mixed Design With Two Random-Effects Factors

The $S \times A \times B$ example can undergo further complications. We might have several groups of participants, perhaps differing in reading ability or grade level. In general we consider of an experiment with a between-participants variance, $A$, having fixed effects (e.g., grade level); a within-participants variance, $B$, also having fixed effects (e.g., font); and a within-participants variance, $C$, having random effects (e.g., words). All combinations of the factors appear in the design. To summarize the design under: there are $a$ groups of $n$ participants and all participants are tested on the same $c$ items at each of $b$ levels of a fixed-effects factor, $B$. We presented this design in Table 14.9 under the assumption that all three factors $-A$, $B$, and $C$ had fixed effects. Table 14.11 reproduces much of Table 14.9; the difference is that $C$ is now assumed to have random effects, the EMS now include additional terms. Those added terms are underlined in Table 14.11.

## 14.6 Constructing Unbiased F Tests in Designs With Random Factors

Consider the $S \times A \times B$ design whose analysis is summarized in Table 14.10. The question of interest in this design is whether there is an effect of type A on participants' recognition of words. If we test $MS_A$ against $MS_{SA}$, we cannot know whether a significant result is $\theta^2_{SA} > 0$ (our null hypothesis is false) or $\sigma^2_{AB} > 0$. To signify a significant result to A, the error term needs to have an expectation of $\sigma^2_{SA} + ba\sigma^2_{AB} + n\sigma^2_{A}$. As seen in Table 14.10, there is no single source of variance that has this expectation.

We consider three possible responses to the problem of constructing an unbiased test of effects of interest in such designs. To anticipate, the approaches discussed in Sections 14.6.1 and 14.6.2 usually do not provide satisfactory solutions to the problem; however we consider them because they help clarify the issue.

### 14.6.1 Preliminary Tests of Interaction

We may be fortunate to find that an effect of interest does not vary in magnitude across participants (i.e., the additive model). For example, if the effect of type font is the same for all of the participants in our repeated-measures design, then the interaction, $\sigma^2_{SA}$, equals zero and may be dropped from all of the $EMS$ terms in Table 14.10. In that event, the EMS for A simplifies to:

$E(MS_A) = \sigma^2_{A} + \sigma^2_{SAB} + n\theta^2_{AB} + nb\theta^2_A$

and $MS_{AB}$ provides an appropriate error term to test because

$E(MS_{AB}) = \sigma^2_{A} + \sigma^2_{SAB} + n\theta^2_{AB}$

A similar situation results if the effect is over over stimuli. In that case, $\sigma_{AB}^2$ equals zero and $MS_{SA}$ then provides an inappropriate error term for the test of A. This suggests a preliminary test of the SA and AB terms against $MS_{SAB}$. For example, if the test of $MS_{SA}$ against $MS_{SAB}$ does not yield a significant result, we might conclude that $\sigma_{SA}^2$ is zero and test against $MS_{AB}$.

We stated at the outset this approach is not completely satisfactory. One limitation is that the SA and AB interactions will be significant; in those instances, the approach is not applicable. Less obviously, even when a nonsignificant interaction is observed, it is possible that the preliminary test will produce a Type 2 error; we wrongly conclude that $\sigma_{AB}^2$ equals zero. In that case, the Type 1 error rate in testing A AB will exceed the nominal level. In studies related to this issue, Type 1 errors have ranged from .07 to .11 when the nominal value is .05 (Janky, 2000), preliminary tests to justify F tests.

## 14.6.2 Separate Tests Against $MS_{SA}(F_A)$ and $MS_{AB}(F_B)$

A frequent approach to the problem of lacking a single mean square to use as an error term is to test two ratios: $F$ against $MS_{SA}$ and $MS_{AB}$ with being required on both to conclude that A has an effect. The presumed logic of this is that the test against $MS_{SA}$ allows generality to the participant population and the test item. It is true that our goal is to generalize results to both population of participants and the population from which we have sampled. Even if both F tests are significant, we cannot claim kind of generality. To see, suppose our null hypothesis is true (i.e., $\theta_{AB}=0$) but A interacts with S and B. Then the ratio of expected means when we test A against SA is

$\sigma_e^2 + \sigma_{AB}^2 + bo_{SA}^2 + \sigma_{AB}^2$

which is greater than one. Similarly, if we test A against AB the S x A variance will spuriously inflate the F ratio.

### 14.6.3 Quasi-F (F') Tests

We have established that a test of A in the S x A x B design in Table 14,10 requires that we identify an error term whose expectation is $\sigma_e^2 + bo_{SA}^2 + n\sigma_{SB}^2 + \sigma_{AB}^2$. Given the expected mean squares of Table 14,10, no single mean square has the appropriate expected value, but we can form a combination of mean squares with the appropriate expectation that meets criterion; thus we create a quasi-F ratio whose numerator and denominator are equal if the null hypothesis is true.
