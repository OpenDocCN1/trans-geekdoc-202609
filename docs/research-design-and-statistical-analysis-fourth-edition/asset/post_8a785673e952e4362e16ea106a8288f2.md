
## 22.6 Regression With Direct and Mediated Effects

It is possible for an independent variable to influence the dependent variable directly, or it can influence the dependent variable indirectly, through its effect on a mediating variable. Figure 22.3 provides an illustration of this distinction. In Panel (a), we have a model where X exerts a direct effect on Y. This causal influence is indicated by the arrow, and the direction of the arrow indicates the direction of causality. In Panel (b), we have a causal model that illustrates both direct and mediated effects. In this case, a change in X directly affects Y but also affects Y indirectly through the mediator variable, X₁. There are many examples of the kinds of variables influencing job perception, which in turn influence productivity; or changes in parents' attitudes towards education can change children's attributes, and in turn influence academic performance. Theories specifying processes that intervene between independent and dependent variables are pertinent in many fields of psychology. A Web of Science reveals more than 54,000 citations of the seminal Baron and Kenny (1986) paper that discussed testing for a mediating effect.

We must consider the possibility of mediation if we try to determine the importance of a variable. If we include the correct variables in the regression equation and there is a causal relationship between X and the outcome, the regression coefficient $b_y$ does not estimate the total influence of X on Y. Rather, the regression coefficient reflects the direct effect of X, on Y, that is, the rate of change of Y with X, holding all the other variables in the equation constant. If changes in X cause changes in some of other variables, in turn changing Y, we would have both direct and indirect effects. In assessing the influence of X, we would be concerned with both the direct and indirect effects. Given a causal structural model, structural equation modeling can be used to assess the total effect of changing a variable. However, as these estimates may be misleading if important variables are omitted from the model or if the model is otherwise invalid.

Again, consider the two causal models in Figure 22.3. In Panel (a), a change of one unit in X causes a change of c units in Y. In Panel (b), we have a causal model that illustrates both direct and mediated effects. In, changing X by one unit directly causes a change in Y of c' units and a change in X of a units. A change of one unit in X causes a change of b units in Y. Therefore, the indirect effect of X on Y through the mediator X₂ is ab, c + ab. Baron and Kenny (1986) have discussed four steps necessary to establish that a data set is consistent with the presence of mediation. These steps, translated into our terminology, are as follows:

- Step 1. Regress Y on X₁. If the coefficient $b_y$ is nonzero, this is consistent (but does not necessitate) X₁ having a causal effect on Y.
- Step 2. Regress X₁ on X. If the coefficient $b_2$ is nonzero, this is consistent with X having a causal effect on mediator X₂.
- Steps 3. Regress Y on both X₁ and X₂. The coefficient of X₁, $b_{y1}$, is the rate of change of predicted Y when X is held constant, and so, in terms of the model in Panel (b), $b_{y1}$ is an estimate of c', the causal path from X₁ to Y. A finding that $b_{y1}$ is nonzero but $b_{y2}$ is zero is consistent with a model in which the entire effect of X on Y is mediated by the intervening variable X₂. A finding that $b_{y2}$ is nonzero, but smaller than $b_{y1}$, is consistent with partial mediation by X₂.

### 22.6.1 Some Cautions

We emphasize that these interpretations are valid only in terms of the causal model given in Panel (b) of Figure 22.2. If our theory does not support this causal model, the best we can say is that our data are consistent with mediation but are also consistent with causal models as well, perhaps involving additional variables. Also we would like to have a statistical test for any mediation effect. One that is widely used is the Sobel test, but a limitation of the test is that it is not very powerful. MacKinnon, Lockwood, Hoffman, and West (2002) offer a useful discussion of methods for testing the statistical significance of a mediating effect. Finally, in any regression analysis, the results and interpretation may be distorted by measurement error. If, for example, the mediating variable $X_2$ is less than $X_1$, the direct effect will appear larger, and the mediating effect will appear smaller than it should. If we want to pursue serious testing of a theory that involves mediation, we should use multiple indicators of the latent variables and use structural equation modeling.

## 22.7 Testing for Curvilarity in Regression

### 22.7.1 Testing for Curvilarity using Continuous Predictor Variables

When we regressed TC on age and BMI (see Figure 22.1), we found that there were significant effects of both predictor variables. However it is possible that the relationship is not strictly linear and that the prediction of TC scores would be better if we included curvilinear components of the predictors as well. A curvilinear relationship would simply mean that the function relating the predictor and dependent measure is not linear over the entire range represented in our data. It might be that Y increases linearly up to some point, and then gradually levels off or even decreases. Or, the predicted values of Y may be piecewise linear, that is, linear over a range of predictor values but with different slopes at different points. If there are only two different slopes, then we may be able to describe the relationship with a quadratic ($X^2$) component; if there are three distinct slopes (e.g., rising, falling, rising, or the reverse), then a cubic component ($X^3$) may be included in the model.

### Research Design and Statistical Analysis

In general, given a dependent variable Y and a predictor X, we can test for a quadratic component by regressing Y on $X^2$ as well as X, so that the regression equation is

$\hat{Y} = b_0 + b_1X + b_2X^2$ (22.2)

If the regression coefficient of the squared term is significant, we can then test for the presence of a cubic component by regressing Y on $X^3$, as well as X and $X^2$, yielding the equation:

$\hat{Y} = b_0 + b_1X + b_2X^2 + b_3X^3$ (22.3)

and so on. We cannot test for the presence of a higher-level component without including the lower-level components as well. For example, we cannot test the presence of a quadratic component by regressing only on $X^3$, because $X^2$ will usually be highly correlated with X. The regression would reflect linear and quadratic effects, and we might get significance if there was no quadratic component. If we wish to test for the presence of a quadratic component, we must partial out the linear component. If we regress TC on BMI and $BMI^2$ for all women aged 65 or less in the Seasons data set, we find the equation

$TC = 79.561 + 8.118BMI - 0.112BMI^2$ (22.4)

Both the coefficients of BMI and $BMI^2$ are significant; $b_1 = 8.118, t(187) = 3.060, p = .003$ and $b_2 = -0.112, t(187) = -2.735, p = .001$, respectively.

There is one bound in the quadratic function. Because the partial regression coefficient of BMI is negative, the rate of change of TC with BMI is negatively accelerated. As the amount by which TC increases as BMI becomes less as BMI gets larger, and for large values of BMI, TC starts decreasing as BMI increases. Had the coefficient of the quadratic component been positive, this would indicate that the rate of change of TC increases as BMI gets larger.

A significant quadratic coefficient in a regression equation implies that the slope is different at different values of X. For example, assuming a causal relationship between BMI and TC, a doctor might prioritize weight loss as a mean of reducing cholesterol for patients with BMI in a lower range where BMI changes more predict TC changes and might prescribe more medication for patients with higher BMI values. For Equation 22.2, we can show that the slope for any value of X is equal to $b_1 + 2b_2X$. Therefore, the slope of the curve at BMI = 25 is $8.118 + 2(-0.112)(25) = 2.518$, and the slope at BMI = 35 is negative. A much larger influence on the predicted value of TC than changing BMI from 35 to 34. The maximum of the curve is found at $BMI = -b_1/2b_2 = -8.118/2(-0.112) = 36.241$. Substituting this value into the regression equation, we obtain $TC \approx 227$.
