How is a researcher to select values of Cohen’s $f$ and $\rho$? One approach to providing values of $f$ is to follow Cohen’s guidelines and use .1, .25, and .4 for small, medium, and large effects, respectively. For repeated-measures designs, we suggest that a reasonable range of values of $\rho$ is .2, .5, and .8 for small, medium, and large values, respectively. If a researcher wants .90 to detect a large effect of A assumes a strong correlation between conditions, the inputs to G*Power will be $f = .4$ and $\rho = .8$. For our example, the total sample size is required only 6. The required sample size will be larger if we assume a smaller effect of A, or a weaker correlation among conditions, or the sphericity assumption is not met. For example, if we use $f =1, \rho = .2$, and $\epsilon = .333$ (worst case for a design with four levels of A), the required sample size is 63.5! These examples make clear, researchers should they do to make reasonable assumptions and, when in doubt, be conservative in their assumptions.

When pilot data are available, those data can be used to select values of $f$, $\rho$, and $\epsilon$. Alternatively, knowledgeable of the relevant literature and/or theoretical considerations may provide estimates of these parameters. GPower 3.1 also provides help with calculating $f$. Select the determine button (see Figure 13.3) and in the window that pops up, enter of the variance among the population means as the Variance explained by special effect and the within-population variance as Variance within groups. Then select the labeled Calculate and transfer to main window. If the two variances were 24 and 25, and a value of $f = .4$ (i.e., $\sqrt{4/25}$) would appear in the main window. Assuming that the other values – e.g., alpha, power, the correlation – has been inserted, selecting the button will yield the required sample size.

The output of GPower 3.1 will be self-explanatory, for the noncentrality parameter, $\lambda$. Lambda characterizes the noncentral F distribution and is defined as

> $\lambda = \epsilon n f^2/(1-\rho)$

Inserting the output of $n$, and the input values of $\epsilon, \alpha, f$, and the correlation (from the left column of Figure 13.3), we have $(1)(6)(4).16/2 = 19.2$, the value of $\lambda = 19.2$, in Figure 13.3. This value can be used in some programs to calculate power. For example, in R, we can use an F distributional calculator, $1 –pf(f = 3.287, ncp = 19.2, df1 = 3, df2 = 15)$, to obtain the result 0.9111, the sample power as reported in Figure 13.3.

## 13.9 Testing Single of Contrasts

### 13.9.1 Pairwise Comparisons

In most studies, pairwise comparisons or more complex contrasts are of more interest than the omnibus F test. Contrasts are distributed on one degree of freedom, so sphericity is not an issue because there is only a single contrast score for each participant. In the example of seasonal effects upon depression, we wish to test all pairwise comparisons. The calculations are straightforward and, in any event, paired F or t tests can be carried out by statistical software. There are two issues, however:

- 1. The error for the six possible pairwise comparisons. Each of the six comparisons in our example is tested against MSA or the variance of the comparison under consideration?
- 2. The control of familywise error (FWE) rate. Should Tukey’s HSD procedure be used, as described in Chapter 9, or is there an alternative preferred?

With respect to the error term, MSA is distributed on more error degrees of freedom, $(a – 1)(n – 1)$, and therefore the F test with this denominator is potentially powerful. Nevertheless, the default error term in statistical packages is usually the variance of the contrast tested. For example, to test the difference between winter and summer depression scores, the mean ($d$) and standard deviation ($s_d$) of the 14 differences are calculated, then

$$t = \hat{d} / (s_d / \sqrt{n})$$
$$1.705 / (2.622 / \sqrt{14}) = 2.433$$

(Some software packages report the $F$ statistic, which is just the square of the contrast instead denominator based on the above $t$). The reason for dividing by the standard error of the contrast instead of $MS_{VA}$ if there is nonempty in the data set, the variances of difference scores may vary greatly across comparisons. Because $MS_{VA}$ is a function of the average of these variances, it will be too small when testing some the comparisons and too large when testing others. Bohr (1981) showed that the denominator of the $t$ (or $F$) is based on $MS_{VA}$, even small departures from sphericity can create serious distortions in Type 1 and 2 error rates and in the widths of confidence intervals.

## 13.10 The Problem of Missing Data in Repeated-Measures Designs

Missing data can be a problem in any kind of research. They are particularly troublesome in repeated-measures designs when the general linear model (GLM) modules of the statistical packages are used to conduct ANOVAs, as cases with missing scores in any condition are completely dropped from the analysis.

There are many possible causes of missing data. There may be some equipment failure, or a participant may not show up for a session or may fail to respond to a questionnaire or stimulus. What are to do? If we drop participants who have scores in most but not all conditions, or if a condition contains too many missing observations, a good deal of effort and a lot of information may be lost. More importantly, cases with complete data may differ systematically with missing data. If missing data occurs nonrandomly, conducting analyses with only complete cases will provide biased estimates of population parameters and standard errors thereby invalidate our inferences.

We can distinguish among three types of missing data: missing not at random (MNAR, or nonignorable missing data), data missing at random (MAR), and data missing completely at random (MCAR). To illustrate the distinction, consider a data set like the one presented in Table 13.1 in which depression scores are collected at different seasons of the year, except assume there are 28 participants, 14 who identify as M and 14 who identify as W.
