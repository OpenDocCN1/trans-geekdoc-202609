# 21.8 Automated Procedures for Developing Prediction

Sometimes we want to predict some criterion of interest by developing a regression equation that contains a subset of the useful predictor variables that are available. In predicting, we are normally concerned with the accuracy of the predictions and with the costs involved in making them. If our only concern was accuracy, then for large samples we would be inclined to include as many predictors as possible in the regression equation. Because in many types most of the predictor variables are correlated with one another, including all of them in a regression would be cumbersome but would also introduce a good deal of redundancy. Several automated procedures have been developed to allow a compromise between these procedures: forward selection, backward elimination, and stepwise regression; all are commonly available in statistical software. For example, in SPSS, the “Method” defaults to “Enter,” which includes all variables, but can be changed to “Forward,” “Backward,” or “Stepwise” from the main linear regression window. Using these automated procedures, it is often possible to select a subset of the predictors that account for nearly as large a proportion of the variance in Y as the pool of predictors. Before deciding the methods, we should emphasize that these automated procedures have been developed solely to produce the best prediction equations according to certain criteria. These equations need not be best – nor good – in any explanatory or theoretical sense. Running an automated routine may be useful for predicting outcomes within a sample but is a poor way to develop theory. Recall our discussion of cross-validation techniques and why they are so important: the best-fitting regression equations are sample specific and may have little ability to predict outcomes accurately in any sample or in the population (see Section 20.4.4). Thus, we recommend that cross-validation be used to validate a predictive model that is generated by any of the automated procedures discussed next.

# 21.8.1 Forward Selection

In the forward selection, the regression equation is built up one predictor variable at a time. On the first step, the predictor that has the highest correlation (positive or negative) with the criterion variable is selected. If it fails to meet the criterion for inclusion, the procedure ends with no predictors in the equation, and the final equation is Y = . If the first predictor meets the criterion and is added to the equation, a second predictor is selected and tested to determine whether it should be entered into the equation. The second predictor considered is the one that would result in the greatest increment in R² if added to the equation. If the second predictor does not meet the criterion for inclusion, the procedure terminates with only a single predictor in the equation. If it meets the criterion, on the third step, a third predictor is selected, and so on. At each step, a partial F test is performed on the selected variable, and the criterion for inclusion is stated in terms of the critical value or the significance level of the F. For example, using the five predictors and 131 cases described in Section 21.4.1, forward selection finds that the best-fitting regression model is Y = 103.423 + 1.847age + .860BMI with R² = .333.

For procedures like forward selection, Wilkinson (1979) has discussed the case in which a subset of k predictors is chosen, where 1 < k < m, and has provided tables for the 95th and 99th percentile points of the sample R² distribution in forward selection based on simulations (other tables and tables of this problem can be found in Hocking, 1983; Rencher & P, 1980; and Wilkinson & Dallal, 1982). These tables are more conservative than the F tables. With N = 35 and α = .05, if all members of a set of predictor variables are to be included in the regression equation, it is appropriate to use the F test for R² significance. When this is done, it is possible the R² exceeds .26 to reject the hypothesis that the population multiple correlation coefficient is 0. If the four predictors are to be selected from a larger set of 20 predictors by a forward selection procedure, according to Wilkinson's tables, the sample R² exceeds .51 to reject the null hypothesis. Many researchers do not seem to be aware of this problem; for a sample of 66 papers that reported forward selection analyses according to the usual F tests, Wilkinson found that 19 were not significant when his tables were used.

# 21.8.2 Backward Elimination

As you may expect, backward elimination is the opposite of forward selection. Whereas forward selection begins with no predictors in the equation and adds them to the equation by one, backward elimination begins with all the predictors in the equation and removes them one by one until the final equation is obtained. At each step, the predictor in equation causes the smallest change in R² is tested to determine whether it should be removed from the equation. Again, the criterion for removal is generally stated in terms of the significance level of a partial F test. If the selected variable is removed, another predictor is selected and tested on the next step. The procedure terminates when a predictor has been selected for testing it does not meet the criterion for elimination; the final equation includes that predictor and all the other predictors remaining in the equation at that point.
