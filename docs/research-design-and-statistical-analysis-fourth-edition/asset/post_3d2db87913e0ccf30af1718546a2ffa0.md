## 3.2 Basic Concepts for Analyzing the Structure of Events

### 3.2.1 Simple Experiments and Elementary Events

Suppose we have a class of 100 students, 60 psychology majors and 40 linguistics majors. The instructor, a kindly statistician, gives no grades lower than C. The number of students in each receiving grade is presented in Table 3.1.

Suppose further that the major of each student, along with their grade, is written on a slip of paper and the 100 slips are placed in a box. We can determine the probability that a slip of paper randomly selected from the box has a particular major and/or grade written on it. We can use this simple experiment to introduce some ideas about probability.

| Major | A | B | C | Total |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Linguistics | 12 | 24 | 4 | 40 |

A simple experiment is a well-defined process that leads to a single outcome (Hays, 1981). All probabilities are defined with respect to an experiment. Randomly drawing a slip of paper from the box and observing the grade and major on the slip is an example of a simple experiment. Another example is recording whether or not Rachel's 20 responses is correct or wrong.

The possible outcomes of a simple experiment are called *elementary events*, and the complete set of elementary events are called the *sample space* for the experiment. In the statistics class example, the sample space consists of the 100 combinations of major and grade across all the students in the class. In the ESP, the sample space consists of every possible sequence of correct and incorrect guesses across trials.

### 3.2.2 Combining Elementary Events into Event Classes

We are not interested in the probability of a particular elementary event as we are in the probability of meaningful collections of events that are called *event classes* or *simply*, events. For example, a student in the statistics class might be interested in the probability of getting a grade of A or B. In the ESP experiment, we would likely be interested in a summary of performance (e.g., proportion of correct responses) across all the trials in the experiment.

Events can be combined in important ways. Events may be “joined” such that an elementary event may be simultaneously classified into two (or more) event classes. In our statistics class example, a person may be a linguistics major and have earned an A in the class. In our ESP experiment, Rachel might pick the correct card on the first and third... and twelfth trial. Thus, we can talk about *joint events* that are comprised of the intersections of two or more events. We will use the conjunction “and” to refer to the joining of two or more events, but we will understand the term to refer to the logical operation of intersection.

Events may *unite* to *form compound events*. In contrast to joint events such as “Linguistics and A,” the *union* of two events occurs if an elementary event may be classified as *either* or *or* of the events. In our statistics class example, the union of linguistics and A if we observe “Linguistics” or “A” or (i.e., a linguistics major who earned a grade of A). In a simplified version of the ESP example, the outcome of at least one correct guess in *two* trials is comprised of the events: “correct on the first trial only” or “correct on the second trial” or “correct on three trials”.

## 3.2.3 Characterizations of Sets of Events

Events may be related to one another in different ways. One important relationship is *complementarity*. The complement of event A is the collection of events that do not qualify as A. The complement of A is “not A” or $\bar{A}$. In our statistics example, the complement of psychology major is linguistics major; the complement of C is “not C” or $\bar{C}$. One useful application of complementarity is that it is sometimes easier to compute the probability of at least one correct in five trials by computing the probability of zero correct and subtracting it from 1.

Another kind of relationship that characterizes some sets of events is *mutual exclusion*. Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot jointly occur. The events A, B, and C are mutually exclusive, as correct and incorrect. If you get an A, you do not get a B. We will find that it is easier to compute probabilities of mutually exclusive events than those involving not mutually exclusive.

Because part of the difficulty of probability calculations it is generally better to define the outcomes of an experiment as mutually exclusive and exhaustive. A set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive events partitions the sample space. That is, it accounts for every event, and so does the set of events “A, B, and C” partitions the sample space for the statistics class; so does the “psychology” and linguistics. Similarly, the six cells of Table 3.1, corresponding to the six combinations of major and grade, also partition the sample space. As a final example, any event and its complement partition a sample space.

Another important relationship between events is that of *independence*. A formal definition of independence must be deferred until probability calculations are introduced. An event is independent if the occurrence of one event provides no information about the likelihood of occurrence of the other event. That is, independent events are unrelated. In our ESP example, it is reasonably able to assume that the trial-to-trial outcomes are independent of one another. If Rachel is randomly guessing, knowing that she made a correct response on the first trial does not help us to predict her performance on any other trial. Because the way in which the terms “independence” and “mutual exclusion” are used in everyday language, students confuse the two concepts. However, the two terms have very different meanings when we talk about probability: If two events, $E_1$, and $E_2$, are mutually exclusive (e.g., correct, incorrect), then knowing that $E_1$ occurs tells us that $E_2$ does not occur (i.e., their joint probability is zero). However, if $E_1$ and $E_2$ are independent, then the probability of their joint occurrence is greater than zero, except in the trivial case where the probability of one of the events is zero. Thus, mutually exclusive events are not independent.
