
## Developing the Fundamentals of Hypothesis Testing

Finally, low powered studies should be avoided. The obvious reason is that low powered designs have a low probability of detecting a real effect of interest; that's just a restatement of the definition of power. A less obvious, and more insidious, reason to avoid low-powered designs is that, when they do conclude that the hypothesis can be rejected, they typically dramatically overestimate the true effect size. This overestimation occurs because, in low-powered designs, only large effect sizes allow the rejection of the null hypothesis, and as we saw in Figure 4.2, smaller samples are more likely to produce sample statistics that are far from the true value. Said differently, when the sample size is small, sampling error is large and only very large effects in the sample provide sufficient evidence to conclude there is an effect in the population. As the sample size increases, sampling error decreases and smaller effect sizes in the sample will be sufficient to demonstrate an effect in the population.

## 4.5.5 Post Hoc Power Calculations Are Not Useful

In contrast to a priori power calculations, which should always be run, post hoc power calculations (after the data have been collected) do not provide useful information and should be avoided. The desire to calculate post hoc power tends to occur in two different situations, both involving a failure to reject the null hypothesis. In the first case, the experimenter hopes to claim that their observed effect is “real” and “only” nonsignificant because the experiment had low power. This argument is often advanced when the effect is in the expected direction, the p-value is close to α and no definitive replication results are available. In the second type of situation, the experimenter hopes to claim that the null hypothesis is true because the experiment had high power to detect a real effect of some reasonable size, and yet failed to do so. These are flawed arguments.

Researchers hoping to claim their nonsignificant result is “real” post hoc power in their experiment using the observed effect size as the best estimate of population effect size. This approach appears reasonable, yet it creates a circular logic: Effect sizes large to yield high power would have resulted in a significant test, and effect sizes that do not produce significant tests will also yield relatively low power estimates. In fact, post hoc power and the p-value are all related: a low p-value is associated with high post hoc power, and versa (Hoenig & Heisei, 2001). In short, computing post hoc power using the observed effect size does not provide any information that is dependent on the p-value.

Researchers hoping to clear the null hypothesis to be true making a logical error. The logic of a hypothesis test that assigns probabilities to outcomes can be used to generate a sampling distribution; we reject the null when we observe an outcome that is very unlikely (p ≤ α), given that distribution. In other words, testing a hypothesis provides information about the probability of the data, given the null hypothesis; contrast, the experimenter is trying to make a claim about a different value, p(H₀|observed data). This logical error is akin to being told that “if it rains, the ground is wet,” observing wet grass and concluding that it must have rained, failing to consider alternative causes such as sprinklers. Researchers interested in learning alternatives to the logic of hypothesis testing should consider courses on Bayesian statistics, which are beyond the scope of this text.

## 4.5.6 Using Software to Compute Power

In Box 4.3, we showed the steps involved in calculating power in a binomial distribution. Those steps including determining the rejection region of the sampling distribution assuming $H_0$ is true and computing the probability of observing an outcome in the rejection region within a specific alternative, $H_a$. The tools presented in Sections 4.3.4 and 4.4.3 allow us to use different calculators for these steps in computing power.

We use R to identify the region under the null hypothesis, as described in Section 4.4.3. For example, using $H_0: \pi=.25, H_a: \pi=.35$, and $n=20$. We start by using the qbinom function to identify the one-tailed rejection region of the sampling distribution assuming the null hypothesis is true: the qbinom(size = 20, prob = .25) returns the value of Y, above which only 5% of the sampling distribution falls when $n=20$ and $\pi=.35$. Using that value of Y and the $H_a: \pi=.35$, the tools described in Section 4.3.4 allow us to compute the probability of observing many successes (or more) assuming the specific alternative hypothesis is true: pbinom(x=8, prob=.35, tail=FALSE)=.236. This means that for a power of size 20, $p(y\ge9|n=.35)$ is equal to .237. Of course, this equals the power we computed in Section 4.2 using Table C.1.

Another helpful tool designed specifically for computing power is a free, convenient and flexible program G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007). ## 4.6 When Assumptions Fail

Suppose the significance test on ESP data allows us to reject the null hypothesis that $\pi=.25$ at $\alpha=.05$. Do we conclude that Rachel has ESP? Not necessarily. For one thing we may have made a Type I error. In our example, a Type I error could occur because Rachel guessed an unusually high number of cards correctly, simply by chance. For such as ESP, we likely insist on replication with a larger sample size and a much smaller value for $\alpha$. Of course, we might remain skeptical that Rachel has ESP even if a large-scale replication is successful. In that case, our conclusions would not be rooted in our statistical analysis but instead depend on pragmatic concerns about the suspicion that some aspect of the experimental design had inadequately controlled. For example, Rachel might have spotted a "tell" in the experimenter's behavior, or perhaps there was collusion between Rachel and the experimenter. There is always a distinction between the empirical result and its implications for decision making with respect to the null hypothesis and the researcher's interpretation of the result.

We must also keep in mind that the variability of our calculations depends on the assumptions we used to generate a particular sampling distribution. Throughout the preceding sections on hypothesis testing and power, we assumed that Y has a particular binomial distribution. The derivation of the equation rests upon the assumption that $\pi$ is constant across all trials and that the probability of an outcome on one trial is independent of the outcome on any other trial. For the ESP experiment, the particular binomial distribution used is the one with $n=20$ and $\pi=.25$ (for random guessing with 20 trials and 4 cards). If these assumptions do not hold, we cannot take our calculations at face value. We try to design our studies so that the assumptions are satisfied, but we must always consider ways in which they might be violated.

### 4.6.1 Nonlinear Sampling

Suppose the ESP experiment the four cards are blue, black, red, and white. We would normally have the experimenter shuffle the pack before selecting a card so that any card would be equally likely to be selected. What would happen if we just handled the experimenter and tell him to select any sequence of 20 cards if he happens to have a strong preference for blue, so that the proportions of blue, black, red, and white selects are .7, .1, .1, and .1? Suppose also Rachel has no ESP but she also prefers blue (or knows of the experimenter's preference). She responds, but with probability .4 for blue and .2 for each of the other colors (as she were randomly selecting with replacement from a box containing two blue cards and one black, red, and one white). Therefore, the probability that she would be correct is not .25 but $p(C) = p(Blue_{guess} \cdot Blue_{selected}) + p(Black_{guess} \cdot Black_{selected}) + p(Red_{guess} \cdot Red_{selected}) + p(White_{guess} \cdot White_{selected}) = (.4)(.7) + (.2)(.1)+(2)(.1) = .28 + .02 + .02 = .34$. This might seem like an extreme example; even more subtle deviations from the binomial assumptions could undermine our conclusions.

It is important to distinguish between general knowledge about preferences and psychic ability. For example, given $k$ options and no obvious reason to choose one option over the others, you might think that people would choose each option with probability $1/k$ – they usually do not. Conduct your "experiment": If you tell a group of people that you are thinking of a number between 1 and 10 and that you want them to guess that number often often, the integers 1–10 will be avoided and more people will guess "7" than any other number.
