Many statistical packages, including SPSS, compute values of t' and t when independent-groups t is performed. SPSS also reports a test of homogeneity of variance, Levene's (1960) test. In Levene's test, an average absolute deviation of scores about group mean is calculated and the ratio of these two averages is assessed using an F distribution. We will describe the family of F distributions in Chapter 8.) The Brown-Forsythe (1974a) test is similar, except the groups are replaced by their means; some statistical packages call both tests “Levene's tests.

It may appear to test the homogeneity of variance assumption before choosing a t. However, there are two important reasons to proceed to Welch's t'. First, Levene's test has low power (Zimmerman, 2004; Nordstcke & Zumbo, 2006; Delacre, Lakens, & Leys, 2017), and Table 6.3 demonstrates that the Student's t is not robust when the groups have unequal variance. Selecting the t test based on variance does not fully correct for the Type 1 error rate. Skipping the test for homogeneity of variance and proceeding directly to Welch's results in a more robust test with power that is as highly as Student's t. Thus, we advocate using Welch's t for all independent samples t tests.

# 6.7 Measuring the Standardized Effect: Cohen's d

In the experiment on the effects of diet on cholesterol level, the difference between the two groups provides a measure of the size of the effect. However, measure is on the original data scale. Although this has the advantage of being meaningful to the researcher, it has accompanying disadvantages: It is difficult to evaluate the importance of differences between means, or to compare such differences with effects of the same independent variable in other groups or laboratories, or combine results of several experiments.

The p-value associated with a hypothesis test is sometimes incorrectly interpreted as a measure of the importance of an effect. A p-value simply indicates the probability that data as extreme as observed in our study, or more, would be sampled from the population if the null hypothesis is correct. Very small, perhaps unimportant in any practical or theoretical sense, may be statistically significant because the sample sizes are large. Conversely, what to effects may occur just by change in small samples, even if the null hypothesis is true. Finally, important effects, even large differences, may not be statistically significant because the sample was too small, or variability too great, for the test to have much power.

In view of these concerns, expressions of effect size have been proposed (e.g., Cohen, 1988; Kraemer, 2005; McGraw & Wang, 1992; Rosenthal & Rubin, 1994, Shieh, 2013), and this remains an active area of research. In all cases, the estimated effect size (ES) is the difference between the sample means measured in standardized units: ES = $ar{Y}_1-ar{Y}_2$.
