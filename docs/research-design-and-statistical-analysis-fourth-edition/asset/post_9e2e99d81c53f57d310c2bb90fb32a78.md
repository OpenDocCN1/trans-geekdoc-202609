### Method 1: Adjusting for all main and interaction effects

| SV | df | SS |
|---|---|---|
| A | a - 1 | $SS_{A,AC} = (R^2_{T,AC,AC} - R^2_{C,AC}) SS_y$ |
| C | c - 1 | $SS_{C,AC} = (R^2_{T,AC,AC} - R^2_{A,AC}) SS_y$ |
| AC | (a - 1)(c - 1) | $SS_{AC,AC} = (R^2_{T,AC,AC} - R^2_{T,AC}) SS_y$ |
| Residual | N - ac | $SS_{residual} = (1 - R^2_{T,AC,AC}) SS_y$ |

Hypotheses tested:

- $A: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_a$, where $\mu_j = \frac{1}{c} \sum y_{ij}$ is the weighted mean of the population means for the $c$ cells in the $j$-th row of $A$
- $C: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_c$, where $\mu_k = \frac{1}{a} \sum y_{ij}$ is the weighted mean of the population means for the cells in the $k$-th col of $C$
- $AC: \mu_{jk} - \mu_{j.} + \mu_{.k} - \mu_{..} = 0$ for all $j, k, j'$

Usage: This method uses Type III sums of squares to test these hypotheses about weighted column and row means, and will be the method of choice when unequal cell frequencies occur by chance. This method is also known as Overall and Spiegel's (1969) method 1, Yates's (1934) weighted means of means, and SPSS's classic regression approach.

### Method 2: Adjusting for the effects of the same and lower order

| SV | df | SS |
|---|---|---|
| A | a - 1 | $SS_{A,AC} = (R^2_{T,AC,AC} - R^2_{Y,C}) SS_y$ |
| C | c - 1 | $SS_{C,AC} = (R^2_{T,AC,AC} - R^2_{Y,A}) SS_y$ |
| AC | (a - 1)(c - 1) | $SS_{AC,AC} = (R^2_{T,AC,AC} - R^2_{T,AC}) SS_y$ |
| Residual | N - ac | $SS_{residual} = (1 - R^2_{T,AC,AC}) SS_y$ |

Hypotheses tested:

- $A: \frac{n_j}{n_{..}} \mu_{j.} - \frac{1}{n_{..}} \sum_{k=1}^c n_{j.k} \mu_{.k} = 0$ for $j=1,2,\dots,a$
- $C: \frac{n_k}{n_{..}} \mu_{.k} - \frac{1}{n_{..}} \sum_{j=1}^a n_{j.k} \mu_{j.} = 0$ for $k=1,2,\dots,c$
- $AC: \mu_{jk} - \mu_{j.} + \mu_{.k} - \mu_{..} = 0$ for all $j, k, j'$

Usage: This method uses Type II sums of squares, which adjust an effect of interest for effects on the same or lower order, and for higher-order effects that if there is no interaction. Method 2 tests its hypotheses with somewhat more power than Method 1. However, if there is the possibility of an interaction, Method 2 should be avoided because it tests data-dependent hypotheses that are not useful (see the hypotheses that are tested above). This method is also known as Overall and Spiegel's method 2, Yates' fitting fitting constant, and SPSS's classic experimental design approach.

Usage: This method tests main effects against the weighted row and column means. These tests may be desirable if the cell frequencies are proportional to the corresponding population sizes. This method is also known as Yates's method for proportional cell sizes.

## 23.4 Testing Homogeneity of Slopes Using Multiple Regression

In Chapters 19 and 22, we discussed tests for equality of slopes when one variable was regressed on another at different levels of a dichotomous (i.e., having two levels) categorical variable. We selected participants in the data set aged 65 years or less with BMI scores over 40 and tested whether the rate of change of total cholesterol level (TC) with age was the same for participants identified as men and women. Although we did not use the term sex in the data is a dummy variable that labels men by 0 and women by 1. We showed we could test the hypothesis that the rate of change was the same for men and women by regressing TC on age, sex, and an additional variable Age × Sex that was formed by multiplying age by sex. The resulting regression equation is

TC = 221.143 − 0.0005 age − 94.612 sex + 1.813 Age × Sex

Because the coefficient of the interaction term age × sex is significant, t(363) = 4.909, p = .000, we can reject the hypothesis that the slope of TC with age is equal for men and women, consistent with what we saw in Figure 22.5.

What if the categorical variable has more than two levels? If so, can we still test the homogeneity of regression slopes by testing whether there is a significant interaction? That is, can we test whether the regression of Y on a continuous variable, X, depends on the level of A when A is a categorical variable with more than two levels. However, now more than one dummy variable is needed to code the categorical variable, the interaction term will have more than 1 df, and we will need to use a partial F test to determine whether the interaction is significant.

Consider, for example, the categorical variable education. We might be interested not only in its effect on TC, but also in its interaction. EL = 1 corresponds to individuals with a high school education or less (school = 1, 2), EL = 2 beyond high school but not including the bachelor’s degree (school = 4, 5, or 6), and EL = 3 to at least a bachelor’s degree (school = 7). We treat the EL as a categorical variable. EL seems to make a difference in TC. Using the same participants as the previous analysis, mean TC is 227, 217, and 210 for EL = 1, 2, and 3, respectively, and an ANOVA with EL as the independent variable is significant, F(2, 359) = 4.908, p = .000.

Now let's consider the effects of EL on TC depend on the level of the participants. Because EL has three levels, we can code it with two dummy variables – we use effect, dummy, or any other kind of dummy coding. We can code the interaction of EL with age using two additional dummy variables obtained, by multiplying the two dummy variables to code EL by age. If we have a continuous predictor X and a categorical variable A with a levels, the proportion of the variability in Y accounted for by a regression on X and the a–1 dummy variables coding the categorical variable A is R²,Y,X.A..

The three methods considered so far are not the only possible approaches to analyzing data from nonorthogonal design. It is possible that logical or theoretical analysis of the research problem might dictate the order in which dummy variables are entered into the regression equation and, therefore, the nature of adjustments. Suppose that A and C indicate levels of child and parental achievement, respectively. It is reasonable to assume that parental achievement may influence a child's educational achievement but the reverse type of influence is quite rare. Despite the potential power advantage that Method 2 may provide over Method 1, we do not recommend using Method 2 because an interaction exists, the main effects are not meaningful. But however failure to reject a null hypothesis runs the risk of Type 2 error. Thus, even interactions that do not approach significance can result in biased tests of main effects (see, for example, Overall & Hornick, 1981). We believe that Method 2 should not be used unless there is a strong priori reason to assume no interaction, and a test for the interaction is not significant.
