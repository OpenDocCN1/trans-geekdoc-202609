# 8.3 The Structural Model

We view the various groups of courses in a study as random samples from populations selected for investigation. Then the question of whether the four methods of Table 8.1 differ in their effectiveness can be rephrased as: Do the means of the four populations differ? To answer this question, we need a way of linking the observed data to the hypothetical populations, of relating statistics to population parameters. We begin by constructing a structural model, a model that relates the scores to population parameters.

We start assuming that the participants in the experiment are identical in identity, motivation, and any other characteristics that would affect their scores. We further assume that they are identically treated – e.g., tested at the same moment in time, and under the exact same conditions. Under these unrealistic assumptions, everyone in an instructional population, and everyone in an instructional group, would have the same score; we can represent this state of affairs with the following:

```
$Y_{11} = Y_{21} = Y_{31} = \dots = Y_{n1} = \mu_1$
Y_{12} = Y_{22} = Y_{32} = \dots = Y_{n2} = \mu_2$
Y_{13} = Y_{23} = Y_{33} = \dots = Y_{n3} = \mu_3$
```

where there are $n$ participants in a group, $Y_{ij}$ represents the $i$th score in the $j$th group, and $\mu_j$ is the mean of the $j$th population of scores. For example, $Y_{52}$ would refer to the score of the fifth participant in the second group.

Of course, this is not realistic; scores of individuals in an instructional group will vary, and differ from the instructional population mean, because of nuisance variables such as motivation level, prior relevant experience, interest in the topic, and conditions at the time of testing. We can represent this complication by saying that the score of the $i$th participant in the $j$th group will differ from the treatment population mean, $\mu_j$, by some amount – an error component, $\varepsilon_{ij}$. This means that an individual’s score equals the mean of the treatment population plus an error that is,

```
$Y_{ij} = \mu_j + (Y_{ij} - \mu_j)$
= \mu_j + \varepsilon_{ij}$ (8.1)
```

Note that $\varepsilon_{ij}$ can be positive or negative; that is, nuisance variables can raise the score above the population mean or lower below it.

We can rewrite Equation 8.1 in a way that more directly expresses the relation between a score and the effect of the condition under that score was obtained. First, we define one more population parameter, $\mu$, the mean of all the treatment populations; i.e., $\mu = \Sigma u/a$, where $a$ is the number of levels of the independent variable. Equation 8.1 is unchanged if we add and subtract $\mu$ from the right side:

```
$Y_{ij} = \mu + (Y_{ij} - \mu) + \varepsilon_{ij}$ (8.2)
```
