If the treatment influences the covariate or is otherwise system-atically related to it, performing an ANCOVA will not simply reduce error variance; rather, it may adjust between-group differences in ways that are difficult to understand and that may lead to biased results.

It is appropriate to use ANCOVA to test the effect of test condition for the Myers et al. (1983) experiment where participants were randomly assigned to the test conditions and there was no way that the treatment could evaluate the covariate. However, suppose everything else was kept the same except that participants were given the quantitative reasoning test after the experiment before they read the text material. In this case, we should not use the quantitative reasoning test as a covariate material given in the text conditions might affect the participant's quantitative scoring. So, if we went ahead and used it, a covariate, the regression adjustment built into the ANCOVA would not only remove some of the error variance in the dependent variable, but it would also remove some of the effect of the treatment.

To illustrate another kind of problem, suppose that than randomly assigning participants to test conditions, the text materials were presented to groups. For example, the LE text was given to a class of psychology majors and S HE texts were given to classes of math and fine arts, confusing test condition with major. We would say that we have a noneivalent design. As noted at the start of this subsection, some researchers seem to believe that performing an ANCOVA properly adjusts the groups for their pre-existing differences. Even though the groups differ on the covariate, the confounding cannot be magically removed by performing an ANCOVA; you cannot salvage a bad experimental design with statistical analysis. The underlying groups differ on many variables, and some of these are not likely to be fully predictable from a covariate. If treatments are applied to groups that differ from one another, ANCOVA presents the same kinds of difficulties that are always associated with interpreting the results of observational studies. Whenever the covariate varies systematically across conditions it becomes correlated with other variables that differ across groups, including the treatment itself. Performing an ANCOVA tends to adjust the effects of all variables, but to different degrees. In some cases, adjusting for some kinds of differences might exacerbate errors.
