Sometimes the researcher may have a priori hypothesis specified that only certain comparisons, pairwise or more complex, would be significant. In that case, the Dun-Bonferroni method should be followed. The per comparison error rate would be the desired-family error rate divided by $K$, the number of tests; this would be the critical p-value for our significance tests.

Another scenario is that the researcher observes the means in Table 10.5 and decides that the difference between the $B_1$ and $B_2$ is large enough to warrant further investigation. Or, after viewing the means, the researcher decides that the $B_1$ is mean enough from the others that it should be tested against all of the other three means. Post hoc contrasts should be tested by the Scheffé procedure described in Box 10.8.

Two points should be noted about the development so far. First, we have identified a family as a set of comparisons to a single source of variance. If we had several levels of $A$, we would also have controlled the FWE at .05 for all comparisons of those marginal means. As we discussed earlier in this chapter, modifications of the test used are indicated when this is not. In particular, the Tukey HSD is not appropriate because the various means have different standard errors. In such a case, even if all pairwise comparisons are of interest, the Bonferroni procedure should be applied with the standard error of the difference between means based on the average within-cell variance of the cells involved in each comparison.

In sum, contrasts to analyze main effects within multi-factor designs follow the same recommendations and procedures presented earlier in the context of the one-factor design.
