
## Research Design and Statistical Analysis

We can obtain $SS_{between}$ as

```
$SS_{between} = SS_A = R_{Y,A}^2 SS_{Y}$
```

where $R_{Y,A}^2$ is the square of the multiple correlation coefficient that results when Y is regressed on a complete set of $a - 1$ dummy variables that code factor A. The within-group variability can be obtained

```
$SS_{within} = (1 - R_{S/A}^2) SS_{Y}$
```

so that the test for A is given

```
$F = \frac{MS_A}{MS_{S/A}} = \frac{R_{Y,A}^2 SS_Y / (a-1)}{(1-R_{Y,A}^2) SS_Y / (N-a)}$
```

The null hypothesis is tested that the population means of Y are identical for each of the levels of the factor A. Performing a one-factor ANCOVA can be thought of as determining the categorical factor A has effects over and above those of a covariate, X, that is correlated with the dependent variable; or, equivalently, there are effects of A if we statistically adjust for differences in. The hypothesis we test is that the Y population means would be identical for each of the $a$ levels of the factor A if each participant had the same value on X. The ANCOVA can be thought of a comparison between two models in which Y is regressed on the covariate X:

- 1. A full ANCOVA model that also contains information about group membership:
$Y_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_j + \beta_{S/A}(X_{ij} - \tilde{X}_j) + \varepsilon_{ij}$ (ANCOVA)

where $\beta_{S/A}$ equals the weighted average of the slopes that would be obtained in separate regressions for each of the groups; i.e.,

```
$b_{S/A} = \frac{\sum b_j SS_j}{\sum SS_j}$
```

- 2. A restricted model that does not include information about group membership:

```
$Y_{ij} = \mu + \beta_{tot}(X_{ij} - \tilde{X}) + \varepsilon_{ij}$
```

where $\beta_{tot}$ is estimated by the slope of the regression of Y on X, ignoring group membership. The test statistic for the ANCOVA does not contain $SS_A$ or $SS_{S/A}$ but rather the corresponding sums of squares that have been adjusted for the covariate, called the adjusted sums of squares:

```
$SS_{A(adj)} = (R_{Y,X,A}^2 - R_{Y,X}^2) SS_Y$
```

## Research Design and Statistical Analysis

Several points about ANCOVA should be noted. First, the parameters in our models are not the same: $\beta_{tot}$ is estimated by the slope of the regression of Y on X, ignoring group membership, whereas $\beta_{S/A}$ is the weighted average of the slopes that would be obtained in separate regressions for each of the groups. The reason to use ANCOVA is that predictable variance from a covariate may result in smaller error term.

Figures 24.2 and 24.3 may be useful in understanding the potential efficiency of ANCOVA over ANOVA. To illustrate the advantage of ANCOVA compared to ANOVA, consider data from Myers et al. (1983; see Table 24.2 and data $pl\_with\_covariate.xlsx$) in which participants were assigned to one of three instructional conditions based on reading types of text: standard text (S), low-explanatory text (LE), or high-explanatory text (HE). The dependent variable is the proportion correct on a test administered after the instruction. When an ANOVA was performed, the null hypothesis that the population mean for the three types of text were identical (i.e., $\mu_S = \mu_{LE} = \mu_{HE}$) could not be rejected, $F(2, 45) = 2.965, p = .062$. However, the main effect of text was then accounted for as a covariate in ANCOVA, $F(2, 44) = 5.081, p = .010$. The data are presented in Table 24.2 and R output for the ANOVA and ANCOVA is presented in panels (a) and (b) of Figure 24.1.

Figure 24.2 indicates how differences between two treatment groups may be observed. The two clouds of data points represent two treatment groups. As seen from the marginal distributions on the right axis, the Y scores for the two groups overlap considerably. However, the distributions of the deviations of the data points from regression lines are superimposed on the data would show much greater separation. We should have more power if we were to test for the vertical separation of the population lines than if we test for differences among the marginal means.
