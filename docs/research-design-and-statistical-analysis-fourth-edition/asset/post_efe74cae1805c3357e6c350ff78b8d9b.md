## 6.3.3 Critical Values and Areas Under the t Distribution

Appendix Table C3 contains critical values of the $t$ statistic corresponding to various levels of significance and degrees of freedom. The head of each column are two numbers corresponding to levels of significance for one and two-tailed tests; each row of the table corresponds to a different number of $df$. As an example of the use of the table, find the column corresponding to a one-tailed proportion of $.025$ (and a two-tailed proportion of $.05$) and row $df = 9$. The critical value in this cell is $2.262$. We interpret this to mean that when there are 9 $df$, a $t$ of $2.262$ is outside the sampling distribution of $t$; $.05$ is greater than $2.262$ or less than $-2.262$. Now look down the same column to the row labeled. The probability is $.025$ of exceeding $1.96$ and the probability of $t > 1.96$ or $t < -1.96$ is $.05$. This is exactly the critical value in Table C2, the normal distribution probability table. In general, the critical value of $t$ decreases as the $df$ increases, rapidly approaching the critical value in Table C2 for the normal. Table C3 usually provides more accurate information than Table C2, although there is little difference when sample sizes are large. This is because $s_k$ is a consistent estimator of $\sigma_k$; as sample size increases, the standard error approaches the population standard error.

Assume we want a $.05$ significance level for a test on 35 $df$ against a two-tailed alternative. We select the column labeled for the two-tailed test and we want a row corresponding to 35 $df$. Table C3 has values for 30 and 40, but not for 35, so we interpolate, taking a value halfway between $2.042$ and $2.021$, or $2.031$. The true value, $2.030$, can be obtained from statistical software. In R we can use the $t$ distribution function, $qt(0.975, df = 35)$, to find the $t$ statistic at $.975$ quantile (i.e., $2.5\%$ of the area above it) where there are 35 $df$ in the data. As expected, it returns $.9749$. Alternatively, we can use the $pt$ function in R to find the proportion of a specific $t$ distribution below an observed value of $t$; input variables are the observed $t$ and the $df$. For example, $pt(2.030, df = 35)$ returns $.975$. See Box 6.1 for more details.

## 6.4.1 The Standard Error (SE) of $\hat{Y}_1-\hat{Y}_2$

We defined the statistic as $t = |(V-E(V))/s|$. In the current example, the random variable, $V$, is the difference in TC level between the two group means: $\hat{Y}_1-\hat{Y}_2$ (control–diet means) or, in general, $\hat{Y}_1-\hat{Y}_2$, the SE of the difference between the sample means, can be understood by considering a sampling experiment. Suppose we drew many independent pairs of random samples of of sizes $n_1$ and $n_2$, from two independent and normally distributed populations of sizes. If we carried out this sampling procedure, we could compute a difference between the means for each pair of samples drawn. The standard deviation of the sampling distribution $SE_E$ is the quantity to be estimated by the denominator of the $t$ in the two-group study. The issue is to calculate that estimate.

If $n_1, n_2$ are independently distributed, the variance of the sampling distribution of the difference between the two group means is the sum of the variances of the means (see Appendix B for a proof). Then the SE of the difference is

$$\hat{Y}_1-\hat{Y}_2 = \sqrt{\frac{\sigmasigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} \quad (6.2)$$

In addition to the assumption that the scores are drawn from independently distributed populations, using the $t$ distributions of Appendix C.3 requires one additional assumption, that the population variances are equal; that is, $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2$. This is referred to as the assumption of homogeneity of variance. Under this assumption, Equation 6.2 becomes

$$\hat{Y}_1-\hat{Y}_2 = \sigma\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}} \quad (6.3)$$

We have a single population variance $(\sigma^2)$ to estimate the variances of the two groups sampled in an experiment. To obtain the best estimate of $\sigma$, we average the two group variances and take the square root of the result. Because variance estimates are consistent statistics, the estimate based on the larger group is more likely to be close to the true variance. Therefore, the best estimate of $\sigma$ is a weighted average of the two group variances. The *pooled-variance estimate*, or $s_{pooled}^2$, is calculated as

```
$s_{pooled}^2 = \frac{n_1-1}{n_1+n_2-2}s_1^2 + \frac{n_2-1}{n_1+n_2-2}s_2^2 \quad (6.4)$
```
