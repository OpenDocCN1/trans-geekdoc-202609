## 14.7 Fixed or Random Effects?

It should be clear by now designating effects as fixed or random has important implications for significance testing, as well as for the degree to which we can generalize our results. We will soon discover that the designation has direct implications for parameter estimation (e.g., effect size). Therefore, we need to consider further the decision about classifying effects.

The decision to classify a variable as having fixed or random is not always a simple one. At one extreme, we have variables that clearly should be viewed as fixed effects. The levels of the variable have been intentionally selected for inclusion in the experiment, and because of the way in which they have been, there is no basis for viewing them as a random sample of a population of levels. This class includes most manipulated variables such as the type of distortion or the orientation of the photo in the Murray et al. (2000) experiment, drug dosing levels, and observed characteristics of individuals such as gender identity and clinical category. It would also include a variable as such because the four seasons exhaust the population of seasons.

At the other extreme, we have random sampling from some well-defined population. This is rarely realized in practice, and it is therefore difficult to determine the population to which our results can be generalized. We can reasonably view the participants in our experiment to be a random sample of adults, college students, college students interested in psychology, or college students who attend the particular university at which the study was run? The answer depends on the sampling process but also on the research topic. In studies of sensory processes like visual, we might generalize to the population of adults having normal vision but we expect those processes to be “hard wired” within the human system. In studies of human learning, we might define the population more narrowly, reserving judgment about whether our conclusions will hold for populations having a differently level of ability from characterizing the institution in which our study was run. When in doubt, generalizations should be restricted to the more defined population.

Even though the population is rarely as well defined as we would like, it should be clear from the preceding comments that we view participants as a random-effects variable. Our justification is that other individuals are provided an equal opportunity to participate and might well serve as replications of the experiment we run.

Classifying experimental stimuli such as words and pictures presents greater difficulty. For many experiments, we can argue on how much the same discussing participants that the stimuli were a random sample from a (possibly ill-defined) population of potential items. That is, the specific stimuli selected were not chosen in such a way that they were the only items with an opportunity to be included in the experiment; there were many other items that had an equal opportunity of being included under the sampling procedure used. In many other experiments, the choice of stimuli is so constrained that the items selected for the experiment virtually exhaust the stimuli that meet the sampling constraints. In studies involving responses to words, restrictions are often placed on the grammatical class, length in both syllables and letters, familiarity, and number of associates each word. The experimenter may find it difficult to identify items that meet those restrictions. Under such existing constraints, it is not clear that stimuli should be treated as having random effects. Two rough guidelines may be helpful. First, under the existing constraints, could independent investigators other samples of items? Assuming there is a reasonably large population of items, the second question is: Was there any likelihood that members of the population could be included in the study? If the answer to this question is positive, then it is reasonable to treat the stimuli as having random effects and all that this implies for our data analyses and the scope of our conclusions.

## 14.8 Understanding the Pattern of Means in Repeated-Measures Designs

Table 14.4 reveals that the orientation of pictures faces (A), the type of distortion (B), and the AB interaction all have significant effects on bizarre ratings. Ordinarily, to better understand the source of effects, we follow these overall F tests and with confidence intervals that more precisely target the differences among means. We might begin by testing comparisons across the marginal means. Comparisons of means are of most interest when there clearly is no interaction, because then there is no evidence that the effects of one factor vary as a function of the other. When an interaction is present, or even when there is some reason to anticipate an interaction prior to data collection, other tests should be of greater interest. Tests of simple effects, such as the difference between $B_1$ and $B_2$ means at the $A_2$ orientation, might clarify the pattern of means. Other possibilities include test of interaction contrasts. For example, we decide to test whether the difference between $B_2$ and $B_1$ is significantly greater at $A_2$ than at $A_1$.

In this section, we illustrate approaches to analyzing means to understand all effects, and repeated-measures designs. We first consider examples from repeated-measures designs, and then present examples from mixed designs. We also discuss contrasts in designs with multiple error rate (FWE). Before dealing with the details of these analyses, we emphasize that there are no definite answers to the questions of which analyses to carry out or how to control the error rate. As discussed in Chapter 10, we may have a *priori* reason to focus on differences among specific cell means. Perhaps more commonly, we want to analyze a pattern of means that was not anticipated. In these circumstances, we will suggest some systematic approaches that might be used to understand the data, or researchers may design specific *post hoc* contrasts to summarize the pattern. Such considerations will influence the choice of contrasts and the alpha-level set for each test.

### 14.8.1 Comparing Marginal Means

In a multi-factor design, the main effect may be of interest if its interpretation is not qualified by the presence of an interaction. In that event, the researcher will want to analyze the pattern of marginal means. Although complex contrasts can be tested if theoretical considerations or the observed pattern of means warrant, researchers most often compute pairwise comparisons among marginal means. As we discussed in Section 13.9.1, the safest approach to testing any contrast in a repeated-measures design is to use an error term based only on the data involved in the contrast (cf. Bock, 1981). If we were to carry out paired comparisons on the marginal means of B for the data in Table 14.3, our error term would not be the SB term from the overall ANOVA presented in Table 14.4; rather, the error term would be the SB interaction based on only the levels of B. A simple way to conduct each comparison is to select the levels of B you wish to include in the analysis, and then perform the usual ANOVA on that subset of data. The source of variance in the resulting table will reflect the difference between the marginal means at the levels of B. What would be our criterion for evaluating the contrasts we conduct? If all pairwise comparisons are of interest, k would be the number of possible comparisons. In this case, FWE is .05/k. One question that arises is what our criterion if we test comparisons among both the B and A means. As we discussed in Chapter 10, we consider comparisons among the levels of a variable to constitute a family; therefore, if we were to do the three pairwise comparisons of the A means and the three comparisons of the B means, each test would be carried out at the .05/3.

### 14.8.2 Comparing Cell Means

We are interested in the pattern of cell means, particularly if we have predicted an interaction or observed one. One common approach to analyzing cell means is to conduct all pairwise comparisons of levels of A within each level of B or vice versa. For the example of Table 14.4, we might compare the three levels of distortion within versa. That is, at each level of A we compare B$_1$, B$_2$, and B$_3$. If we want to keep the FWE at .05 for the full set of tests, we use an alpha-level of .05/9, approximately .005, for significance of any comparison (i.e., Bon-ferroni). We selected Compare means using Paired-Tests from SPSS’ Analyze menu; then we selected the nine pairs to test. Only two of these comparisons were not significant at the .005 level: A$_1$–A$_2$, A$_1$–A$_3$ (p = .546) and B$_2$–B$_3$ (p = .025). These are the two smallest differences in Figure 14.1. This accounts for the interaction; it apparently largely due to the lack of a difference in the distortion effects of B$_2$ and B$_3$ at A$_1$, and possibly at A$_2$. These results should focus the researcher on the question of why these effects are different from the others that were tested.
