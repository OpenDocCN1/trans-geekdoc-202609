The basic principles in computing power are those that dictated the power calculations of Section 4.5.2 and Box 4.3. Power is an important concept to understand, let's briefly review the logic of the calculations. We begin by determining the theoretical sampling distribution of $H_0$, assuming $H_0$ is true. This distribution tells us how likely each outcome is, if $H_0$ describes the population. Next, we use $H_i$ to identify the region – the set of possible outcomes that are so unlikely, given the null hypothesis, we would conclude that the null hypothesis could be rejected in favor of an alternative. Then, we assume a specific version of alternative hypothesis, $H_a$, is actually true and we determine its theoretical sampling distribution. Any observed outcome in the rejection region will lead to reject the null hypothesis, and the sampling distribution of $H_a$ allows us to calculate how probable those outcomes are. The power of the sampling distribution for $H_a$, that falls within the rejection region is the power: The probability of observing an outcome that allows rejection of the null hypothesis when $H_a$ is true.

To carry out the calculations for our proposed replication experiment, we need the standard error of the mean. To find the final value of $SE$, we assume $\sigma_{change} = 3.897$, the value of $s_{change}$ observed in the Seasons study, so that $SE = 3.897/\sqrt{100} = .390$. We can now calculate the prior power of that proposed study.

+   1. Find the critical value of z that defines the rejection region. Assume that $\alpha = .05$ and the null hypothesis is $H_0$: $\mu_{danger} = 0$ and the alternative hypothesis is $H_a$: $\mu_{danger} > 0$. The rejection decision is: if $z > 1.645$. We can use Appendix Table C.2, or the techniques described in Section 5.5.6, to find this critical value of z.
2. Establish $H_a$, the specific alternative hypothesis. Our best guess of the mean change observed in the Massachusetts study, 0.557. Because that the sample size is large N ($n = 215$), it is probably well estimated. Bears repeating that significant results obtained in small samples tend to overestimate the population effects, so for small samples we might compensate by using a smaller value than the observed effect. Because our rejection region under the null hypothesis was described described in terms of standard deviation units – z – we convert .557 to a z score:

```
$z = (\mu_A - \mu_{hyp}) / \sigma_Y$
=(.557 - 0) / .390
= 1.429$
```

Thus, the mean of the $H_A$ distribution is 1.429; it falls 1.429 standard deviations above the mean of $H_0$.

+   3. Compute power. Power is the area under the $H_A$ distribution that falls above the critical value of z specified by $H_0$. 1.645.
