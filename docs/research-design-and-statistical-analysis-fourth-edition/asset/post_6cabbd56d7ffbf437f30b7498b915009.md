
# One Factor-Between-Participants Designs 203

Unequal $n$ complicates calculations in the one-factor design, which might tempt researchers to discard scores from some conditions to equate $n$. This is not a good idea for a few reasons. Discarding data to equalize the group sizes will reduce degrees of freedom and, consequently, power. Discarding participants also may misrepresent the relative size of the populations sampled. If so, the effects of some conditions may be weighted heavily or too lightly in the data analysis. Finally, computational case should not be a consideration when software programs are available to handle the calculations.

## 8.6.1 The ANOVA With Equal $n$

The ANOVA for unequal group sizes is a straightforward modification of the $n$ case, at least in the one-factor-between-participants design. (Complications arise when more than one factor is involved; these will be treated in Chapters 9 and 23.) Table 8.4 presents the ANOVA formulas and expected mean squares for the unequal $n$ case; the squared deviations in the $SS_E$ formula and the $n_i$ are weighted by the group size. Note that if the $n_i$ are equal, these formulas reduce to the formula in Table 8.3.

Table 8.5 presents statistics based on Beck Depression scores for four groups of male participants from the University of Massachusetts Medical School; the statistics are based on averages. For the purposes of this example, we excluded participants (those having no or only high school education, and with vocational training or an associate’s degree). The remaining groups are HS (high school), C (some college), B (Bachelor’s degree), and GS (graduate school). The statistics of Table 8.5 and the box plots of Figure 8.2 indicate that the groups differ in their average depression score. Both means and medians are noticeably higher for those who had only a high school education.

### Table 8.4 The analysis of variance for the one-factor-between-participants design with unequal group sizes

| Source | df | SS | MS |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Between (A) | $a - 1$ | $\sum_{j=1}^{a}(\bar{Y}_j - \bar{Y}_{\cdot})^2$ | $SS_A/df_a = MS_A$ | $\sigma^2 + \frac{1}{a-1}\sum_{j=1}^{a}n_j\sigma_j^2$ |
| Within (S) | $N - a$ | $\sum_{j=1}^{a}\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}(Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_j)^2$ | $SS_S/df_{sa} = MS_S$ | $\sigma^2$ |
| Total | $N - 1$ | $\sum_{j=1}^{a}\sum_{i}(Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_{\cdot})^2$ | | |

Note: $n_j$ is the number of scores in the $j$th group and $N = \sum_{j=1}^{a}n_j$.

## 8.6.2 Measures of Importance With Equal $n$

As in the $n$ design, $\eta^2 = SS_B/(SS+SS_W)$. For the Beck Depression data, substituting values from Table 8.6, $\eta^2 = 186.501/230.562$, or .079. Remember that this estimate is positively biased, and it can be obtained using the *eta_squared* function in the effectsize package in R or using SPSS (see Section 8.5). The formulas for $\omega$ and Cohen's $f$ undergo a very slight modification. We replace the $n$ in Equation 8.14 by the average $n$, $\bar{n}$, where $\bar{n} = \sum n_i / a = n/a$. We can simplify this thing by replacing $\bar{n}$ with $n$, the sample size. Using these estimations, $\sigma^2_A$, $\omega^2$, and Cohen's $f$ with no further changes.

## 8.7 Deciding on Sample Size

In the one-factor-between-participants design, unequal $n$ complicates calculations, which might tempt researchers to discard scores from some conditions to equate $n$. This is not a good idea for a few reasons. Discarding data to equalize the group sizes will reduce degrees of freedom and, consequently, power. Discarding participants also may misrepresent the relative size of the populations sampled. If so, the effects of some conditions may be weighted heavily or too lightly in the data analysis. Finally, computational case should not be a consideration when software programs are available to handle the calculations.

One possibility is to use Cohen's guidelines to establish effect size. For example, suppose that in designing the experiment described in 8.2, we need power up to .90. In the *Output Parameters* column, we found that the required $N$ is 96, or 24 in each group.
