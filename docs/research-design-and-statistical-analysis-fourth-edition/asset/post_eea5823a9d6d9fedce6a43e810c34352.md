It is rare to find perfect multicollinearity unless redundant variables such as subscale scores and total score, age and year of birth, are included. However, it is not rare to find situations where the predictors are highly collinear. In such cases, statistical software will perform the regression, but the values of the regression coefficients may be extremely unstable. The reason is that variability in Y that is shared by the predictors will be “split up” among them. The nature of this split will depend on the details of the data and may vary widely from sample to sample, leading to dramatically increased standard errors for the regression coefficients.

### Box 22.1 A Physical Demonstration of Multicollinearity and Its Consequence

- 1. Place three objects of different heights, such as a soda can, a wine bottle, and a bottle of hand sanitizer, on a table in a roughly triangular arrangement. Conceptually, one corner of the table defines the point ($X_1 = 0, X_2 = 0$), and thus the edges of the table define the values of $X_1$ and $X_2$ for the 'objects' locations. The Y value at each $(X_1, X_2)$ is represented by the height of each object.
- 2. Place a flat object like a piece of paper or a hardcover book, so that it touches the tops of your three objects. This surface is the best-fitting plane of Y predicted from $X_1$ and $X_2$.
- 3. Notice that the regression plane fits perfectly and uniquely: Any other placement of the plane creates a gap between the top of an object and its predicted value on the surface of the regression plane, which is residual.
- 4. Next, rearrange the three objects so that they are in a straight line in order of their heights.
- 5. By placing the marker on the objects. You will find that you can do so, but that there is no unique solution: The regression plane can be rotated around the line that connects the tops of the three objects. That is a physical demonstration of multicollinearity, because the value of $X_2$ can be perfectly predicted from $X_1$, and vice versa.
- 6. Finally, shift the line of objects from step 4 to one side of the table (call it $X_1$). As you rotate the regression plane, notice that the slope of the plane along $X_1$ is invariant because it is determined by the relative heights of your objects. In contrast, the slope along $X_2$ (the perpendicular side of the table) changes as the plane rotates: It is sometimes positive, negative, and occasionally zero. This is one possible consequence of multicollinearity: The regression coefficients are underdetermined and, under some conditions, may change sign.

### 22.5.1 Measures of Multicollinearity

Two measures are useful for diagnosing multicollinearity problems: The tolerance and its reciprocal, the variance inflation factor (VIF). Tolerance is a measure of how non-redundant a predictor is with the other predictor variables. With only two predictor variables, the tolerance is 1 minus the square of the correlation between the two predictors. In general,

> tolerance of the $j^{th}$ predictor variable = $1 - R_j^2$.

$R_j^2$ is the square of the multiple correlation when $X_j$ is regressed on all the other predictor variables in the equation. In other words, the tolerance is a measure of how well $X_j$ can be predicted by the other predictor variable(s) in the equation.

The variance inflation factor is 1/tolerance; there is one VIF for each predictor. As you might expect from its name, the VIF provides an indication of the amount that the variance in each regression coefficient increased compared to an analysis in which all predictors are uncorrelated. We can see this by considering the standard error of the regression coefficients, which we defined in Equation 9.7 for the bivariate case:

$SE(b_1) = \frac{s_x}{\sqrt{SS_X}}$ where $s_x$ is the standard error of the estimate and $SS_X$ is the sum of squares of X. In multiple regression, the standard error of the coefficient of the $j^{th}$ predictor can be shown to be a function of its tolerance:

$SE(b_j) = \frac{s_x}{\sqrt{SS_j(1-R_j^2)}} = \frac{s_x}{\sqrt{SS_j}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{tolerance of } X_j}}$ (22.1)

where $SS_j = \sum(X_{ij} - \bar{X}_j)^2$ is the sum of squares of the $j^{th}$ predictor. From Equation 22.1, we can see that as the tolerance of $X_j$ decreases, the corresponding VIF increases, and so does the estimated SE. If the predictor $X_j$ has a tolerance of 0 (i.e., $R_j^2 = 1$), this means that $X_j$ can be perfectly predicted by a linear combination of the other predictors and is therefore redundant. Consequently, if any of the predictors have a tolerance of zero, we cannot obtain least-squares estimates of the regression coefficients because the set of equations that must be solved does not have a unique solution. In general, a test of the regression coefficient $b_j$ will have less power as the tolerance becomes smaller. This makes sense because the more $X_j$ is redundant with the other predictors, the less information it provides, and the less we expect it to contribute to the predictions of Y.

What values of tolerances or VIFs a multicollinearity problem? One commonly recommended rule of thumb is a VIF of 10 or more, equivalently, a tolerance of .10 or less indicates reason for concern. Cohen et al. (2003) make a strong case that these cutoff values are too liberal, as the negative consequences of multicollinearity can occur at much lower values of VIF. As they note, however, the problems stemming from multicollinearity are continuous in nature, and thus there is no easy decision to guide us.

The demonstration in Box 22.1 shows that one possible consequence of multicollinearity is a change of sign and/or a large change in magnitude of a regression coefficient as other predictors are included in the analysis. Thus, a comparison of the bivariate regression coefficient for each predictor along the corresponding coefficient in the multiple regression equation can provide an additional check on potential multicollinearity issues. Course the correlations among the predictors also provide some clues to potential problems. In short, the assessment of multicollinearity requires checking several aspects of the data and applying sound judgment.

### 22.5.2 Using Software to Obtain Measures of Multicollinearity

Statistical software provides diagnostic information that can be used to determine whether there is a potentially serious multicollinearity problem. In R, assume that the regression results from the lm function have stored in `reg_out`. Then the vif function in the [car] package `reg_out` returns a vector of variance inflation factors, for each predictor. Tolerances are simple `1 / vif(reg_out)`. In SPSS, set up the regression and click on the Statistics button, then check the box for Collinearity diagnostics. The resulting output will include tolerance and VIF for each predictor in the model.

### 22.5.3 Strategies for Dealing With Multicollinearity

Possible remedies have been suggested if we find that there is a multicollinearity problem in our data:

- Deleting some of the predictors that are responsible for the problem. If we have several different measures of the same latent variable, that may be appropriate. However, if we have several different kinds of measures that are highly correlated, this cure be worse than the disease. Deleting variables may result in specification errors and correlations between predictors and the error component themselves can have very serious sequences, as we discussed earlier. A better approach may be to combine predictors, as we describe next.
- Combining highly related predictor variables into new variables that represent common underlying factors. Decisions about which variables to combine should be based on theoretical considerations. Procedures such as principal components analysis and factor analysis can also provide suggestions about possible underlying processes, providing information about which variables might be combined. Roughly speaking, these approaches identify weighted combinations of predictor variables that account for systematic variability in Y. By evaluating the weights of the predictors, the factors can be interpreted as reflecting latent variables such as "verbal ability" or "self-esteem." Those methods are the scope of this book.
- Centering. This involves replacing each score by its standard deviation score; that is, the score mean of the variable. For example, when we regressed age and BMI data from women 20–65 with BMI scores not exceeding 40, the mean of the age scores was 46.928. To center the age variable, we would replace age with a new variable, age = age - 46.928. Centering can be useful in providing more interpretable regression coefficients (e.g., see Sections 22.7.2 and 22.8.4) and will also often reduce multicollinearity.
