## Integrated Analysis II

## 11.1 Overview

In this chapter, we review the material presented in the preceding chapters dealing with the analysis of data from between-participants designs. We introduce a hypothetical two-factor experiment, and then analyze the data, including exploratory statistics, the ANOVA, and follow-up tests. Finally, we discuss the results.

## 11.2 Introduction to the Experiment

The effects of the organization of factual material upon memory have been studied by several researchers. In one classic study (Myers, Pozdek, & Coulson, 1973), participants read a series of 25 sentences, each of which related the name of five countries with an attribute (e.g., climate, principal industry, language). The sentences were presented to participants in five paragraphs, with each paragraph on a separate page. For the Name organization group, a paragraph consisted of five sentences describing five attributes of a single country; each attribute was from a different category. For the Attribute organization group, each paragraph presented attributes for a single category; a page might state the different climates of the five countries, or the different languages. For the Random organization, the 25 facts were randomly placed, to a paragraph. Subjects read each paragraph for 40 seconds and after reading all five paragraphs, wrote down as many of the 25 facts they could. The Attribute organization resulted in significantly better recall than either of the two types of organization; there was little difference between the Name and Random results.

In a hypothetical follow-up study, we imagine using the same materials allowing time to produce perfect recall. Each of the three organizational groups is divided into three groups to be retested after a delay of 1, 2, or 3 days. With respect to the main effect of organization of the 25 facts, there are several possibilities: (1) the order of the Name, Attribute, and Random organization means may be the same as in the original; (2) when perfectly memorized, the differences among the three organizations may be eliminated in the delayed tests; (3) the groups that performed poorest in the original study (the Name and Random organizations) may do better than the Attribute group because participants are likely to spend more time.
