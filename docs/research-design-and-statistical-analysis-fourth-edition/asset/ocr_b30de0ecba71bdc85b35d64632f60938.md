# 22.6.1 Some Cautions

We emphasize that these interpretations are valid only in terms of the causal model given in Panel (b) of Figure 22.2. If our theory does not support this causal model, the best we can say is that our data are consistent with mediation but are also consistent with causal models as well, perhaps involving causality and additional variables. Also, we would like to have a statistical test for any mediation effect. One that is widely used is the Sobel test, but a limitation of the test is that it is not very powerful. MacKinnon, Lockwood, Hoffman, West, and Sheets (2002) offer a useful discussion of 14 methods for testing the statistical significance of a mediating effect. Finally, in any regression analysis, the results and interpretation may be distorted by measurement error. If, for example, the mediating variable $X_c$ is less than $X_{13}$, the direct effect will appear larger, and the mediating effect will appear smaller than they should. If we want to pursue serious testing of a theory that involves mediation, we should use multiple indicators of the latent variables and use structural equation modeling.

# 22.7 Testing for Curvilarity in Regression

# 22.7.1 Testing for Curvilarity using Continuous Predictor Variables

When we regressed *TC* on age and BMI (see Figure 20.7), we found that there were significant effects of both predictor variables. However, it is possible that the relationship is not strictly linear and that the prediction of *TC* scores would be better if we included curvilinear components of the predictors as well. A curvilinear relationship would simply mean that the function relating the predictor variable(s) and the dependent measure is not linear over the entire range represented in our data. It might be that *Y* increases linearly up to some point, and then gradually levels off or even decreases. Or, the predicted values of *Y* may be piecewise linear, that is, linear over a range of predictor values but with different slopes at different points. If there are only two different slopes, then we may be able to describe the relationship with a quadratic ($X^2$) component; if three distinct slopes (e.g., rising, falling, rising, or the reverse), then a cubic component ($X^3$) may be included in the model.$^2$
