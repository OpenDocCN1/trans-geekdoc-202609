In a study of basic arithmetic skills, Royer, Tronsky, and Chan (1999) collected both accuracy and response time scores from students in grades 1 to 8 for addition, subtraction, and multiplication. Table 2.1 present the perfect correction score for 28 students in second grade. What do we do with these numbers? Even with only 28 values, it can be hard to get a sense of how the students are doing. For example, is not easy to see all of the children are doing well learning addition, or if there are particular individuals who are lagging behind or need more change. Let's use different types of graphs to visualize the data. Many of these graphs are available in the Explore module of SPSS or from the Graphs menu. Similarly, basic plots can be generated in Excel, and We will focus on plotting using R because doing so is easy and the results are nicely customizable.

# 2.2.1 Histograms

One of the simplest graphical summaries of data is a histogram. Histograms plot the frequency of scores. Figure 2.1 presents a histogram for the data set of Table 2.1. In this graph, the label on the left y-axis (the ordinate) shows the number of scores represented by each bar, that is, the frequency. Histograms sometimes show the proportion of scores in each bin, rather than the frequency, which changes the y-axis but not the distribution of data. The x-axis (the abscissa) represents the addition scores, which have been divided into intervals, bins of five points each (e.g., 96-100, 91-95, etc.). The width of bins or equivalently, the total number of bins, can be adjusted to best capture the shape of data. The goal in constructing a graph of distribution is to summarize the important characteristics of the distribution. A summary involves data reduction and a corresponding loss of information; however, an accurate summary must retain any important information. Thus, researchers must exercise judgment in deciding on an appropriate degree of resolution (i.e., bin size) for a graph.

Once the histogram is constructed, important characteristics of the distribution should be more evident than they are from the list of scores in Table 2.1. It is clear in Figure 2.1 that most students performed well. Indeed, the greatest number of scores appears in the bin showing 96%–100% correct. However, it is also evident that a few students performed very poorly, and there is a gap of at least 10 percent points between the three lowest scores and the lowest of the remaining 25 scores. This gap is typical of many real data sets, as is the obvious asymmetry in the distribution. Miccer (1989) examined 440 distributions of achievement scores from the classic bell shape as asymmetry (skew) and “lumpiness” (more than one mode, the most frequently observed value). Similarly, after analyzing many distributions based on standard chemical analyses of blood samples, Hill and Dixon (1982) concluded that real-life distributions were “asymmetric, lumpy, and have relatively unique values” (p. 393).
