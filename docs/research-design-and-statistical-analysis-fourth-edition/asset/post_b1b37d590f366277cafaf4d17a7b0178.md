## 16.5.3 Estimates of General Omega-Squared ($\omega^2$)

The expected mean squares in Table 15.11 provide the basis for estimating $\omega^2$ for the delay and duration terms and their interaction. Because of the variance of the estimates are negative, they were set to zero. Table 16.8 contains the formulas for the estimates of the population variances and for the estimates of $\omega^2$. As in our analysis of the hierarchical design, it is again clear that only the duration of the videos has no effect on participants' ratings of unpleasantness.

## 16.5.4 Which Design?

Whether we consider $F$ tests or measures as $\omega^2$, the hierarchical and Latin square designs yielded similar conclusions, although the $\omega^2$ values are slightly larger in Table 16.8 than in Table 16.4. The similarity of results is not surprising because the analyses were based on the same data set. If we had run two experiments, using a different design in each, there would have been some differences in the two sets of rating data. This leaves the question of which design is preferable. Although there is no single answer, the following questions should be considered before choosing a design:

- 1. Will items contribute considerable variance? If this is likely, counterbalancing the assignment of treatments to item sets will permit the removal of much of the item variability, increasing the efficiency of the Latin square design.
- 2. Will treatments and items interact? If so, the between-cell residual is likely to be inflated by that interaction, reducing the efficiency of the Latin square design.
- 3. How many factor levels will there? Larger squares have more error degrees of freedom associated with the BCR and WSR terms, thus increasing the effectiveness of the Latin square design. Although we used a small square for our example, generally it is wise to use squares with at least five levels of the Latin factors.

If the Latin square is the design of choice, the data should be properly analyzed. We have found that in many, perhaps most, instances counteract the assignment of factor levels and then analyzed the data as if the design were a standard repeated-measures design, or a mixed design if there was a between-participants factor. If substantial sources of variance were neglected, ratios of mean squares may not be distributed as F and error rates may be increased.
