Once the estimates of population variances have been calculated, we can calculate $\hat{\omega}^2$. We will follow the notational conventions introduced for $\eta^2$ to distinguish three variants of $\omega^2$, namely, $\hat{\omega}$ without the subscript, $\hat{\omega}$ introduced in Chapter 8, $\omega^2_p$ denoting partial $\omega^2$, and $\omega^2$ denoting general $\omega^2$. We need only a slight revision of the rule we formulated for calculating an estimate of general $\hat{\omega}^2$.

The denominator of $\hat{\omega}^2$ includes the estimates of variances for the effect of interest, for the within cell error term, and for all intrinsic effects and their interactions.

Some examples clarify this rule. Assume that we have three extrinsic factors; say, format, instruments and time to test the material. Then, the estimate of general $\hat{\omega}^2$ for format is

```
$$\hat{\omega}^2(F) = \frac{\sigma^2_F}{(f-1)(MS_F - MS_{Scells})/N}{\sigma^2_F + \sigma^2_A + (f-1)(MS_F - MS_{Scells})/N + MS_{Scells}}$
```

Substituting either the previously calculated estimates of variances or mean of squares from Table 9.12, $\hat{\omega}^2(F)=227$.
Now assume the design of Table 9.11 with one factor, A, is intrinsic. In that case,

```
$$\hat{\omega}^2(F) = \frac{\sigma^2_F}{\sigma^2_F + \sigma^2_A + \sigma^2_{IA} + \sigma^2_{FIA} + \sigma^2_{error}}$
```

Now the estimated variance due to experience and all its interactions contributes to the denominator. Subinating numerical estimates,

```
$$\hat{\omega}^2(F) = 45.341 / (0 + 0.325 + 0 + .716 + 154) = .227$
```

Because A and its interactions contribute little variance in this particular experiment, the estimate is unchanged from when we assumed that all factors were extrinsic. In either case, Cohen's suggested guidelines (see Chapter 8) indicate that format has a large effect.
To summarize developments so far, we have presented an approach to computing measures of effect size that we endorse the approach advocated by Olejnik and Algina (2003) based distinguishing extrinsic and intrinsic factors or equivalently, manipulated and measured variables. Although this approach has not been widely adopted, general $\eta^2$ and general $\hat{\omega}$ do a better job of achieving the goal of comparability across experimental designs than the more conventional statistics of partial $\eta^2$ and partial $\omega^2$.
