Now that we have a numerical value for our test statistic

Once we have decided on a value of $\alpha$, we can establish a rejection region for our study (see Box 4.2). Because our alternative hypothesis is nondirectional, we will consider against the null hypothesis both sample means that are well below and sample means that are well above the hypothesized mean of 200. Thus, we need to determine the values of $z$ that cut off the lower 2.5% and the upper 2.5%. Turning to Appendix Table C.2, we find that 1.96 is excluded by .025 of the standardized normal curve; because the curve is symmetric,.025 of the area lies below -1.96. Thus if the null hypothesis is true, there is only .5% probability of obtaining a value greater than 1.96 or less than -1.96. Equivalently, we reject $H_0$ if the absolute value of $z$, $|z|$, is greater than 1.96. Obviously, the $z$ we calculated is much larger than 1.96 and we reject $H_0$. The obtained value of the test statistic, 8.53, falls well within the rejection region, so we can reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the TC level of males over 50 is greater than the recommended maximum of 200.

Recall from Chapter 4 that there is an alternative approach that we can use for testing hypotheses by computing the $p$-value of the observed result (see Box 4.1). The $p$-value is the probability that the value of the test statistic would be at least extreme as we actually obtained, if the null hypothesis was true. We reject the null hypothesis if $p \le \alpha$. Here, $p$ is $p(z > 8.53) + p(z < -8.53)$; to three decimal places, $p = .000$.
