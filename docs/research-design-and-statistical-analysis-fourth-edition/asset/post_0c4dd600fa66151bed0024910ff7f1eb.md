We developed a simple example of an experiment for the purposes of sketching the role of a sampling distribution in the process of evaluating a specific hypothesis. The experiment that we used – a four-trial experiment in which Rachel guessed among equally likely alternatives – is one specific example from a more general class of experiments. If we had chosen to do a 20-trial experiment and changed the experimental procedure to require Rachel to guess among 10 alternatives, it should be obvious that we would need to specify a new sampling distribution to derive the possible outcomes for such ambitious experiment. However, the approach to deriving the appropriate sampling distribution would be the same as that illustrated for our simpler experiment. We turn now to the task of generalizing our approach.

## 4.3 The Binomial Distribution

Figure 4 presents the probability distribution for the number of correct responses when there are four trials ($n=4$) and the probability of a correct response on each trial is .25 ($\pi = .25$). For different values of $n$ and $\pi$ we could always find the desired probabilities by drawing the appropriate tree diagram and applying the multiplicative and additive laws; however, using a tree quickly becomes tedious as the sample size, $n$, increases. It is helpful to have a general formula for the probability distribution given any combination of $n$ and $\pi$. Specifically, we are interested in developing a formula that will give us a way to derive the sampling distribution for any experiment with the following three characteristics:

- 1. On each trial there are two possible outcomes; examples might be correct/error, success/failure, head/tail, or live/die. The two outcomes on each trial might be referred to as $A$ and $\bar{A}$ with probabilities $\pi$ and $1-\pi$, respectively.
- 2. The value of $\pi$ is constant over trials.
- 3. Trials are independent. The probability of any outcome of any trial does not depend on the outcome of any other trial.

### 4.3.1 The Binomial Function

We want a formula for calculating the probability of $y$ as function of $n$ and $\pi$. We denote the binomial function $p(y; n, \pi)$ to indicate that it is the probability of obtaining $y$ responses of type $A$ when there are $n$ trials with $p(A) = \pi$ on each trial.

In determining the probability of each possible outcome, $y$, of a four-trial ESP experiment, we first identified each of the 16 possible sequences in the experiment. We found that the probability of any given sequence could be computed using the multiplication rule for independent events. Suppose we wish to find the probability of obtaining exactly three $A$ responses in four trials. For example, the probability of the sequence $\langle A, A, A, \bar{A}\rangle$ would be $\pi(\pi)(\pi)(1-\pi)$ or $\pi^3(1-\pi)$.

Further, we observed that there were three distinct sequences for most values of $y$. For example, there are four combinations with three $A$s and one $\bar{A}$, that $p(3; 4, \pi) = (\langle A, A, A, \bar{A}\rangle, \langle A, A, \bar{A}, A\rangle, \langle A, \bar{A}, A, A\rangle, \langle\bar{A}, A, A, A\rangle)$. The probability of three A responses and one A response in any order is the sum of the probabilities of these four combinations, or $4\pi^3(1-\pi)$. In general, to find the probability of having $y$ A responses, we calculate the probability of a combination having exactly $y$ responses and then multiply by the number of ways that outcome can occur.

The approach can be generalized to any value of $n$. If we assume the trials are independent, the probability of any specific combination of $y$ A responses and $(n-y)$ A responses is $\pi^y(1-\pi)^{n-y}$. The probability of exactly $y$ A responses and $(n-y)$ A responses is

$$p(y;n) = k\pi^y(1-\pi)^{n-y} \quad (4.1)$$

where $k$ is the number of combinations consisting of exactly $y$ A and $(n-y)$ A responses. We have just about a binomial function; we need a formula for $k$, the number of ways in which $y$ A and $(n-y)$ A responses can be combined. This number of combinations can be shown to be (see Appendix 4.1):

$$k = \frac{n!}{y!(n-y)!} \quad (4.2)$$

where $n! = (n)(n-1)(n-2)..(3)(2)(1)$ and $0! = 1$. Note that $\binom{n}{y}$ have the same value. Substituting $y=0, 1, 2, 3,$ and $4$ into Equation 4.2, verify that $k$ takes the values $1, 4, 6, 4,$ and $1$, respectively; these are the numbers of combinations that appear in Table C.1. Replacing $k$ in Equation 4.1 with the right side of Equation 4.2 yields this binomial probability:

$$p(y;n) = \binom{n}{y}\pi^y(1-\pi)^{n-y} = \frac{n!}{y!(n-y)!}\pi^y(1-\pi)^{n-y} \quad (4.3)$$

Equation 4.3 describes a family of distributions, with a different probability for each combination of $\pi$ and $n$. Table C.1 in Appendix presents binomial distributions for selected values of $\pi$ and $n$. We restrict our examples to those described by Table C.1 to save ourselves the trouble of computing binomial distributions from scratch. In Section 4.3.3, we will show how software can be used for these calculations.

Now we can calculate the probabilities of various outcomes Rachel was generating. If $n=20$ and $\pi=.25$, we can find the probability of say, seven or more correct responses. In Table C.1, we find the row that corresponds to $n=20$, and then we use the column corresponding to $p=.25$. Reading from the table, we see that $p(y=0)=.0032, p(y=2)=.0669$, and so on. These are the same values that would be obtained using Equation 4.3. We can find the probability of seven or more responses in several ways. One way is to find the probabilities for $7, 8, 9..20$ correct and add these numbers together, then subtract the sum from 1. The probability of getting 0–6 correct responses is $.0032 + .0211 + .0669 + .1339 + .1897 + .2023 = .6171$. So the probability of seven or more responses must be $1 - .6171 = .3829$.

Putting this all together, we see that a result of seven correct responses in 20 trials is not very convincing evidence that Rachel was doing anything other than randomly guessing on each trial because there would be better than two chances in 10 of getting least seven correct by guessing.

A fourth point to note Figure 4.2 is that the probability of getting a value of $Y/n$ close to $\pi$ as $n$ increases. This illustrates Bernoulli's theorem in action. ### 4.3.3 Using Software to Compute the Binomial Function

In 4.3.1 we calculated the probability of $Y$ successes out of $n$ independent trials, each with probability of success equal to $\pi$ (see Equation 4.3). We showed how Table C.1 presents specific binomial distributions for selected values of $\pi$ and $n$. The values, as well as their sums, can also be obtained with distribution calculators in software such as R. For example, in R, the probability of observing exactly $Y$ correct responses out of $n$ trials, each of which has a probability of $\pi$ occurring, is calculated by the `binom` in [stats] package. The function has three input parameters, one for $Y$ (called x), $n$ (called size), and $\pi$ (called prob). For example, `binom(x=1, size=4, prob=.05)` returns 0.17145, which when rounded to four decimal places, can verify the entry in Table C.1; Figure 4.3 shows this example.

```
> dinom(x = 1, size = 4, prob = .05)
[1] 0.17145
>|
```
