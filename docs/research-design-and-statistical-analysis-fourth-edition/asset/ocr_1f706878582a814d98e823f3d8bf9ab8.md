+   6. What happens when variables are changed? The Pearson correlation coefficient, in contrast to other statistics as the mean, standard deviation, covariance, and the unstandardized regression coefficient, is a dimensionless quantity. By this we mean that r is not expressed in any units; it is simply a number between -1 and +1. Remember that r is the average product of z scores, and that a z expresses in deviation units the location of a score in a distribution. The values of z do not change if we change the units in which we measure X and Y (i.e., if we multiply each by a constant and/or add a constant to the score, as when we change units from ounces to kilograms, or from degrees Fahrenheit to degrees Celsius). It follows that the correlation between two variables tells us nothing about the mean or variance of the variables. In contrast, changing units affect b, and the raw-score variability about the best-fitting lines.
Unfortunately, the fact that the correlation coefficient has no units is one possible reason for its popularity. When we use r or r² or any other standardized measure of effect size, we do not think in terms of units and therefore may not pay attention to our measures. What does a one-point difference on a seven-point anxiety scale mean? What does the difference between 6 and 7 on the 7 scale mean the same thing as the difference between 3 and 4? +   7. The correlation coefficient is not resistant to the effects of outliers; it is not robust. The value of the correlation coefficient can be greatly influenced by the presence of extreme data points. If there are extreme data points, it is important to identify them and, if possible, determine where they are extreme. Consider the large size of the squared prediction error for the least point in Figure 17.8(a). If we omit this data point, the correlation drops from .59 to .39. There are measures of correlation that are more resistant than the Pearson coefficient because they diminish the importance of extreme scores. An example is the Spearman rho coefficient for which the X and Y scores are first ranked, and then the ranks are correlated. We will have more to say about such measures in the next chapter.
