# 7.3 Method

# 7.3.1 Participants

The pharmaceutical researcher posted a request for volunteers on the bulletin board of a local center. Volunteers were assessed in individual sessions to ensure that they were not suffering from any form of dementia. The assessment included a brief test of memory, and questions about facts that were judged to be common knowledge (e.g., the name of the vice president of the United States). From the pool of potential volunteers, 50 were chosen, ranging in age from 65 to 75.

An important point is that participants volunteer for an experiment may differ in manner from people who choose not to volunteer. For example, self-selected volunteers may be younger, healthier, wealthier, or simply have more time available in their schedules. They may be better educated or concerned about their memory. We must remember these biases in our sample because they limit the population to which our results generalize.

# 7.3.2 Experimental Design

As discussed in Chapter 1, there are several design options to consider. We could employ a within-participants (correlated scores) design, testing the participants prior to their taking the memory pill and then testing again, later after taking the pill. The researcher decided against it, feeling that prior exposure to the situation might raise scores in the subsequent test, reducing the effect of the drug. Furthermore, a test-test design would necessitate constructing a second memory test that was equated for difficulty with the first one. Still another possible problem was that age-related memory decline might accompany the period between test and test, potentially masking any benefit of the pill. Finally, just knowing they were given a pill to aid memory might have motivated participants, producing an unwanted reluctance to the pill itself. Modifications of the test-test design could address these concerns; the researcher decided that the simplest procedure was to use a within-participants design, randomly assigning the volunteers to either an experimental pill condition or a control (i.e., placebo) condition. A single research assistant collected the data but did not know the condition to which each participant was assigned. As noted in Chapter 1, this experiment is a double-blind procedure because neither the participants knew who was in which condition.

Further modifications of these independent-groups design ensure that the assignment of participants to the groups had equal numbers of men, women, and nonbinary individuals would equate any effects to gender and facilitate investigating effects. A correlated-scores design in which participants were assigned so that the groups were equated for, or for the memory test given to all volunteers would reduce error variance. However, these variables could also be used in regression analysis after the data were collected (e.g., analysis of covariance; see Chapter 24), or in a larger, experiment if this pilot study yielded promising results. Another possible design would involve a combination of the correlated-scores and independent-groups design; controlling for any changes due to prior exposure to the test, or age-related memory decline. Although several of these designs, as well as others, might result in reduced error variance or greater control of nuisance variables that could bias the results, the experimenters opted for a relatively simple design for their pilot study.
