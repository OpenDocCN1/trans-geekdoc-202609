There are two caveats to keep in mind when using this approach to construct contrasts on subsets of observations. The second caveat is that the reported p-values are unlikely to reflect control of the family error rate. In general, the researcher must ensure that the appropriate criterion for significance has been applied, through other options in the software or, when comparing the test statistic against the appropriate contrast.

## 10.11 The Sum of Squares Associated With a Contrast

The $t$ used throughout this chapter provides one approach to testing hypotheses about contrasts. An alternative, equivalent test, is based on the idea that any contrast of the group means corresponds to a component of $SS_A$, the sums of squares for the variable, A. That component of squares, $SS_C$, will be distributed on $1 df$ and the contrast can be tested by dividing it by $MS_{S/A}$. We develop this approach now to emphasize the continuity between the analysis of variance and tests of contrasts.

Consider the square of the $t$ statistic as presented in Equation 10.5 for testing contrasts:

$$t^2 = \frac{(\sum w_i \bar{Y}_i)^2}{\sum \frac{w_i^2}{n_i}} = \frac{(\sum w_i \bar{Y}_i)^2}{MS_{S/A} \sum \frac{w_i^2}{n_i}}$$

The numerator, $(\sum w_i \bar{Y}_i)^2 / \sum \frac{w_i^2}{n_i}$, is distributed as a sum of squares on one degree of freedom. We then define this sum of squares by $SS_C$; that is,

$$SS_C = \frac{(\sum w_i \bar{Y}_i)^2}{\sum \frac{w_i^2}{n_i}} (10.20)$$

Then

$$t^2 = \frac{SS_C}{MS_{S/A}} (10.21)$$

but a mean of squares, we may construct an F test, so that

$$t_{a(n-1)}^2 = \frac{SS_{\phi}}{MS_{S/A}} = F_{1,a(n-1)} \quad (10.22)$$

Let's consider the contrasts in our memory experiment. The means of the four conditions are shown in Table 10.1, and the ANOVA and several contrasts are displayed in Table 10.4. Applying Equation 10.20, we obtain the sum of squares for each of these three contrasts. For the first contrast,

$$SS_{\phi_1} = \frac{[(3(6.5)+(-1)(12.1)+(-1)(10.7)+(-1)(10.5)]^2}{\frac{3^2}{10} + \frac{(-1)^2}{10} + \frac{(-1)^2}{10}} = 158.7$$

If we assume equal variances, we may construct an F test, using Equation 10.22: $F(1, 36) = 158.7 / 17.944 = 8.8442$. The square root, $\sqrt{8.8442} = 2.974$, equals the $t$ for this contrast shown in Table 10.4(c). Analogous calculations for the other contrasts in Table 10.4(b) yield $SS_{\phi_2} = 5.4$ and $SS_{\phi_3} = 9.8$. An important point is that the sum 173.9, $SS_A$ shown in Table 10.6. That is,

$$158.7 + 5.4 + 9.8 = 173.9$$
$$SS_A = SS_{\phi_1} + SS_{\phi_2} + SS_{\phi_3} = SS_A$$

Think of the sum of squares as a pie and each contrast as a piece of the pie. In the example of Table 10.4, the pieces do not overlap, and they account for the whole pie. Here, the contrast of the control group with the average of the three treatment conditions ($\psi_i$) is the biggest piece, accounting for over 90% (158.7/173.9) of the variability among the condition means.

Not every set of contrasts results in sums of squares that add to $SS_A$. In the memory experiment, the contrasts making up the set had a special property that resulted in their accounting for different, non-overlapping, portions of the variability, for independent pieces of the pie. When this is the case, the contrasts are said to be *orthogonal*. The maximum number of orthogonal contrasts in each case is equal to $a - 1$, the degrees of freedom of the treatment sum of squares. ## 10.12 Summary

This chapter developed the following points:

- Contrasts are specific comparisons on a set of means that allow researchers to pose detailed questions. They capture components of the variance we intend to analyze.
- Procedures for conducting hypothesis tests and constructing confidence intervals are straightforward in Chapter 6.
- In an experimental design of any complexity, there are many possible contrasts that might be of interest to a researcher. The probability that least one Type 1 error will occur in a set increases as a function of the size of the family. It is therefore important to control the probability of a Type 1 error across a family (i.e., FWE).
- Just as there are many types of human families, there are many kinds of families of contrasts. These include the family of comparisons prior to data collection; the family of all possible contrasts of members of group; the family of all comparisons of experimental groups with group mean; and the family of post hoc contrasts determined after viewing the data.
- Different methods have been developed for controlling the FWE, depending on the kind of family and on whether intervals are desired. Some of these methods involve the usual $t$ statistic, or its close relation, the Studentized range statistic, or when heterogeneity of variance is suspected – Welch’s $t$.

One reason for the many different procedures in Table 10.7 is that the control of Type 1 errors is influenced by considerations such as whether the assumption of homogeneity has been met and whether $ns$ are equal. It is paramount that a given procedure controls Type 1 errors at close to the nominal level.
