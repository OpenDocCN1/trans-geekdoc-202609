Assumptions about the distribution of the terms that were in Equation 13.2 are unchanged. The interaction effect associated with the $ith$ cell is defined as

```
$(\eta\alpha)_{ij} = (\mu_{ij} - \mu_j) - \eta_i - \alpha_j$
$= (\mu_{ij} - \mu) - (\mu_{i, \cdot} - \mu)$
$= \mu_{ij} - \mu_i, \mu_j + \mu$ (13.5)
```

Because the independent variable, A, is a fixed-effect variable, the sum of the interaction effects for any participant in an experiment equals 0; i.e., $\sum_j(\eta\alpha)_{ij}=0$. However, because participants are random-effects variable, and therefore only $n$ of the infinitely possible population effects at any level of A are sampled $(\eta\alpha)_{ij}$ rarely equal zero, although the expectation of all population values is zero; i.e., $E(\eta\alpha)_{ij}=0$. These properties of the $S \times A$ interaction effects have implications for the EMS, and therefore for the $F$ tests. We consider this matter.

### 13.5.2 Expected Mean Squares (EMS)

Table 13.3 presents the sources of variance (SV), degrees of freedom (df), and EMS under the additive and nonadditive models. The numerical values of the sums of squares and the mean squares are the same for the two models. Furthermore, in both cases, the Source of variance would be tested against $MS_E$. However, there is a difference between the two cases: The interaction variance, $\sigma_{SA}^2$, contributes to $MS_S$ and SA terms in the nonadditive case. Why does the SA variance contribute to $MS_{SA}$ but not to $MS_S$ the variance among the participant members? The answer lies in the distinction between fixed- and random-effects variables: using EMS, we will see that the random-effects variables tend to spread out, interacting with other variables and putting proverbial "fingers" in other parts of the variance pie. ## One-Factor Repeated-Measures Designs

### 13.5.3 Consequences of Nonadditivity

To briefly summarize, there are several consequences of nonadditivity:

- No proper F test. Given the EMS of Table 13.3, the source of variance would be negatively biased.
- Unbiased estimates of some effect size are usually biased because it is not possible to isolate $\sigma^2$.
- The sphericity assumption will usually be violated. This assumption, also called circularity, is an assumption about variances.
- The efficiency of the design is reduced because the interaction effects added “noise” to the data set.

## 13.6 The Sphericity Assumption

In order for $MS_A/MS_{SA}$ to have a F distribution, the data must meet an assumption that we did not encounter in between-participants designs. This is usually referred to as the *sphericity*, or *circularity*, assumption (Huynh & Feld, 1976; Rouanet & Lepine, 1970). In the following pages, we define and provide examples of sphericity; in Sections 13.6.2 and 13.11, we consider alternatives to the standard F test when the assumption is violated.
