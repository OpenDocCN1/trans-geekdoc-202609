# Answers to Selected Exercises

# Chapter 1

+   1.1 (a) The study is an experiment. Participants are randomly assigned to each condition.

+   (b) The sample might best be characterized as a random sample of (presumably) overweight volunteers. The volunteer aspect is important because it's not clear that the effects of the diets would be the same if used by individuals less motivated to lose weight.

+   (c) One alternative approach is to observe individuals who have selected a particular diet. This might be done by advertising for individuals who are on the diet of interest, requesting volunteer as participants in a study and then simply assessing their weight. This is not as attractive an option as the experiment for several reasons. First, it is not an experiment, so conclusions about the causal role of diet in weight are not possible. Second, the researcher wouldn't have baseline information on weight and health prior to the start of the diet. Some concerns are that the targeted diets may not be equally represented in the volunteer sample, and individuals who choose different diets may differ systematically with respect to other factors that could affect weight.

+   1.3 (a) There are several factors other than long hours in day that may increase aggressive behavior. Two possibilities are that (1) parents might be more likely to make more aggressive children in day care to provide a socializing experience or for other reasons; (2) parents who are more stressed may foster aggression in their children and may place children in day care for longer hours to reduce their stress or allow them to work more/earn money.

+   (B) Ideally, a measure of aggressive behavior prior to the first enrollment in day care should be obtained. If variation in aggressive behavior as a function of hours/day manifests only after the first placement in day care, there is evidence that time itself has an effect. (Of course, this does not reveal whether the relation is due to the day environment or separation from parents and/or siblings.) If children who were more aggressive after placement in day care were also more aggressive prior to first placement, there is an indication that factors such as those listed in part (a) are involved. Other measures such as measures of parental stress and rated quality of the home environment may also prove useful. Using the regression procedures referred to in this chapter, and developed in Chapters 19 and 20, we can investigate whether such measures predict variability in aggressive behavior beyond that predicted by hours in day care.
