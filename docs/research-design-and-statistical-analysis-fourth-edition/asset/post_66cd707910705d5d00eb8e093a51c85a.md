## 15.4.1 Partitioning the Sum of Squares

To make the example in Table 15.6 more concrete, assume a hypothetical experiment in which 20 participants might quickly identify a target (e.g., a digit) in a noisy display. The independent variable is the number of distractors elements (e.g., Letters): $A=6, 8, 10$, or $12$. A block contains the same number of distractors for all 25 trials, although the target and distractor items change from trial to trial. The errors (incorrect or failures to respond within a time limit) in each block of 25 trials are the basis for the measures in Table 15.6. The errors increase as the number of distractor increases.

We may begin the process of partitioning the variance in the design by viewing it as a mixed design with one-participants factor of group and one-participants factor corresponding to the number of distractors, A. That is analyzed in the left panel of Table 15.7. We may confirm these results by analyzing the individual scores in the Web Tables file, Table $15.6\_LatinSqData$. However, our partitioning is incomplete because it fails to recognize the blocking variable, $B$, as a component of the experimental design. Because $B$ was systematically incorporated in the design, we can measure the variance associated with $B$ and the $Groups \times A$ ($GA$) interaction. This is easily accomplished by conducting a second mixed-factors ANOVA in which $B$ replaces $A$ as the treatment variable, producing the output summarized in the middle panel of Table 15.7.

The results of the two analyses are then used to obtain the partitioning summarized in the right panel of Table 15.7. In this analysis, we are able to distinguish not three, but four within-partitions sources of variability – the treatment variable, A; the blocking variable, B; a “between-cells residual” term as $(S_{GA} - S_{HB})$, or equivalently $(S_{GB} - S_{AB})$; and a term that we call “within-partitions residual” (WSR), which corresponds to SA/G from our original analysis or, equivalently, SB/G from our second analysis. The $df$ for the between-cells residual (BCR) term is assumed as the difference between the $df$ for the GA interaction and the $df$ for the main effect of $B$; that $df_{BCR} = df_{GA} - df_{B} = (a-1)^2 - (a-1) = (a-1)(a-2)$. The $df$ for the remaining terms are fixed in the usual fashion.

The right-most partitioning is what we recommend for this experimental design. An astute reader may wonder why the partitioning does not explicitly contain a treatments × blocks (AB) interaction. In fact, every possible combination of A and B is represented in the design, so it is possible to recover information about the interaction. However, there is a confounding effect in the design that prevents us from identifying a single term representing the AB interaction. Groups may differ because each represents a different subset of assignments; that is, a subset of AB interaction (see Exercise 15.8). This accounts for $(a-1)$ of $(a-1)(a-1)$ associated with the interaction. The remaining AB variability is embedded in the BCR source. A simple demonstration of this is to show that the sum of squares for AB is divided between the G and BCR sources from Table 15.6.

$SS_{AB} = 5 \times [(7 - 8.425)^2 + (10 - 8.425)^2 + \dots + (8 - 8.425)^2] - 86.65 - 76.85 = 86.45$

$SS_G + SS_{BCR} = 33.75 + 52.20$, which also equals 86.45. Furthermore, we view the Groups and BCR sources as capturing some of the AB variability. The AB interaction is confounded with the Groups and BCR sources. With the partitioning of the variability established, we are now able to consider alternative models of the data, corresponding expected mean squares, and F results.

## 15.4.2 Expected Mean Squares and F Tests

We assume the following model for the replicated-square design:

$Y_{ijkm} = \mu + \eta_{ijm} + \alpha_j + (\beta)_{jk} + \varepsilon_{ijkm} \quad (15.3)$

where $i$ indexes the participant within a row ($i=1, \dots, n$), $j$ indexes the effect of $A$ ($j=1, \dots, a$), $k$ indexes the level of $B$ ($k=1, \dots, b$), and $m$ indexes the row within the group ($m=1, \dots, a$). The population effects, $\eta_{ijm}$, are assumed to be independent and normally distributed with mean zero and variance $\sigma^2_{S/G}$. The effects of $A$, $\alpha_j$, are assumed to be fixed. $B$ may have fixed or random effects, the AB interaction effects, $(\beta)_{jk}$, are distributed independently normally with mean zero and respective variances $\sigma^2_B$ and $\sigma^2_{AB}$.

The expected mean squares for the replicated design are presented in Table 15.8. Note that there are two panels: one containing the EMS when $B$ is assumed to have fixed effects and another when $B$ is assumed to have random effects. In both panels, the AB interaction variability contributes to Group and BCR expectations, as we showed.

In discussing the partitioning of squares of squares, for this reason we use the label (AB)' as a second designation of those sources; this designation is operational when we apply the rules of thumb to determine EMS. Note that the AB interaction contributes to A source of variance only when B has random effects, the rules of thumb dictate. This is the reason for the difference between the two panels in Table 15.8.

In the detection experiment in which the number of distract elements was varied and counterbalanced over blocks of trials, it is reasonable to assume that trials have fixed effects. In case, the expected mean squares are in the left panel of Table 15.8 and the error term for testing A (number of elements) and B (trial blocks) are the same error term in the mixed designs of Chapter 14 (remember that $SS_{WSR} = SS_{S/G} = SS_{B/G}$). This would be the case for any design in which the blocking is assumed to have fixed effects.

In other experiments, recall scores might be obtained for lists that have been studied for various amounts of time, comprehensibility ratings might be recorded for sets of pictures varying in syntax, or brain activity might be recorded for emotional content. In these examples, the blocks in Table 15.6 represent sets of stimuli. It is reasonable to assume the stimuli were randomly sampled from some large population and randomly divided into several sets. Suppose each group has a different pairing of set with study time or emotional content, with the pairings arranged so that the design forms a Latin square, as in Table 15.6. The appropriate error term for testing A (e.g., study time or emotional content) is the BCR source.
