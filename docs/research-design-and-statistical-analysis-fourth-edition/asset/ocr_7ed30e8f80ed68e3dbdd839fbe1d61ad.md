# Control by Measurement

Often, blocking is not practical. Morrow and Young (1997) studied the effects of exposure to literature to the reading scores of third-graders. Although reading scores were obtained before the start of the school year (pretest scores), the composition of the third-grade classes was established by school administrators prior to the study. Therefore, the blocking design was not possibility. However, the pretest score could still be used to reduce error variance by removing that portion of the dependent variable (i.e., the posttest score) that was predictable from the pretest score. This adjustment, called analysis of variance, is thus a statistical means to reduce error variance, as opposed to the design approach of blocking. (Analysis of variance is discussed in Chapters 12 and 24).

Generally, the efficiency that comes with more complicated designs and analyses has a cost. For each, additional information is required for both blocking and the analysis of variance. Furthermore, the appropriate statistical analysis associated with more efficient approaches is usually based on assumptions about the nature of the data. In this view, major theme of this book is that there are many possible designs and analyses, and many considerations among them. We would like to select our design and method of data analysis with the goals of minimizing potential confounds and reducing error variance as much as possible. However, our decisions in matters may be constrained by the resources that are available and by the assumptions that must be made about the data. Ideally, the researcher should be aware of the pros and cons of the different designs and analyses and the tradeoffs that must be considered in making the best choice.

# Control by Sample Size

The final strategy for dealing with error variance is the choice of sample size. The size of the sample does not actually affect the individual error variance in the design; however, it does influence the amount of error in estimates of the variables. As sample size increases, estimates of effects become more precise; as a consequence, the power to detect the effects increases. Thus, in general, increasing sample size increases statistical power in any design. The relationship between sample size and power is well defined for a given design, so we can and should use that relationship in planning how many observations to make in any experiment or observational study. Sometimes, we find that sample size required to achieve a certain level of power is impractical. However, it is better to know before we begin a study that we can modify our plans by revising our design or choosing less, variable measures.

# 1.7 Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses should be performed after the data are collected, of course. However, the analyses should be planned before the data are collected, as part of evaluating the adequacy of the research design. Indeed, some researchers argue that the analyses should be recorded (even "preregistered") before the data are collected to reduce the number of choices available to the analyst once the data have been viewed, because those choices can inflate the probability of reaching erroneous conclusions.
