# 5.5.3 A Test of the Null Hypothesis

We originally asked whether the mean of the sampled population of TC differed from a value of 200. The confidence interval limits we calculated suggests the answer is “yes”. We reason as follows: First, we have 95% confidence that the computed interval, has the limits 219 and 230, contains the population mean. Second, that the interval does not contain 200. Thus, we conclude with 95% confidence that the population mean TC score differs from 200.

Many researchers skip calculating the confidence interval and, instead, directly test the population mean the theoretical value. We believe this is a mistake because it disregards the question, Is the mean? Nevertheless, the practice of hypothesis testing is widespread. Furthermore, the presence of the test permits us to address related concepts as Type 1 and Type 2 errors. For these reasons, we will use the standardized normal distribution to test whether μ differs from 200. In Section 5.7, we will present a more detailed discussion of the relation between the confidence interval and the significance test.

When we introduced the logic of hypothesis testing in Chapter 4, we emphasized that the logic of the procedure is the same for any situation (see Boxes 4.1 and 4.2). The first step is to establish a null and an alternative hypothesis; these are designated H₀ and H₁ respectively. Of course, our hypotheses in Chapter 4 concerned the probability of an event, whereas we are now concerned with the value of a population mean. Letting μ_TC represent the mean of the population of TC scores, the null hypothesis is that μ = 200 and is stated as

> H₀ :μTC = 200

and the alternative hypothesis is

> H₁ :μTC ≠ 200

Once these two hypotheses have been formulated, we need a test statistic whose will enable us to decide between them. We will estimate the population mean with the mean TC from our sample, and convert the sample mean to a z score. The z statistic will serve as our test statistic. Because the sample mean is normally distributed for a large sample, the sampling distribution for our test statistic, z, is also normally distributed.

Recall the general form of the z statistic (Equation 5.9): z = [V - E(V)]||. To test whether the mean TC score is significantly different from H₀ (μ_pop), we replace E(V) by ŶTC(V) of the population mean specified by H₀ (μ_pop), and σ_TC by the SE. Consequently, we have

```
z = \frac{\widehat{Y}_{TC}-\mu_{pop}}{\sigma /\sqrt{n}}} (5.12)
```
