# Consistency

Again, let $\hat{	heta}$ be some estimator of $\theta$. $\hat{	heta}$ is a consistent estimator of $\theta$ if its value is more likely to be arbitrarily close to $\theta$ as $n$ increases. A familiar example is the sample mean of $n$ independently distributed scores. From Appendix 5.1, $\sigma_{\hat{\theta}}^2 = \sigma^2/n$; therefore, the sampling variability of $\hat{	heta}$ must decrease as $n$ increases. Because inferences based on consistent estimators are more likely to be correct as sample size increases, consistency is an important property of an estimator.

# Relative Efficiency

The third major criterion for selecting an estimator is the variance of the sampling distribution of that estimator, particularly compared to the variance of an alternative estimator. The less variable the sampling distribution of the estimator, the more likely it is that any single estimator will have a value close to that of the population parameter. As you might expect, the relative efficiency of two estimators, $\hat{	heta}_1$ and $\hat{	heta}_2$, can be expressed as a ratio:

```
$RE_{1 \text{ to } 2} = \frac{E(\\hat{	heta}_2 - \theta)^2}{E(\hat{	heta}_1 - \theta)^2} \quad\quad\quadquad (5.4)$
```

is a measure of the efficiency of the estimator in the denominator, $\hat{	heta}_1$, relative to that in the numerator, $\hat{	heta}_2$.

# 6.2.5 Which Estimator?

Most of the estimation and hypothesis-testing procedures presented in statistics texts, make use of the sample mean, and the unbiased variance estimator, $s^2$. If the population from which the data are drawn has a normal distribution, these statistics will be efficient relative to their competitors. Consequently, estimates based upon them are more likely to be close to the true value of the parameter being estimated, and hypothesis tests likely to lead to correct inferences.
