The Dunn–Bonferroni interval is narrower than the Scheffé interval in Box 10.8, revealing the price we pay in precision of estimation and power when contrasts are not. Whenever possible, it is a good strategy to plan all contrasts that might conceivably be of interest, and then use the Dunn–Bonferroni or Fisher-Harter method. Although the power of these methods decreases as the contrast increases, a larger number of comparisons must be planned before the Scheffé criterion (see Perlmutter & Myers, 1973, for a more detailed comparison of the Dunn–Bonferroni and the Scheffé methods).

Experimenters who used both the standard ANOVA tests and the Scheffé procedure have sometimes seen to find that the null hypothesis is rejected by the ANOVA test but no contrasts are significant by the Scheffé criterion. The source of apparent contradiction is that the overall F has the same power as the maximum possible contrast tested by the Scheffé procedure. That contrast be of little interest, so it may not have been tested. It could be something like $(11/37)\mu_1 + (26/37)\mu_2 - (17/44)\mu_3 - (28/44)\mu_4$. In summary, rejection of the null hypothesis indicates that at least one contrast is significant by the Scheffé criterion, there is no guarantee that any other contrast will be significant.
