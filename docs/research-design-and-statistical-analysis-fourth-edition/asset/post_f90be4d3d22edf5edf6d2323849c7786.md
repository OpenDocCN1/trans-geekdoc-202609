Sometimes the researcher may have a priori hypothesis specified that only certain comparisons, pairwise or more complex, would be significant. In that case, the Dun-Bonferroni method should be followed. ### 10.1.2 Testing Hypotheses By Simple Effects

Having a significant difference between the marginal $B_1$ and $B_3$ means, we may ask whether this difference is significant in either or both age groups. Also, is it possible that there are other significant differences in one of the two age groups. Furthermore, a reasonable next step is to compare the draws at each level of $A$. The issue is familywise error rate will be acceptable. We view the set of contrasts as a single family and recommend that the FWE at each level of $A$ be .05 divided by the number of levels of $A$. In the example Table 10.5, pairwise comparisons within each of the two age groups were tested with the critical value of $q$ at the .025 level. Appendix Table C.8 contains .01, .05, and .10 values for the Studentized range, both the Tukey and TukeyHSD functions in R allow selection of an alpha-level. The required value of $q$ is 4.196 when $a=4$ and the error $df=40$.

It may seem that the first step would be to perform an omnibus F test at each level of $A$. However means is a test of the omnibus hypothesis that all means are equal. Therefore, we proceed directly with pairwise comparisons at each level of A. Assuming that the population variances are homogeneous, the critical difference is the standard error (across all eight cells) times the critical value of $q$; i.e., $d_{crit} = 2.5 \times 4.196$, or 10.49. At $A_j$, the difference between the $B_i$ and $B_j$ means, which the $B_i$ and $B_j$, are the largest, but neither exceeds the critical value. Accordingly, we cannot reject the hypothesis that the $B$ populations at $A_j$ are equal. At $A_i$, however, there are significant differences: the $B_1$ mean differs significantly from the $B_i$ and the $B_j$. Our analysis of the simple effects of the drugs has provided important information. Although we cannot be sure that differences among the drugs do not effect (because we cannot accept the hypothesis), we have found that in the second group drug $B_1$ is clearly superior to drugs $B_i$ and $B_j$.

There may be circumstances where only some of the possible contrasts within each level are of interest. For example, assume we have $a priori$ hypotheses about three contrasts at $A_i$. In that case, the Dun-Bonferroni procedure with $K = 5$ is appropriate; assuming the contrasts are equally important, each test would be performed with $\alpha = .01$. One of the contrasts was $a priori$ more than the others, the FWE could be distributed unequally (e.g., with $\alpha = .04$ for that contrast and .025 for each of the other). On the other hand, assume that the same contrasts appear to be of interest only when the means cell. In that case, the Scheffé procedure described in Box 10.8, is appropriate with $df_c = (a-1)(b-1)$.

### 10.10.3 The Relation Between Interactions and Tests of Simple Effects

It is a common misconception that simple effects are performed only to help us understand the causes of a significant interaction. Simple effect tests sometimes useful for understanding interactions, particularly differences among means are significant at one level of a factor but not others, as in our analysis of the means in Table 10.5. However the results of interaction and corresponding tests of simple effects are not always consistent. We may have a significant interaction but fail to find any significant tests of simple effects. The interaction may be significant even if there is no simple effect. These apparent discrepancies are due to differences in power of tests of interactions and simple effects due to differences in significance criteria and variances involved. A common result that sometimes puzzles researchers is illustrated by the following means:

| $B_1$ | $B_2$ |
|---|---|---|
| $A_1$ | 15 | 5 |
| $A_2$ | 8 | 6 |

Assume nine scores in a cell and an average cell variance of 10. Then, the $t$ test of interaction is

$t_{interaction} = [(15 - 5) - (8 - 6)] / (10\sqrt{4} / 9) = 1.20$

The interaction is not significant. However, if we compare the simple effects of $B$ at $A_1$ we have

$t_{B/A_1} = (15-5)/(10\sqrt{2}/9) = 2.12$

which is significant at the .05 level. The test at $A_2$ does not yield a significant result ($t=0.42$). Even controlling the FWE at .05 for each test contrasting simple effects, we conclude that there is a difference between the B means. We now have an apparent contradiction: the test of interaction does not provide evidence of an interaction in the population. However, the significant result of the test at $A_1$, coupled with a non-result of the test at $A_2$, suggests that there is an interaction with $B$ effects at $A_1$, but not at $A_2$. The reason for this inequality is that the standard error of the interaction involves the variance of differences among four means, whereas the standard error for the simple effect involves the variance of only two means. Therefore, it is possible to have a pattern of results for simple effects that is not consistent with the result of the test of interaction because the power of the test of sample is greater than the test of interaction.

### 10.10.4 Testing Hypotheses About Interaction

Various proposals have been made about the proper follow-up analyses to perform to understand a significant interaction (Gams, 1973; Marshall & Levin, 1972, 1973; Tukey, 1991). Remember that an interaction occurs when the simple effect of one variable differs across levels of another. So, a $2\times2$ interaction compares one simple effect across another. Thus, the most direct approach to understanding an interaction effect is to test the embedded $2\times2$ interactions. By bypassing all the $2\times2$ interactions, we can identify the simple effects that differ from one another.

For example, in Table 10.5, we test the interaction involving the two age groups and the $B, drugs$ groups. This interaction effect size is $(12+3)-(15+6)=-16$. Dividing this by its standard error, $\sqrt{MS_{error(a,b)}4}$, we have the $t$ statistic or squaring, we have the $F$. If this test has been planned, the alpha-level would be at .05. However, if such analyses are performed, they are typically post hoc. In that case, we have two options for controlling the familywise error. Assuming homogeneous variances, we can use Scheff’s method; we

$S = \sqrt{(a-1)(b-1)F_{error(a,b)}/(a-1)(b-1)}.$ In our example, $(a-1)(b-1)=3$ and $ab(n-1)=40$.

The F required for significance at the .05 level with 3 and 40 $df$ is 2.84. So $S=2.92$. An alternative is to use the simple $2\times2$ interaction contrasts as a family and use the Bonferroni correction. With the FWE at .05, the alpha-level for each test $t$ is .056, or .0083. Assuming a two-tailed test, the critical value of $t=2.78$, slightly smaller than the critical value of $S$. In this case its advantage will be it slightly more powerful, though as the number of interaction contrasts increases. When variances are homogeneous, as they are here, the choice between methods rests on a comparison of the critical values (see Perlmutter & Myers, 1973).
