Note that the two (2!) possible sequences of A and B, paired with the (3!) possible combinations of C, D, and E, correspond to one combination (A and B elected); C, D, and E not elected). In general, $r!(n-r)!$ will correspond to a single combination when $n$ items are split into one with $r$ items and one with $n-r$ items. Therefore, the number of multiplications is $n!/(n-r)!$. In our example, the number of ways the election can turn out

$$\left(\binom{5}{3}\right) = \left(\binom{5}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{120}{2!(6)} = 10$$

In general, the number of different ways of selecting $r$ items from $n$ items is

$$\left(\binom{n}{r}\right) = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$$

Read this as "$n$ choose $r$".

# Appendix 4.2

### Sample Size and Violations of the Independence Assumption

Assumptions often lead to erroneous inferences, sometimes (though not always) can be minimized by using large samples. The violation of the independence assumption in calculating probabilities provides a nice illustration of this point. Consider an urn containing five red and five black balls. We draw a marble three times from the urn. If we assume that the marble is replaced and the urn is thoroughly shaken after each draw, that we have independence, according to the multiplication rule, the probability of drawing three red marbles is $p(R_1, R_2$ and and $R_3) = p(R_1)p(R_2)p(R_3) = (5/10)^3 = .125$. However, suppose that the drawn marble is not replaced each time. This violates our assumption of independence. We can see this by following the analysis: If a red ball is drawn on trial 1, the probability of drawing a second red ball is now 4/9, whereas if a black ball is drawn on the first, the probability of a red on the second draw is 5/9. In fact, the probability of drawing a red (or black) ball on any trial depends on the sequence of preceding draws. Although our assumption of independence leads us to conclude that $p(R_1$ and $R_2$ and $R_3) = p(R_1)p(R_2|R_1)p(R_3|R_1, R_2) = (5/10)(4/9)(3/8) = .063$, roughly half the inferred probability.

# Developing the Fundamentals of Hypothesis Testing

Now suppose the urn consists of 50 red and 50 black balls. Our assumption of independence leads us to the same probability of drawing three red balls in a row, .125. This time, however, the true probability if we select without replacement is $p(R_i$ and $R_j) = (50/100)(49/98) = .121$, and the true and inferred probabilities are quite close; thus, the violation of the independence assumption did not lead to a very large error.

There are two implications of examples that extend beyond simple probability calculations and violations of the independence assumption. First, violations may lead to wildly incorrect conclusions, as the urn with 10 marbles attends. Second, the consequences of violated assumptions may be more damaging when the sample size is large. Neither of these statements will be true for every inferential procedure, but they are often true and therefore bearing in mind.
