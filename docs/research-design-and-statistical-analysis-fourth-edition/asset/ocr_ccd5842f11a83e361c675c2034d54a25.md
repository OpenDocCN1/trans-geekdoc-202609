# Research Design and Statistical Analysis

# Method 1

In considering the effect of A, we might decide to attribute to A only the variability uniquely associated with A. This is the variability in A that does not overlap with the other effects in the design and is represented by the area $a$ in the upper circle of Figure 23.6. It can be obtained from

```
$SS_{AC,AC} = (R^2_{Y,A,AC} - R^2_{Y,C,AC})SS_Y$
```

where the subscript on the SS term to the left of the equation is “A adjusted for C and AC.” When we adjust the sum of squares by removing the overlapping variability of all the main or interaction effects, what we call are Type III sums of squares. This is the default for SPSS, but not in R. You may wish to review Appendix 9.1, which describes how to obtain these different sums of squares.

# Method 2

An alternative approach is to adjust the A effect for the other main effect C, yielding the variability represented by areas t and w in the upper circle of 23.6,

```
$SS_{AC} = (R^2_{Y,A,C} - R^2_{Y,C,C})SS_Y$
```

where the subscript on the SS term to the left is “A adjusted for C.” In general, Method 2 adjusts any effect for all effects of the same order or lower order (e.g., main effects adjusted for other main effects, but not for interactions). This method is known as Type II sums of squares.

# Method 3

Finally, we may decide not to adjust for the contributions of the other effects at all. This yields

```
$SS_A = R^2_{Y,A}SS_Y$
```

In Figure 23.6, the overlap of the circles is ignored; the effect of A is treated as the sum of areas t, u, v, and w. Using the terminology of Chapter 9, this is Type Ia sums of squares.

It should be clear that the three methods of analyzing effects in nonorthogonal designs will not produce equal estimates of effects of A. Because A will account for variance in Y that is also accounted for by C and AC, $SS_{A}SS_{AC}$ and $SS_{AC,AC}$ will generally not be equal. For example, for the data in Table 23.3, $SS_{AC} = 4139.42$, $SS_{AC} = 3609.95$, and $SS_Y = 3,581.08$. Depending on the covariations with the effects are positive or negative, the adjusted sum of squares may be smaller or larger than if there were no adjustment.$2$
