# 13.6.2 Dealing With Nonsphericity

There are two data-analysis strategies that protect the researcher against inflation of Type 1 error rates due to nonsphericity. The first of these is the multivariate analysis of variance, or MANOVA, which is beyond the scope of this book. Harris (2001) and Morrison (2004) are two excellent sources on MANOVA. The second strategy for dealing with nonsphericity is a univariate F test with sphericity-adjusted degrees of freedom. We focus on this latter solution because it is effective and easy to apply.

When the assumption of sphericity is violated, the Type 1 error rate is inflated if the sphericity is biased. One way to control the Type 1 error rate in this situation is to conduct the F test with degrees that are adjusted. When the sphericity is violated, the mean of squares is still distributed as F, provided the numerator and denominator of freedom are multiplied by a factor, $\varepsilon$ (epsilon); that is, $df_1 = (a-1)$ and $df_2 = (a-1)(n-1)$. The value of epsilon ranges between 1, when the sphericity assumption is met, and $a-1$ when the assumption is severely violated. In general, as nonsphericity increases, epsilon and the degrees of freedom decrease, with the result that a larger value of F is required for significance. In this way, the epsilon adjustment compensates for the inflation of Type 1 error rate caused by the failure of the sphericity assumption.

One estimator of $\hat{\varepsilon}$ ("epsilon-hat»), was developed by Box (1954), and later extended to the mixed Designs of 14 by Greenhouse and Geisser (1959). The Greenhouse-Geisser $\hat{\varepsilon}$ tends to be conservative, providing a larger adjustment to the $df$. Subsequently, Huynh and Feldt (1976) developed a more liberal estimator, $\varepsilon$. Both estimators require calculations involving the elements of the variance-covariance matrix. Frequently, common statistical programs do these calculations.
