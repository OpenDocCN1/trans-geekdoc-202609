# 13.6.1 Sphericity Defined

Consider the typical repeated-measures design with n participants, each of whom is tested under a conditions. For each participant, we calculate (1/2)(a)(a-1) differences, based on the number of pairs of treatments. For example, when there are three treatments, we can construct three pairs and calculate the difference between the scores in each pair. We have done in Table 13.5. The left-most three columns of the table contain the scores for five participants at three levels of A, and the right-most columns contain all possible difference scores for each participant, with the value of the variances, $s^2_a$, at the bottom of the columns. These data fit the assumption of sphericity because the three values of the variances are the same; sphericity exists when the population variances of all possible difference scores are equal.^1. If this assumption is violated, Type 1 error rates will be higher than the nominal-level.

Nonnormality, or heterogeneity of difference scores, is similar in both form and consequences to heterogeneity in between-participants designs of Chapters 8-11. In Chapter 8, the error term, $MS_{error}$ was the average of the group variances. The null hypothesis is true and if there are n scores at each treatment level, $E(MS_i) = E$ even if the groups are very different from each other. Even though this heterogeneity of variance does not affect the ratio of expected mean squares, it affects the sampling distribution of the ratio of mean squares. More precisely, when the null hypothesis is true and group sizes are equal, heterogeneity inflates the probability of sampling large F values; the F test is always biased.

We have a similar situation in the repeated-measures design. The error term, $MS_{error}$, the relation between the difference scores and $MS_{error}$ will hold for any number of levels of A and will be true regardless of the values of $s^2_i$. Therefore, $MS_{error}$, and consequently the ratio of expected mean squares, will not be affected by differences in the variances of difference scores. However, the distribution of the F is affected and the Type 1 error rate inflated if the variances are very different, analogous to the case when group variances differ in the between-participants. A test of the sphericity assumption was derived by Mauchly (1940) and is available in many computer packages (for example, in SPSS's Repeated Measures analysis and as part of the anova_test function in R's [stats] package).
