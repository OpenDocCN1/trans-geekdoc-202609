In general, if data are MAR, analyzing complete cases will result in biased parameter estimates and invalid inferences.

+   2. Missing data on a variable Y is MAR if the probability of missing data is not associated with the value Y after controlling for the other variables in the data set. For example, missing depression scores would be MAR if men are less likely to respond to items on the depression scale than women, but the tendency to have missing depression is not associated with level of depression for either gender identity. If we use complete cases in our analyses, MAR missing data can also result in biased results, though the problem is not as severe as for, men to have lower depression scores than women and more men are dropped from analyses because missing data, the overall mean depression score will be higher because of the missing data.

+   3. Missing data on a variable Y is MCAR if the probability of missing data Y is unrelated to the value of Y or to the values of any other variables in the data set. Perhaps data were not collected on few trials because of a power outage. Or a participant did not show up for a session because of a crash accident. In our illustration, missing depression scores would not be more likely to occur for more depressed or less depressed individuals, nor for one group or another. Participants with complete data can be thought of as randomly selected from the original sample. If we analyze only complete cases, the MCAR missing data results in less power, but no bias.

If the missing data is MAR or MNAR, the pattern of missing observations may provide useful information about our research. We can code the missing data by Is and then look to see whether there are systematic tendencies for the missing data to be associated with particular values on the other variables. SPSS has a missing values module as optional add-on that is able to imput missing scores for a variety of assignments. Similar functionality is available in R (e.g., in the [mice] package); see Gomila and Clark (2022) for clear examples and solutions to missing data. In addition, stand-alone, free software is also available for imputing data; for example, Norm (Schafer, 1999), which can be downloaded, along with some documentation, from https://scholarps.psu.edu/resources/156307cb-2a99-488b-b069-a4fed633. Of course, the best solution to solve the problem of missing data is to design the test materials and collect complete procedures in ways as responsive as possible.

In addition to the availability of procedures to estimate missing scores, a set of statistical procedures have been developed to deal with designs that produce correlated scores. These procedures are called depending on the context, mixed-effects, random coefficient, multilevel, or hierarchical linear models. Good discussions may be found in several books and articles (e.g., Baagen, Davidson, & Bates, 2008; Luke, 2004; Quené & den Bergh, 2004; Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002).

Readers interested in pursuing the topic of missing scores can find several excellent references. Cohen et al. (2003) has a readable chapter on the topic, and Allison (2002) offers an excellent, more comprehensive introduction. Two standard, advanced texts on the problem of missing data are Little and Rubin (1987) and Schafer (1997).
