
The design of the research is a plan for data collection. We can minimize the effects of error variance by choosing a design that permits us to calculate the contribution of one or more nuisance variables. In the data analysis, that error variance is subtracted from the total variability in the design. Many of the design variations enable the researcher to equate treatment conditions for one or more nuisance variables, ensuring that those variables are not responsible for any differences that are found between conditions.

For independent variables, even greater efficiency is achieved if we test the same participant at each level of the independent variable. This variant of blocking is known as a repeated-measures design. A common example occurs in studies of forgetting in which measures are obtained at various times after the material was studied. The advantage of repeated-measures designs is that participants can be treated as another independent variable in the statistical analysis, thus removing from the data variability due to differences between participants. Ideally, the remaining error variance will be due to errors of measurement caused by random fluctuations over time factors such as the participant's attentiveness, the difficulty of the stimulus, and the temperature in the room. Thus, the repeated-measures design is a particularly effective way to minimize error variance, although it is not without its drawbacks. The major drawbacks of the design are the possibility of systematic biases due to time-related effects (e.g., practice and fatigue) and the possibility of carryover effects (i.e., influences of participation in earlier conditions on performance in subsequent conditions). (A full treatment of this design is presented in Chapters 13–16.)

# Control by Measurement

Often, blocking is not practical. Morrow and Young (1997) studied the effects of exposure to literature to the reading scores of third-graders. Although reading scores were obtained before the start of the school year (pretest scores), the composition of the third-grade classes was established by school administrators prior to the study. Therefore, the blocking design was not possible. However, the pretest score could still be used to reduce error variance by removing that portion of the dependent variable (i.e., the posttest score) that was predictable from the pretest score. This adjustment, called analysis of variance, is thus a statistical means to reduce error variance, as opposed to the design approach of blocking. (Analysis of variance is discussed in Chapters 12 and 24).

Generally, the efficiency that comes with more complicated designs and analyses has a cost. For each, additional information is required for both blocking and the analysis of variance. Furthermore, the appropriate statistical analysis associated with more efficient approaches is usually based on assumptions about the nature of the data. In this view, major theme of this book is that there are many possible designs and analyses, and many considerations among them. We would like to select our design and method of data analysis with the goals of minimizing potential confounds and reducing error variance as much as possible. However, our decisions in matters may be constrained by the resources that are available and by the assumptions that must be made about the data. Ideally, the researcher should be aware of the pros and cons of the different designs and analyses and the tradeoffs that must be considered in making the best choice.

# Control by Sample Size

The final strategy for dealing with error variance is the choice of sample size. The size of the sample does not actually affect the individual error variance in the design; however, it does influence the amount of error in estimates of the variables. As sample size increases, estimates of effects become more precise; as a consequence, the power to detect the effects increases. Thus, in general, increasing sample size increases statistical power in any design. The relationship between sample size and power is well defined for a given design, so we can and should use that relationship in planning how many observations to make in any experiment or observational study. Sometimes, we find that sample size required to achieve a certain level of power is impractical. However, it is better to know before we begin a study that we can modify our plans by revising our design or choosing less, variable measures.

# 1.7 Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses should be performed after the data are collected, of course. However, the analyses should be planned before the data are collected, as part of evaluating the adequacy of the research design. Indeed, some researchers argue that the analyses should be recorded (even "preregistered") before the data are collected to reduce the number of choices available to the analyst once the data have been viewed, because those choices can inflate the probability of reaching erroneous conclusions. Gelman and Loken (2014) referred to this as the "garden of forking paths"; p-hacking (Simmons, Nelson, & Simonssohn, 2011). The analysis of data broadly consists of two phases:

(1) an exploratory or descriptive phase, in which measures of central tendency (e.g., means, medians), variability, and shape of distributions should be calculated and graphed; and (2) an inferential phase, in which population parameters are estimated and hypotheses about them are tested.

Too often, researchers neglect the first, exploratory phase. However, the descriptive statistics are the results of an experiment and they therefore deserve particular attention. Attention begins during the planning phase. Research plans should include decisions about what descriptive statistics will be calculated and what plots of the data will be used to view. The researcher should imagine, for example, patterns of means across conditions, and what different patterns would reveal the behavior being studied. We will present the reasons for, and examples of, exploratory analyses in the next chapter. Now, we note that such analyses may suggest additional hypotheses and may inform us about potential problems for the statistical analyses to be carried out in the next phase.

The goals of the research will often dictate various levels of the independent variable, are there comparisons between levels that are of particular interest? Or, if the independent variable is quantitative, is it of interest to examine features of the function relating dependent and independent variables, such as whether it is represented by a straight line or a curve? There specific questions should be considered before the data are collected for two reasons. First, considering specific questions before the data are collected ensures that the design allows us to answer those questions. Second, inferences based on tests of hypotheses formulated before the results have less likelihood of yielding erroneous conclusions from tests of hypotheses suggested by the observed results.

The power of the statistical test should be an important consideration in planning the research. Estimates of effect size and error variance obtained from related or pilot studies, with a desired level of power to detect effects of a minimum size, should determine the amount of data collected. Just how this is done is considered in several chapters in this book, and computer software is provided on the website for the book to carry out the calculations.

Finally, it is useful to specify the inferential tests in design to make that the experimental design has been thoroughly evaluated and all potential sources of variability have been identified. This practice will help guard against oversights in planning the design. For example, an educational researcher planning an experiment to compare two methods of teaching science might assign two classes of students at random to a lab-based teaching method and two other to a text-based method. However, this plan is flawed because it neglects an important influence on performance; namely, individual classes may differ in performance regardless of their assignment to different teaching methods. An implication of this observation is that the research plan should include many than four classes so that variability due classes may be adequately evaluated.
