Two other properties of the mean, proven in Appendix A, may help convey the sense in which it reflects the central tendency of a data set:

+   1. The mean is the balance point of the data in the sense that the sum of the deviations about the mean is zero; $\Sigma(Y-\bar{Y}) = 0$.
2. The sum of squared deviations of scores from the mean is smaller than the sum of squared differences from any point other than the mean; that is, $\Sigma[(Y-\bar{Y})^2]$ has its smallest value when $k=0$.

Many of the advantages of the mean as a descriptive statistic are attributable to the fact that every value in a set of scores is incorporated into the calculation of the mean. However, the mean has a potential weakness that derives from this same fact. Namely, a few extreme scores can bias the value of the mean and may result in the mean taking on a value that does not typify the location of the data. We gave an example in this chapter where a single outlier had a substantial effect on the mean. In such a situation, the median the better description of location because its value is affected only by score ranks, not their individual values. Because the median changes little when values of individual scores are changed, we may say the median is a resistant or robust statistic. Resistance is a desirable property in a statistic, but it is not a property of the mean. We will find that this fact has several implications for our analyses of mean when we use inferential tests.

# 2.3.2 Measures of Variability: Variance, Standard Deviation, and Standard Error

One way to measure the spread or variability of a set of scores is to compute its variance. The sample variance, $S^2$, is computed as $\Sigma(Y-\bar{Y})^2/n$. The information summarized by this formula. First, note that the calculation is based on a measure of the discrepancy of individual scores from the average score, $(Y-\bar{Y})$. This makes the variance a nice complement to the mean. Also, like the mean, the variance incorporates the value of each individual score and, thus, the variance possesses some of the statistical properties we observe in the mean. Second, the squaring of the deviation scores is necessitated by the fact that $\Sigma(Y-\bar{Y}) = 0$, as we observed in Section 2.3.1. Finally, the squared deviations are summed and divided by $n$, the final value represents the squared deviation of scores about the mean.

Although the formula defining the sample variance involves division by $n$, we will divide by $n-1$, as most packages do. This results in a better estimate of the population variance, a fact that is used instead of $n$ because will be explained in Chapter 5. We defined this revised formula by $s^2$, rather than $S^2$, to indicate that we are using $n-1$. Our formula for $s^2$ is:

```
$s^2 = \Sigma(Y-\bar{Y})^2/(n-1)$
```

Generally, $s^2$ will be computed with the aid of a calculator or statistical package; however, we illustrate its calculation with a simple data set to make sure that the formula is understood. For the following set of scores, $Y=[1, 2, 3, 5, 9, 10, and 12]$, the sum of the scores is 42, and therefore, $\bar{Y}=42/7=6$. The deviations from the mean are $(Y-\bar{Y})=-5, -4, -3, -1, 3, 4, and 6. Squaring these deviations, $(Y-\bar{Y})^2=25, 16, 9, 1, 9, 16, and 36.
