Table 12.3 presents the results of two analyses: An analysis of variance in which the preset scores are not relevant, and an analysis of covariance in which the preset scores are used to account for some of the variance in the original data set. Note that the adjusted sum of squares and sum of squares in the ANCOVA are smaller than the adjusted terms in the ANOVA. These reductions can be explained by correlation between the X and Y. The adjusted sum of squares is portions of the total of squares that cannot be predicted from X. Although we do not actually calculate the terms in Table 12.3, we could obtain the adjusted sum of squares (3,614 in our example) by firstly r, the correlation between all X and Y scores. The square of r is the proportion of the variance of the Y scores that are predictable from knowledge of X (see Chapter 24). Therefore, the proportion of the sum of squares that is not predicted by X is 1 - r², and the amount of total variance not predicted is $SS_{totaladj} = (1 - r²)SS_{total}$. In our example, $r^2 = .2415$ and $(1 - .2415) 	imes 4764.7$, or 3613.9. This equals the adjusted total sum of squares in the ANCOVA results of Table 12.3. Similarly, the ANCOVA error variance is adjusted depending on the within-condition correlation of the covariate and dependent variable; as a result, the error term is reduced relative to ANOVA.

Chapter 24 presents ANOVA as a special case of multiple regression. Now, the important point to understand is the increased efficiency of the covariance analysis is a function of the variability in Y predictable from X that would otherwise show up in the error term of ANOVA. This reduction is achieved with the loss of only one degree of freedom, but the cost of more complexity and more assumptions.

# 12.4.2 Comparing ANOVA to the CR and T × B Designs

We have indicated that when its assumptions are met, ANOVA can provide more power than the CR analysis. But how does ANOVA compare with analyses based on blocking? One simple answer is ANOVA “costs” only one degree of freedom, whereas a blocking design is more costly as the number of blocks increases. As stated earlier, Feldt (1958) derived the optimal number of blocks to be used in the blocking design. Using numbers of blocks, he concluded that the one-ANOVA was always more efficient than a treatments x blocks design. However, the differences were often small, especially for larger sample sizes (N ≤ 50) and as we will see in Chapter 24, ANCOVA comes with some stringent assumptions that the blocking design avoids.
