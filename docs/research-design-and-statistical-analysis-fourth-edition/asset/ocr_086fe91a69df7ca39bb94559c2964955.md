# 8.4.2 Degrees of Freedom (df)

The three terms in Equation 8.5, the sum of squares, are numerators of variances (see Equation 2.4), and, as much, must be divided by their corresponding degrees of freedom (df) to be converted into variances, called *mean squares*. The *df* associated with a particular SS term is the number of independent observations contributing to that estimate of variability. For our three SS, we have the following df:

+   1. The *total degrees of freedom*, $df_{total}$. The *sum of squares*, $SS_{total}$ is the numerator of the variance of all an scores. Therefore, $df_{total} = an - 1$.
2. The *between-groups degrees of freedom*, $df_A$. The *between-groups sum of squares*, $SS_A$ is the numerator of the variance of a $a$ group about the group mean and is distributed on $a - 1$ df.
3. The *within-groups degrees of freedom*, $df_{SA}$. The *within-groups sum of squares*, $SS_{SA}$ is the sum, "pool" of the numerators of each group variances. Because of the $a$ group variances is distributed on $a(n-1)$ df. Note that the degrees of freedom partition simply, in the same way as the sums of squares:

```
$an-1 = a(n-1) + (a-1)\ndf_{tot} = df_{SA} + df_A$ \quad (8.6)
```

Equation 8.6 demonstrates that the degrees of freedom are partitioned into two parts that correspond to the sums of squares. This partitioning of the degrees of freedom provides a partial check on the one-factor design is simple, keeping track of the number of distinguishable sources of variance becomes more difficult in complex designs. There are also designs in which it is a challenge to analyze the relations among the factors in the design. Therefore, when designs have many factors, it is wise to find the degrees of freedom associated with each source of variability and to check the df sum to the total number of scores minus one.

# 8.4.3 Mean Squares, Expected Mean Squares, and the F Ratio

The ratio of a sum of squares to its degrees of freedom is called a *mean square*. In the one-factor design, the relevant mean squares are the $A$ mean square, where

```
$MS_A = SS_A / df_A$
```

and the $S/A$ mean square,

```
$MS_{S/A} = SS_{SA} / df_{S/A}$
```

Under the assumptions summarized in Box 8.1, the ratio $MS_A/MS_{S/A}$ has a sampling distribution called the $F$ distribution if the *null hypothesis is true*.
