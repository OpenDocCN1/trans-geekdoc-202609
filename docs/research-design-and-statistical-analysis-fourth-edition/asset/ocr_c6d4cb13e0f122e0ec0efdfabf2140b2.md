If a family consists of six independent tests each conducted at EC = .05, substitution in Equation 10.12 results in FWE = 1 - (.05)⁶ = .265; that, if the population means are all equal, the probability is .265 that one or more of the tests will be significant. If the tests are not independent, the exact value of FWE is difficult to calculate, but it is still greater than .05. In general, the larger the family, the more FWE exceeds the EC. This suggests that to control FWE, we need to adjust EC by an amount that will depend on the size of the family of comparisons. This line of reasoning requires that we decide how to specify a family of contrasts. We will consider three alternatives.

An investigator working in a research area over a period of years might perform hundreds of experiments and test thousands of hypotheses. Might these consider these tests to form a single family and set FWE equal to .05. This is not a reasonable specification of a family of tests, because it would result in an EC that would be infinitely small. In that case, the Type 1 error rate would be well controlled, but the Type 2 error would soar to unacceptable levels. Experimenter could find that significant results revealed real effects but miss finding many real effects. Because lowering the EC results in a reduction of power, the definition of family must be based on a compromise between Type 1 and Type 2 errors.

Another, reasonable, choice for a family of comparisons would be the set of tests conducted to analyze the results of an experiment. This approach to specifying families of comparisons can be criticized on the grounds that more experimental designs have more tests than could be performed. Simple experiments with fewer factors smaller families of tests than for complex, multi-factor experiments with many conditions. Defining a family of comparisons in this way is also contrary to the strong arguments in favor of controlling for systematic, but uninteresting, sources of variance that would end up in the error variance: if those factors count the family of comparisons, the power of the test of interest is limited by the reduction in EC.

This brings us to a third and more reasonable approach to specifying families of comparisons; identify families of tests with sources of variance in an experimental design. For example, in an experiment with three factors, a set of tests to understand the AB interaction would constitute one family of comparisons, and a set of tests to understand the main effect of C would constitute another. FWE would be controlled independently for the two families.

Identifying the set of comparisons conducted to analyze a single source of variance seems a reasonable approach to defining contrasts in family in few senses. First, it seeks a balance between control of Type 1 errors and power considerations by keeping the size of the family manageable. Related to this, compared to the two previously considered definitions of a family, this approach results in more consistency in the size of a family. Finally, there is a clear, substantial basis for the definition of a family when a family is identified with a source of variance.

Although the identification of a family of comparisons with a source of variance is a good job of addressing some issues associated with controlling FWE, a researcher generally has many decisions to make regarding which contrasts to perform when analyzing a source of variance. For example, suppose we are comparing the contrasts of four different drugs on depression. There are six possible pairwise comparisons. In addition, we could compare each one of the drugs with the average of the other three, and with the average of two of the other three. That leads to a total of 22 possible significance tests.
