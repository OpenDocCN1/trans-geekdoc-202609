Although recall scores should be worse the longer retest is delayed, the interaction of organization and delay is of interest. Will the effect of delay be smaller in the Attribute condition than the others? Alternatively, if learning in the Name and Random organizations required more effort than in the Attribute condition, then perhaps they will show smaller effects of delay. These questions are appropriately addressed with a planned contrast, which we evaluate in the Results section.

## 11.3 Method

### 11.3.1 Participants

The experimenters decided that the interaction of organization and delay of test was of primary interest, so they planned to have good power to detect an interaction effect of medium size (Cohen's f = .25). Using a priori condition in G*Power 3.1 (see Figure 11.1), they specified 4 df for the numerator of the F test of the interaction and 9 for the number of conditions in the experiment. They found that 196 participants yield power of .8, which corresponds to approximately 22 participants per condition. The 196 participants were recruited from students in undergraduate psychology courses.

### 11.3.2 Experimental Design

The design was a 3 (organization of the texts) x 3 (delay of the test) with participants randomly assigned to the nine cells of the design with the constraint that there were 22 in each cell.

### 11.3.3 Procedure

The experiment had three phases. In the first phase, participants studied on their own time five-page booklet with each page, organized according to the organizational condition to which they had been assigned. They were told to study the materials until they memorized 25 facts and return on an assigned day. In the second phase, participants were tested in groups of nine; remained in the lab until they could correctly reproduce all 25 facts they had previously studied. They were told that they could leave when they had produced a written sheet with all 25 facts correct, and that the purpose of the experiment was to see how much time was required to recall what they had previously studied. They were then assigned a day (1, 2, or 3 days later) on which they were to return to participate in “another experiment.” In the third phase, participants were tested for recall of the 25 facts. After completion of this phase, debriefing they were asked whether they had studied the list between the second and third phase; none reported having done so.

### 11.4.1 Exploring the Data

Several points should be noted about the means. With respect to organizational effects, the Attribute organization yields better recall than the other two in all the delay conditions. In addition, the Name group perform similarly to the Random groups, except after a 2-day delay when the Name condition has an advantage of over four words. With respect to delay effects, recall deteriorates as a function of delay of test, as expected; however, the drop is only about four items in the Attribute condition from days 1 to 3, whereas it is more than seven items in the Name and Random conditions for the same period. These different rates of memory decay suggest an interaction.

We used the Shapiro-Wilk test to examine possible violations of the normality and homogeneity of variance. (In R, use `dplyr` to first `group_by`(Organization, Delay) and then `summarise`(SW = shapiro.test(Recall)$p) to see the p-values of each condition). In eight of the nine groups, the data departed from normality. However, the groups are large enough that we assume that the ratios of mean squares will be distributed approximately as F.

To examine the possible violation of homogeneity of variance assumption, we used the Brown-Forsythe test based on absolute deviations over the group means (see Section 8.4), finding the p-value to be .075. Considering that the ratio of the largest to the smallest of the nine groups is only about 2 to 1 (see Table 11.1), and with 189 df tests are powerful enough to detect trivial differences, we conclude that heterogeneity is not a problem.

### 11.4.2 The ANOVA

Table 11.2 contains the results of analysis of variance of the recall scores. The effects of organization and delay are both significant, whereas the interaction p-value falls short of the .05 level. The η² values are ratios of the effect sum of squares to the sum of effect and error of squares. These statistics reinforce that the main effects contribute most of the variance. By Cohen's guidelines, which f = .4 indicates a large effect, whereas their interaction contribution falls halfway between small (f = .1) and medium (f = .25).

Let us analyze these effects further for a better understanding of the differences between organizations. Although the interaction of delay and organization failed to reach significance, we will carry out our planned comparison of the delay effects in the Attribute condition against the average of the Name and Random organizations. This test is justified because it was planned in advance of data collection (see Section 10.6); we had looked at the data before developing the hypothesis, then the post hoc test would capitalize on risk and inflating the Type 1 error rate.

### 11.4.3 A Planned Contrast

The experimenter predicted that delay would have a different effect for the Attribute condition compared to the other conditions, although the direction of the effect was not specified. A planned contrast to test the prediction is

$$\hat{\psi} = (day3 - day1)_{Attribute} - \frac{(day3 - day1)_{Name}}{2} + (day3 - day1)_{Random}$$

Substituting the means from Table 11.1, we find $\hat{\psi} = 4.2$, and applying Equation 10.5, $t(189) = 1.79$. The p-value is found using a distributional calculator. Because the test is two-tailed, we calculated the probability of a t at least large as 1.79; in R, the command `2*(1-pt(1.79, 189))` reveals $p = .074$. We conclude that the experiment provides no clear evidence in favor of the hypothesis that effect of test delay is different for the Attribute organization than the average of the Name and Random organizations.

### 11.4.4 Post Hoc Tests and Confidence Intervals

Turning to the effect of organizations, the results of the Tukey HSD test in Table 11.3 reveal that the significant variance among the Three organization is largely due to the advantage of the Attribute condition over the Name and Random conditions; the difference between the latter two means is not significant. The confidence intervals can be obtained earlier in Box 10.3. The formula for the bounds and its application to the Attribute-Name contrast is

$$CI = \hat{\psi} \pm (q_{0,a,b,n-1} / \sqrt{2}) (MS_{S/AB} \sqrt{2 / ab}) = 5.39 \pm (3.35 / \sqrt{2})(1.103)$$

Completing the calculation, the lower bound of the confidence interval is 2.79 and the upper bound is 8.00.

Because statistical significance does not always mean that an effect is of practical or theoretical importance (particularly when the large number of error degrees of freedom provide power to detect even very small–perhaps uninteresting–effects), we can compute estimates of Cohen’s d from the output in Table 11.3. Each value is the mean divided by the square root of 40.16, the mean square. For the three contrasts in Table 11.3, they are 0.85, 1.16, and 0.31. The estimates of d are quite large for comparisons of the Attribute mean with the other organizational means, and relatively small for the comparison of the Name and Random means, providing further confirmation that the significant variance of the organization is largely due to the differences between the Attribute and the other two means.

Other contrasts are possible. Noting that the Name and Random organizations are similar to each other but markedly lower than the Attribute mean, we might wish to conduct a post hoc test of the difference between their average and that for the Attribute participants.
