
- Distinguished three designs in ANCOVA is often used: (a) designs in which participants are randomly assigned to treatments, (b) designs in which participants are assigned based on their score on the covariate, and (c) designs in which treatments are assigned to pre-existing non-equivalent groups. ANCOVA is an appropriate statistical procedure for analyzing designs (a) and (b), but its use with (c) is problematic the fact that it is commonly used in situation.

# Chapter 25 Integrated Analysis

## 25.1 Overview

In the first part of this chapter, we discuss multiple regression analyses to explore the relationships between physical activity and depression, using real data collected as part of the Seasons study. We find that higher levels of leisure-time physical activity predict lower scores on the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) for participants who identify as women, whereas higher levels of work- and household-related physical activity do not, when the three measures are included in a regression. However, there are several possible reasons for this pattern that cannot easily be sorted out without conducting an experiment in which physical activity is manipulated. Therefore, in the last part of the chapter, we discuss a hypothetical experiment that might resolve the ambiguity, as well as analysis of a data set that might result.

## 25.2 Introduction to the Study

Lin, Halgin, Weil, and Okene (2008) have reviewed some of the literature discussing the psychological benefits of physical activity. For example, the International Society of Sport Psychology (ISSP, 1992) has published a position statement which concludes that physical activity relates to decreases in mild depression, neuroticism, and stress. Most studies that have examined the effect of physical activity on depression have considered leisure-time exercise as walking, running, and cycling, and found them to be associated with lower levels of depression. For most people, leisure-related exercise comprises a small part of total activity. To assess the potential beneficial effects of leisure-time, occupational, and household activity, the relevant data are contained in the Seasons activity data set on the book's website.

## 25.3 Method

### 25.3.1 Participants

The Seasons study was a large project primarily directed at assessing seasonal variation in blood levels. A detailed account of the methodology may be found in Merriam, Okene, Hebert, Rosal, and Matthews (1999), and a statement of the results are presented in Okene et al. (2004). As part of the study, data were collected on physical characteristics such as height and weight, diet, activity, psychological measures, marital and employment status, health, work environment, and exposure to sunlight. There were two sets of eligibility criteria. The first required that participants be between the ages of 20 and 70 years, have a telephone, be literate in English, and plan to remain in the area for a year. There was also a second set of criteria dealing with the participant's health record. Participants could not be taking medication to lower lipids, be on a cholesterol-lowering diet, have a history of cancer within the past five years, or suffer from a psychiatric disease or other condition that would limit their participation in the study. Participants were recruited from the membership of the Fallon Healthcare System, a large health maintenance organization serving central Massachusetts. Of the 5,300 potential participants contacted by telephone, 1,254 met the first set of requirements and made an appointment. Of these, 641 participants (331 who identified as men and 310 who identified as women) kept their initial appointment, all met the eligibility requirements, and completed the baseline questionnaires. Of these, 285 men and 243 women were employed and able to provide data on occupational activity.

## 25.2 Measures Used in the Analysis

Several measures were taken on each participant.

### Physical Activity

In each of 15 interviews spread through their five quarters of participation in the study, participants were asked to recall the amount of time they spent in each of three categories of activity (leisure, occupational, and household) and at each of four intensities (light, moderate, vigorous, and very vigorous) during the 24-hour period. Estimates of physical activity energy expenditure were determined using standard metabolic equivalent (MET) values. MET is a measure of the rate at which adults burn calories and is defined as the ratio of the metabolic rate to the resting metabolic rate: 1 MET is approximately 1 kcal per kilogram of body weight per hour and is roughly equivalent to the energy cost of sitting quietly. The weighted sums of activity intensities were used: light activity = 1.5 METs; moderate = 4.0 METs; vigorous = 6.0 METs; and very vigorous = 8.0 METs. Activity scores in both hours of activity and the corresponding MET scores were averaged over the interview sessions yielding the variables tot_hours_leisure and tot_MET_leisure; tot_hours_occup and tot_MET_occup; tot_hours_house and tot_MET_house.
