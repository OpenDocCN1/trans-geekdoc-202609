What criteria should be used when selecting the statistic to provide a point estimate of a population parameter?

In our marketing example, we focused on the sampling distribution of the mean. However, every statistic has a sampling distribution, because each time a new sample is drawn from a population, the same statistic is based on a set of values. Just as we imagine a distribution of sample means, there are sampling distributions of medians, modes, variances, standard deviations, and so on. And any distribution of a random variable has a characteristic shape, a mean, a variance, and other properties. The criteria we will require for good point estimates relate to several properties of the estimator's sampling distribution: The mean and variance of that distribution and how rapidly the mean of the sampling distribution approaches the parameter value as size increases. Before discussing these properties, it will prove useful to briefly consider the concept of an expected value.

## 5.2.4 Expected Values and Desirable Properties of Estimators

The expected value of a random variable is the mean of the variable's theoretical distribution. In the case of an individual scenario, Y, its expected value is the population mean; that is, $E(Y) = \mu$. The theoretical variance of Y is $\sigma^2 = E((Y - \mu)^2)$; that is, the population variance is the average squared deviation of selectors about the population. With respect to the issue of criteria for selecting estimators, we are interested in the mean and variance of the sampling distribution of potential estimators of population parameters. (For more information about values, and important proofs involving them, see Appendix B.). In general, we may may think in terms of a population parameter that we will represent by the Greek letter $\theta$ (theta); this could be the population mean, variance, a measure of skewness, or any other measure that in theory might be a function of the random variable of interest. There are many possible estimators of $\theta$; we will use the symbol $\hat{\theta}$ (theta-hat) to represent an estimator. We will briefly consider three criteria for choosing an estimator: unbiasedness, and relative efficiency.

## Unbiasedness

An estimator, $\hat{\theta}$, is an unbiased estimator of the population parameter, $\theta$, if the mean of its sampling distribution equals the parameter being estimated. That is, if 

$$E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta$$

In Appendix B, we show that the sample mean, $\hat{Y}$, is an unbiased estimator of the population mean, $\mu$. That seems intuitively reasonable. However, because a statistic and a parameter share the same name does not guarantee that the statistic is an unbiased estimator of the parameter. An example of a biased estimator is $S^2$, where $S^2 = \sum(Y_i - \hat{Y})^2/n$. In Appendix B, we show that

$$E(\hat{s}^2) = \sigma^2$$

## Consistency

Again, let $\hat{\theta}$ be some estimator of $\theta$. $\hat{\theta}$ is a consistent estimator of $\theta$ if its value is more likely to be arbitrarily close to $\theta$ as $n$ increases. A familiar example is the sample mean of $n$ independently distributed scores. From Appendix 5.1, $\sigma_{\hat{\theta}}^2 = \sigma^2/n$; therefore, the sampling variability of $\hat{\theta}$ decreases as $n$ increases. Because inferences based on consistent estimators are more likely to be correct, consistency is an important property of the estimator.

## Relative Efficiency

The third major criterion for selecting an estimator is the variance of the sampling distribution of that estimator, particularly compared to another. The less variable the sampling distribution of the estimator, the more likely it is to be close to the parameter. As you might expect, the relative efficiency of two estimators, $\hat{\theta}_1$ and $\hat{\theta}_2$, can be expressed as a ratio:

$$RE_{21} = \frac{E(\hat{\theta}_2 - \theta)^2}{E(\hat{\theta}_1 - \theta)^2} \quadquad (5.4)$$

## 6.2.5 Which Estimator?

Most of the statistics presented in texts make use of the sample mean. When the population from which data are drawn is normal, the mean is more efficient and therefore the best estimator. But what if the population distribution is not? We will address this question by considering the relative efficiencies of several estimators.
