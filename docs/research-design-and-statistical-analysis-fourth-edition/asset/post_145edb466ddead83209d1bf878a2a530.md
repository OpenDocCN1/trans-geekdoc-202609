## 4.2 What Do We Need to Know to Test a Hypothesis?

Not all hypotheses that a researcher might generate are amenable to the procedures of hypothesis testing. We need two things to directly test a hypothesis. First, we must be able to specify a "point hypothesis". A *point hypothesis* is an exact statement about the state of the world. For example, the hypothesis that Rachel is guessing is a point hypothesis because it can be expressed by a precise probability statement. In our four-choice procedure, the guessing hypothesis can be stated as *p(correct on any trial)* = 0.25.

Second, we must be able to use the point hypothesis to derive the "sampling distribution" for the outcomes of interest. A *sampling distribution* is a probability distribution that assigns a probability to every possible outcome that might be observed ("sampled") in an experiment given our point hypothesis. A sampling distribution may be thought of as the predictions of a hypothesis. Such, a sampling distribution provides the objective information needed to evaluate the tenability of a hypothesis. For example, the guessing hypothesis predicts that a result of four correct guesses in a four-trial experiment has a probability of only (1/4) or .039. This information provides an objective basis for deciding if Rachel is correct on all trials, she almost surely is not guessing in the experiment.

Let's develop these ideas with our ESP experiment. To keep things manageable, we will start by considering a four-trial experiment. We will measure performance in the experiment by counting the number of correct responses across the four; we will call this measure Y. Each response is either correct (C) or in error (E), Y can now take on only five possible values (0–4 correct).

We want to determine the probability of each possible experimental outcome under the hypothesis that Rachel does not have ESP so is randomly guessing on each trial. Under this hypothesis, p(C) = .25 because she is guessing four equally likely alternatives. This hypothesis refers to performance on individual trials, but our interest centers on the summary measure of performance in the experiment, Y, which we will use to evaluate the hypothesis. Therefore, we must translate our hypothesis about trial performance into corresponding values for the different possible values of Y.

The first step in deriving the probability distribution for Y is to identify all the ways in which trials may combine to form experimental outcomes. With four trials, there are 2^4 or 16 possible distinct patterns of correct and error responses, each associated with a possible value of Y. In Chapter 3, we found a tree diagram of the sequences for our four-trial experiment and Table 4.1 partitions the sample space for the experiment.

Having specified the structure of the sample space for the experiment, we want to use our analysis to compute the probability of each possible value of Y. The probability distribution of Y is obtained by using a statistical model, which is a set of assumptions about how responses are generated. This model would capture the essential features of what we mean by random guessing and allows us to calculate the probability of each possible value of Y. We employ the following model for the ESP experiment:

1. The probability of a correct response, p, is .25 on each trial. The first assumption is based on the fact that Rachel chooses among equally likely alternatives.
2. The responses are independent of one another; that is, the probability of a correct response on any trial seems reasonable because each card is replaced in the pack after each trial, the cards are well shuffled, and the selection of each card is done at random. The result on each trial should not be influenced by the outcome of any previous trial.

Given these assumptions, we can apply the multiplicative law to compute the probability of each sequence of outcomes because each sequence corresponds to a joint event. We assume trials are independent, so the probability of any sequence may be found by multiplying probabilities. The probability of the sequence <C_i, C_j, C_k> = (.25)(.25)(.25) = .015625. The probability of the sequence <E_i, E_j, E_k> = (.75)(.75)(.75) = .421875. The probability of each single sequence corresponding to one correct and two errors is greater than .05. The four sequences are mutually exclusive and we are interested in any one of them, so the additive law may be used to compute the probability of the compound event. Summing up the probabilities of the four sequences gives us a value of .42 for the probability of one correct in four trials. By the same approach, we can compute that the probability of two correct in four trials is .219, and the probability of three correct in four trials is .0469.

We have now translated our hypothesis that Rachel is guessing into a distribution that represents the probability of each possible outcome of our four-trial experiment if Rachel does, indeed, guess on each trial. That probability distribution is summarized graphically in Figure 4.1.

Keep in mind when the probability distribution presented in Figure 4.1 is useful to decide whether the responses were produced by guessing, we need some idea of what to expect if they were, in fact, produced by guessing. The probability distribution we generated is a theoretical distribution because it was generated with a statistical model. Here, our statistical model is a theory about the patterns of responses that would be produced by guessing. If the assumptions we made are valid and we performed many random replications of the ESP experiment, in .31 of the experiments there would be zero correct responses; and so on. In short, the patterns of experiments yielding various values of Y would match the theoretical values in Figure 4.1.

The idea of repeating an experiment many times and obtaining the value of some statistic from each experiment is simply the inferential procedures described throughout this book. The idea is important enough to summarize the general steps:

1. A model is formulated.
2. On the basis of this model, we obtain the theoretical distribution of a statistic of interest given many repetitions of the experiment. This is the sampling distribution of the statistic.
3. The sampling distribution is then used with the collected data to draw inferences about the population.

How can we use the theoretical distribution (Figure 4.1) to evaluate the hypothesis that Rachel is guessing on trial (i.e., π=.25)? We are interested in knowing whether Rachel actually has ESP, which would mean the true probability of making a correct response is greater than .25. Therefore, a finding that Rachel is correct more than 25% of the time would be evidence supporting the ESP hypothesis over the guessing hypothesis. How much better than 25% correct match Rachel before we conclude that she is not just guessing? One possible decision rule is to conclude that the population probability is greater than .25 if two or more responses in the sample (i.e., at least 50%) are correct. However, this is not a very good rule because of Figure 4.1 we see that it would not be surprising to get at least two correct responses simply guessing. The probability of two or three correct responses if π=.25 is (.2109+.0446+.0039)=.267. Here, if Rachel is just guessing, there is greater than one chance in four that she would answer correctly twice in four trials.
