### 14.2.3 Repeated-Measures Designs With S × A × B Design by More Factors

It is straightforward to extend the S × A × B design by including additional factors. For the current example, one possible extension might be to include a third within-participants factor of exposure (C, long or short) to the faces. This added factor will double the number of combinations: 18 conditions represent every combination of the two exposures with the three orientations and the three distortions. As before, every participant will see examples of all conditions in random order.

Adding C increases the number of three main effects of interest (A, B, C), three interactions of interest (AB, AC, BC), and a three-way interaction (ABC); the F tests of these each involve a different error mean square (i.e., there is nothing new here; we discussed the interpretation of three-way interactions in Chapter 8). Furthermore, the underlying assumptions of the analysis are the same as in the S × A and S × A × B designs. Sphericity is still assumed and software will adjust degrees of freedom for violations of this assumption.

### 14.3 Mixed Designs With A and B Fixed

Designs that incorporate a mixture of between- and within-participants factors are called mixed designs. Our development relies on the univariate analysis of variance, although we note that there are other approaches to such designs involving at least one between-participants variable and one within-participants variable. They are a compromise between a desire to employ within-participants factors to reduce variance (and increase power and the precision of estimates) and the reality that certain variables simply cannot be treated as within-participants factors. Examples of variables that are inherently between-participants factors are those whose levels are observed rather than manipulated (e.g., individual variables such as gender, age, and clinical diagnostic category) and manipulated variables that entail carryover effects (training method).

## Multi-Factor Repeated-Measures and Mixed Designs

As an example of a mixed design, Table 14.5 (the data are also in the table 14.5 Mixed Data) presents a data set for a hypothetical experiment in which one group of students was taught algebra by a standard instructional method ($A_1$), a second group received additional problems from a computer that provided immediate feedback ($A_2$). All three groups were tested at the end of the instruction, and then once every two weeks until different tests had been given. Assuming that the tests were easy enough so that any differences could be attributed to the passage of time, this design permits us to compare the instructional methods ($A$) and the test course ($B$) of performance the end of instruction for each method. Notice that the design is a mixture of the between-participants design of Chapter 8 and if we average the four test scores for each participant, we have a between-participants design and can conduct the ANOVA exactly as in Chapter 8. If the other hand, we retain the four test scores but ignore the instructional method, we have an $S \times B$ design in which 18 participants have scores at the four levels of B.

### 14.3.1 The Structural Model and EMS for the Mixed Design

In designs such as the one in 14.5, $n$ participants are tested at $A_i$, $n$ other participants are tested at $A_j$, and so on. All $n$ participants are tested at each of the $b$ levels of the independent variable $B$. Therefore, $ab-1$ df must be accounted for in the analysis of variance. With respect to notation, we refer to $Y_{ijk}$ where $i$ indexes the participant $(i=1, \dots, n)$, $j$ indexes the level of the within-participants $(j=1, \dots, a)$, and $k$ indexes the level of the within-participants $(k=1, \dots, b)$. In Table 14.5, $n=6$, $a=3$, and $b=4$.

As always, the structural model underlies the partitioning of the total variability in the ANOVA. The model used for the data in Table 14.5 is:

```
Y_{ijk} = \mu + \alpha_j + \eta_{ij} + \beta_k + (\alpha\beta)_{jk} + (\eta\beta)_{kj} + \varepsilon_{ijk}  (14.2)
```

There are important differences between this equation and Equation 14.1 for the $S \times A \times B$ design. These differences rest on nested variables and crossed variables. Participants are nested within different participants at each level of $A$; the same is for SB effects. We have indicated these relationships by inserting a slash before $j$ in the subscripts for participants and SB effects at levels of $A$ $[n_{ij}(\eta\beta)_{kj}]$. Also, because SB effects are nested at levels of $A$, there are no SAB interaction effects in the model. In contrast, SB interaction effects participants cross with $B$, that is, all participants are tested at all levels of $B$. The parameters and their variances are defined in Table 14.6.

Table 14.7 contains the sources of variance, $df$ and $SS$ formulas, and the EMS. The sources of variance- and within-participants terms follow Equation 14.2. In panel $A_i$, we have organized them into between- and within-participants to emphasize the relationship of designs encountered.

## Table 14.6 Components of the structural model for the mixed design

| Population parameter | Definition | Variance |
|---|---|---|
| $\eta_{ij}$ | $\mu_{ij} - \mu_j$ | $\sigma_{S/A}^2 = E(\eta_{ij}^2)$ |
| $\alpha_j$ | $\mu_j - \mu$ | $\sigma_{\alpha}^2 = \sum_{k} \alpha_j^2 / a$ |
| $\beta_k$ | $\mu_k - \mu$ | $\sigma_{\beta}^2 = \sum_{i,j} \beta_k^2 / ab$ |
| $(\eta\beta)_{kj}$ | $(\mu_{ij} - \mu_j) - \eta_{ij} - \beta_k$ | $\sigma_{SB/A}^2 = E((\eta\beta)_{kj}^2) / b$ |
| $(\alpha\beta)_{jk}$ | $(\mu_{jk} - \mu_k) - \alpha_j - \beta_k$ | $\sigma_{AB}^2 = \sum_{i,j} (\alpha\beta)_{jk}^2 / ab$ |
| $\varepsilon_{ijk}$ | $Y_{ijk} - Y_{jk}$ | $\sigma_{\varepsilon}^2 = E(Y_{ijk} - Y_{jk})^2$ |

> Note: $\mu_i = E(Y_i)$, $\mu_{ij} = E(\mu_{ij})$, $\mu_j = \sum_k \mu_{ijk} / b$, $\mu_k = \sum_{i,j} \mu_{ijk} / ab$, $\mu_i = \sum_{j,k} \mu_{ijk} / ab$, $\mu_{ij} = E[\sum_k \mu_{ijk} / b] / a$, and $\mu = E(\mu_j)$. The random effects—$\eta_{ij}$, $(\eta\beta)_{kj}^{j}$, and $\varepsilon_{ijk}$—are assumed to be normally distributed with mean zero and variance as noted in the table.
