## 10.7.4 When $ns$ are Unequal: The Tukey-Kramer Test

Tukey's HSD procedure assumes an equal number of participants in each condition. However, it is common for $n$ to vary across conditions. In the Royer study, the number of students in the fifth-eighth grades varied; see Table 10.2 for the group means, variances, and $ns$ . A modification of Tukey's HSD test suggested by Klemm (1956) applies in such situations. The standard $t$ statistic is calculated and compared with $q_{FWE,df} / \sqrt{2}$. Box 10.4 illustrates the test as applied to a comparison of the fifth- and sixth-grade speeds.

## 10.4 An Example of the Tukey-Kramer Test When $ns$ are Not Equal

- 1. Find the critical value of $q$. In the example of the Royer study data, $a = 4$ and the error $df$ are 86. If $FWE = .05$, we can use the Tukey(ns, means = 4, df = 86) to find the critical value $q$ is 3.705.
- 2. Calculate the critical value of $t$: $t = q_{FWE,df} / \sqrt{2} = 3.710 / 1.414 = 2.623$.
- 3. To test the difference between the fifth- and sixth-grade mean speeds, calculate the usual $t$ statistic presented in Chapter 6 (with $s_{pooled}$ replaced by $MS_{qq}$). The Tukey-Kramer reveals the mean difference ($\hat{Y}_{ij}$) value divided by the std. error ($s_{ij}$);

$$t = \frac{\hat{Y}_{Grade6} - \hat{Y}_{Grade5}}{MS_{S/Grade}} \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_6} + \frac{1}{n_5}} = \frac{.560 - .350}{(.032)} = .21 / .051 = 4.101$$

Harter, Clemm, and Guthrie (1959) provide a method of nonlinear interpolation when the exact $df$ are not in the table and R is not available. For example, when the $df = 86$, $q_{05,4,60} = 3.74$ and $q_{05,4,120} = 3.69$ from the table, and the reciprocals of 86 (.016), 60 (.0167), and 120 (.0083). The critical value for 86 is given by:

$$q_{05,4,86} = 3.74 - \frac{.0167 - .0116}{.0167 - .0083}}(3.74 - 3.69) = 3.710$$

## 10.7.5 When Variances Are Unequal

Both the Tukey HSD test and the Tukey-Kramer test assume equal variances across treatment so an alternative test is needed when the homogeneity of variance assumption is violated. In Chapter 8, in our analysis of the effects of educational level upon depression scores of males in the Seasons study, we find that the variances were quite heterogeneous. Several methods have been proposed to deal with this problem. Most use Welch's $t$ and $df'$ (see Section 10.3.3) but differ in the criterion which $t'$ is evaluated. In the Games-Howell test (1976), $t'$ is compared with $d_{FWE,a,df'} / \sqrt{2}$. The procedure is illustrated in Box 10.5 using the Seasons depression data.

## 10.5 The Games–Howell Procedure for Testing All Pairwise Comparisons When Variances Are Not Equal

- 1. Compute Welch's $t'$ and $df'$, using Equations 6.15 and 6.16. For the HS and C statistics of Table 10.3, Panel $a$, we have:

$$t' = \frac{\tilde{Y}_{HS} - \tilde{Y}_{C}}{\sqrt{\frac{s_{HS}^2}{n_{HS}} + \frac{s_{C}^2}{n_{C}}}} = \frac{6.903 - 3.674}{\sqrt{\frac{34.541}{19} + \frac{5.970}{33}}} = 2.284$$

and

$$df' = \frac{\left(\frac{s_{HS}^2}{n_{HS}} + \frac{s_{C}^2}{n_{C}}\right)^2}{\left(\frac{s_{HS}^2}{(n_{HS}-1)}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{s_{C}^2}{(n_{C}-1)}\right)^2} = \frac{\left(\frac{34.541}{19} + \frac{5.970}{33}\right)^2}{(19)^2(18) + (33)^2(32)} \approx 22$$

- 2. Obtain the critical value of $q$ from Appendix C.8 or R's Tukey function. Using our example with $df=22$, interpolate in Table C.8 between $df=20$ and $df=24$, with $a=4, FWE=.05$. The critical $q$ value is approximately 3.93. Then $t_{04,2} = 3.93 / \sqrt{2} = 2.779$.

- 3. Because $2.284 < 2.779$, we reject $H_0: \mu_{HS} = \mu_{C}$. In similar fashion, values of $t'$ and $df'$ can be calculated for each of the pairwise comparisons. Note that the critical value of $t$ must be recalculated for each comparison.

- 4. Alternatively, use the `games_howell_test` function in the rstat package to see all pairwise comparisons simultaneously. For example, if the data from Table 10.3 is in a data frame called `data`, `games_howell_test(mean ~ educ.level, data = data, detailed = TRUE)` will return all pairwise comparisons, including each $t'$, $df'$, adjusted $p$-value, and confidence interval.

The Games-Howell method generally does a good job of controlling FWE with reasonable power. However, there are some circumstances under which FWE may be biased with Games-Howell. If the variances are fairly homogeneous and the group sizes are less than 6, the FWE for the Games-Howell method may be as high as .07 when the nominal probability is .05 (Dunnett, 1980; Games & Keselman, 1981). When the variances are not homogeneous, the FWE may be inflated with $ns$ less than 6. However, the FWE is close to the nominal level under other conditions. Furthermore, the test is more powerful than any of the several competitors that have been proposed and have narrower intervals for each comparison. If the researcher is concerned about the possible inflation of FWE, Dunnett's T3 test (Dunnett, 1980) appears to be the most powerful of several alternatives that maintain the FWE at or equal to the nominal value. The test requires the Studentized maximum distribution. Miller has described the procedure (1981, pp. 70–75) and has provided tables of the distribution. The test is also available in several packages: in R, the `DunnettT3` function in the PMCMR package will provide the adjusted pvalues for all pairwise comparisons. For the data summarized in Table 10.3, one of the comparisons are significant by this test.

## 10.7.6 Pairwise Comparisons: Summing Up

The rather detailed set of recommendations for controlling FWE over all pairwise results is an attempt to satisfy two criteria. The first is that we want a method that adequately controls the Type 1 error rate over the family. We have seen in previous chapters that two factors that affect Type 1 error rates are heterogeneous variances and unequal $n$. We again find that the same two factors require adjustments of our procedures for controlling error rates.

Given that we can identify alternative procedures that satisfactorily control Type 1 rates, our second consideration is to choose the procedure that has the most power. Seaman, Levin, and Serlin (1991) simulated tests of all pairwise comparisons under conditions of equal $ns$ and variances. Over the conditions examined in their study, Seaman et al found that the Fisher-Hayter method had a power advantage over the Tukey HSD method that varied between 2% and 9%; Tukey, in turn, had a power advantage of 2%–3% over the Dunn-Bonferroni method. The other distinction among procedures that is relevant to power concerns is the distinction between simultaneous and sequential methods. Sequential tests have more power than simultaneous tests, although confidence intervals are not available when sequential methods are used.

Box 10.6 summarizes our recommendations with respect to the control of FWE over families of pairwise comparisons.

### Box 10.6 Recommendations for Controlling FWE on Families of Pairwise Comparisons

- 1. If the researcher wants to construct confidence intervals to estimate the differences between group means:
- a) If the variances are homogeneous and $ns$ are equal, use Tukey HSD.
- b) If the variances are unequal, use Games–Howell (or Dunnett T3 if the $ns$ are less than 6).
- c) If the $ns$ are unequal, use Tukey–Kramer.
- d) If only a subset of all pairwise comparisons are planned, use Equation 10.19 to determine whether Tukey HSD or Dunn–Bonferroni will have more power.
- 2. If the researcher only wants to conduct hypothesis tests:
- a) If the variances are homogeneous, use Fisher–Hayter.
- b) If the variances are unequal, use Games–Howell (or Dunnett T3 if the $ns$ are less than 6).

We have excluded from our discussion of pairwise comparison procedures that are sometimes used; namely, Fisher’s LSD test (1935), the Student–Newman–Keels test (Keels, 1952; Newman, 1939), and Duncan's multiple range test (1955). We advise against the use of these procedures because they yield FWEs that are often greater than the nominal value. We have also excluded procedures that maintain the FWE at or below the nominal level and have slightly more power (e.g., Peritz, 1970; Ramsey, 1978; Shaffer, 1979, 1986; Welsch, 1977). Based on various sampling studies, the slight power advantage of these methods (1% or 2%) does not warrant the added complexity they usually entail. Descriptions of these methods, together with results of sampling experiments, may be found in the article by Seaman et al. (1991); multiple comparison procedures are also reviewed by Zuck (1993), Shaffer (1995), and Toothaker (1993).

## 10.8 Comparing a–1 Treatment Means With a Control: Dunn’s Test

Dunnett (1953, 1964) proposed a test for studies in which the researcher plans to contrast each of several treatments with a control. If these are the only comparisons of interest, methods that control the FWE for a family consisting of all pairwise comparisons will be overly conservative; power will be lost and confidence intervals will be better than necessary. The Dunn–Bonferroni procedure with $K=a-1$ will be an improvement but still offer less power and wider intervals than the Dunnett test.

Assuming the group sizes are equal and that variances are homogeneous, the test is quite simple. Box 10.7 illustrates the procedure using the data from the memory study. However, if the group sizes are not equal, replace $2/n$ in the equation for $t$ by $1/n_i + 1/n_c$ and use the Dunn–Bonferroni procedure with $K=a-1$. If any of the group variances differ, use Welch’s $t'$ and again use the Dunn–Bonferroni procedure.
