The best of obtaining a more realistic estimate of the population $R$ is to employ a procedure called cross-validation that avoids capitalizing on chance by developing the regression equation using one sample (the screening or training sample) and testing it by a second sample (the calibration or test sample). The cross-validated $R$ is the correlation between (1) the predicted $Y$ scores when the regression equation developed in the screening sample is applied to the calibration sample and (2) the actual $Y$ scores in the calibration sample. Because the regression coefficients are obtained from one sample and the $R$ is obtained from a second sample, the cross-validated $R$ cannot systematically capitalize on sampling variability.

The problem of capitalization is most when the variables are chosen from a larger pool of possible predictors - a common situation. Variables in the pool that are useful for predicting in the sample will be those to be added to the regression equation and thus increase the correlation. In this situation, chance variation in the sample will be what determines the choice of variables in the equation. If the $N/p$ ratio is small and variables in regression equation are chosen from a larger set of possible predictors, the shrinkage achieved by cross-validation can be dramatic.

We can illustrate this with the Seasons data. We selected the data from a random 25% of the participants identified as women aged 65 years or less with BMI scores of 40 or less. Then we used TC as the dependent variable and arbitrarily choose height, BMI1, bost_1, anger1, anxiety1, durduc1, beck_1, beck_2, beck_3, and beck_4 as 11 predictors. Using an automated procedure called stepwise regression (to be discussed in Chapter 21), the regression equation containing five predictor variables that best predicts TC is^1

```
$tc = 204.43+10.876 bost_1 - 11.216 anxiety1 + 2.231 beck_d1$
```

Here, the regression equation is based on 32 randomly chosen cases in the sample having data on the dependent variable and all 11 predictors, the $N/p$ ratio is 2.9. The R obtained for the sample is .42, and the adjusted $R$ is .30. We cross-validated by (1) using the same equation to predict TC scores for the remaining 94 cases with data on all 11 original predictors, and then (2) finding the correlation between the predicted and actual TC scores for these 94 participants. This correlation is very small, $r = .10$, so that the cross-validated $R$ is $.10^2 = .01$. The cross-validated $R$ of .10 is a more realistic measure of how well the regression equation fits the population than the $R$ of .42 or the adjusted $R$ of .30 in the substantial used to generate the equation. In other, because of the small sample and the large number of potential predictors, the regression equation predicts very poorly in the sample that was used to generate it.

In summary, if the $N/p$ ratio is small, the multiple correlation coefficient for a sample will overestimate the usefulness of the regression equation in the population and in all samples. This overestimation is larger when the predictors in the equation are chosen from larger pools of potential predictors. We strongly recommend the use of cross-validation to counter the effects of capitalization on chance.
