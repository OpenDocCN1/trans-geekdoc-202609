The answer is that if the two mean squares estimate the same population variance, or variances, pooling is proper. By example of Equation 9.17, the assumption is that both $MS_{AB}$ and $MS_{SAB}$ estimate $\sigma^2$ (i.e., $\sigma_{AB}^2 = 0$). The advantage of pooling two or more estimates of the population error is that $MS_{pool}$ is distributed on more than $df_{SAB}$; F tests based on the pooled error may be more powerful than tests based on $MS_{SAB}$. Of course, we never know for certain that $\sigma_{AB}^2 = 0$. If it is not, we may lose power in using the pooled error to test a false null hypothesis about a effect. To see why, look at Equation 9.17. As a result, the test F of a main effect will be negatively biased; the expectation of the error term involves more than Type 2 errors. Perhaps surprisingly, when the null hypothesis about the effect is true, there may be an increase in Type 1 errors. The reason for this is that $MS_{AB}$ and $MS_{SAB}$ do not estimate $\sigma^2$; the ratio of $MS_A$ (or $MS_B$) to $MS_{pool}$ will not be distributed as $F$, and the tail area may be larger than the nominal. # 9.9.2 When (If Ever) to Pool

One approach is to apply a times-pool rule; interactions are tested against the within-cell error and the two mean squares are pooled if $p$ is greater than some criterion value because a large value of $p$ is assumed to reflect a clear lack of difference, justifying pooling. With respect to the designs of the current study, by Mead, Bancroft, and Han (1975) is relevant. For a design with two fixed-effect factors and equal cell frequencies, with the criterion for the preliminary test of the AB source as .5, the times-pool rule often resulted in a loss of power when the null hypothesis about the main effect was false, and an increase in Type 1 error rate when it was true. Therefore, in designs in which all factors have fixed effects, we recommend never pooling. We will consider whether the times-pool rule is advisable in other designs when we discuss these designs.

# 9.9.3 Unintended Pooling

Researchers often pool without realizing they have done so. Typically, in cases there are one or more treatment variables and one “nuisance” variable that the researcher regards as irrelevant. For example, the position of a reward may appear often in all experimental designs of a discrimination study, or each experimenters may run an equal number of participants in each condition. Ignoring such factors essentially pools them with the within-cell error term. As we noted in discussing Mead et al.’s (1975) results, such pooling leads to the risk of a loss of power if the null hypothesis is false, or an increased Type 1 error rate if it is true.
