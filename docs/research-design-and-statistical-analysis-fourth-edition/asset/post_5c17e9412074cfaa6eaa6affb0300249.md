Conversely, if we select a sample that represents low and has high values of X (i.e., largely selected from regions A and C in Figure 17.9), r will tend to be larger than p. Investigators interested in the relationship between two variables will sometimes drop the middle scores for one or both of them. Although procedure may be acceptable for determining whether there is a linear component to the relationship, the correlation in this type of sample should definitely not be considered as an estimate of the value of the correlation in the whole population.

This dependence on sample variability raises the question of what comparisons of correlations from different samples actually are. Two correlations may be different not because the samples differ in the variability of the regression line or because the slopes are different, but merely one of the samples has a broader range of X values. Because the correlation coefficient is sample specific and generally does not provide a description of the linear relationship between X and Y, some others (e.g., Aiken, 1982; Tukey, 1969) have recommended that the correlation coefficient should not be used.

We agree with Aiken and Tukey that characteristics of the regression equation such as the intercept, slope, and standard error of the estimate describe a linear relationship more use than r. Further, researchers should often be interested in the rate of change of Y with X (i.e., by b) even when they think they are interested in r. However, we do not believe that the correlation coefficient can or should be abandoned; it should be used with an understanding of exactly what it does and does not measure.

- 3. How large must a correlation coefficient be in order to indicate that there is a “meaningful” linear relationship? Cohen (1977, 1988) has discussed guidelines according to r-values of .10, .30, and .50 correspond to small, medium, and large effects.

Cohen arrived at these values by noting the sizes of correlations encountered in the behavioral sciences and by considering how strong a correlation would have before the relationship could be perceived by an observer. These values should be considered only as very rough guidelines and not criteria for importance. In some texts, even small correlations might be of great practical significance. We should also emphasize that unless the sample is large, the correlation may be quite different in the sample than the population from which the sample was selected. Symmetry. We have already noted that expressions for the correlation coefficient are symmetric in X and Y. The correlation between cholesterol level and age is the same as the correlation between age and cholesterol level. This is not the case for regression equations (see Figure 17.8). The equation for predicting Y from X will be the same as that for predicting X from Y only if (a) there is a perfect linear relationship between X and Y and (b) X and Y both have the same mean and standard deviation – that is, if Y = X.

- 5. Linearity. The Pearson correlation coefficient is a measure of strength of the linear relationship between X and Y. It provides no information about whether there is a systematic nonlinear relationship between the two variables. As can be seen in Panels (e) and (g) of Figure 17.5, two variables can have a systematic curvilinear component to their relationship in addition to, or instead of, a linear one. Therefore, finding a correlation coefficient of zero does not necessarily mean that the variables are independent.

The data points in all panels of Figure 17.10 have identical correlations and best-fitting straight lines. For each of the four panels, r = .82, and the slope of the least-squares regression line that predicts Y from X is 0.5. However, whereas Panel (a) displays a moderate linear relationship with no curvilinear component, Panel (b) displays a systematic curvilinear relationship with a linear component. It cannot be emphasized strong enough that if you want to understand how variables are related, you must plot them and not simply rely on statistics as the correlation coefficient or the slope of the best-fitting regression line.

- 6. What happens when variables are changed? The Pearson correlation coefficient, in contrast to other statistics as the mean, standard deviation, covariance, and the unstandardized regression coefficient, is a dimensionless quantity. By this we mean that r is not expressed in any units; it is simply a number between -1 and +1. Remember that r is the average product of z scores, and that a z expresses in deviation units the location of a score in a distribution. The values of z do not change if we change the units in which we measure X and Y (i.e., if we multiply each by a constant and/or add a constant to the score, as when we change units from ounces to kilograms, or from degrees Fahrenheit to degrees Celsius). It follows that the correlation between two variables tells us nothing about the mean or variance of the variables. In contrast, changing units affect b, and the raw-score variability about the line. Unfortunately, the fact that r has no units is one reason for its popularity. What does a correlation mean? What does a correlation show? What does a correlation tell us? These are all questions that can be confusing. For example, what does a correlation of .30 mean? What does a correlation of .70 mean? What does a correlation of .90 mean? These are not questions that can be answered by looking at the correlation coefficient alone. The correlation coefficient tells us about the strength and direction of the linear relationship, but it does not tell us about the importance of the relationship. It also does not tell us about the cause of the relationship. It does not tell us whether the relationship is linear. It does not tell us about the range of the variables. It does not tell us about the variability of the variables. These are all things that can be answered by looking at other statistics.

7. Correlation does not imply causation. This is one of the most common errors in interpreting correlations. Just because two variables are correlated does not mean that one causes the other. It is possible that a third variable causes both variables. It is also possible that the correlation is due to chance. To determine causality, one must conduct an experiment.

8. The correlation coefficient is sensitive to outliers. Outliers can significantly affect the product of products used in the calculation of the correlation coefficient.

- 8. How large must a correlation coefficient be in order to indicate that there is a “meaningful” linear relationship? Cohen (1977, 1988) has discussed guidelines according to r-values of .10, .30, and .50 correspond to small, medium, and large effects.

9. Measurement error. Because there is always error in measuring X and/or Y, r will be smaller than the true correlation which would be obtained if X and Y could be measured without error.

10. The shapes of the X and Y distributions constrain the possible values of r. If X and Y have identical distributions, it is possible that any value from -1 to +1 might occur, depending on how the values of X and Y are paired. However, if X and Y have distributions that are different from one another, if one or both of the distributions are asymmetric, the full range from -1 to +1 cannot occur.
