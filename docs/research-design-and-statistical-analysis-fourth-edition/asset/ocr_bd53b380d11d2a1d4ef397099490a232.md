In SPSS, we begin by creating a variable, x, with value 0.95. This value is the probability for which we seek a critical value. Next, use the Transform and Compute Variable pull-down menus and then select the Inverse DF function group. Within this group, choose the ldf.Normal function, clicking on the “up” arrow to move the function to the Numeric Expression box. The Idf.Normal function has three inputs, the probability (x) and the mean and standard deviation of the normal distribution. Entering Idf.Normal(0.95, 0, 1) and OK will store the result, 1.645, in the variable named Target Variable. This result is the critical value of z that has 95% of scores below it and 5% above it. We can also identify the x with a certain proportion of data below it in the population. For example, Normal(0.95,500,15) will return 524.7, the value of x at the 95th percentile of an N(500, 15) distribution.

# 5.6 The Power of the z Test

In planning an experiment, the researcher might wish to know what sample size would be needed to attain a certain degree of power in the null hypothesis for a given effect size. Another investigator might wish to know what the power would be if a different sample size were used in a replication of a previously published study. In the latter case, the researcher seeks to determine power for known values of n, σ (or an estimate), and a specific effect size (as such a difference between two means). Building on the developments in Chapter 4, we provide an example of how power is calculated when n, σ, and a specific size are given. Further examples of the determination of power, and of n, will be presented in Chapter 6.

# 5.6.1 Determining the Power of the Normal Probability (z) Test

A situation similar to the analysis of cholesterol levels is one in which we have two or more scores for each participant, each in different conditions. For example, in the Seasons study, the researchers were interested in seasonal change. Accordingly, we might obtain a single score for each subject by subtracting a score in one season from another, thus obtaining a single change score. We did this for 215 participants who identified as female, subtracting their spring Depression scores from their winter scores (select sex=1 in the data file). The link is on the Seasons page of the book's website. The mean change score was .557 and the SE was .266. Using our methods of analysis of total cholesterol scores, and assuming $H_{0}:\Delta_{change}=0$ and $H_{1}:\Delta_{change}>0$, the value of the z statistic, 2.094, is significant with a p of approximately .018. We reject the null hypothesis.

Now suppose that a group in another part of the country wants to know whether the observed effects of seasonal change on depression scores can be replicated in their area, an area where climate differs from Massachusetts. Further, suppose their sample of female-identifying participants is limited to an n of 100, a smaller sample than the 215 tested by the University of Massachusetts researchers. Would this second group of researchers have reasonable power to reject the null hypothesis if it is false?
