# Integrating the Components

Our box plot provides a sense of several important aspects of the data. The median of 89 tells us that at least half of the second-grade students have a good grasp of addition. We also see from the position of the lower hinge that approximately 75% of the students scored in the high 70s or above. We also see the staggering lower tail, unless referred to as negative skew or left skew; the top 25% of the students have scores between 95 and 100 whereas the lowest 25% fall between 47 and 79, reminding us there are several students who had problems with the test.

One caution with boxplots already: that they can sometimes obscure important aspects of the data. We notice there are only two points displayed for the three outliers in scores. Another example occurs when the data is bimodal, with two clusters of scores. In that case, the central box may hide the scores. This limitation can be overcome in R by including the individual scores in the boxplot (“jittered”) slightly so that they can be seen individually), as in the lower panel of Figure 2.2. Notice that plotting the individual observations reveals three outliers in these data, not just the two suggested by the boxplot.

# 2.2.3 A Graphic Check on Normality

Because many commonly used statistical procedures assume that the data were sampled from a normally distributed population, it is helpful to have ways of looking at possible violations of this assumption. Specific characteristics of a distribution imply nonnormality. For example, because the normal distribution is symmetric, skewness (asymmetry) in a data plot indicates a distribution that is not normal. Another indication is a nonzero value of kurtosis (roughly, more, or fewer, scores in the tails than we expect in a normal distribution). However, a more direct indication is available in many computing packages. These programs rank the scores and then the standardized scores (z scores; see Section 2.4 for further discussion). Figure 2.3 presents two plots. Using R's qnorm function in the [stats] package, we first multiplied response times in the Roy data for students in the fifth through eighth grades. If data are normally distributed, the points will fall on a straight line. This is clearly not the case for the multiplication response times (RT). However, if we transform the multiplication times into response times by taking the reciprocals of the responses, we obtain the second plot shown in Figure 2.3. The multiplication speeds, except for a few scores at extreme, do quite close to the line, indicating that the response speeds are more nearly distributed than the response times. Implicit in this illustration is a possible strategy for dealing with situations where we desire normally distributed observations, but the data do not conform to the distribution; namely, we can sometimes transform the data. We will have to say more about transformations, as well as other distributional forms, in later chapters.
