We developed a simple example of an experiment for the purposes of sketching the role of a sampling distribution in the process of evaluating a specific hypothesis. The experiment that we used – a four-trial experiment in which Rachel guessed among equally likely alternatives – is one specific example from a more general class of experiments. If we had chosen to do a 20-trial experiment and changed the experimental procedure to require Rachel to guess among 10 alternatives, it should be obvious that we would need to specify a new sampling distribution to derive the possible outcomes for such ambitious experiment. However, the approach to deriving the appropriate sampling distribution would be the same as that illustrated for our simpler experiment. We turn now to the task of generalizing our approach.

# 4.3 The Binomial Distribution

Figure 4 presents the probability distribution for the number of correct responses when there are four trials ($n=4$) and the probability of a correct response on each trial is .25 ($\pi = .25$). For different values of $n$ and $\pi$ we could always find the desired probabilities by drawing the appropriate tree diagram and applying the multiplicative and additive laws; however, using a tree quickly becomes tedious as the sample size, $n$, increases. It is helpful to have a general formula for the probability distribution given any combination of $n$ and $\pi$. Specifically, we are interested in developing a formula that will give us a way to derive the sampling distribution for any experiment with the following three characteristics:

+   1. On each trial there are two possible outcomes; examples might be correct/error, success/failure, head/tail, or live/die. The two outcomes on each trial might be referred to as $A$ and $\bar{A}$ with probabilities $\pi$ and $1-\pi$, respectively.
2. The value of $\pi$ is constant over trials.
3. Trials are independent. The probability of any outcome of any trial does not depend on the outcome of any other trial.

# 4.3.1 The Binomial Function

We want a formula for calculating the probability of $y$ as function of $n$ and $\pi$. We denote the binomial function $p(y; n, \pi)$ to indicate that it is the probability of obtaining $y$ responses of type $A$ when there are $n$ trials with $p(A) = \pi$ on each trial.

In determining the probability of each possible outcome, $y$, of a four-trial ESP experiment, we first identified each of the 16 possible sequences in the experiment. We found that the probability of any given sequence could be computed using the multiplication rule for independent events. Suppose we wish to find the probability of obtaining exactly three $A$ responses in four trials. For example, the probability of the sequence $<A, A, A, \bar{A}>$ would be $\pi(\pi)(\pi)(1-\pi)$ or $\pi^3(1-\pi)$.

Further, we observed that there were three distinct sequences for most values of $y$. For example, there are four combinations with three $A$s and one $\bar{A}$, that $p(3; 4, \pi) = (<A, A, A, \bar{A}>, <A, A, \bar{A}, A>,, <A, \bar{A}, A, A>,, <\bar{A}, A, A, A>)$.
