## 14.3.2 Assumptions in the Mixed Design

As in repeated-measures design, the univariate F test requires the assumption of sphericity; it is assumed that within each level of A the population variances of difference scores for all pairs of B are level. Violations of this assumption will result in inflation of Type 1 error rates. However, in the pure-within design, the epsilon-adjusted F test provides an approximately correct p-value. Whether testing B or AB, the numerator and denominator df are multiplied by the Greenhouse-Geisser or Huynh-Feldman estimate of epsilon, reducing df and thus requiring a larger F for significance.

Consider the populations of scores at a level of A. Our second assumption, which is less critical is that the variances and covariances of these populations are the same at all levels. Generally, if the group sizes are equal, the ratios of mean squares will be distributed although the degrees of freedom from within-participants terms may require correction if the sphericity assumption is violated.

## 14.4 Designs With More Than One Random-Effects Factor: The Fixed- as Random-Effects Distinction Again

Recall from Section 13.3 that the distinction between random- and fixed-effects refers to how levels of a factor are sampled. In case of a fixed-effects factor, the levels of the factor are either chosen in such a way that exhaust all possible levels or they are selectively from all possible levels. An example of the former is taking observations from all four seasons in the Seasons data. Examples of the latter are common, as in our hypothetical experiment where face orientation and distortion orientations are manipulated. For both, the experimenter intentionally chose the specific orientations and particular distortions to be included in the experiment. In both cases, the levels of the variables were presumably chosen to be a representative range of variation on each dimension.

In case of a random-effects factor, the levels of the factor do not exhaust all possible levels of the variable nor are they selected in a systematic way. Instead, levels are randomly chosen subset of a much larger potential set of observations. The sole example of a random-effects factor that we have encountered in this point is participants.
