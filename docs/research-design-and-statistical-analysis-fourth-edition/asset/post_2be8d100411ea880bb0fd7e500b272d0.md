## 5.8 Summary

Chapter 5 has continued the development of inferential concepts and principles begin in Chapter 4. Central to both chapters has been the emphasis on the distributions of random variables, but in Chapter 5 we have used a continuous distribution to illustrate several important points concerning estimation and hypothesis testing:

- Every statistic that has been calculated a sampling distribution whose properties are important in assessing criteria for estimators, and affecting the width of confidence intervals, and influencing the power of hypothesis tests.

- The quality of parameter estimates depends on several criteria. A good estimator is unbiased; the mean of its sampling distribution equals the parameter. A good estimator is consistent; as sample size increases, the estimator is more likely to be to the estimated parameter. And a good estimator is relative to other estimators of the same parameter; its sampling distribution has a smaller variance about the parameter.

- The central limit theorem states that the sampling distribution of the mean will tend toward normality for large samples even when the underlying population distribution is not normal. However, the speed with which the sampling distribution approaches normality as a function of sample size will depend on how closely the underlying distribution resembles the normal.

- Confidence intervals around population means will be narrower, and hypothesis tests involving means will be more powerful, the variability of the sampling distribution decreases; as the standard error of the mean (SEM) decreases. The SEM will decrease as sample size increases and as the sample variance decreases.

We illustrated the calculation of confidence intervals and hypothesis tests using obtained from the table of the normal distribution and from the distribution functions in R and SPSS. Although in most cases, such calculations would be carried out with the t distribution, the concepts and principles are the same and extend to all the procedures in this book, and many others as well. The interpretation of p-values, confidence interval bounds, Type 1 errors, and power (or its complement, the probability of a Type 2 error) have been a focus of both Chapters 4 and 5. They are critical to interpreting the results of data analysis.

### Appendix 5.1

### The Variance of combination of n observations, L, has the general form.

$L = w_1Y_1 + w_2Y_2 + \dots + w_nY_n$

where the weights, the $w_i$ are any real numbers. A special case is one in which $n=2$ and the weights are both 1, in which $L$ is a sum, or 1 and -1, in which $L$ is a difference. We consider this case first.

Variances of Sums and Differences. The variance of a sum or difference of two random variables depends on the variances of the variables and on their covariance. The covariance is a function of the variances and the correlation coefficient; as we stated in Chapter 2, the sample correlation coefficient of two variables, $X$ and $Y$, is defined as

$$r_{XY} = \frac{\Sigma(X - \bar{X})(Y - \bar{Y}) / (n-1)}{S_X S_Y}$$

and the covariance is defined as the numerator, $\Sigma(X - \bar{X})(Y - \bar{Y})/(n-1)$. We will denote the covariance as $s_{xy}$. First consider the variance of $X+Y$; by definition of the sample variance,

$$s_{X+Y}^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\Sigma_i[(X+Y) - (\bar{X}+\bar{Y})]^2$$

But

$$\overline{X+Y} = (1/n)\Sigma_i(X_i+Y_i) = \bar{X}+\bar{Y}$$

Therefore,

$$s_{X+Y}^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\Sigma_i[(X+Y) - (\bar{X}+\bar{Y})]^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\Sigma_i[(X_i-\bar{X})+(Y_i-\bar{Y})]^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\Sigma_i[(X_i-\bar{X})^2+(Y_i-\bar{Y})^2+2(X_i-\bar{X})(Y_i-\bar{Y})] = s_X^2+s_Y^2+2s_{xy}$$

where $s_{xy} = r_{xy}s_Xs_Y$, the covariance of $X$ and $Y$. Therefore,

$$s_{X+Y}^2 = s_X^2+s_Y^2+2r_{XY}s_Xs_Y$$

The variance of the difference has a similar form:

$$s_{X-Y}^2 = s_X^2+s_Y^2-2r_{XY}s_Xs_Y$$

The only difference in the two expressions is in the sign of the covariance term. Note that if the two variables are uncorrelated (i.e., $r = 0$), both the variance of $X + Y$ and $X - Y$ are $s_1^2 + s_2^2$, the sum of variances of $X$ and $Y$. Analogous arguments hold when we consider population parameters. The population variances for $X+Y$ and $X-Y$ are

$$\sigma_{X+Y}^2 = \sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2+2\rho_{XY}\sigma_Xs_Y$$

$$\sigma_{X-Y}^2 = \sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2-2\rho_{XY}\sigma_Xs_Y$$

The General Case. We previously defined $L = w_1Y_1 + w_2Y_2 + \dots + w_nY_n$. The variance of $L$ can be shown by an extension of the formulas for two observations:

$$s_L^2 = \sum_{i} w_i^2s_i^2 + \sum_{i}\sum_{j} w_iw_jr_{ij}s_is_j$$

For example, given two tests, T1 and T2, and a final exam score, F, and supposing the grade is calculated by weighting the two exams by 1/4 and each by 1/2, the variance of the grade is

$$s_{grade}^2 = (1/4)s_{T1}^2 + s_{T2}^2 + (1/4)s_F^2 + (2)(1/4)(1/2)(s_{T1}r_{T1,T22}) + (2)(1/4)(s_{T1}r_{T1,F}) + (2)(1/2)(s_{T2}r_{T2,F})$$

The Variance of the Mean. An important consequence of these developments is that we can readily derive an expression for the variance of the mean of $n$ independently distributed scores. The mean is $(1/n)(Y_1 + \dots + Y_n)$. We view the variance of a score as the variance of scores over many samples. Then $s_1^2 = \dots = s_n^2 = s^2$ and each value of $w$ is $1/n$. Because the scores are independently distributed, all covariances equal zero, and substituting into Equation 5.18,

$$var(\bar{Y}) = n\times s^2 / n = s^2 / n$$

and the standard error of the mean is (defined in Chapter 2).

$$SEM = \sqrt{s^2 / n}$$
