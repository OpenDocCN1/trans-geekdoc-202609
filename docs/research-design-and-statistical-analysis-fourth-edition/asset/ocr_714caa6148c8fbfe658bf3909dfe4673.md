Given our understanding of what $MS_A$ and $MS_{S/A}$ estimate, we are in a position to understand the logic of the F test, where $F = MS_A/MS_{S/A}$. First, assume that the null hypothesis is true, and also, that there is homogeneity of variance; that

```
$\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_a$ and $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = \dots = \sigma_a = \sigma_d$
```

Under these assumptions, $MS_A$ is an estimate of the error variance to the $a$ treatment populations. In terms of $E(MS_A)$, $\phi_A = 0$ so $E(MS_A)$ is an estimate of error variance, $\sigma^2_e$. Thus, if the null hypothesis is true, $MS_A$, and $MS_{S/A}$ both estimate the same population variance and their ratio would be about 1. Of course, it would be surprising if two independent estimates of the same population variance were identical; then, the ratio of $MS_A$ to $MS_{S/A}$ has a distribution of values. More precisely, if $H_0$ is true, the ratio $MS_A/MS_{S/A}$ is distributed as $F$ on $a-1$ and $n-1$ df.

But what if the null hypothesis is false, false? For example, suppose that the method of study also affects the recall in the Table 8.1. The means of the groups in Table 8.1 will differ not only because the scores in different groups differ by, but also because the groups were studied by different methods. In other, if $H_0$ is false, $\phi_A > 0$ so $E(MS_A) = \sigma^2 + \phi_A$. The group-variance does not change: $MS_{S/A}$ should not be affected by the independent variable because all participants in a group receive the same treatment. Therefore, if $H_0$ is false, the ratio $MS_A/MS_{S/A}$ is expected to be greater than 1.

In summary, under the assumptions of the null hypothesis, homogeneity of variance and independently distributed scores $MS_A$ and $MS_{S/A}$ are two independent estimates of the population error variance, $\sigma^2_e$. If we also assume that the population of scores is normally distributed, the ratio of two independent estimates of the same population variance has an $F$ distribution. Therefore, under the assumptions summarized in Box 8.1, the ratio $MS_A/MS_{S/A}$ has an $F$ distribution. The numerator is based on an estimate of the variance of a population means, it has $a-1$ df. The denominator has $a(n-1)$ df because the estimate for each group is based on $n-1$ df and $MS_{S/A}$ is an average of variance estimates.

Appendix Table C.5 presents several values of the F distribution. As an example of its use, suppose we have groups of 11 participants. Then the numerator $df = a-1=2$, and the denominator $df = a(n-1)=30$. Turning to the column headed by 2 and the row labeled 30, if $\alpha = .05$, we would reject the null hypothesis of no difference between the treatments if the F critical value is greater than 3.323. Interpolation may be needed for degrees of freedom not listed. Alternatively, we can use R to find the critical value for any combination of degrees of freedom. Recall from Box 6.1 in Chapter 6 R includes several distributional tables. The F distribution abbreviation is f, and for example, the command `qf(.95,df2=30)` will return the critical value of $F(2,30)$ with 95% of the area below it and 5% above. Of course, most analyses performed with software will report the p-value associated with the empirical value of F.
