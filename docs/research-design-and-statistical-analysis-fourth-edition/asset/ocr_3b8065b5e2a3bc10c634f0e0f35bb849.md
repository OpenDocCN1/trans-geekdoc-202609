# 14.9 Effect Size

# 14.9.1 The S × A × B Design

Common measures of effect size include partial eta-squared and partial omega-squared. However, as was noted in Chapter 13, both measures suffer from the limitation that their denominators change across designs. Because it is desirable to have measures of effect size that are comparable across designs, we have argued for the use of general eta-squared and general omega-squared as measures of effect size (Olennik & Algina, 2003).

The denominator of general eta-squared is the sum of squares squares terms; $SS_{effect}$ plus the sum of squares includes participant variance. For example, in $S 	imes A 	imes B$ design, general eta-squared for A is computed as

```
$$\eta_g^2 = \frac{SS_{A}}{SS_{SA} + SS_{SS} + SS_{AB} + SS_{SAB}}}{SS_{Total} - SS_{B} - SS_{AB}} \(14.12)$
```

However, as noted in Chapter 13, general eta-squared tends to overestimate the proportion of population variance to a variable. For that reason, we prefer general omega-squared as a measure of effect size.

The definition of general omega-squared ($\omega_g^2$) in the $S 	imes A 	imes B$ design is like that in Chapter 13 for the one-within-participants $(S 	imes A)$ design. For example, if A and B are extrinsic factors, we define $\omega_g^2$ as

```
$$\omega_g^2(A) = \frac{\sigma_A^2}{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_{SA}^2 + \sigma_{SAB}^2 + \sigma_{SAB}^2} \(14.13)$
```

Unfortunately, $\omega_g^2(A)$ presents a problem because the interaction terms cannot be estimated from expected mean squares in Table 14.2. An approximate solution is obtained if we assume that $\sigma_{SAB}^2 = 0$. With this modification, the $EMS$ of Table 14.2 provide estimates of the variances in Equation 14.13. Those estimates are presented in Table 14.4. Substituting into Equation 14.13, multiplying numerator and denominator by $abn$ and $MS_{SAB}$ terms, we
