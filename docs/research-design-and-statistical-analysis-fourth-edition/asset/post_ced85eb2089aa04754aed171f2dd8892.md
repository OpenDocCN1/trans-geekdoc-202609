## 8.8.4 The Homogeneity of Variance Assumption

Variances may differ under conditions for one of several reasons. One possible cause of heterogeneity of variance is an interaction of an experimental treatment with individual characteristics. For example, a drug tested for its effects on depression may result in a higher variance, but the same mean score as, a placebo. This would suggest that some individuals had improved but others had been adversely affected by the drug. A second reason for unequal variances is that some populations are more variable than others on a particular task. For example, older adults tend to have longer times on many tasks, they also have to have a higher variance in RTs. Still another possible factor in findings of heterogeneity of variance are floor, or ceilings effects. That is, variability may be reduced in one condition to another because of a lower, or upper, limit on performance to the measuring instrument. Finally, variances tend to be correlated with means, usually positively; the normal distribution is the sole exception in which the means and variances are independently distributed. For all these reasons, variances are often unequal, heterogeneous, in the populations sampled in our research. In what follows, we summarize some consequences of the failure of this assumption and then consider alternatives to the standard F test. Again, the problem and possible solutions are those described for t tests in Chapter 6.

### Consequences of Heterogeneity of Variance

When there are the same number of scores in all conditions, heterogeneous variances will usually cause Type 1 error rates to be slightly inflated. The inflation is usually less than .02 at the .05 level, and less than .05 level, provided the ratio of the largest variance is no more than 4 to 1, and n is at least 5. Even larger ratios may not be a problem, but this will depend upon sample size, the number of groups, and the shape of the population distributions. The results of computer simulations employing these factors are discussed in several articles, including those by Clin and Keselman (1982), Tomarken and Serlin (1986), and Cribbie, Fiksbaum, Keselman, and Wilcox (2012).

When there are different numbers of scores in each given condition, simulations clearly demonstrate that heterogeneous variances are a problem. Sampling from heavy-tailed and skewed distributions using variance ratios from largest to smallest as high as 16:1, L and Keselman (1998) found that error rates were as high .50 in some conditions. Sampling from sets of three or four normally distributed populations, Tomarken and Serlin found that at a nominal .05 level, the actual Type 1 error rate was as low .02 when the group size was positively correlated with the variance (i.e., larger groups associated with greater variances) or was negative. This is because the average-group variance (i.e., the error term, MSew) is increased when the largest groups have the largest variances and, conversely, is decreased when the largest groups have the smallest variances. The consequences of unequal variances were present in tests, as we described in Chapter 6. When the variances are unequal, a positive relation between group size and variance negatively biases the F test whereas a relation will positively bias the test.

There is evidence in the research literature that extreme variance occur (Wilcox, 1987), and simulation studies clearly make that heterogeneity of variance can inflate Type 1 error rates or deflate power, depending upon various factors such as sample size and the type of distribution. That leaves us with two questions. First, for a given data, how do we decide whether to abandon the standard ANOVA for some remedial procedure? Second, if we decide that unequal variances are a threat to the validity of the standard F test, what alternative should we use? As we will, the answers to these questions for t tests are described in Chapter 6.

### Detecting Heterogeneity of Variance

As we argued, that researchers begin the data analysis by examining summary statistics and plots of the data. Box plots for the Beck Depression as a function of educational level were presented in Figure 8.2, and as we noted, differences among the groups in shape and spread are quite equal. The range of variances in Table 8.5 exceeds the 4:1 rule of thumb and suggests that the alpha-level reported in Table 8.6 may not be the actual probability of a Type 1 error. For confirmation of this, we may wish to test whether the variances are homogeneous. tests of homogeneity have been proposed. Some are overly sensitive to violations of the normality assumption (Bartlett, 1937; Cochran, 1941; Hartley, 1950; Levene, 1960) or lack power relative to other procedures (Box, 1953; Games, Keselman, 1979; Scheffé, 1959).

We recommend the Brown-Forsythe test (Brown & Forsythe, 1974a) based on deviations from the median. Sampling studies indicate that it has only a slightly inflated Type 1 error rate and good power relative to various competitors when ns are not equal and distributions departed markedly from the normal (Games et al., 1979). In this test, the absolute deviation of each score from its group median, |Y_ij - Y_j|, is computed, and these values are rarely submitted to the analysis of variance. Although these deviations do not directly represent the variance, their variance is an index of the spread of scores. For the depression scores summarized in Table 8.5, the levene test in the (car) package of R, when used with the center = median option, provides the Brown-Forsythe test.’ In SPSS, the test is available by clicking the “options” button in the standard ANOVA window and checking the box for “homogeneity of variances test.” With either software package, the test statistic is 4.511, on 3 and 12 df, is significant (p = .005). This indicates that the mean absolute deviation varies significantly as a function of education level, confirming our sense that the spread of scores was a function of the educational level.

Once we conclude that the population variances are not equal, the next question is: What shall we do about it? One response is to seek a transformation of the data that yields homogeneity of variance on the scale rooting the transformation. As discussed in Chapter 6, data transformations can make it difficult to interpret the results; in practice, they are used infrequently. A second possibility is to consider an alternative to the usual F test. We consider this approach.

## Dealing With Heterogeneity of Variance: Welch's F Test

Several alternatives to the standard F test of the equality of a population means have been proposed (Alexander & Gordon, 1994; Brown & Forsythe, 1974b; James, 1951, 1954; Welch, 1951), but no one test is under all conditions. When the data are normally distributed and as equal, most of the procedures are reasonably robust with respect to Type 1 error rate; however, the standard F is slightly more powerful if the population variances are equal. When the variances are not equal, the choice of test depends upon the degree of skew and kurtosis, which others are, the degree of heterogeneity of variance, the relation between group sizes and group variances, and the total N (Clinch & Keselman, 1982; Coombs, Olftman, 1996; Cribbie et al., 2012; Grissom, 2000; Les-Keselman, & Keselman, 1996; Tomarken & Serlin, 1986).

Although there is rarely a clear-cut for any given data, simulation studies have concluded that the Welch test, F<sub>w</sub>, provides a good alternative when both Type 1 error rates and power are considered (Delace, Ley, & Lakens, 2019; Lik et al., 1996). Fww performs well relative to various competitors when are highly skewed (skew > 2.0) or group sizes are less than 10 (Lik et al., 1996; Tomarken & Serlin, 1986). Furthermore, the test is readily available in statistical packages. For example, in R we use the Welch test in the [oneway] package; in SPSS, simply click the “options” button for “Welch test,” and continue as normal.
