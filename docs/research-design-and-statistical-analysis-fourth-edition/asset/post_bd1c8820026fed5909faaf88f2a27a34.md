For any given sample, the standardized and unstandardized coefficients will have the same sign, and their significance will yield identical results. Does one of these coefficients better characterize the nature of the relationship? Sources such as the publication of the American Psychological Association state that both the standardized and unstandardized coefficients should be reported in results. We have no problem however, unless we are specifically interested in changes stated in terms of standard deviations or in terms of relative standing, we will usually be better off with the unstandardized coefficients for the following reasons:

- 1. If the scales are meaningful, one-unit changes are more understandable than changes of one standard deviation.
- 2. If the scales are not meaningful or if there is a great deal of measurement error, the use of unit-free measures such as $R^2$ and standardized coefficients is more likely to obscure this fact. Standardizing poor will not help. As Achen (1977, p. 806) puts it, "To replace the unacceptable by making it unacceptable."

- 3. The standard deviations of X and Y in two separate samples will be different, so that standardized regression coefficients are sample specific in the same way as correlation coefficients. In fact, bivariate regression, the standardized regression coefficients are the correlation coefficients and therefore should not be used to compare across groups.
- 4. The standardized regression coefficient is equal to the unstandardized coefficient multiplied by $s_y/s_x$. Therefore, with two separate samples, A and B, we may find that the unstandardized coefficient is larger in A than in B because of differences in the standard deviations (see Exercise 19.5).

## 19.7 Checking for Violations of Assumptions

Because our conclusions can be in error if there are severe violations of the assumptions, next discuss how to check for violations. First, we emphasize that if we try to understand our data, we should not rely on summary statistics like the correlation coefficient or the slope of the regression line. It is also important to plot the data and to use the diagnostics that are usually provided by statistical software. In Figure 17.10, we presented scatterplots for four very different data sets that have identical values of N, $b_1, b_0, SS_X, SS_{regression}, SS_{residual}, SE(b_1), \hat{Y}, b_1, b_0, SS_Y, SS_{regression}, SS_{residual},$ and $r$ (Ans, 1973). As discussed in Chapter 17, although these summary statistics suggest that the relationship between Y and X is the same for all data sets, comparison of four scatterplots makes clear that this is not one. One plot (a) is well fit by a straight line whereas a second (b) is curved. A third plot (c) is just one point that deviates from a straight line, and a fourth would have zero X variance if one point were deleted. To understand the relationship between Y and X, we must simply look at the scatterplot, determine whether there are systematic deviations from linearity, determine whether there are influential data points, and check the other assumptions that underlie inference.

### 19.7.1 Checking Regression Assumptions Using Residuals

Valuable information about whether the assumptions of the regression model are valid may be obtained by studying residuals; that differences between observed and predicted Y. The residuals, $e_i = Y - \hat{Y} = Y - (b_0 + b_1X)$, provide information about the population error components, $\epsilon_i = Y - (\beta_0 + \beta_1X)$. Statistical software generally provides residuals and standardized residuals which allow them to be plotted in a variety of ways. If the distribution of residuals differs strongly from that assumed for the error components, the aspects of the model may not be satisfied. Moreover, the nature of the difference can tell us which assumptions have been violated and suggest appropriate measures.

Residuals cannot provide us with information about the assumption $E(\epsilon) = 0$ because least-squares regression is used, the residuals can, however, provide useful information about whether there are violations of linearity, homogeneity of variance, and independence. If the assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity (homogeneity of variance) are both valid, when residuals are plotted against either X or Y, the data points should lie within a horizontal band, as indicated in Panel (a) of Figure 19.5. Any other pattern suggests that in Panel (b) indicates that the relationship between Y and X is nonlinear and that the appropriate model should contain additional predictors such as $X^2$. Plots such as in Panel (c), in which the residuals are more spread out for some values of X than others, indicate that the variance of estimate is not constant (homoscedasticity assumption is violated). We plot residuals against predicted values rather than Y because it is not correlated with Y (or, therefore, with X) but has a correlation of $\sqrt{1-r^2}$ with Y.

Another assumption of the regression model is that relevant variables have included in the model. If a relevant variable is omitted, the error component, $\epsilon$, will consist of more than chance variance. We can determine whether an additional variable, W, belongs to the model by plotting the residuals against W. If the residuals systematically with W, then W should be included in the model.
