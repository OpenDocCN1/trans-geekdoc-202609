That is, the specific stimuli selected were not chosen in such a way that they were the only items with an opportunity to be included in the experiment; there were many other items that had an equal opportunity of being included under the sampling procedure used. In many other experiments, the choice of stimuli is so constrained that the items selected for the experiment virtually exhaust the stimuli that meet the sampling constraints. In studies involving responses to words, restrictions are often placed on the grammatical class, length in both syllables and letters, familiarity, and number of associates each word. The experimenter may find it difficult to identify items that meet those restrictions. Under such existing constraints, it is not clear that stimuli should be treated as having random effects. Two rough guidelines may be helpful. First, under the existing constraints, could independent investigators other samples of items? Assuming there is a reasonably large population of items, the second question is: Was there any likelihood that members of the population could be included in the study? If the answer to this question is positive, then it is reasonable to treat the stimuli as having random effects and all that this implies for our data analyses and the scope of our conclusions.

Table 14.4 reveals that the orientation of pictures faces (A), the type of distortion (B), and the AB interaction all have significant effects on bizarre ratings. Ordinarily, to better understand the source of effects, we follow these overall F tests and with confidence intervals that more precisely target the differences among means. We might begin by testing comparisons across the marginal means. Comparisons of means are of most interest when there clearly is no interaction, because then there is no evidence that the effects of one factor vary as a function of the other. When an interaction is present, or even when there is some reason to anticipate an interaction prior to data collection, other tests should be of greater interest. Tests of simple effects, such as the difference between $B_1$ and $B_2$ means at the $A_2$ orientation, might clarify the pattern of means. Other possibilities include test of interaction contrasts. For example, we decide to test whether the difference between $B_2$ and $B_1$ is significantly greater at $A_2$ than at $A_1$.
