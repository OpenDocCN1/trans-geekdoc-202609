If we regress on Age, BMI, age², BMI², and Age × BMI, the interaction term is significant, t(17) = -2.077, p = .039, even though we include the squared terms in the regression, and the squared terms are not significant (regardless of whether we center the variables). Therefore, we conclude that there is a negative interaction between age and BMI.

There is another complication—the differential reliability of terms in the regression. All things being equal, predictors that are less reliable (i.e., have more measurement error) have smaller effect sizes and less power. Reliability will be lower for squared and product terms than for the first-orders (see Busemeyer and Jones, 1983; McCall & Mar, 1995). For the typical situation in which the predictors are at least correlated, the attenuation will be worse for the quadratic terms than the product terms. As a result of this differential reliability, when the predictors are measured with error, interaction effects will be underestimated and quadratic terms will be underestimated more. Furthermore, if the true model contained interaction and quadratic effects of equal size, the power and observed effect size would tend to be larger for the interaction term. Because of these complications, we must be cautious about making strong conclusions from the data unless measures are reliable and the regression survives validation assessments.

# 22.9 Limitations of Ordinary Least-Squares Regression

We mentioned alternatives to OLS regression when assumptions of the model have been violated. For example, we noted that weighted least-squares regression can be a useful alternative when the data exhibit heteroscedasticity and principal components analysis may be useful when there is substantial multicollinearity in a data set. Two other procedures that address designs that are problematic for OLS are logistic regression and multilevel modeling.

OLS regression cannot be applied when the dependent variable is dichotomous and the goal is to predict outcome. For example, a researcher may wish to predict whether a criminal defendant will receive a guilty verdict on predictors such as rate, strength of the evidence, severity of the crime, primary criminal record, and so forth. One clear problem for OLS regression is the assumption that standard error of the mean is normally distributed, which it must predict outcomes other than 1 (guilty) or 0 (not guilty). A common solution to this problem is to use logistic regression, which transforms the linear combination of predictors to probabilities, p using a function called the logistic transformation results in predictions that are bounded at 0 and 1 and have a smooth, “S-shaped,” transition from one value to the other. The interpretation of logistic coefficients is quite different than what we have discussed so far, and new concepts must be learned to perform and interpret the results. Comprehensive introductions to the topic are available in Cohen et al. (2003) and Menard (2002). A standard reference for the book is Hosmer and Lemeshow (2001).
