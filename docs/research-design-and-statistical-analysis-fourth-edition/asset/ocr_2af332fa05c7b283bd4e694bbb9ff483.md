Here we briefly discuss several of the more important measures to assess.

In bivariate regression, outliers in X can be identified by looking at the distribution of X or at the scatterplot of X vs Y. However, in multiple regression, we must depend more heavily on measures such as the leverage, $h_{ij}$, to identify combinations of values on the predictors because multivariate outliers can occur in subtle ways. For example, it may be an outlier because there are correlated deviations from the mean for several predictors; there may not be an extreme deviation for any predictor. Even with large leverage may not be influential; the degree of influence is a function of the dependent variable, $Y$, whereas leverage is a function of the predictor variables.

As mentioned in Chapter 19, it can be shown that the sum of the leverages is $\sum b_{ij} = p+1$ where $p$ is the number of predictor variables; the mean value of $h_{ij}$ is $(p+1)/N$. Hoaglin and Welsh (1978) suggest that $h_{ij}$ greater than $2(p+1)/N$ should be considered large. Belsley et al. (1980) indicate that if $h_{ij}$ is small but not because it is easy to remove and identify many points if $p$ is small but use other guidelines mentioned by Kuter, Nachtheim, and Wasserman (1996) as that $h_{ij}$ values with very high leverage whereas between .2 and .5 indicate moderate leverage.

SPSS users should recall that SPSS reports only leverages, $b_{ij} = 1/N$, for case. To use the cutoff, we can either modify the criteria so that they apply to centered leverages or simply transform the centered leverages to regular leverages by adding I/N to each of them – here we do the latter. For the regression of TC on age and BMI, for women aged 20–65 with BMI of $\le 40$, the largest leverage is .05 so that the largest $h_{ij}$ is .055+.061, a low value adequate to the Nether et al. (1996) guidelines, although it is larger than the largest Hoaglin and Welsh coefficient of .033. We see from the data set that this leverage value comes from case 569 (ID = 1068), a woman with both predictor values (age = 64, BMI = 39) near their cutoffs.

As we pointed out earlier, with outlying residuals do not necessarily have the great influence on the regression equation or the fit. We introduced Cook's distance, $CD_j$, in Chapter 19 as a measure of the change that would result the overall fit of the model if the $j$th case was omitted. $CD_j$ can be written as

```
$CD_j = \frac\sum ((\hat{Y}_{i(-j)} - \hat{Y}_i)^2}{(p+1)s_j^2}$
```

where $\hat{Y}_{i(-j)}$ is the prediction of $Y$, calculated from regression coefficients with the $j$th case deleted. A simple guideline given by Cook and Weisberg (1982) is that a Cook's distance of 1 should be considered large. However, a guideline that takes sample size and number of predictors into account is that Cook's distance values should be considered large if they exceed the cutoff $F_{50,p+1,N-p}$. Case 569 has a Cook's distance of .029, well below the critical $F_{50,3,178} = .79. The largest Cook's distance value for the regression is .084, again well below the critical value.
