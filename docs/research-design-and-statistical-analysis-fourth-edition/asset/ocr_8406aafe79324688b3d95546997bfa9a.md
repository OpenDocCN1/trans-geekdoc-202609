The df associated with the t test are the df on the estimate of the error variance is based; the df associated with $MS_{W/A}$$ or 36. The choice of an appropriate critical value of t value will be deferred until we discuss the concept of familywise error rate. However, the computed t value of .348 is clearly not significant by any reasonable criterion.

The computations illustrated for the hypothesis test might have been used to construct a confidence interval for the estimate, $(1/2)|\mu'_{loc} + \mu'_{age} - (1)\mu'_{dyne}|$. In Chapter 6, we learned how to construct an interval estimate on the difference between two populations. The generalization of that to any contrast is

```
$$\hat{\psi} \pm (t_{critical})s_{\psi}$$ (10.6)
```

That is, the confidence interval is a point estimate for a statistic plus minus the product of a measure of variability for that contrast and a critical value. Again, discussion of the selection of a critical value of t will be deferred for you. The contrast and standard error are computed the same way as for the hypothesis test, and the standard error is, again based on 36 in our example.

We have been considering a contrast where some of the weights on the means are fractions (i.e., 1/2 and 1/2). When using software to do such computations, it is sometimes useful to express fractional weights in the contrast (e.g., .5). To avoid a repeating decimal fraction (e.g., 1/3 converts to .3333...), the weights in a contrast can be multiplied by a constant to convert the weights to integers. For example, multiplying the weights in example 2 converts the weights to 1, 1, and -2 (see row 4 of Table 10). Multiplying the numerator and denominator of the t ratio are multiplied by the same constant. However, a change in the weights of a contrast will affect the point estimate for the contrast, $\hat{\psi}$, as well as its standard error, $SE_{\hat{\psi}}$, both of which are multiplied by the constant. Confidence intervals for the contrast are also affected: The point estimate and width of the interval will be multiplied by the constant. The upper and lower bounds of a confidence interval should be returned to its original scale by dividing the boundary values by the constant. Using the weights 1, 1, and -2 to verify that the value of the t ratio is unaffected by the contrast weights, the whereas in confidence intervals are multiplied by 2.

Most statistical packages will perform the calculations in this section and will do even if group sizes or variances are not equal. For example, SPSS's One-Way ANOVA module (in the Analyze menu) has a Contrast that enables the user to select groups. The process in R is detailed in Box 10.1. To take advantage of available software, the researcher must be comfortable with specifying the weights on each condition and expressing the weights (as integers, SPSS). Finally, if the interest is in constructing confidence intervals and the contrast weights are all integers, it is important to remember to convert the bounds on each interval back to the original scale.
