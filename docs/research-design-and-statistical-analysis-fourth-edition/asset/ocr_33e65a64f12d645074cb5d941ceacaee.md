# 19.8.3 Integrating Analyses of Outlying Points and Influential Points

We have discussed several measures designed to help us detect data points that may have been recorded erroneously or may have a disproportionate influence on our regression statistics, or how are we to use this information? There is no single answer; rather, we must evaluate each case on our own terms. As an example, let's look back at our regression of TC for women in Table 19.5, where case 311 seems to deserve attention. It has not only an Extended Residual of $4.068$, but also a $DFITS$ of $.487$, which is greater than the criterion of $.195$. It also has $DFBETAS$ values of $.452$ and $-397$ for $b_h$ and $b_j$, respectively, which exceed the criterion of $.137$. Cook's distance for case 368 is close to the size of the largest value. When we examine case 311, we find that the data comes from a participant who is relatively young (23 years old), but who has TC (320) and BMI (41.1). Values there has not been any obvious error in recording the data, the cholesterol levels for this participant are over 300 in each season.
We cannot drop a data point from analysis just because we do not like it, but we can assess—and how much—our conclusions would change if the point were excluded. If we redo the regression excluding case 311, the results are the same: the slope changes from $1.71$ to $1.79$, the intercept from $131.87$ to $127.49$, and the correlation between the observed and predicted values from $.51$ to $.54$. In the present example, dropping an outlier did not affect the regression greatly because our analysis was based on a large number of data points. But what if the removal of this case had changed the overall analysis? After checking to make sure that our extreme points did not result from some sort of equipment or error, we could present the results with the extreme points included and without them. If our basic conclusions were supported by both sets of analyses we could feel more confident about them. We could also collect additional data. We could conduct analyses using some form of *robust regression*—the term refers to procedures that are less sensitive to extreme points and some violations of assumptions.
