# 10.3.3 Testing Contrasts When Variances Are Not Equal

To this point, we have assumed that our test populations have the same variance and have therefore used MS$_{s/a}$ in computing the standard error of any contrast. If the assumption of homogeneity of variance is incorrect, we are not justified in using MS$_{s/a}$ doing so may bias the test. In fact, relatively small differences in the variances of the contrasted groups can inflate the Type 1 error.

There are two situations in which MS$_{s/a}$ is an inappropriate error. In one case, the variances corresponding to the conditions in the contrast are very similar but different from the variances of those conditions not included in the contrast (that is, having weight). The standard t is appropriate here, but the denominator should be based on the variances corresponding to the included conditions. Degrees of freedom are lost, due to the omitted group, but the standard error of the contrast is a valid denominator for the t test. For example, if there are three groups with variances 20, 21, and 5, and the means of the first two groups are to be compared, the variance of 5 should not be included in the denominator because the estimate of the standard error of the contrast will be too small, and the Type 1 error will be inflated.

The second situation is one in which there is heterogeneity of variance within the set of means that are to contrasted. In this case, an extension of Welch's test (t'; Welch, 1947; see Section 6.6.2) should be calculated. Recall that when variances are assumed to be homogeneous, the contrast variances are pooled; however, when variances are assumed to be heterogeneous, the variances are not pooled and should be weighted according to the different weights on the contrast. Also, the df are adjusted downward when unequal variances are assumed.
