# 6.2 Design Considerations: Independent Groups or Correlated Scores?

Consider a hypothetical experiment in which we wish to compare the effectiveness of a prescribed diet to reduce total cholesterol (TC) level with a control condition in which no diet has been prescribed. The first issue is how to design the experiment; that is, whether each participant will be tested in only one condition (i.e., control or treatment) or in both conditions. Before analyzing our hypothetical data, we consider the relative merits of these two approaches.

In the independent-groups, or between-subjects, n participants are randomly divided into two groups, usually with the restriction that there are the same number of participants in each condition. In this case, all n participants are independent of each other. In the correlated-scores, or paired-samples, design, each is tested in each of two conditions, or n participants are selected so that one member of each group is matched on one or more relevant dimensions (e.g., age, gender, identity, initial TC level) with one member of the other group. The design in which each participant is tested twice is often referred to as a repeated-measures or within-participants design, and the design involving matching of participants is often referred to as a matched-pair design. In these designs, a difference is calculated between the scores of each participant or between the scores of members of each matched pair, and the n difference scores are analyzed.

When both types of design are feasible, as are in the study of diet on TC the independent-groups design has two advantages. First, it involves more independent observations. Each group provides an independent estimate of the population variance, each estimate is based on n independent scores, and altogether there are 2 independent scores. The correlated-scores design involves only n independent observations because only the n difference scores are independent. As we see in Section 6.3, the power of the t test increases and the confidence interval is narrower as the number of independent observations increases. Therefore - if all things are equal – the independent-groups design has a power advantage. However, all other things are usually not equal when comparing the independent-groups and correlated-scores designs.

A potential advantage of the independent-groups design is practical. Participants are not required to return for a second session as is in the case of the repeated-measures variation of the correlated-scores design, nor is there a need to obtain any additional measures to establish pairs, as in the matched-pairs variation.

Why, then we use a correlated-scores design?
