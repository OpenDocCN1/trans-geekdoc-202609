# 17.5.1 Explaining the Pearson Correlation Coefficient

The Pearson correlation coefficient is a measure of the extent to which quantitative variables are linearly related. We indicated in the previous section that the more tightly the data points are clustered around a straight line, the stronger the degree of linear relationship. However, if we plotted rows, the appearance of the scatterplot and the apparent degree of clustering around the best-fitting straight line would depend on the units in X and Y are measured. This is not true for z scores, which have no units and are defined in terms of the relative standings of scores within their distributions. The correlation coefficient is a measure of similarity of the corresponding z scores of X and Y.

In Chapter 2, we indicated that each member of a set of scores $X_1, X_2, X_3, \dots, X_n$ can be converted to a z score using

```
$z_X = \frac{X - \bar{X}}{s_X} \quad (17.1)$
```

where $\bar{X}$ is the mean of the set of scores and $s_x$ is the standard deviation. The z score corresponds to $X_i$, tells us the number of standard deviations that $X_i$ is above or below the mean of the distribution. We also showed that the mean of a complete set of z scores is zero, and that the standard deviation and variance both have a value of 1.

The Pearson correlation coefficient for two variables, X and Y is given by

```
$r_{XY} = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^N z_{Xi}z_{Yi} \quad (17.2)$
```

The symbol $r$ is used to denote the Pearson correlation coefficient in a sample, and $\rho$ (Greek letter rho) denotes the correlation in a population. The correlation coefficient is basically the average of the products of corresponding z scores (it would be the exact average if we divided by $N-1$ when we obtained the standard deviations of X and Y).
