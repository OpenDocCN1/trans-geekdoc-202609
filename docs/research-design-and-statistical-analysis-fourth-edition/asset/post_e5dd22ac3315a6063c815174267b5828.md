
## 13.6.1 Sphericity Defined

Consider the typical repeated-measures design with $n$ participants, each of whom is tested under a conditions. For each participant, we calculate $(1/2)(a)(a-1)$ differences, based on the number of pairs of treatments. For example, when there are three treatments, we can construct three pairs and calculate the difference between the scores in each pair. We have done in Table 13.5. The left-most three columns of the table contain the scores for five participants at three levels of $A$, and the right-most columns contain all possible difference scores for each participant, with the value of the variances, $s^2_a$, at the bottom of the columns. These data fit the assumption of sphericity because the three values of the variances are the same; sphericity exists when the population variances of all possible difference scores are equal1. If this assumption is violated, Type 1 error rates will be higher than the nominal-level.

Nonnormality, or heterogeneity of difference scores, is similar in both form and consequences to heterogeneity in between-participants designs of Chapters 8-11. In Chapter 8, the error term, $MS_{error}$ was the average of the group variances. The null hypothesis is true and if there are $n$ scores at each treatment level, $E(MS_i) = E$ even if the groups are very different from each other. Even though this heterogeneity of variance does not affect the ratio of expected mean squares, it affects the sampling distribution of the ratio of mean squares. More precisely, when the null hypothesis is true and group sizes are equal, heterogeneity inflates the probability of sampling large F values; the F test is always biased.

We have a similar situation in the repeated-measures design. The error term, $MS_{error}$, the relation between the difference scores and $MS_{error}$ will hold for any number of levels of $A$ and will be true regardless of the values of $s^2_i$. Therefore, $MS_{error}$, and consequently the ratio of expected mean squares, will not be affected by differences in the variances of difference scores. However, the distribution of the F is affected and the Type 1 error rate inflated if the variances are very different, analogous to the case when group variances differ in the between-participants. A test of the sphericity assumption was derived by Mauchly (1940) and is available in many computer packages (for example, in SPSS's Repeated Measures analysis and as part of the anova_test function in R's [stats] package). However, if the population distributions are not normal, Mauchly test will yield significant results when the violation of the sphericity assumption is small (Keselman, Rogan, Mendoza, & Breen, 1980; Rogan, Keselman, & Mendoza, 1979). Therefore, we do not recommend relying on the outcome of Mauchly's test.

As we will see in the next section, one straightforward solution to a violation of sphericity is an adjustment to the degrees of freedom. The magnitude of the adjustment varies continuously with the degree of nonsphericity, which is another reason to avoid relying on the binary outcome of Mauchly's test.

## 13.6.2 Dealing With Nonsphericity

There are two data-analysis strategies that protect the researcher against inflation of Type 1 error rates due to nonsphericity. The first of these is the multivariate analysis of variance, or MANOVA, which is beyond the scope of this book. Harris (2001) and Morrison (2004) are two excellent sources on MANOVA. The second strategy for dealing with nonsphericity is a univariate F test with sphericity-adjusted degrees of freedom. We focus on this latter solution because it is effective and easy to apply.

When the assumption of sphericity is violated, the Type 1 error rate is inflated if the F test is biased. One way to control the Type 1 error rate in this situation is to conduct the F test with degrees that are adjusted. When sphericity is violated, the mean squares is still distributed as F, provided the numerator and denominator of freedom are multiplied by a factor, $\varepsilon$ (epsilon); that is, $df_1 = (a-1)$ and $df_2 = (a-1)(n-1)$. In general, as nonsphericity increases, epsilon and the degrees of freedom decrease, with the result that a larger value of F is required for significance. In this way, the epsilon adjustment compensates for the inflation of Type 1 error rate caused by the failure of sphericity assumption.

One estimator of $\hat{\varepsilon}$ (epsilon-hat) was developed by Box (1954), and later extended by Greenhouse and Geisser (1959). The Greenhouse-Geisser $\hat{\varepsilon}$ tends to be conservative, providing a larger adjustment to the $df$. Subsequently, Huynh and Feldt (1976) developed a more liberal estimator, $\varepsilon$. Both estimators require calculations involving the elements of the variance-covariance matrix. Frequently, common statistical programs do these calculations.

Figure 13.2 presents the results of ANOVA on the data from the [stats] package. At the top of the figure, where depression levels vary significantly over the season, $F(3, 39) = 3.001, p = .042$. Mauchly's test of sphericity is identically nonsignificant in this example, $p = .408$. Nonetheless, we see two estimators of epsilon in the lower section of Figure 13.2. The Greenhouse-Geisser ($GGe, \hat{\varepsilon}$) indicates that the sphericity assumption is violated, and this causes a reduction in the degrees of freedom required to evaluate the F statistic; the adjusted $df$ are reported with the “Sphericity Corrections” as $DF=2.49, 32.43$. However, consistent with the results of the Mauchly test, the Huynh-Feldt ($HFe, \hat{\varepsilon}$) estimate is actually greater than 1 (it would be reported as 1; there are no “free” degrees of freedom). Using the $H-F$ estimate of $\varepsilon$, the variance among the mean depression scores for the four seasons is significant at the .05 level.
