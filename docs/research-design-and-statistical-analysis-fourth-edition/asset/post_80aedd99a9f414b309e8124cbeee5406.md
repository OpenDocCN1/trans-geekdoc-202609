There are several ways to calculate the quartiles. The definition usually found in introductory statistics feels often awkward, requiring linear interpolation. Somewhat simpler to calculate are the hinges (Tukey, 1977); these are used by the boxplot function in R, and in SPSS, for the top and bottom of the box. As an example of their calculation, consider the Royer data of Table 2.1. Then take the following steps.

- 1. Find the location, or *depth*, of the median; $d_m = (n+1)/2$. With 28 scores, $d_m = 29/2 = 14.5$. When $d_m$ has a fractional value – that is, when $n$ is an even number – drop the fraction. We use brackets to represent the integer; that is, $[d_m] = 14$.
- 2. Find the depth of the lower and upper hinges, $d_{LH}$ and $d_{UH}$. These are simply the medians of the lower and upper 14 scores. The depth of the lower hinge is

$$d_{LH} = ([d_M|] + 1)/2 = (14 + 1)/2 = 7.5$$

and the depth of the upper hinge

$$d_{UH} = (n+1) - d_{LH} = (28 + 1) - 7.5 = 21.5$$

The depth of 7.5 for the lower hinge means that the lower hinge is midway between the seventh score (76) and the eighth score (82), or 79. The depth of the upper hinge is 21.5, which is midway between the seventh and eighth scores from the top; corresponding score is 94.5 in the Royer data. The length of the box in Figure 2.2 is therefore 94.5–79, or 15.5. This distance between the hinges is usually referred to as the H-spread, to signify the spread of scores between the hinges. The box-plot statistics can also be expressed in percentiles. In that case, quartiles are computed by linear interpolation and the values of the first and third quartiles are 77.5 and 94.75.

## The Median

The horizontal line between the top and bottom of the box represents the median. The median is the middle score in an ordered set of scores. In the data of Table 2.1, in which the number of scores, $n$, is an even number, the median falls halfway between the middle two scores. Those scores are the 14th and 15th positions when the scores are ordered from lowest to highest. If the two scores had differed, the median would have been computed as the mean of the two values.

The median is useful in an initial exploration of data because it is not affected by extreme scores. Unlike the mean, value change if any individual score is changed, the median is considered a *resistant* or *robust* statistic because it is unaffected by changes in the value of any single score. For example, if we replaced the 47 in the data set of Table 2.1 with a score of 67, the median would be unchanged, but the mean would be increased. Because of this property of the median, there are circumstances in which it provides a more representative index of the location of a distribution than does mean. For example, if the salaries of corporate officers are included in representing mean salaries at a company, we may have a distorted sense of how everyone is paid because the officers' salaries will greatly increase the mean.

## The Whiskers

The lines extending vertically from the box are often called whiskers, and they extend to the lowest and highest scores that are not outliers. Note that the median is closer to the top of the box than to the bottom and the top whisker is shorter than the bottom one. This reflects the strangling tail that we reflected in the histogram of Figure 2.1. In other words, both figures illustrate that the data fall in a relatively narrow range between 89 and 100, whereas the remaining half are spread over a wider range of scores; the distribution is asymmetric or skewed.

## Outliers

The circles in the box represent outliers, the two scores of 50 and the score of 47 in Table 2.1. Notice that only two circles are shown, even though there are three outliers. That is simply because the two points reflecting the scores of 50 appear in exactly the same location, superimposed on one another. The step to define an outlier is:

- 1. Calculate the H-spread. This is 94.5–79, or 15.5, in the present example.
- 2. Find 1.5 times the H-spread; this is (1.5)(15.5) = 23.25.
- 3. Subtract the preceding result (23.25) from the bottom of the box and add it to the top limit. In the current example, these values are 79–23.25 = 55.75 and 94.5 + 23.25 = 117.75.
- 4. An outlier is any score beyond those limits. Therefore, the scores of 47 and 50 are outliers. Extreme outliers are scores that are more than three times the H-spread above or below the hinges. SPSS displays outliers with a circle and extreme outliers with an asterisk. In R's boxplot function in the [graphics] package, the range parameter is the multiplier on the H-spread. It has a default value of 1.5 (as in step 2 of defining an outlier) and can be set to 3.0 to identify outliers.

Outliers are important to detect because they often correspond to cases that merit special attention. In the current example, they draw attention to three students who are performing much worse than any other student in the group of second-grade males. These require remedial aid, or there may be causes of their poor performance that require investigation.

Outliers are also to detect in studies where the goal is to draw inferences about the population from which the sample is drawn. Because many statistical procedures assume that the data are normally distributed, inferences drawn from the sample can be suspect. It is important to be aware of the distribution, as the outliers may indicate a lack of normality.

### 2.2.3 A Graphic Check on Normality

Because many commonly used statistical procedures assume the data were normally distributed, it is helpful to have ways of looking at possible violations of this assumption. Specific characteristics of a distribution imply nonnormality. For example, because the normal distribution is symmetric, skewness (asymmetry) in a data plot indicates a distribution that is not normal. Another indication is a nonzero value of kurtosis (roughly, more, or fewer, scores in the tails than we expect in a normal distribution). However, a more direct indication is available in many computing packages. These programs rank the scores and then the standardized scores (z scores; see 2.4 for further discussion). Figure 2.3 presents two plots. Using R's qnorm function in the [stats] package, we first multiplied response times in the Roy data for students in the fifth through eighth grades. If data are normally distributed, the points will fall on a straight line. This is clearly not the case for the multiplication response times (RT). However, if we transform the multiplication times into response times by taking the reciprocals of the responses, we obtain the second plot shown in Figure 2.3. The multiplication speeds, except for a few scores at extreme, do quite close to the line, indicating that the response speeds are more nearly distributed than the response times. Implicit in this illustration is a possible strategy for dealing with situations where we desire normally distributed observations, but the data do not conform to the distribution; namely, we can sometimes transform the data.
