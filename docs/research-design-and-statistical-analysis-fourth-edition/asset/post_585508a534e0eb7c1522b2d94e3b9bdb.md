## 8.8 Assumptions Underlying the F Test

In Chapter 6, we discussed the consequences of violations of assumptions of independence, normality, and homogeneity of variance for the F test and considered several remedies including transformations, tests that take heterogeneous variances into account, trimming the data, and tests based on ranks. The F test in one-factor between-participants design runs on assumptions and in addition the data should be consistent with the underlying model. In the following sections, we consider each assumption in turn, describing both the consequences of violations as well as the remedies proposed in Chapter 6.

### 8.8.1 The Structural Model

The analysis of variance for one-factor design begins with the structural model of Equation 8.3. This equation implies that only one factor systematically influences the data, and $MS_e$ represents random error. However, researchers sometimes ignore factors that have been manipulated but are of interest. If those factors contribute significant variability, the one-factor model is not valid for the research design. Common examples are when gender effects are ignored, different experimenters are responsible for half of the data collection, or the position is counterbalanced. While these variables may be relevant to the purpose of the research, they may still influence the scores. So, $MS_e$ represents both error and variance due to gender, or position. For example, if our sample includes an equal number of participants in each group, variance due to "irrelevant" variables will not contribute to $MS_e$. The analysis based on one-factor model then violates the principle that the numerator and denominator of F ratio have the same expectation when $H_0$ is true. In such situations, the denominator has a larger expectation because the irrelevant variable has a large effect. The result is a loss of power, which can be considerable. The F test is negatively biased in this case, meaning that the Type I error rate will be less than its nominal value if the null hypothesis is true. Generally, researchers should formulate a complete structural model which incorporates all relevant factors, even those thought to be irrelevant to the independent variable of interest (or position), carrying out the analysis described in Chapter 9.

### 8.8.2 The Independence Assumption

When only one observation is obtained from each participant and participants are randomly assigned to treatments or randomly sampled from distinct populations, the assumption that the scores are independently distributed is likely to be met. However, there are exceptions that sometimes are not recognized by researchers. For example, suppose we want to compare attitudes of older and college students. The scores of individuals who were part of the same discussion group will tend to be potentially correlated. Another example comes from educational research, where disruptive children can affect learning for other students in the classroom (Glass & Stanley, 1970). If these failures of the independence assumption are ignored (as has been in some studies; Anderson & Anger, 1978), there will be a positive bias — inflation of Type I error rate — in the F test of the effect (Myers, DiCecco & Lorch, 1981; Myers & Well, 1995). A class of analyses referred to as multilevel or hierarchical (e.g., Raudenbusch & Bryk, 2002) provides a general approach to this type of data analysis.

### 8.8.3 The Normality Assumption

Violations of the normality assumption are relatively common and merit attention because they can reduce the power of the F test.

#### Consequences of Violating the Normality Assumption

The Type I error probability associated with the F test is often described to be little affected by sampling from nonnormal populations unless the samples are small and the departure from normality is extremely marked (e.g., Donaldson, 1968; Lindquist, 1953, pp. 78–90; Scheffé, 1959). This is true when the independent variable is discretely distributed, as is when rating data or response frequencies are analyzed. In all the most skewed distributions, simulation studies indicate that Type I error rates are relatively unaffected when such measures are submitted to an analysis of variance or if the distributions are highly similar across groups (Bevan, Denton, & Myers, 1974; Hsu & Feldt, 1969; Lunney, 1970).

Although in many instances the Type I error rate is unaffected by departures from normality, loss of power and reduction of effect size are concerns when distributions are long- or heavy-tailed (kurtotic), skewed, or include outliers. In each of these situations, variability is high relative to the normal distribution. Thus, procedures that address the increased variability often have more power than the conventional F test. Several potential remedies, familiar from Chapter 6, are considered next.

#### Dealing With Violations of Normality Assumption: Tests Based on Trimmed Data

As explained in Chapter 6, merely deleting scores is not a valid procedure. However, a ratio of mean squares distributed approximately as F can be constructed by an approach like the trimmed t test illustrated in Chapter 7 (see Section 7.7). In essence, we estimate the variance of the means from the trimmed data, and we use the winsorized data to estimate the error variance. An example should help us understand how this is done. Table 8.7 presents groups of 11 scores; each $Y$ are the original values, sorted in ascending order within each group. Recognizing the presence of some outliers in each group tail, we trimmed the lowest and highest scores in each group, yielding the $T$ data. We then replaced the deleted scores with the closest remaining scores to create the winsorized $W$ set. This is illustrated in Box 8.2.

### Box 8.2 The Trimmed F Test Applied to the Data of Table 8.7

After performing the ANOVA of the T and W scores, we do the following:

- 1. From the ANOVA of the T data, the between-groups mean square $MS_{tk}$ (tk refers to trimming $k$ scores from each tail); $MS_{tk} = 9.19$.
- 2. From the ANOVA of the W data, the within-sum of squares $SS_{wk}$; $SS_{wk} = 27.455$. This is divided by the degrees of freedom for the trimmed data, $a(n-1-2k) = (3)(10-4) = 18$. The winsorized error mean square, $MS_{wk}$ is $27.455/18 = 1.523$.
- 3. The trimmed F statistic is $F = MS_{tk}/MS_{wk} = 9.19/1.523 = 6.03$, and is distributed on 2 and 18 df. Then $p = .01$.
