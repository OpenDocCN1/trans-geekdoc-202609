## Chapter 4: Developing the Fundamentals of Hypothesis Testing Using the Binomial Distribution

### 4.1 Overview

Our goal in conducting research is to use our data as a window on the population. To make this generalization, we must have a way to distinguish from the sample. The procedures collectively known as inferential statistics. Most of the subsequent chapters are concerned with two general inference procedures: Testing specific hypotheses about population parameters and estimating population parameters. The focus of the current chapter is on developing the logic of hypothesis testing.

In Chapter 3, we established that probability theory is central to the decision-making of inferential statistics. To elaborate that role and to facilitate presentation of of the abstract concepts, we will continue to use our ESP experiment as an example. We again have Rachel choosing among four cards in a 20-trial experiment. Because we are skeptics, we are unwilling to conclude that Rachel has ESP unless guessing is ruled out as a reasonable explanation. If Rachel randomly guesses on each trial, the probability of a correct response is 1/4 or .25. Therefore, finding five correct responses on the 20 trials is consistent with chance performance and would provide no evidence for ESP. But suppose that 7/20 or .35 of Rachel's responses were correct. This is different enough to convince us that she performed better than what could be expected by guessing? If not, how many correct responses would it take to convince us? 10/20? 15/20? 20/20? How are we to select a criterion level of performance to decide that Rachel is not simply?

We must develop a conceptual framework to answer this question, and the first sections of this chapter provide that Rachel achieved a .35 success rate in the sample of 20 trials. In addressing the issue of ESP, we are concerned with the entire population of potential responses that characterizes Rachel's ESP ability, not just the 20 responses we happened to collect in the experiment. If we ran the experiment again, it would almost certainly turn out differently. This is the fundamental obstacle to making correct inferences from a sample to a population: Because our observations are subject to sampling error, any conclusion we make about the population may be wrong.
