# 19.3.2 Obtaining a Confidence Interval for a New Value of Y at X_j

We showed how to find a confidence interval for the conditional mean of the Y scores at $X = \bar{X}$. We showed how to find a confidence interval for the Y score of a *new individual* who has $X = X_j$. That is, we wish to estimate one *scores* from the population of scores with mean $\mu_{Y,X}$, where $Y_{new} = \mu_{Y,X} + \\epsilon$. In our college admissions example, this situation is relevant to decide about the admission of a particular applicant to the college. The estimate of the conditional mean, $\hat{\mu}_{Y,X} = b_0 + b_1X_j$, is an unbiased estimate of $Y_{new}$. Therefore, if we wish to find the confidence interval for $Y_{new}$, the variability associated with $\hat{\mu}_{Y,X}$ contributes to our error estimate. We must also consider the variance of the individual Y scores around the conditional mean. By combining these two sources of *variability*, $s_{Y,X}^2 + s_{Y,X}^2b_j^2 = s_{new}^2(1+b_j^2)$, so the standard error of $Y_{new}$ is given by $s_{Y,X}\sqrt{1+b_j^2}$ where $b_j$, the leverage, was defined by Equation 19.1.

For the search experiment data, the predicted reaction for a new participant with array size of 4 is $381.90 + (23.92)(4) = 477.58$, as the predicted conditional mean. The estimated standard error

```
$31.452 \sqrt{1 + \frac{(4-5)^2}{100}} = 32.38$
```

so that the 95% confidence interval for $Y_{new}$ is $477.58 \pm (2.101)(32.38) = 477.58 \pm 68.03$. This interval is much wider than the 95% confidence interval for the conditional mean that we previously found to be $477.58 \pm 16.19$: predicting an individual is harder than predicting a mean.

Note that this 95% confidence interval does not allow us to conclude that 95% of the population of Y scores corresponding to $X = 4$ is $477.58 \pm 68.03.
