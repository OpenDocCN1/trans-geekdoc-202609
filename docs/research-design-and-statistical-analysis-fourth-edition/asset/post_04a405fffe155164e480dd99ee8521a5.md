## 4.4.2 Confidence Intervals for the Independent-Groups Design

The general form of the confidence interval, with confidence level $1-\alpha$, is

```
$P[V - (t_{df,\alpha/2V}V) \leq E(V) \leq V + (t_{df,\alpha/2V}V)] = 1-\alpha \quad (6.10)$
```

and the confidence limits are

```
CI = V \pm (t_{df_{\alpha/2}}S_V) \quad (6.11)$
```

In other words, the CI is the observed statistic of interest +/- a critical value multiplied by a measure of variability for the statistic. For our TC example, df = (2)(36 - 1), or 70, and assuming we want 95% confidence, alpha/2 = .025. Then Equation 6.11 becomes

```
CI = (\tilde{Y}_1 - \tilde{Y}_2) \pm (t_{70,.025}S_{\tilde{Y}_1-\tilde{Y}_2}) \quad (6.12)$
```

The difference between means is 224.50 – 207.71 = 16.99 and the standard error (Section 6.4.1) is 10.31. Turning to Appendix Table C.3, in R or tdf.T(.975, 70) in SPSS (Box 6.1), we find that the critical t value is 1.99. Putting our values into Equation 6.12, we obtain the bounds for the 95% confidence interval:

```
CI = 16.79 \pm(1.99)(10.31) = -3.77, 37.35
```

Because these bounds encompass a value of zero we know that a two-tailed test at the .05 alpha-level will not reject the null hypothesis of no difference between the diet and control means. Also, the fact that the confidence interval is quite wide suggests that our test of the hypothesis may be low in power, which we will explore further in this chapter.

## 6.4.3 Interpreting Confidence Intervals

Section 5.5 described the interpretation of confidence intervals in detail. These points are briefly reviewed here because confidence intervals are often misinterpreted. Specifically, the following points:

- Ninety-five percent confidence does not mean that there is a .95 probability that the computed bounds enclose the true value of $\mu_{control} - \mu_{diet}$. The bounds either enclose the population difference or they do not.
- Ninety-five percent confidence does not mean that if the experiment were repeated many times, 95% of the observed differences between means would lie between -3.771 and 37.353, the bounds calculated from the TC data.
- Instead, ninety-five percent confidence means that if the experiment is repeated many times, and a new set of bounds is calculated each time, 95% of those bounds would contain the true value of $\mu_{control} - \mu_{diet}$.

In summary, calculating a confidence interval accomplishes several things:

- The CI provides a range of plausible values for the parameter being estimated. Higher confidence levels result in wider confidence intervals.
- The CI width provides an index of precision of our estimate that is sensitive to the level of confidence, the variability, and the sample size.
- The CI provides a test of an infinite set of null hypotheses. Those hypothesized values falling outside the interval can be rejected at $\alpha = .05$, whereas those inside the interval cannot.

## 6.4.4 The Student's t in the Independent-Groups Design

A direct test of $H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0$ against the hypothesized alternative $H_1: \mu_1 - \mu_2 \neq 0$ follows from the preceding developments. The test statistic follows from Equation 6.1 is

```
$t = \frac{\bar{Y}_1 - \bar{Y}_2}{\hat{S}_{Y_1-Y_2}} \quad (6.13)$
```

Substituting numerical values, we obtain $t = 16.79 / 10.31 = 1.63$. This value of $t$ is less than the critical value, that we cannot reject the null hypothesis. The exact $p$-value is .108 (see Section 6.4.5). Note that this implies a one-tailed test of the .05 level would also fail to reject $H_0$, because $p$ would equal .054 for that test. The validity of the confidence interval and the $t$ statistic we calculated rests on certain assumptions. In Section 6.6 describe procedures to respond to their violations.

## 6.5 Data Analyses inrelated-Scores Design

For reasons earlier in this chapter, many comparisons of conditions are made in a correlated-scores design in which each participant is tested in two conditions, or participants are matched on dimension(s) to the dependent variable. We illustrate the analyses of correlated-scores data with an artificial set of 36 pairs of total cholesterol (TC) scores.

The data file Table 6_TC_CS.xlsx (from the page on the book's website) has three columns. The first, labeled Before, contains the TC scores for the 36 subjects before any treatment. The second column, labeled After, contains scores for the same individuals after one year on a diet prescribed by the researcher. The third column of scores, labeled Change, contains the difference in TC level, obtained by subtracting the After scores from the Before scores for each participant.

## 6.5.1 Confidence Intervals in the Correlated-Scores Design

Our estimate of the mean of the population of change scores is $\bar{Y}_{change}$, the average of the 36 change scores. From Table 6.2, this is 16.33. In order to calculate confidence interval bounds on the mean of the population change scores ($\hat{\mu}_{change}$), we need this value, also the standard deviation of the 36 change ($s_{change}$), the number of changes ($n$), and the critical value of $t$. Calculations of confidence intervals and significance tests follow the procedures developed in Chapter 5, except that the critical value is obtained from the $t$ rather than the normal probability distribution. The general form of the confidence interval in the one-sample is

```
$p \left[ \frac{\bar{Y} - t_{n-1,\alpha/2}s}{\sqrt{n}} \leq \mu \leq \frac{\bar{Y} + t_{n-1,\alpha/2}s}{\sqrt{n}} \right] = 1 - \alpha \quad (6.14)$
```

where $\bar{Y}$ is the sample mean, $s/sqrt{n}$ is the $SE$ of the mean, and $t_{n-1,\alpha/2}$ is the value of $t$ such that $\alpha/2$ of the distribution on $n-1$ df lies to the right of it. In the example of the 36 change scores, $df = 35$, and therefore the critical $t$ value for a 95% confidence interval is 2.03.
