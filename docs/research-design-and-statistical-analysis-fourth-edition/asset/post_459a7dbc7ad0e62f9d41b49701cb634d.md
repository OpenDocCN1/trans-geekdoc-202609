
One Factor-Between-Participants Designs are often preferred (e.g., Kroes & Finley, 2023). We turn to such estimates, bearing in mind that our results rest on the assumptions underlying the derivation of the EMS; i.e., independence of the $\epsilon_{ij}$ and homogeneity of variance.

## 8.5.2 Estimating Strength of Association in the Population: $\omega^2$ (Omega-Squared)

Where $\eta^2$ describes the sample- and design-specific strength of association between the dependent and independent variables by forming a ratio of sum of squares, $\omega^2$ is a measure of the strength of association in the population; unlike $\eta^2$, it is a ratio of population variances:

$$\omega^2 = \frac{\sigma_A^2}{\sigma_\epsilon^2 + \sigma_A^2}$$

The numerator of the ratio is the variance of the treatment population means (the $\mu_i$) or,

$$\sigma_A^2 = \frac{\sum_1^a (\mu_i - \mu)^2}{a}$$

The denominator of $\omega^2$ is the total population variance; that is, the treatment error variance, $\sigma_\epsilon^2$, plus the variance of the treatment population means, $\sigma_A^2$. Thus, $\omega^2$ assesses the magnitude of the treatment effect relative to the total variance in the design. We cannot know the ratio described by Equation 8.10 but we can derive estimates of $\sigma_A^2$ and $\sigma_\epsilon^2$. We begin with the EMS equations of Table 8.2:

$$E(MS_A) = \sigma_\epsilon^2 + n\theta_A^2$$

and

$$E(MS_{S/A}) = \sigma_\epsilon^2$$

To obtain an estimate of $\sigma_A^2$ we first extract Equation 8.13 from Equation 8.12, and divide by $n$; then we have

$$\frac{MS_A - MS_{S/A}}{n} = \hat{\theta}_A^2$$

where the "hat" above $\theta_A^2$ means "an estimate of." of $\omega^2$ Equation 8.10 involves $\sigma_A^2$, not $\theta_A^2$, and noting that $\sigma_A^2 = [(a-1)/a] \times \theta_A^2$, our estimate of $\sigma_A^2$ is

$$\hat{\sigma}_A^2 = \frac{a-1}{a} \left( \frac{MS_A - MS_{S/A}}{n} \right)$$

We have estimates of the numerator and denominator of $\omega^2$, therefore, substituting into Equation 8.10, we estimate of $\omega^2$ for the one-factor-between-participants design:

$$\omega^2 = \frac{(a-1)/a(n)MS_{SA} - MS_{S/A}}{[(a-1)/a(n)MS_{SA} - MS_{S/A}) + MS_{S/A}]} \quad (8.15)$$

Okada (2013) has shown that $\omega^2$ very slightly underestimates $\omega^2$, with good efficiency and consistency.

We write Equation 8.15 in a different form, which allows us to calculate $\omega^2$ from knowledge of the $F$ ratio, $a$, and $n$. The advantages are that the expression is simpler and, more importantly, most research reports contain this, we can estimate the strength of association for data calculated other investigators. By defining $F = MS_{SA}/MS_{S/A}$, multiplying the numerator and denominator of Equation 8.15 by $a$, and dividing by $MS_{S/A}$, we have

$$\omega^2 = \frac{(a-1)(F_A-1)}{(a-1)(F_A-na)} \quad (8.16)$$

Let's review Equations 8.15 and 8.16 represent. If we replicate the experiment many times, the average value of the right-hand term will approximately equal $\omega^2$, the portion of the total variance is attributable to the variance of their means. We say "approximately equal" because the expected value of a ratio is not the same as the expected values. The expression is reasonably accurate and the expression is much simpler than for the exact experiment.

One other equation 8.16 should be noted. Because the numerator and denominator of the $F$ reflect two independent estimates of the population error variance, the null hypothesis is true or the effects of $A$ are very small, the $F$ may be less than 1. Then, $\omega^2$ would be less than 0. Since a variance cannot be negative, we conclude that $\omega^2 = 0$; that is, none of the total variance is attributable to the independent variable.

We can apply Equation 8.16 to the memory data in Table 8.1. In this experiment, $a=4$, $n=10$, and (from Table 8.3) $F=3.220$, then, inserting these values into Equation 8.16,

$$\omega^2 = \frac{(3)(2.23)}{(3)(2.23)+40} = .143$$

This is very close to the value of 0.146 noted earlier for adjusted $R^2_{adj}$. That of $R^2_{adj}$ and $\omega^2$ are close is no unusual; Maxwell et al. (1981) reported that the two rarely differ by more than02. With respect to assessing the importance of each measure, Cohen (1988) suggested that values of .01, .06, and .14 may be viewed as small, medium, and large, respectively. According to those guidelines, the proportion of variability accounted for by the study may be judged to be large. Again, however, we caution that the importance attached to each value must be assessed in the context of the research problem and the investigator's knowledge of the literature.

## 8.5.3 Cohen's f

In Chapter 6, we presented Cohen's $d$, a measure of the standardized effect for designs in which two means are compared. Cohen's $f$ (1988) is a similar measure for situations in which the variance of more than two means is interest. The parameter $f$ is defined as

$$f = \frac{\sigma_A}{\sigma_e} \quad (8.17)$$

We estimate $f$ by substituting the estimate in Equation 8.14 in the numerator and $MS_{S/A}$ in the denominator. Then we have

$$\hat{f} = \sqrt{\frac{(a-1)(MS_{SA}-MS_{S/A})}{anMS_{S/A}}} \quad (8.18)$$

can also be written as

$$\hat{f} = \sqrt{\frac{(a-1)(F_A - 1)}{an}} \quad (8.19)$$

For the data of Table 8.1, substituting the $F$ value from Table 8.3 into Equation 8.19, we have

$$\hat{f} = \sqrt{\frac{3(2.23)}{40}} = .409$$

Cohen has suggested values of $f$ of .1, .25, and .4 be viewed as small, medium, and large. Therefore, as $\omega^2$ and $f$ lead to the same conclusion about the size of effects follows the relationship between them; given an estimate of $f$, we can also calculate an estimate of $\omega^2$, and vice versa. The relations are

$$f^2 = \frac{\omega^2}{1-\omega^2} \quad \text{and} \quad \omega^2 = \frac{f^2}{1+f^2}$$

A useful property of $f$ is that it is closely related to the noncentrality parameter of the $F$ distribution; specifically,

$$\lambda = Nf^2 \quad (8.20)$$

The parameter, $\lambda$ (lambda), determines areas under the noncentral $F$ distribution, and therefore the power of the $F$ test. The relation between $f$, $\lambda$, and power will be developed further in Section 8.7.

## 8.5.4 Measures of Importance: Limitations

In an introductory chapter to an edited edition hastily published, “What if there no significance tests?” Harlow (1997, pp. 5–6) reported that 11 of the book's chapters “were very much in favor” of reporting measures as $R^2$, $\omega^2$, and the remaining two contributors “at least endorse such.” Similar support for measures as these can be found in the American Psychological Association's guidelines for statistics (Wilkinson & Task Force, 1999), which urge researchers to report effect size statistics. Nevertheless, there are potential pitfalls. Values of these statistics may depend on the experimental design, the choice and number of levels of the independent variable, the dependent variable, and the population sampled. Estimates of $\omega^2$ and $f$ assume homogeneous variances and independence of observations (cf. Grissom & Kim, 2001), who discuss the variance assumption and suggest alternative approaches for the two-group case). Another concern is that squared coefficients tend to be small, and it is sometimes enough to dismiss an effect as trivial because of a small value of $\omega^2$.

These arguments suggest that we must be careful when interpreting these measures, generalizing the results of any one, or making comparisons across studies that differ with respect to the factors just cited. In addition, we should treat guidelines as those set by Cohen (1988) as labels, not as definitive boundaries between important and unimportant effects. Even a small advantage of therapy over another may be important. In theoretical work, a small effect predicted by a theory may be important support for that. Funder and Ozer (2019) provide many psychological examples that help ground our understanding of effect sizes. In summary, if care is taken in interpreting measures of strength, such as $f$ and $\omega^2$ are useful additions to the test statistics.
