We used the Shapiro-Wilk test to examine possible violations of the normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions.(In R, use `dplyr` to first `group_by`(Organization, Delay) and then `summarise`(SW = shapiro.test(Recall)$p) to see the p-values of the Shapiro-Wilk test for each condition). In eight of the nine groups, the data departed significantly from normality. However, the groups are large enough that we assume that the ratios of mean squares will be distributed approximately as F.

To examine the possible violation of the homogeneity-of-variance assumption, we used the Brown-Forsythe test of heterogeneity of variance based on absolute deviations over the group means (see Section 8.8.4), finding the p-value to be .075. Considering that the ratio of the largest to the smallest of the nine groups is only about 2 to 1 (see Table 11.1), and with 8 and 189 df's tests are powerful enough to detect trivial differences among population variances, we conclude that variance heterogeneity is not a problem.

# 11.4.2 The ANOVA

Table 11.2 contains the results of analysis of variance of the recall scores. The effects of organization and delay are both significant, whereas the interaction p-value falls short of the .05 level. The η² values are ratios of the effect sum of squares to the sum of effect and error sums of squares and the F estimates calculated using the formulas in Table 9.13. These statistics reinforce that the main effects contribute most of the variance among the nine cells. By Cohen's guidelines, according which f = .4 indicates a large effect, both organization and delay make large contributions to the population variance whereas their interaction contribution falls about halfway between small (f = .1) and medium (f = .25).

Let us analyze these effects further for a better understanding of the causes of the differences between organizations. Although the interaction of delay and organization failed to reach significance, we will carry out our planned comparison of the delay effects in the Attribute condition against the average of the Name and Random organizations. This test is justified because it was planned in advance of data collection (see Section 10.6); we had looked at the data before developing the hypothesis, then the post hoc test would capitalize on risk and inflating the Type 1 error rate.
