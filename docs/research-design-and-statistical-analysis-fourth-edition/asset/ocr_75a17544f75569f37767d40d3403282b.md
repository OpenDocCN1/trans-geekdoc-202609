The example also demonstrates that, depending on the context, even a small correlation can correspond to an important effect. In the example, the value of the correlation between treatment and survival is .2; that is, only .2² = .04 of the variance in survival is accounted for by the treatment. Yet this small correlation corresponds to a 20% increase in survival rate when the ϕ coefficient is administered, a difference that is obviously important.
The ϕ coefficient is closely related to the $\chi^2$ test for independence, and we can show that

```
$$\chi_1^2 = N\phi^2 \quad (18.13)$
```

The $\chi^2$ test with 1 $df$ can be used to test the hypothesis that X and Y are independent in the population, whereas the ϕ coefficient (or $\phi^2$, which can be interpreted as the proportion of variance accounted for) can be used as a measure of the strength of the relationship between X and Y.
Note that with large samples, even very small effects may be significant. Using Equation 18.13 and Appendix C.4 (or qchisq(95, df = 1 in R)), we find that we can reject the null hypothesis that X and Y are independent at $\alpha = .05$, if $\chi^2 = N\phi^2 > 3.84$ or $\phi > 3.84 / N$. It follows that for $N=1000$, we would be able to reject the null hypothesis of independence even if $\phi$ was only .0384, so that $\phi = .062$. In this case the "significant" correlation would only occur for about-third of 1% of the variance.

# 18.5.2 Correlation Coefficient for Ranked Data: Spearman rho

Sometimes we want to obtain correlations for data that occur in the form of ranks. We may, for example, have two judges rank a set of stimuli according to some quality, and obtain the correlation between the two sets of rankings as a measure of reliability. Even if X and Y are continuous variables, we may convert to ranks, because we do not believe that equal differences in the X/or Y scores necessarily correspond to equal differences in the underlying variable that is measured or we desire measures that are more resistant to the outliers than the Pearson $r$.
The special case of the Pearson $r$ for ranked data is referred to as the Spearman correlation coefficient ($r_s$) or sometimes as the Spearman rho coefficient. Although the value of the Spearman can always be obtained by applying any of the Spearman $r$ formula to the ranked data, one frequently encounters a simple formula that takes advantage of the characteristics of ranks. If there are no ties, the $N$ scores are the integers 1 to $N$. Therefore, the mean of a set of $N$ ranks is $(N+1)/2$, and the variance of the ranks can be shown to be $N(N+1)/12$. Substituting these expressions into the Pearson $r$ formula and simplifying yields

```
$r_s = 1 - \frac{D^2}{N(N^2-1)} \quad (18.14)$
```

where $D_i$ is the difference between the X and Y ranks for the ith case. An example of a calculation using Equation 18.14 is given in Table 18.3.
