Another possible predictor of interest is education level. Although we have only a crude measure of education (see the description in the Materials section), it is significantly correlated with leisure-time activity and negatively correlated with BMI, poorness of health, depression, and occupational physical activity, for groups of participants. If we add education to the list of predictors in Table 25.4, its regression coefficient is not significant and the pattern of significance for the other predictors is not changed for men or women. However if we regress BMI on education and the three measures of physical activity, we see in Table 25.5 that education is a significant predictor of depression for both men and women, but, controlling for education level, none of the regression coefficients for the activity measures are significant.

So, what we can conclude with the other activity measures, leisure-time physical activity is a significant predictor of lower BMI scores for women. However, our other analyses show that the predictive power of leisure-time exercise may come about because of its relation with health, education, BMI, and the other measures of activity. Leisure-time physical activity may have therapeutic effects with respect to depression, but it is extremely difficult to disentangle the effects of other variables we have considered in this observational study. If we define normative answers to questions about the benefits of leisure exercise, we should design an experiment in which participants are assigned to conditions that correspond to different amounts of activity level and the effects of variables such as education, BMI, and perceived health are controlled by randomization or by using blocking including them as independent variables in a multi-factor design.
