# 30 Research Design and Statistical Analysis

Let's demonstrate how means are combined, using the data in Table 2.3, which represent hypothetical depression scores from four different clinics. It is tempting to add the four means and divide by 4 to get the mean of the combined data sets. However, because the four sample sizes vary, this won't. The mean for Clinic C should carry more weight and for Clinic B less weight in combining the means because of their relative sample sizes. The correct approach is to obtain the sum of all the scores in four data sets and then divide by N, the sum of the ns. We obtain the sum of scores for each clinic by multiplying the mean by n. Summing these fours, and dividing the total by N, the total number of scores, we have the weighted mean of all scores:

```
$$\hat{Y} = [(26 \times 17.5) + (17 \times 18.3) + (31 \times 19.2) + (24 \times 22.6)] / 98 = 19.426$
```

We might have rewritten this:

```
$$\hat{Y} = \frac{26}{98}(17.5) + \frac{17}{98}(18.3) + \frac{31}{98}(19.2) + \frac{24}{98}(22.6)$
```

This equation suggests that the mean can be represented as a sum of weighted values, where the weights are proportions or probabilities. The weight for Clinic A is 26/98 because the 98 depression scores come from Clinic A. To take somewhat different example, consider a student who has spent two semesters in College A and compiles a 3.2 average. She then transfers to College B, where she earns a 3.8 average for the next three semesters. The student's overall grade point average (GPA) for the five semesters is calculated as in the preceding example of the clinic means. The overall GPA is a weighted mean where the weights are 2/5 and 3/5:

```
$$\hat{Y} = (2 / 5)(3.2) + (3 / 5)(3.8) = 3.56$
```

In general, the preceding calculations may be represented by

```
$$\hat{Y} = \sum p(y) \cdot y$                               (2.3)
```

Equation 2.3 is the formula for a weighted mean. It indicates that each distinct value of Y is to be multiplied by its weight, $p(y)$, the proportion of all scores with that value. All of these products are added together. Note that the usual expression for the arithmetic mean, $\bar{Y} = \sum Y/n$, is a special case of the preceding formula for the weighted mean; here each of the $n$ scores in the sample is given a weight of $1/n$.
