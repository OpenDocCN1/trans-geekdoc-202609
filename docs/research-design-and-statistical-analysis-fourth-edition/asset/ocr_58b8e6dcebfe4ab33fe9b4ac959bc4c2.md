# 19.7.1 Checking Regression Assumptions Using Residuals

Valuable information about whether the assumptions of the regression model are valid may be obtained by studying residuals; that differences between observed and predicted Y. The residuals, $e_i = Y - \hat{Y} = Y - (b_0 + b_1X)$, provide information about the population error components, $\epsilon_i = Y - (eta_0 + eta_1X)$. Statistical software generally provides residuals and standardized residuals which allow them to be plotted in a variety of ways. If the distribution of residuals differs strongly from that assumed for the error components, the aspects of the model may not be satisfied. Moreover, the nature of the difference can tell us which assumptions have been violated and suggest appropriate measures.

Residuals cannot provide us with information about the assumption $E(\epsilon) = 0$ because least-squares regression is used, the residuals can, however, provide useful information about whether there are violations of linearity, homogeneity of variance, and independence. If the assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity (homogeneity of variance) are both valid, when residuals are plotted against either X or Y, the data points should lie within a horizontal band, as indicated in Panel (a) of Figure 19.5. Any other pattern suggests that as in Panel (b) indicates that the relationship between Y and X is nonlinear and that the appropriate model should contain additional predictors such as $X^2$. Plots such as that in Panel (c), in which the residuals are more spread out for some values of X or Y than others, indicate that the variance of estimate is not constant (the homoscedasticity assumption is violated). We plot residuals against predicted values of Y rather than Y because it can be shown it is not correlated with Y (or, therefore, with X) but has a correlation of $\sqrt{1-r^2}$ with Y. We also note that although plots of residuals against X and Y provide equivalent information for bivariate regression (because Y is a simple linear function of X), this will not be the case when there is more than one predictor variable.

Another assumption of the regression model is that all relevant variables have included in the model. If a relevant variable is omitted, the error component, $\epsilon$, will consist of more than chance variance. We can determine whether an additional variable, $W$, belongs to the model by plotting the residuals against W. If the residuals systematically with W, then W should be included in the model.
