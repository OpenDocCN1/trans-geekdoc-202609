This form of the equation raises the question: When is it proper to average two means squares?
The answer is that if the two mean squares estimate the same population variance, or variances, pooling is proper. By example of Equation 9.17, the assumption is that both $MS_{AB}$ and $MS_{SAB}$ estimate $\sigma^2$ (i.e., $\sigma_{AB}^2 = 0$). The advantage of pooling two or more estimates of the population error is that $MS_{pool}$ is distributed on more than $df_{SAB}$; F tests based on the pooled error may be more powerful than tests based on $MS_{SAB}$. Of course, we never know for certain that $\sigma_{AB}^2 = 0$. If it is not, we may lose power in using the pooled error to test a false null hypothesis about a effect. To see why, look at Equation 9.17. Contrary to the assumption upon which pooling is based, $E(MS_{AB}) = \sigma^2 + nB\theta$, the weighted average of this expectation and $\sigma^2$, will be larger than $\sigma^2$. As a result, the test F of a main effect will be negatively biased; the expectation of the error term involves more than Type 2 errors. Perhaps surprisingly, when the null hypothesis about the effect is true, there may be an increase in Type 1 errors. The reason for this is that $MS_{AB}$ and $MS_{SAB}$ do not estimate $\sigma^2$; the ratio of $MS_A$ (or $MS_B$) to $MS_{pool}$ will not be distributed as $F$, and the tail area may be larger than the nominal. In some of these considerations, it is not clear when, ever, to pool. We consider this issue.

## 9.9.2 When (If Ever) to Pool

One approach is to apply a times-pool rule; interactions are tested against the within-cell error and the two mean squares are pooled if $p$ is greater than some criterion value because a large value of $p$ is assumed to reflect a clear lack of difference, justifying pooling. With respect to the designs of the current study, by Mead, Bancroft, and Han (1975) is relevant. For a design with two fixed-effect factors and equal cell frequencies, with the criterion for the preliminary test of the AB source as .5, the times-pool rule often resulted in a loss of power when the null hypothesis about the main effect was false, and an increase in Type 1 error rate when it was true. Therefore, in designs in which all factors have fixed effects, we recommend never pooling. We will consider whether the times-pool rule is advisable in other designs when we discuss these designs.

## 9.9.3 Unintended Pooling

Researchers often pool without realizing they have done so. Typically, in cases there are one or more treatment variables and one “nuisance” variable that the researcher regards as irrelevant. For example, the position of a reward may appear often in all experimental designs of a discrimination study, or each experimenters may run an equal number of participants in each condition. Ignoring such factors essentially pools them with the within-cell error term. As we noted in discussing Mead et al.’s (1975) results, such pooling leads to the risk of a loss of power if the null hypothesis is false, or an increased Type 1 error rate if it is true. The message is simple: Include all factors in the analysis, whether they are of interest or not.

## 9.10 Advantages and Disadvantages of Between-Participants Designs

We will consider further tests of hypotheses in between-participants designs in the next chapter. However, this is a good point at to review the major advantages and disadvantages of between-participants factorial designs:

- 1. Assuming equal frequencies, the analysis of the data from the between-participants design is much simpler than for other designs.
- 2. For any given number of scores, the error degrees of freedom in the analysis of the between-participants design will be larger than for any comparable design.
- 3. The requirements of the underlying model are more easily met by between-participants designs than by other designs, and violations of assumptions are less likely to affect the distribution of the F ratio.
- 4. However, there is one major disadvantage in using between-participants designs. Because the error variance is large due to individual differences, designs that permit the adjustment of the error variance for the differences among individuals will enable more powerful tests and more precise estimates of population parameters, and often do so with fewer observations.

Because of the large error variance associated with between-participants designs, these designs are most useful whenever participants are relatively similar with respect to the dependent variable, or when a larger N is available to compensate for the variance among individuals. Also, there are many experiments in which the nature of the independent variable (e.g., the method of instruction or the educational level of participants) will constrain the design. However, there are many situations in which several designs are feasible and desirable. In Chapter 12, we will introduce the idea of design efficiency and consider the efficiency of alternative designs, and in Chapters 13-16, we will consider these designs and their analyses more closely.

# Obtaining Type I, II, and III Sums of Squares Using Software

Using R. Several different functions are available for calculating ANOVAs. The aov function in the [stats] package Type I sums of squares. For two-factor designs, aov(data = Dframe, DV ~ A*B) will provide Type Ia SS on the data frame named “Dframe” where the dependent variable is called “DV” and the factors are “A” and “B”. Using A* tells where to include both factors and their interaction. Changing the model to DV ~ B*A will provide Type Ib SS.

Type II sums of squares are the default in the ANOVA function in the [car] package. For two-factor designs, the command anova(data = Dframe, DV ~ B*A) returns an ANOVA table of the results;

Type III sums of squares are in the ANOVA function; either type = 3 or type = “III” can be used. We must also specify the effects of interest by using a contrasts option, contrasts =list(A = “contr.sum”, B = “contr.sum”), which states that we want to compare the cell means of A averaged over levels of B and the cell means of B averaged over levels of A. Contr will be described in Chapter 10. The full command, anova(data = Dframe, DV ~ A*B, contrasts =list(A = “contr.sum”, B = “contr.sum”), type = “III”), will return an ANOVA table.

Using SPSS. Choose the General Linear Model from the Analyze pull-down menu, and select Univariate. Move the dependent variable from the variable list to the box on the right, move the factor variables to the “Fixed effects” box. Next, click on the “Model” button. At the bottom of the window that pops up is a selector for Sum of Squares, defaults to Type III. Change this option to select Type I or Type SS, and then click “Continue” and “OK” to run the ANOVA. Note that Type Ia and Ib SS depend only on the order in which factors are listed in the “Fixed effects” box: if Factor A is listed first, Type Ia SS are computed; if Factor B is listed first, Type Ib SS are computed.
