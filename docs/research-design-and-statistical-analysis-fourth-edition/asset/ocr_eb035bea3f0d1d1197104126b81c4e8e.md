# 22.6 Regression With Direct and Mediated Effects

It is possible for an independent variable to influence the dependent variable directly, or it can influence the dependent variable indirectly, through its effect on a mediating variable. Figure 22.3 provides an illustration of this distinction. In Panel (a), we have a model in X, exerts a direct effect on Y. This causal influence is indicated by the arrow, and the direction of the arrow indicates the direction of causality. In Panel (b), we have a causal model that illustrates both direct and mediated effects. In this case, a change in X, directly affects Y but also Y indirectly through the mediator variable, X₁. There are many examples of the kinds of variables influencing job perception, which in turn influence productivity; or changes in parents' attitudes towards education can change children's attributes, and in turn influence academic performance. Theories specifying processes that intervene between independent and dependent variables are pertinent in many fields of psychology. A Web of Science reveals more than 54,000 citations of the seminal Baron and Kenny (1986) paper that discussed testing for a mediating effect.

We must consider the possibility of mediation if we try to determine the importance of a variable. If we include the correct variables in the regression equation and there is a causal relationship between X and the outcome, the regression coefficient bᵧ does not estimate the total influence of X on Y. Rather, the regression coefficient reflects the direct effect of X, on Y, that is, the rate of change of Y with X, holding all the other variables in the equation constant. If changes in X cause changes in some of other variables, in turn changing Y, we would have both direct and indirect effects. In assessing the influence of X, we would be concerned with both the direct and indirect effects. Given a causal structural model, structural equation modeling can be used to assess the total effect of changing a variable. However, as these estimates may be misleading if important variables are omitted from the model or if the model is otherwise invalid.

Again, consider the two causal models in Figure 22.3. In Panel (a), a change of one unit in X causes a change of c units in Y. In Panel (b), we have a causal model that illustrates both direct and mediated effects. In, changing X by one unit directly causes a change in Y of c' units and a change in X of a units. A change of one unit in X causes a change of b units in Y. Therefore, the indirect effect of X on Y through the mediator X₂ is ab, c + ab. Baron and Kenny (1986) have discussed four steps necessary to establish that a data set is consistent with the presence of mediation. These steps, translated into our terminology, are as follows:

+   Step 1. Regress Y on X₁. If the coefficient bᵧ is nonzero, this is consistent (but does not necessitate) X₁ having a causal effect on Y.

+   Step 2. Regress X₁ on X. If the coefficient b₂ is nonzero, this is consistent with X having a causal effect on the possible mediator X₂.

+   Steps 3. Regress Y on both X₁ and X₂. The coefficient of X₁, bᵧ₁, is the rate of change of predicted Y when X is held constant, and so, in terms of the model in Panel (b), bᵧ₁ is an estimate of c', the causal path from X₁ to Y. A finding that bᵧ₁ is nonzero but bᵧ₂ is zero is consistent with a model in which the entire effect of X on Y is mediated by the intervening variable, X₂. A finding that bᵧ₂ is nonzero, but smaller than bᵧ₁, is consistent with partial mediation by X₂.
