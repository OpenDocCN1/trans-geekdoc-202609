To protect against excessive Type 1 error, Steiger (1980) has recommended the routine of using a simple test of the hypothesis that all off-diagonal elements of a correlation matrix are equal to zero. If this hypothesis is not rejected, then the individual considerations in the matrix are not likely to be meaningful unless motivated by a priori considerations. The hypothesis can be tested using

```
$$\chi^2 = (N-3)\sum_{j>i}\sum Z_{ij}^2$ (18.7)
```

with $k(k-1)/2$ df, where $Z_{ij}$ is the Fisher Z-transformed value of $r_{ij}$ and the summation is over $k(k-1)/2$ squared $Zs$s that correspond to the $rs$ that are above (or below) the diagonal. Let's turn now to the general question of whether a particular variable, X, has a different correlation with variable Y than variable Z. For example, we might want to determine whether the correlation between TC and *age* is significantly different than the correlation between TC and BMI. Note that these are *dependent* rather than *independent* correlations because both are based on data from the same participants. Although this is a reasonable question to ask, it turns out that testing hypotheses about correlations involves expressions that are complicated, untimely and often tedious to calculate, especially when the correlations to be tested do not vary a variable in common.

Useful sources that discuss tests of dependent correlations are Meng, Rosenthal, and Rubin (1992), Olkin and Finn (1990, 1995), and Steiger (1980). The simplest test for dependent correlations that have a variable in common is given by Meng et al. (1992). Given the null hypothesis $H_0: \rho_{Y_1X_1} = \rho_{Y_2X_2}$, we can use the test statistic

```
z=(Z_{Y1X1}-Z_{Y2X2})\sqrt{\frac{N-3}{2(1-r_{X1,X2}^2)b}} (18.8)
```

where

```
b=\frac{1-fr^2}{1-r^2}
```

and

```
f=\frac{1-r_{X1,X2}}{2(1-r^2)}
```

and $r_{Y_k}^2$ is the mean of $r_{Y_k}^2$. To test whether the correlation between $TC$ and $age$ (.513) is significantly different from the correlation between $TC$ and BMI (.152), we substitute into Equation 18.8. Here $r^2=.143$, $f=.543$, and $b=1.076$, so that $z=4.18$. Therefore, we may reject the null hypothesis at $p=.000$. The test statistic in Equation 18.8 is only appropriate if the two correlations have a common variable. If we wish to test the hypothesis $H_0: \rho_{sy} = \rho_{pow}$ the test statistic is considerably more complicated (see Steiger, 1980).

## 18.2.8 Using Software to Compare Correlations

The option to compare correlations has not been incorporated in the pull-down menus in SPSS. To compute inferential statistics or confidence intervals requires the development of an SPSS syntax that takes correlations and sample size as input and the necessary steps in the relevant equation (e.g., Equation 18.8 for dependent correlations with a variable in common).

In R, the *concor* function in the [coc] package is a highly flexible function that can compare independent or dependent correlations. For independent correlations, the data must be stored in two separate data frames. Suppose that the data frame *datW* contains variables *name* and *age* for the 220 participants who identify as men, and *datM* is analogous for the 220 participants who identify as women. The command *concor(list(datW,datM),tc + age)* returns Fisher's Z test (Equation 18.4), where $z=5.10$, with a 95% CI for the difference from Zou (2007). To compare dependent correlations that have a variable in common, we can use the *concor(list(datW,datM),tc + age)* returns Fisher's Z test (Equation 18.4), where $z=5.10$, with a 95% CI for the difference from Zou (2007).

## 18.2.9 Some About Tests That Compare Correlations

In Sections 18.2.6–18.2.8, we discussed how to test whether two correlations differ in size. However, given that we have previously emphasized that correlations are dependent on sample characteristics such as the variance of X and Y, we should give serious thought to exactly what we are testing when we compare two correlations. Suppose, for example, we find that the correlations between income and years of education are significantly different for people living in two different states. This means there is a stronger relationship in one group than the other. However, the correlations may differ between the two groups, rather than because the rate of change of income with years of education is different. Also, a researcher who wishes to compare correlations should carefully evaluate whether constraints on the sampling of observations might have resulted in a biased estimate of rho for either or both coefficients (see Section 17.7).

Our second point is that we are often more interested in the slope of the regression line than the strength of the linear relationship. If our concern about how much income changes with additional education this differs across groups, we should compare regression slopes, not correlation coefficients. Just because the correlations are significantly different for the two groups does not necessarily mean that the slopes will be. Conversely, finding that the slopes are significantly different does not necessarily mean that the correlations are significantly different.
