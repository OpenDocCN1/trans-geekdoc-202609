
More generally, if $p$ predictors account for the non-error variability in Y and are mutually uncorrelated,

```
$SS_{regression} = SS_{Y,1} + SS_{Y,2} + \dots + SS_{Y,p}$
```

and

```
$R_{Y,12..p}^2 = r_{Y1}^2 + r_{Y2}^2 + \dots + r_{Yp}^2 = \sum r_{Yi}^2$
```

In words, the predictors are uncorrelated with one another, the proportion of the variability of Y they collectively account for is the sum of the proportions of variability accounted by the individual predictors.

However, unless we have a true experiment, predictor variables are almost always correlated with one another. The predictors usually share variability, as in Panel (b) of Figure 20.6, where the correlation between $X_i$ and $X_j$ is indicated by the overlap of their circles. Note that if the overlap of Y with $X_i$ is the same as the overlap of Y with $X_j$, the area labeled (b) is added twice. When any of $p$ predictors are correlated, the proportion of variability in Y becomes the sum of the proportions accounted by the individual predictors; an expression for $R^2$ that takes the correlations between predictors into account is

```
$R_{Y,12..p}^2 = \frac{\sum r_{Yj}b_j}{s_Y} = \sum r_{Yj}b_j' \quad (20.4)$
```

where $b_j$ is the unstandardized regression coefficient of $X_j$ in the multiple regression equation; $s$ and $s_Y$ are the standard deviations of $X_j$ and $Y$, respectively; and $b_j'$ is the standardized regression coefficient of $X_j$. For example, when we regress TC (Y) on *age* ($X_1$) and BMI ($X_2$) in Section 20.5, we will obtain the following results:

| TC | Age | BMI |
|---|---|---|
| | 1.737 | 1.474 |
| | 0.492 | 0.231 |
| 39.173 | 10.630 | 4.670 |

so, substituting in Equation 20, we have $R^2 = [(0.492)(1.737)(10.630) + (0.231)(4.670)]/39.173 = 0.272$

In the current example, the sum of the $r^2$ values for the correlations between TC and *age* and between TC and BMI, 0.295, is not very different from the value of 0.272 obtained earlier, because the correlation between *age* and BMI is small (see Figure 20.1).

The increase in $R^2$ when $X_2$ is added to a regression equation that already contains $X_1$ is the square of the semipartial correlation coefficient introduced in Chapter 18. As mentioned there, $r_{Y(2|1)}^2$ is the correlation of Y with the component of $X_2$ that is not predictable from $X_1$. In terms of Panel (b) of Figure 20.6, we may think of $r_{Y(2|1)}^2$ as the proportion of the Y that overlaps $X_2$ but not $X_1$. In general, the squared semipartial correlation coefficient is the increase in $R^2$ that follows from adding a $(p+1)^{th}$ predictor to a regression equation that contains $p$ predictors. That is,

```
$R_{Y(p+1)|12,.\cdot}}^2 = R_{Y_{12,.p+1}}^2 - R_{Y_{12,.cdot}}^2$ (20.5)
```

In Equation 20.5, $r_{Y(p+1)|12,.\cdot}}$ is the correlation between $Y$ and $X_{p+1}, X_{2}, \dots, X_p$ where the latter represents the residuals of $X_{p+1}$ on $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_p$. Applying Equation 20.5 to the TC data, when $age$ is added to a regression equation that contains BMI, the proportion of variance of TC accounted for increases by .219. If BMI is added to an equation when age is already included as a predictor, the increase is .030. Note that because age and BMI are somewhat correlated these increases in variance in TC are smaller than the proportions of variance accounted for age and BMI when each is the only predictor in the equation. In Chapter 21, we discuss "partial $F$ tests" to allow us to test whether the addition of one or more variables significantly increases the variability accounted for by a regression equation.

## 20.4.4 The Adjusted (or Shrunken) Multiple Correlation Coefficient and Cross-Validation

When a regression equation is developed from a sample of data, the multiple correlation coefficient $R$ and its square are commonly used indices of how well the equation fits the sample. These measures are also often used as estimates of how the regression equation fits the population from which the sample was taken. However, using $R$ or $R^2$ as measures of fit can be misleading because $R$ is a *positively biased* estimator of the population. Particularly if the sample is small and there are many predictors, a regression equation predicts well in the sample may predict poorly in the population.

With enough predictors, the regression equation *must* fit the sample well no matter how the predictors and the criterion are related in the population. Just as any two data points can be fit perfectly by a straight line, any $p+1$ points can be fit perfectly by a linear regression equation with $p$ predictor variables and therefore, the resulting value of the sample $R$ must be 1. With $p+1$ data points the $R$ in the sample need not be 1, but will tend to be larger than $R$ in the population if the ratio of the number of cases, to predictors, is small. This bias occurs because of *capitalization on change*: The regression equation takes advantage of chance fluctuations in scores that for increased predictability in the sample but not in the population. The bias in $R$ can be reduced by working with larger samples: Although the recommended sample size depends on extent the nature of the research problem and the purpose of the research, the $N/p$ ratio should be large–30 or more–perhaps if the size of $R$ is to be taken into account.

A common adjustment for positive bias has been provided by Wherry (1931). The proportion of variance in the population between Y and a set of $X_s$ can be accounted for by $\rho^{XY}$, which can be expressed

```
$\rho_{XY}^2 = 1 - \frac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_Y^2}$
```

## Introduction to Multiple Regression

If we replace the population variances with our unbiased estimates, we have

```
$R_{adjusted}^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{residual}/(N-1)}{SS_{Y}/(N-1)}$
```

which can be rewritten as

```
$R_{adjusted}^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{residual}}{SS_{Y}} \frac{N-1}{N}$
```

The adjusted (or "shrunken") multiple correlation coefficient is provided in the regression output of most statistical analysis. For the regression of TC over age and BMI, Equation 20.6 yields an adjusted $R^2$ of $1-(.272)(80/78) = .264$. Note that if we set the adjusted $R^2$ to zero and solve Equation 20.6 for $R^2$, we get

```
$R^2 = \frac{p}{N-1}$
```

This gives the value of $R^2$ for which the adjusted $R^2$ will be 0. Using this, we can see that if we have 10 predictors and 50 cases, an $R^2$ value of .204 (or multiple $R$ of .452) would correspond to an adjusted $R$ of 0. In short, unadjusted values of $R$ and $R^2$ could overestimate the strength of the relationship between Y and a set of predictors when the $N/p$ ratio is small.

If our interest is prediction, the Adjusted adjustment may not be ideal because we are interested in how a regression equation fits the population than how well fits another sample drawn from the same population. Wherry (1969) gives two adjustment to estimate $R^2$ if we want to use the regression equation developed in sample 1 to make predictions for the data of sample 2. If the predictors are random variables, as is usual in non-experimental research, the adjustment is

```
$R_{adj}^2 = 1-\frac{N-1}{N-p-1}\frac{N-2}{N-p}\frac{N+1}{N}(1-R^2)$ (20.7)
```

If the predictors are fixed, the adjustment is

```
$R_{adj}^2 = 1-\frac{N-1}{N}\frac{N+1}{N-1}(1-R^2)$ (20.8)
```

The shrinkage is greater if we use the Wherry equations instead of Wherry equation. For example, given a sample of size 100 with 10 predictors and an $R^2$ of .50, $R_{adjusted}^2 = .444$, whereas $R_{adj}^2 = .374$, $R_{adj}^2 = .383$. In our current example, because of the high $N/p$ ratio, there is not much shrinkage: $N = 81, p = 2$, and $R^2 = .272$, so that $R_{adjusted}^2 = .264$ and $R_{adj}^2 = .252$.

The best of obtaining a more realistic estimate of the population $R$ is to employ a procedure called cross-validation that avoids capitalizing on chance by developing the regression equation using one sample (the screening or training sample) and testing it by a second sample (the calibration or test sample). The cross-validated $R$ is the correlation between (1) the predicted $Y$ scores when the regression equation developed in the screening sample is applied to the calibration sample and (2) the actual $Y$ scores in the calibration sample. Because the regression coefficients are obtained from one sample and the $R$ is obtained from a second sample, the cross-validated $R$ cannot systematically capitalize on sampling variability.

The problem of capitalization is most when the variables are chosen from a larger pool of predictors - a common situation. Variables in the pool that are useful for predicting in the sample will be those to be added to the regression equation and thus increase the correlation. In this situation, chance variation in the sample will be what determines the choice of variables in the equation. If the $N/p$ ratio is small and variables in regression equation are chosen from a larger set of possible predictors, the shrinkage achieved by cross-validation can be dramatic.

We can illustrate this with the Seasons data. We selected the data from a random 25% of the participants identified as women aged 65 years or less with BMI scores of 40 or less. Then we used TC as the dependent variable and arbitrarily choose height, BMI1, bost_1, anger1, anxiety1, durduc1, beck_1, beck_2, beck_3, and beck_4 as 11 predictors. Using an automated procedure called stepwise regression (to be discussed in Chapter 21), the regression equation containing five predictor variables that best predicts TC is 

```
$tc = 204.43+10.876 bost_1 - 11.216 anxiety1 + 2.231 beck\_d1$
```

Here, the regression equation is based on 32 randomly chosen cases in the sample having data on the dependent variable and all 11 predictors, the $N/p$ ratio is 2.9. The R obtained for the sample is .42, and the adjusted $R$ is .30. We cross-validated by (1) using the same equation to predict TC scores for the remaining 94 cases with data on all 11 original predictors, and then (2) finding the correlation between the predicted and actual TC scores for these 94 participants. This correlation is very small, $r = .10$, so that the cross-validated $R$ is $.10^2 = .01$. The cross-validated $R$ of .10 is a more realistic measure of how well the regression equation fits the population than the $R$ of .42 or the adjusted $R$ of .30 in the substantial used to generate the equation.

In summary, if the $N/p$ ratio is small, the multiple correlation coefficient for a sample will overestimate the usefulness of the regression equation in the population and in all samples. This overestimation is larger when the predictors in the equation are chosen from larger pools of potential predictors. We strongly recommend the use of cross-validation to counter the effects of capitalization on chance.
