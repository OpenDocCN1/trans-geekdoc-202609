# 9.5 More Than Three Independent Variables

The analyses of data from between-subject designs involving more than three factors are in respects straightforward generalizations of the material presented for two- and three-factor designs. Designs with four or more factors become unwieldy in a couple of respects. Each variable and each possible combination of variables is a potential contributor to the total variability, and so is the variability among scores within each cell of the design. As the number of factors increases, the number of possible effects to test increase rapidly; with f factors there are 2'-1 possible effects to test. For example, with five factors there are 31 possible effects: 5 main effects, 10 two-way interactions, 10 three-way interactions, 5 four-way interactions, and 1 five-way interaction.

The calculations of higher-order interactions might follow directly from what we learned for two- and three-factor interactions. As might guessed, the degrees of freedom for any higher-order interaction are a product of the degrees of freedom for the variables entering into the interaction. For example, an ABCD interaction would have (a-1)(b-1)(c-1)(d-1) df.
