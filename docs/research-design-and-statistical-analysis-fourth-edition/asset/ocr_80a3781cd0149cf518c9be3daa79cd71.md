However, because our alternative hypothesis is directional, we choose our rejection region in just one of the sampling distribution; specifically, the region consists only of the last 5% of the $z$ distribution. Therefore, again assuming that $\alpha=.05$, we will reject $H_0$ if the $z$ calculated from our data is greater than 1.645. In the one-tailed case, the $p$-value is determined only by the part of the distribution beyond the value of the test statistic in the direction consistent with the alternative hypothesis.

The choice between one- and two-tailed tests should be made before the data are collected; it is determined by the hypothesis of interest, not the observed data. To understand why, consider the following scenario. Suppose we hypothesized that the average TC score be above 200; we have a one-tailed hypothesis. However, upon examining the data, we find the mean is less than 200, and we restore our hypotheses, testing at the .05 level for a significant difference in the direction opposite to that we hypothesized. This procedure capitalizes on a chance outcome and will result in the null hypothesis results falls in the upper .05 of the normal distribution ($z\geq1.645$) or if it falls in the lower .05 of the distribution ($z\leq1.645$). Thus, if we operate in such a fashion, the actual probability of a Type 1 error is the probability of $z>1.645$ or $z<-1.645$. Switching the hypothesis from one to two-tailed after looking at the data is also a bad idea if it is held constant. That decision process also capitalizes on chance because it is determined by the data (which randomly from sample to sample) rather than the theoretical hypothesis of interest.

Why not always use the two-tailed test? Doing so would allow us to test for departures from the null hypothesis in both directions. There are two issues to consider. If the theoretical claim is directional, then the most appropriate test is a one-tailed test. If the theoretical claim is simply one of a difference between conditions or from a specific issue ($TC=20$), clearly a two-tailed test should be used. The second is statistical power. With $\alpha=.05$, a two-tailed test requires a cutoff of 1.96 in the right-hand tail of the normal distribution, whereas the one-tailed test requires a cutoff of 1.645. In other words, if the alternative hypothesis is that the population mean is greater than 200, the one-tailed test has a more lenient criterion for rejection. Therefore, as we illustrated with the binomial distribution of Chapter 4 (see Figure 4.5), when the null hypothesis is true the one-tailed test has more power against alternative. When the null hypothesis is false, the one-tailed test accurately has more power than a two-tailed test.

# 5.5.5 Hypothesis Tests and Confidence Intervals

To understand the relationship between confidence intervals and hypothesis tests, let's consider the decision rule for a two-tailed test: Assuming $\alpha=.05$, reject $H_0$ if

```
$\bar{Y}-\mu_{byp}/\sigma_{\bar{Y}} >1.96 \quad \text{or} \quad \bar{Y}-\mu_{byp}/\sigma_{\bar{Y}} < -1.96$
```

Some algebra will show that this is equivalent to the rule, if

```
$\mu_{byp} < \bar{Y}-1.96\sigma_{\bar{Y}} \quad \text{or} \quad \mu_{byp} > \bar{Y}+1.96\sigma_{\bar{Y}}$
```
