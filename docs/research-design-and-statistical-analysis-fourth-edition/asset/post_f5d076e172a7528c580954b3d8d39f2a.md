
## Research Design and Statistical Analysis

The $\mu_{jk}$ may vary for any of three reasons; namely, they correspond to different levels of $A$ and different combinations of those levels. We can represent this idea by the following identity:

```
$(\mu_{jk} - \mu) = (\mu_j - \mu) + (\mu_k - \mu) - [(\mu_{jk} - \mu) - (\mu_j - \mu) - (\mu_k - \mu)] \quad (9.3)$
```

A simpler notation

```
$$\mu_{jk} - \mu = \alpha_j + \beta_k + (\alpha\beta)_{jk} \quad (9.4) \
```

Equation 9.4 states that $\mu_{jk} - \mu$ is a sum of three effects. The first of these, $\alpha_j$ is the *main effect of treatment* $A_j$; its value represents the extent to which the average score in the population defined by the treatment $A_j$ differs from the mean of all the scores in the $ab$ populations. Similarly, $\beta_k$ is the main effect of treatment $B$ and reflects the extent to which the average score in population $B$ differs from the average of all scores in the $ab$ populations. Finally, $(\alpha\beta)_{jk}$ is the *interaction effect* of $A$ and $B$. The interaction is the difference between $\mu_{jk}$ and $\mu$ that remains after removal of the main effects of $A$ and $B_j$; that is,

```
$(\alpha\beta)_{jk} = (\mu_{jk} - \mu) - (\mu_j - \mu) - (\mu_k - \mu) \quad (9.5a)$
```

This interaction is more often represented by simplifying the right-hand term in Equation 9.5a:

```
$(\alpha\beta)_{jk} = \mu_{jk} - \mu_j - \mu_k + \mu \quad (9.5b)$
```

From Equation 9.4, we can substitute for $\mu_{jk} - \mu$ in Equation 9.2. Recognizing that nuisance variances will also contribute to the observed data, we have the structural model underlying tests of hypotheses for the two-factor design:

```
$Y_{jk} - \mu = \alpha_j + \beta_k + \varepsilon_{jk} \quad (9.6)$
```

In reality, the variability among the populations has four possible sources: the effects of manipulating A; the effects of manipulating B; the joint, or interaction, effects of A and B; and error component. A detailed summary of the model components and related assumptions is presented in Box 9.1.

## 9.1 Components of the Structural Model

1. The main effect of treatment A, $\alpha_j = \mu_j - \mu$. Factor A is assumed to have fixed effects; that is, the levels have been arbitrarily selected and are viewed as representing the population of levels. Then $\sum_j \alpha_j = 0$. The $F$ test of the A main effect tests the null hypothesis

```
$H_0 : \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_j = \dots = \mu_u.$
```

or, equivalently,

$H_0 : \alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \dots = \alpha_i = \dots = \alpha_a = 0$

- 2. The main effect of treatment B, $\beta_k = \mu_k - \mu$. Factor B is also a fixed-effect variable and so $\sum_k \beta_k = 0$. The F test of the main effect tests the null hypothesis that

$H_0 : \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_k = \dots = \mu_b$

or, equivalently,

$H_0 : \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_k = \dots = \beta_b = 0$

- 3. The interaction effect of A, and $B_k$, $(\alpha\beta)_{jk} = \mu_{jk} - \mu_j - \mu$. Because both A and B have fixed effects, $\sum_j (\alpha\beta)_{jk} = \sum_k (\alpha\beta)_{jk} = 0$. The relevant null hypothesis is

$H_0 : (\alpha\beta)_{11} = (\alpha\beta)_{12} = \dots = (\alpha\beta)_{jk} = \dots = (\alpha\beta)_{ab} = 0$

- 4. The error component, $\varepsilon_{jk} = Y_{jk} - \mu_{jk}$. The errors are independently and normally distributed, with mean zero and variance $\sigma^2_{\varepsilon}$, within each treatment group defined by a combination of A and B.

Equation 9.6 provides the basis for the analysis of variance for the two-factor design. We will develop this relation between the analysis of variance and the structural model in Section 9.3. However, before doing so, we will try to provide a better understanding of what the main and interaction effects represent.

## 9.2.2 Understanding Main and Interaction Effects

Assume that we have a $2 \times 4$ design (e.g., factor A has 2 levels and factor B has 4 levels, like the Wiley–Voss example). Further assume that the population means are those in Panel $a$ of Table 9.2. The main effects $(\alpha_i$ and $\beta_k)$ are calculated by subtracting the grand mean (obtained by averaging over all cells) from the marginal means (obtained by averaging all the means in a row or column). For designs with equal $n$, the effects are independent or *orthogonal*. That is, knowing how the means of one factor across levels tells us nothing about how the means vary on the other factor (Chapter 3).
