# 6.4.1 The Standard Error (SE) of $\hat{Y}_1-\hat{Y}_2$

We defined the statistic as $t = |(V-E(V))/s|$. In the current example, the random variable, $V$, is the difference in $TC$ level between the two group means: $\hat{Y}_1-\hat{Y}_2$ (control – diet means) or, in general, $\hat{Y}_1-\hat{Y}_2$, the SE of the difference between the sample means, can be understood by considering a sampling experiment. Suppose we drew many independent pairs of random samples of sizes $n_1$ and $n_2$, from two independent and normally distributed populations of sizes. If we carried out this sampling procedure, we could compute a difference between the means for each pair of samples drawn. The standard deviation of the sampling distribution $SE_E$ is the quantity to be estimated by the denominator of the $t$ in the two-group study. The issue is to calculate that estimate.
If $n_1, n_2$ are independently distributed, the variance of the sampling distribution of the difference between the two group means is the sum of the variances of the means (see Appendix B for a proof). Then the SE of the difference is

```
$$\hat{Y}_1-\hat{Y}_2 = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} \quad (6.2)$
```

In addition to the assumption that the scores are drawn from independently distributed populations, using the $t$ distributions of Appendix Table C.3 requires one additional assumption, that the two population variances are equal; that is, $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2$. This is referred to as the assumption of homogeneity of variance. Under this assumption, Equation 6.2 becomes

```
$$\hat{Y}_1-\hat{Y}_2 = \sigma\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}} \quad (6.3)$
```

We have a single population variance $(\sigma^2$) to estimate the variances of the two groups sampled in an experiment. To obtain the best estimate of $\sigma$, we average the two group variances and take the square root of the result. Because variance estimates are consistent statistics, the estimate based on the larger group is more likely to be close to the true variance. Therefore, the best estimate of $\sigma$ is a weighted average of the two group variances. The *pooled-variance estimate*, or $s_{pooled}^2$ is calculated as

```
$s_{pooled}^2 = \frac{n_1-1}{n_1+n_2-2}s_1^2 + \frac{n_2-1}{n_1+n_2-2}s_2^2 \quad (6.4)$
```
