Suppose we have the view that the elements of $\mathbf{g}$ are drawn from a multivariate normal distribution, $\mathbf{g} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_g)$. Given this prior distribution, $p(\mathbf{g})$, and the likelihood, $p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{g})$, implied by the normal distribution of the regression residuals $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, Bayes’ rule tells us that the posterior distribution of $\mathbf{g}$ given the observed data $\mathbf{y}$ follows

$$p(\mathbf{g}|\mathbf{y}) \propto p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{g})p(\mathbf{g}). \tag{2.21}$$

With normal prior and likelihood, the posterior distribution is normal, too. The mean of this posterior distribution takes a form akin to a generalized least-squares (GLS) regression, but with an additional term in the inverse (Lindley and Smith 1972):

$$\hat{\mathbf{g}} = \left(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{X}+\Sigma_g^{-1}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{y}. \tag{2.22}$$

The additional term $\Sigma_g^{-1}$ in the inverse induces shrinkage that tilts the estimates away from the GLS estimates toward the mean of the prior distribution (which we assumed to be a vector of zeros here). If we specialize to uncorrelated homoskedastic regression residuals, $\Sigma=I_N\sigma^2$, and uncorrelated and homoskedastic elements of $\mathbf{g}$ under the prior distribution, $\Sigma_g=I_K\sigma_g^2$, we obtain

$$\hat{\mathbf{g}} = \left(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}+\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_g^2}I_K\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{y}. \tag{2.23}$$

This expression is identical to the ridge regression estimator in (2.7) with $\gamma=\sigma^2/\sigma_g^2$. This means that the ridge regression penalty has a Bayesian interpretation that relates $\gamma$ to the dispersion in the prior distribution. If $\sigma_g^2$ is large, which implies that we do not have a precise prior view about the likely magnitude of the elements of $\mathbf{g}$, $\gamma$ is small and there is little shrinkage toward the prior mean. In contrast, if the prior distribution is tightly concentrated around the prior mean, with $\sigma_g^2$ small, then shrinkage is strong.

The Bayesian framework allows us to give a more precise interpretation of the concern about “overfitting” that we used earlier to motivate regularization and shrinkage. Overfitting means estimating a model without giving proper weight to prior information that we have about the parameters.
