# Chapter 8: Cryptocurrency Mining Explained

![](image_rsrc1NY.jpg)

Ask as many people as you like what they think cryptocurrency mining is. Some will look at you like you've grown an extra head, while many others will say it's just a way of getting more crypto coins. In a way, it is, but it involves so much more. Mining is also about validating transactions on the blockchain and adding them in blocks to the ledger. Perhaps its most important job, though, is to prevent double-spending.

Think about the physical fiat currency you spend. When you make a purchase, your bank updates your account, showing the payment leaving your account and going into another one. It works the same way with cryptocurrencies. When a crypto purchase is made, the digital ledger is updated, showing the debit from one account and the credit to another. However, digital platforms are constantly being hacked or manipulated. The Bitcoin ledger overcame this challenge by creating a system where transactions could only be updated on the ledger by verified miners. This ensures those miners are responsible for ensuring double-spending cannot take place.

While the miners keep the network secure, new crypto coins are being generated. These are given to the miners to reward them for their work. Because there is no central authority on a distributed ledger, mining is critical to ensure transactions are validated, hence the incentive for the miners to take part - if they validate the transaction, they win the reward. A consensus protocol is in place to ensure that only those verified miners can do the work. This is known as a **proof-of-work** protocol, and it also keeps the network secured against outside attacks.

## Proof-of-Work

You can think of cryptocurrency mining as akin to mining precious gems or metals. While these miners will uncover diamonds, gold, silver, etc., cryptocurrency miners unearth new coins and bring them into circulation. A miner needs to use a powerful machine to solve complicated mathematical problems - cryptographic hashes to earn the rewards on offer. A **hash** is a shortened digital signature representing the data in the transaction, generated to keep the data on public networks secure. Miners compete against one another to solve the **hash value** that each transaction generates - the first one to solve it adds the transaction block to the blockchain and gets the reward. The **hash function** for each transaction block contains a reference to the previous block. This continues right back to the first block, known as the **genesis block**, creating an unbroken chain. This is why each network node can verify and validate blocks and can verify whether the winning miner correctly solved the hash. As more miners join the network and more powerful computation resources are used, the equations become more difficult to solve. This increases the competition and adds to the cryptocurrency's scarcity.

## How to Start Mining

To start mining on any cryptocurrency blockchain, you need a powerful computer with the right software designed to solve the cryptographic equations. In the early days of blockchain technology, you could mine simple cryptocurrencies with a home computer containing a simple CPU chip. However, as the difficulty levels have risen over the years, those CPU chips no longer do the job. Today, you need one of two things:

*   An **ASIC** (application-specific integrated circuit) miner
*   A specialized **GPU**

The GPUs must also be connected to a fast, reliable internet connection 24/7. Each cryptocurrency miner must also be part of an online mining pool.

## Different Mining Methods

The mining method you choose will dictate the length of time it takes. In the early days, **CPU mining** was the chosen method. However, it is incredibly slow and is no longer practical because of the length of time needed to accrue a tiny profit - it can take months, and mining rigs use a significant amount of electricity and cooling. The cost is often way beyond what the profits are.

**GPU mining** is now a more common method, using a set of GPUs in a single mining rig to maximize the computational power. The rig also requires a motherboard and an effective cooling system.

**ASIC mining** is also becoming popular. ASIC mining rigs are designed ONLY to mine cryptos, unlike GPU rigs that can be used as ordinary, albeit powerful, computers. Because of this, ASIC miners can produce more crypto units, but they are more expensive - the more difficult mining becomes, the more likely it is that these rigs will disappear.

**Cloud mining** is slowly taking over, eliminating the rising costs of ASIC and GPU mining. People who do cloud mining can leverage power from dedicated mining facilities and large corporations. As an individual miner, you can find paid and free mining hosts and rent a rig for a set period, making it a hands-free mining method.

## Mining Pools

While you are perfectly at liberty to mine alone, many people choose to join a **mining pool**. All the miners' resources are combined, providing more computational power to increase the chances of solving the equation and earning the reward. Should a pool succeed, the reward is split among all members, proportionate to the level of resources each contributes.

Most cryptocurrency mining applications already have a mining pool, but you also have the option of creating a pool by joining other like-minded enthusiasts online. Once you join a pool, you are not tied to staying with it - you can move between pools as much as you want, and many people move because some pools earn higher rewards. However, many miners feel that the official pools are better - they are more reliable, offer technical support, and are updated regularly by the hosting companies. If you think joining a mining pool might be for you, have a look at **CryptoCompare** to compare the different ones available based on profitability, reliability, and different crypto coins.

## Is Crypto Mining Worth It?

Whether mining is right for you is a matter of personal choice, and several factors will affect your decision. Regardless of the type of mining you choose - CPU, ASIC, GPU, or Cloud - perhaps the most important things to consider are:

*   The **hash rate** produced by the mining rig
*   Power consumption
*   Overall costs

Typically, a good cryptocurrency mining rig will cost anywhere between $3000 and $10000+. They also give off a lot of heat and use high amounts of electricity. For example, a typical ASIC miner used to create one Bitcoin every 10 minutes will use roughly 72 terawatts of power - 1 terawatt is sufficient to power 10 billion 100-watt lightbulbs simultaneously, just to give you an idea of how much power that is! As blockchain technology gets better and the mining difficulty becomes harder, that power consumption figure will change. You also need to factor in the costs for keeping the rig cool, especially when using ASIC or GPU rigs. Lastly, you must consider the difficulty level surrounding the cryptos you want to mine to decide if there is enough profit in it for you.
