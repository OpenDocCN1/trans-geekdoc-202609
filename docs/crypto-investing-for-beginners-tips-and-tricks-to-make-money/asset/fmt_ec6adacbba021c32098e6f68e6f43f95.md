
# Open-Source Cryptocurrencies

Open-Source Cryptocurrencies are classed as open-source, meaning that anyone can join in and participate in the network and the mining or harvesting process without paying a fee.

# Public Ledger

Ledgers are a standard method of keeping records, i.e., recording data and other information. A ledger is still used with cryptocurrencies, but it is public. Anyone can see a public blockchain and see any transaction that happens on them. It is worth knowing that the public ledger is not a given for a blockchain because not all will use one. Some choose private ledgers, thus hiding the transactions from the public. However, this is contradictory to what blockchains are all about.

# Smart Contracts

Also known as digital, blockchain, or self-executing contracts, these are similar to a written contract but are entirely digital, removing the middleman you get in traditional contracts. Using a smart contract allows you to set up investment products, automatic payments, and other similar things without needing to use a bank or other central authority. Smart contracts are not written on paper; they are written into small computer programs stored on the blockchain and run from there. Every transaction through one of these contracts is distributed, and there is no central authority in control of any aspect of the transaction. And because the blockchain is used to store the program, it makes the smart contract immutable, which means it cannot be changed once entered onto the blockchain. However, immutability has disadvantages. Because the smart contract cannot be changed in any way, if there are any errors in the code, they cannot be fixed. This means that security surrounding smart contracts is quite difficult. Some companies choose to audit their smart contracts to get around this issue, but that can be expensive. In time, we expect the development life cycles to improve and better coding practice to help combat these security issues. At the end of the day, although successful so far, smart contracts are still very young and have plenty of time to prove themselves.

# Cryptocurrency Forks

Cryptocurrency forks have the potential to be lucrative. Some of today's more popular cryptocurrencies came about as the result of a cryptocurrency fork or split, but what exactly is this? Sometimes, not all the developers on a network agree to a rule change or the direction a crypto is heading. Often, they decide to split away from the main blockchain and start a fork. Think about the fork you eat your meals with - one handle and a few breaches (the tines). That is what a cryptocurrency fork is. Some cryptocurrencies are built on open-source software, and they all have a protocol that all network members should follow. Some examples of the rules the network should follow include:

*   The size of the transaction blocks
*   Rewards given to "winning" network participants, harvesters, or miners
*   How the transaction fees are calculated

However, it is unlikely that cryptocurrencies will ever finish being developed because they are nothing more than projects built on software. As with anything, there is always some space for improvement, and developers often release updates to increase their crypto's performance or fix issues. Some may be small updates, while others change how a cryptocurrency works - that same crypto they loved when they first developed it. In all relationships, we grow strong together, or we grow apart. When developers or network members strongly disagree on something, they have two options - shut up and stick with what's what, or move on and create a protocol of their own.

## Hard vs. Soft Forks

A fork is never just a fork; it could be a soft fork or a hard one. The majority of cryptocurrencies comprise a protocol (the rules) and the blockchain. If some of the network decides they want their own protocol, they will copy the original code and then change it - this can only be done where a cryptocurrency is fully open-source. Once the changes have been put in place, the developers decide where they want their fork to start, which will be a specific block number. The network will divide when the original cryptocurrency hits that block number on the blockchain. Some of the network opt to stay with the original while the rest go to the new fork. Each separate network will then start to add new transaction blocks to their fork, rendering the two chains incompatible. That is a hard fork, and the two resulting blockchains will never interact again, not even to acknowledge the old blockchain's transactions or nodes.

A soft fork is somewhat different. Think of a relationship that breaks up, but the partners stay friends - that's a soft fork. The developers or network may decide to split the blockchain while ensuring that their new protocol changes are compatible with the original. Let's say the developers choose block 800 for the soft fork. Most of the network may choose to follow the strong blockchain that follows both sets of rules. If a consensus is reached between the two sides, the newer rules are implemented across the entire network. Any nodes that choose not to follow and continue mining are simply wasting their time and resources. The community rejoins, and everyone is happy until the next disagreement rolls around.

## Free Money

When a blockchain forks, the new one is based on the old one, so all the transactions previously recorded will also be on the fork. Simply put, when the chain forks, the developers take a "snapshot" of the ledger at the block they choose for the fork to happen, and the new chain is a duplicate of the old one up to that point. So, if you held 10 BTC in your wallet at the point the chain forks, you still have 10 BTC afterward. So how do you get free money? When a blockchain forks, you must hold cryptocurrency on a platform supported by the fork, and it must be before the chosen block number before the fork happens - this is classed as free money. However, its value depends entirely on the performance of the new fork and its subsequent popularity.

## Example - Bitcoin vs. Bitcoin Cash

Bitcoin has been the subject of forks in the past, and one of the best-known occurred on 1st August 2017, Bitcoin's birthday. The developers were in disagreement over block sizes - some said it should be increased to 2 MB, while others said it should be 32MB. Some of the network fell for the big idea, but the rest just thought making the block size that big was crazy. In the end, the two groups split and went their own way. Bitcoin Cash was created and got a new symbol - BCH. Those who followed the fork and already had BTC holdings were given an identical amount in BCH placed in their wallets. Today, BCH is worth just over $287, while BTC is worth almost $40,000. It will take time to know if BCH will ever get past the value of the original protocol, but the network members who followed did, at least, get something for their troubles.
