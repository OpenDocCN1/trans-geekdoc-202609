Chapter 1 What Is Cryptocurrency? ![](image_rsrc1NY.jpg)Unless you are in the know, you are probably wondering what a cryptocurrency is. Sure, you've heard of Bitcoin – who hasn't? You'd likely heard of Ethereum, Litecoin, Ripple, and others but aren't sure what they are. ![](image_rsrc1NZ.jpg)In simple terms, cryptocurrency is digital money. Traditional currency, like the Euro or U.S dollar, can be digitally transferred, but that's completely different from digital money. Right now, you can use Bitcoin to pay for some goods and services in the same way you use traditional money to make electronic payments. In time, cryptocurrencies will become more mainstream, and more places will accept them. Cryptocurrency really differs from traditional cash in the technology it is built on. Most people are only concerned with the cash they have, not the technology behind it, but traditional systems have more than their fair share of problems. These include: 

*   Wire transfers, credit cards, and other payment systems are outdated 
*   Intermediaries cause transactions to be slow and expensive 
*   Global equality in the financial world is fast-growing 
*   3 billion people do not have access to financial services 

The idea behind cryptocurrency is to solve these problems and many more. Cryptocurrencies The money you currently use is backed by the government and held in banks. You need an ATM or internet banking to transfer money to others or get some for yourself. Cryptocurrencies aim to do away with all this by relying on blockchain technology. Blockchain is decentralized, so there are no intermediaries, and no one person is in control. Instead, a network of computers confirms every transaction but more about how the blockchain works in a later chapter. First, let's learn more about cryptocurrencies. What Is Money? For money to be considered valuable, it needs to demonstrate the following: 

*   Enough people have money 
*   It must be accepted as a payment form 
*   It must be trusted by society as valuable now and will remain so 

In early times, actual coins and banknotes didn't exist, and people used to trade or barter items. However, when real money came about – those banknotes and coins, even credit cards we rely on today – it brought about real changes in the trust model. Transaction ease is also a major factor, and one of the primary reasons for money being invented was the logistical difficulty in transferring gold bars being countries. Credit cards followed, but these are also based around government-backed currency, known as fiat money. The more interconnected the world becomes the more concern there is about authorities in control and whether they have our interests in mind. Because of that, cryptocurrencies are set to provide a viable alternative. A Brief History of Cryptocurrency We all know that Bitcoin was the first cryptocurrency, created after Satoshi Nakamoto, who remains anonymous to this day, released a white paper in 2008 detailing a "peer-to-peer version of electronic money." Since then, many other cryptocurrencies have been created, some successful, some not. Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies are created in one way only – mining. However, rather than the conventional methods of mining precious metals, ores, and gems, cryptocurrency mining is done by powerful computers solving mathematical riddles. We'll talk in more depth about mining later in the book. In 2011, some people began to notice that Bitcoin was flawed. That led to the creation of altcoins to make Bitcoin's design better – they were faster, more secure, and anonymous, among other things. Litecoin was the first altcoin, but many thousands more have been created since then, and right now, we have thousands to choose from. Later, I'll introduce you to some of the most popular. Cryptocurrency Benefits These are some of the solutions cryptocurrencies aim to solve by being decentralized: 

1.  Easier Transactions 

Cryptocurrency transactions are cheaper, easier, and more secure than traditional ones. You can use a phone app, crypto, or exchange wallet to send and receive crypto, and you can even buy some at Bitcoin ATMs, which can then be transferred to your phone app or crypto wallet without needing a bank account. 

2.  More Secure 

Cryptocurrencies are heavily based on blockchain and cryptographic technology, ensuring they are more secure. The security is based largely on hash rate – the higher it is, the more computer power is needed for the network to be compromised. Bitcoin has the highest hash rate of all cryptocurrencies. The only real way to lose access to your stored cryptocurrency is to lose your private key, or it is stolen – more about keys later. 

3.  Accessibility 

While many people are locked out of banking systems for one reason or another, the same doesn't apply to cryptocurrencies. So long as you have a smartphone or a computer with a fast internet connection, you can quickly set up a cryptocurrency wallet without needing ID or credit checks. 

4.  Privacy 

When you open a bank account, you need to provide ID, go through background checks, credit checks, and provide proof of income. You don't need to do any of this with cryptocurrency, ensuring your privacy is secure. Every transaction is classed as "pseudo-anonymous," which means your wallet address is an identifier that doesn't contain any specific information that can identify you. 

5.  Transparency 

Every cryptocurrency transaction happens on the blockchain ledger. This is publicly distributed, allowing every person on the network to see it. Some tools allow you to look at transaction data, including how much cryptocurrency was sent from a wallet and when and where it went. You can also see how much cryptocurrency is in a wallet. This all helps reduce the chances of fraud because when money is sent and received, it can easily be proven, and people can also prove they can pay for a transaction. 

6.  Diversification 

Cryptocurrency provides investors with a way of diversifying away from stocks, bonds, and other financial assets. Much of the diversification comes down to the prices not correlating to other markets. When you combine assets with little to no correlation, your returns are much steadier. Should your stock portfolio fall, you could make gains from your crypto assets. However, remember that crypto is highly volatile, so don't focus heavily on crypto in your portfolio. 

7.  Protection Against Inflation 

A lot of people consider cryptocurrency as a way of providing inflation protection. Bitcoin is capped, and there will only ever be a set number of coins in circulation – when the last one is mined, there will be no more. As the money supply grows beyond the Bitcoin supply, its price should increase. Other cryptocurrencies also offer capping and protection against inflation. Chapter 2 Advantages and Disadvantages ![](image_rsrc1NY.jpg)Cryptocurrencies have been increasing in importance in the last few years, not surprising given their potential to disrupt the current financial world in a big way. In fact, it may even make us question everything we know about the current financial infrastructure, particularly in terms of security and privacy. ![](image_rsrc1P0.jpg)Most would-be investors want to know the advantages of cryptocurrency, but it's also important to know the disadvantages. That way, you can make a fair and informed decision. Advantages Cryptocurrencies are relatively new, but there is nothing to suggest they will fail. On the contrary, their popularity continues to increase, not least because of the benefits they offer. Cryptocurrencies have loads to offer, from the potential of decent returns to being able to trade all day, every day, on a highly secure platform. These are the main advantages: High Risk, High Rewards There are thousands of cryptocurrencies available for trading, each with its own features and quirks. However, they all share some common traits – for example, they can all experience sudden spikes and sharp drops in value. Cryptocurrency values are mostly driven by supply and demand, resulting in significant returns. For example, Ethereum doubled its price between July and December 2021 – great returns for anyone who invested when the price was low. Secure Underlying Technology One of the biggest benefits is linked to the supporting infrastructure rather than the cryptocurrencies. The blockchain is a decentralized distributed ledger where every transaction is publicly tracked. Once a transaction is entered onto the blockchain, it can’t be removed. And because thousands of computers store the same blockchain, there is no way a hacker can access it and change transactions or steal currencies. In that way, all transactions are completely safe. More Transparent System The current financial system has central authorities and intermediaries responsible for processing transactions. Whenever a transaction is made, you trust the authority or intermediaries to ensure it goes through. When the 2008 recession hit, many people wondered if the trust wasn’t, perhaps, misplaced. With cryptocurrencies and blockchain technology, we have a viable alternative. Anyone on the network can see all transactions – and most blockchains have thousands of people on them – allowing you to participate in the financial markets without needing that intermediary or central authority. You Can Trade 24/7 Another huge advantage is that the cryptocurrency markets don’t close, whereas banks and traditional stock markets do. Coins are being mined 24/7, and transactions are constantly recorded, so there’s no need to wait for the exchanges to open to start buying, selling, and trading in cryptocurrency. This has had a huge impact, so much so that the stock exchanges are now thinking about trading outside of banking hours, although we may be waiting a while for that to happen. Of course, the fact that you can trade 24/7 also means that no matter where you are in the world, you can start generating returns regardless of the time of day – or night. Potential to Beat Inflation Because cryptocurrencies are not tied to an economy or other currency, their value reflects demand rather than inflation. However, many potential investors are also concerned about cryptocurrency inflation, but you don’t have to worry about that. Because each cryptocurrency caps the number of coins in circulation, the available amount can never get out of hand, which means there is no potential for inflation. Bitcoin has a hard cap, which means no more than 21 million will be created, while Ethereum has an annual cap of 18 million ETH (Ether). Whether a currency is hard or annually capped, inflation is kept away. Fast Transactions Sending payments via cryptocurrency is much faster than traditional payment systems. For example, if you send money to the U.S, a traditional institution will take up to five days to process it, while wire transfers take 24 hours or more and stock trades take three days. With cryptocurrency, the transaction can be settled in minutes. When the block containing your specific transaction is added to the blockchain, the funds are made available, and the transaction settled. Lower Transaction Fees Transaction costs are typically much lower with cryptocurrency than with traditional financial services. For example, a wire transfer could cost $20 to $40, and if you need to send money internationally, that cost can rise considerably. While there may be occasions when cryptocurrency fees are slightly higher than usual, typically due to the level of demand on the blockchain, they are still much lower than the fees charged for wire transfers. Cryptocurrency Is More Accessible Around three billion people are currently underbanked or cut off from banking. Some of this is a personal choice due to living in a country where the government and financial institutions are corrupt. However, cryptocurrency is accessible by anyone with a computer or smartphone. It’s easy to set up a cryptocurrency wallet faster than opening a bank account, and you don’t need to verify your ID or go through credit checks. Cryptocurrency provides the unbanked and underbanked a way of using financial services without needing to get past central authorities first. Not everyone wants a bank account, mostly because they don’t trust them to look after their money, and cryptocurrency ensures your money is safe and you can easily send it where it needs to go. More Security Crypto wallets provide you with a public and private key. The public key is just that – public – but your private key is what allows you to unlock your wallet. If you lose your key, forget it, or someone steals it, they can access your funds, and there is no recourse. Keep it safe, and your money is much safer than in a traditional bank account. The blockchain also secures transactions by its decentralized nature and the fact that transactions need to be verified by the entire network. The more nodes (computers) added to the network, the more secure each transaction is. Lastly, for the network to be hacked, a significant amount of computing power would be required to ensure multiple fraudulent blocks are verified before the remainder of the network could verify the accuracy. That kind of power would be too expensive in most cases, thus limiting the risk. You've no doubt heard of hacked accounts, but this is mostly down to insufficient exchange security. This is why you should store your cryptocurrency in a secure wallet, not on an exchange. Better Privacy Because there is no requirement to have a bank account to do cryptocurrency transactions, your privacy is secure. Transactions may be linked to a public identifier, which is your wallet address, but they do not give out any personal information. More Transparency Because every transaction is done on a public, distributed ledger, everyone can see the data; they know how much currency was sent, when and from where, and they can also see how much is in a given wallet. This ensures fraudulent transactions are all but eliminated. Plus, crypto owners can prove they have the currency before a transaction is allowed to go through, thus removing the chance of double-spending. Disadvantages So, there are plenty of advantages as you can see but what about the disadvantages? Surely, it’s not all gold and glitter? You’re right; it isn’t, so let’s see the disadvantages: Cryptocurrency Isn’t Easy to Understand It can take time and no small amount of effort to understand cryptocurrencies, especially if you are not a digital whizzkid. And when you try to invest money in what you don’t understand, you are taking a huge risk, one that likely won't pay off. This book will help you understand cryptocurrencies, and there are plenty of resources online that will help. However, most important is to put time into understanding cryptocurrency fully, including all its pros and cons. They Are Extremely Volatile We’ve all seen the price of Bitcoin spike incredibly high – in October 2021, it crossed the $60,000 mark, which was great for those who invested at the right time. However, by January 2022, the price had plummeted to just over $37,000, which happens all the time in cryptocurrency. Significant drops usually follow significant spikes, so cryptocurrency may not be your best bet if you are looking for a safe investment and a quick return. The cryptocurrency market is highly speculative, and because it is much smaller than traditional non-digital markets, it is far more vulnerable to fluctuations. In turn, that can destroy a coin’s value, which is one of the biggest disadvantages of this modern marvel. They Are Unproven as Long-Term Investments Cryptocurrencies may be popular, but they have only existed for about 12 years. That is not long enough to determine their worth as a long-term investment. In contrast, the stock market boasts a history going back centuries – the London Stock Exchange has been around since 1801 – and gold has a long investment history too. No one can tell what will happen with cryptocurrencies in the future, and it’s a brave person who heads off into uncharted waters. There Are Issues with Scalability No one would blame you for thinking that digital currencies are fast and, to a certain extent, they are. However, at some point, they hit problems that stop them from large-scale rollout. The providers have never shied away from admitting this, and those behind the Ethereum platform say that the blockchain has limitations in capacity, slowing the transaction processing rate. This is nothing short of frustrating for those involved in the transactions, and it also has the potential to result in financial losses. Those New to Cryptocurrency May Run into Security Issues While cryptos don’t come with the same risk as centralized institutions, they are not without problems, especially for new investors. If you lose your private key, or it gets stolen, you lose access to all your stored cryptocurrency, and there is no way back from that. A private key is not the same as a traditional password that you can recover or change if you can’t remember it – if you forget it, you can’t get another one. You can also lose your virtual wallet or accidentally delete your holdings – again, there is no way back from this. On top of that, you have to deal with malicious attempts to get your cryptocurrency, and new investors are more vulnerable because they don’t know what to look out for. No Centralized Authority or Regulation While this is a major advantage, some see it as a disadvantage. The FCA (Financial Conduct Authority) doesn’t regulate cryptocurrency, so nothing is in place to protect your money. Mostly, the banks, governments, and other financial institutions see decentralization as a disadvantage because they no longer have control. Cryptocurrency is kept secure by its decentralized nature, anonymity, and strong cryptographic encryption, making it almost impossible for governments to know who is using it. While this is great for the individual, the lack of regulation makes money laundering and criminal activity easier. No Refunds Perhaps the biggest drawback is the fact that you cannot get your money back. If you pay the wrong person by mistake, it’s tough – your money is gone. Sadly, cryptocurrencies have gained a reputation, primarily because of the get-rich-quick stories surrounding Bitcoin, which attracts scammers. Because you can’t get your money back, scammers can steal it with no recourse. The Best Cryptocurrency It’s impossible to say which cryptocurrency is the best to invest in because it is a personal choice. However, there are certain things you should look out for: 

*   Your risk tolerance – how much money can you comfortably afford to lose should your investments lose value? Can you afford to sit on your investment and wait for it to gain value again? 
*   Is your only reason for buying cryptocurrency to make a quick return, or are you hoping to make purchases with it? 
*   Is it only about money, or are you looking for a coin with an environmental or social impact? 

Perhaps one of the easiest approaches is to choose one of the top-performing cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin. After all, it was the first cryptocurrency and has the largest customer and information base. Later, we’ll be talking about some of the best cryptocurrencies to put your money into, but the next chapter will delve into blockchain technology. 

Chapter 3 Blockchain Technology Explained ![](image_rsrc1NY.jpg)Ask 100 people, and I guarantee that most of them have heard of Bitcoin, but few have heard of blockchain. And most of those who have heard of blockchain believe it only has one use – to run Bitcoin. That couldn’t be further from the truth. Yes, Bitcoin is the most famous use of the blockchain, but the technology can do so much more. In fact, it is set to disrupt just about every industry in the world in the next few decades. ![](image_rsrc1P1.jpg)What Is Blockchain? The simple answer is that a blockchain is a digital method of storing data. All the data is stored in blocks which are then chained together, ensuring the data is immutable, i.e., it cannot be changed. All data entered onto the blockchain is publicly available all the time, and anyone can see it whenever they want, in the exact way it was entered onto the blockchain. That makes it a revolutionary technology because it means records can be kept and tracked for just about anything, including identities, property rights, medical records, and so on, without the risk of those records being tampered with. If I were to go and purchase a house, photograph the property rights and add that photo to a blockchain, I would always have proof that I owned those property rights at some point in time. That information cannot be changed once it goes on the blockchain. That all sounds great, but how does this blockchain work? It works like this: Step One – The Transaction Data Let’s make this easy and use the Bitcoin blockchain since it was the first one created and the oldest one in use right now. Each block on this blockchain has about a megabyte of data in it. Right now, the Bitcoin blockchain stores approximately 525,000 MB of data, which equates to 525,000 blocks. This data exists in the form of Bitcoin transactional data and is a massive record of every Bitcoin transaction that has ever taken place, right back to the very first one. For the purposes of the rest of this chapter, we will assume that all blockchains store transaction data, although many store other digital assets — however, regardless of what is stored, the principles on how the blockchain works remain the same. Step Two - Using a Hash to Chain the Blocks Let's say we have several blocks containing transaction data: ![](image_rsrc1P2.jpg)Here we have three blocks with transaction data. Right now, they are nothing special, akin to documents that each contain some data on transactions and the impact on balances. Each block describes up to a megabyte of transactions, and now they need to be chained together. This requires each block to have a digital signature, all unique, corresponding to the data in the block. A new signature must be generated if anything within a block is changed. This process is called hashing and will be explained in the next step. Let's assume there are two transactions in the first block, and both total 1 MB (on the real blockchain, 1 MB of data would be many more transactions.) This block is given a unique signature referencing the string of data in it. Let's say X32 is the signature – it would look like this: ![](image_rsrc1P3.jpg)Now, if one digit in this block were changed, a new signature would be generated, and therein lies the primary reason why transactions cannot be changed. Block 2 contains the signature from block 1, thus linking them and basing block 2 partly on block 1s signature. This goes on throughout the entire chain, with each block linked to the previous one through the signature. Now, let's say block 1s data is changed. Instead of Daniel sending Gary 100 BTC, the data is changed to read 500 BTC. That means block 1 now contains different data and needs a new signature – we'll call it W10. The problem now is that block 2 contains the wrong signature for block 1 – it has X32, whereas the new signature is W10. As such, the first two blocks are now unchained, which informs the blockchain network that data has been changed. The blockchain is designed to be immutable, so the network doesn't verify the change, rejects it, and goes back to the previous blockchain record, which was chained to block 2. That is why it is so difficult to alter information in the blocks. The only way the change could go ahead undetected is block 1's new signature to replace the one currently in block 2. What happens if block 2s data changes? It would need a new signature, too – we'll call it PP4, whereas it was 9BZ. The result is that block 2 is no longer chained to block 3 and so on throughout the blockchain. Anyone can see every block on a blockchain, so for a change to stay undetected, every block must be correctly linked together. When a block is altered, every single block that follows it must have a new signature, right through to the end of the blockchain. This is virtually impossible to do but understanding why requires knowing how these signatures get created. Step Three – How to Create the Hash (Digital Signature) Let's look at a new block 1, which has just one transaction in it – Tony sends Donald 100 BTC. The block now needs a signature, created using a cryptographic hash function, a complex formula that turns any input string into a 64-digit output string – completely unique. Try it for yourself using [https://passwordsgenerator.net/sha256-hash-generator/](https://passwordsgenerator.net/sha256-hash-generator/). If we input the word "Unicorns" into the hash generator, we get the following output: C3BFAD747914BB49344B9477A561CD3E0162CACB3E31B55EBE23C0F14DA7A504 If we change just one character in that input, even swapping a capital letter for a lowercase, adding a period or a space, you get a totally different output. In this case, we'll change the capital letter at the start of the word to a lowercase letter, and we get this output: B660C5090EC2685B8BC11100BA183C0243497D95F91F78AAD987686EFB5F06B5 Change it back to a capital letter, and get the original output. When you use a cryptographic hash function, it will always provide the same output when the same input is used, but we get a new output when the input is changed. The Bitcoin blockchain uses this hash function, with the block data being the input data and the signature relating to the block being the output. So, back to our block again – Tony sends Donald 100 BTC. ![](image_rsrc1P4.jpg)The data string would look something like this: Block 1 Tony -100 Donald +100 Insert this string into the hash function and the signature is: 6CC40EDA53664491E8123EFC867A5FC17A8AD946FD190F23E08E56F572FE05E3 Now that signature gets added to the second block. Now we'll say that Donald sends Simon 100 BTC. Block 2 would look like this: ![](image_rsrc1P5.jpg)The data string is now: Block 2 Donald -100 Simon +100 6CC40EDA53664491E8123EFC867A5FC17A8AD946FD190F23E08E56F572FE05E3 And when this is input into the hashing function, we get this digital signature for block 2: 7763361E1940E7FB3C357976D236370E148AD70DF800D9F68DF9BBD0F4078EB9 The question is, how do these digital signatures prevent a person from inputting a new signature for every block when they alter one? The simple answer is that the blockchain has specific requirements, which are only met via these signatures. This is known as mining and will be explained in the next step. Step Four - Who Signs Blocks and When Do Signatures Qualify? I'll answer the second part of that question first - not every signature will qualify. Blocks can only be accepted onto the blockchain if the digital signature starts with something specific, i.e., a specific number of consecutive zeros, for example, 10. However, each data string is bound to a single unique hash, but what if that hash doesn't have the 10 zeros at the start of it? The only way to ensure that a signature's block matches the requirements the block's string data must be repeatedly changed until the signature starts with the 10 zeros. That said, the transaction data has to stay as it is, as does the metadata (the timestamp, block data, and so on). In that case, a tiny piece of specific data is placed into each block. This data only has one purpose - to be repeatedly changed to find an acceptable signature, and it is called a nonce (number used once). This block nonce is a random string containing only numbers. So, to summarize, block 1 has three things: ●     The transaction data ●     The previous block's signature ●     A nonce The process by which the nonce is repeatedly changed and the block's data is hashed to find an acceptable signature is known as mining - this is what blockchain miners do. Miners use computational power in the form of electricity when they repeatedly change the nonce and hash it to get that signature. The higher the amount of computational power, the faster the block compositions (nonce) can be hashed, and the faster the acceptable signature is found in a process that is nothing more than trial and error. Anyone who participates in the blockchain can do this process - all you need to do is download the appropriate mining software and start it. That way, you contribute to the network's computational power to solve the block's nonce. Now, as mentioned earlier, a hash signature has a specific number of zeros at the start of it - that number is currently 19. It isn't easy to find a hash with that many zeros, and it can take some serious computation power and no small amount of time and luck. Sometimes, a miner will get lucky and quickly find the right signature. One example of that is a recent block on the Bitcoin blockchain. A small miner who only had a small amount of computational power found the right signature when the combined computational power of the rest of the network was 7 trillion times higher than his. To put that in perspective, there is a one in 292 million chance of winning the Powerball Lottery - 24,000 times easier than it was for that one miner to beat an entire network to the right answer. Anyway, the most important takeaway is that it is not easy to find an acceptable signature. Step Five - How Does All This Result in an Immutable Blockchain? As mentioned earlier, when you change a block, it becomes unchained from the blocks that come after it. The network will only accept a changed block if it is chained to subsequent blocks. One change means all other subsequent blocks also need to get new signatures, and they must all meet the specified requirements. To ensure that every block after a changed one has a new signature would take a significant amount of time and computational power. In theory, it can be done, but, in practice, it is virtually impossible, and here's why. Let's say we have a corrupt miner. He has changed a transaction block and now needs to get new signatures for each subsequent block so that the network accepts the block he changed. Here's the rub - while the corrupt miner is doing this, the network is already getting new signatures for subsequent blocks. That means our corrupt miner will also need to generate new signatures for those and any others added to the chain in the meantime. For his changed block to be accepted, he must ensure that every other block that comes after it remains linked, including every new block added in the meantime. Unless he has significantly more computational power than all the network put together, he cannot hope to catch them up. Right now, the Bitcoin blockchain has millions of miners, and it's safe to assume that one or two corrupt miners have nowhere near the combined computational power of the rest of the network. That means any changes they make will never be accepted, which ensures the blockchain's immutability. However, as we mentioned earlier, there is an exception. It's called the 51% attack, and it has happened before. A 51% attack is also known as a double-spend attack. One or more miners attempt to make two purchases with their Bitcoins, i.e., they double-spend them. As an example, I purchase a car costing 15 Bitcoin. The car is delivered, and my Bitcoin goes from my wallet to the car seller's wallet - this may take a few days. If I were to carry out a 51% attack, I could potentially reverse the transaction, keeping my 15 Bitcoins and the car. That means I can spend those Bitcoins on something else. A full explanation is beyond the scope of this book, but suffice it to say, it is highly unlikely that it will ever happen again. Step Six - How the Blockchain Is Governed and How the Rules Are Determined All blockchains follow a democratic governance model, and the transactions record is updated based on what the majority of the network agrees on. This is done automatically by the blockchain protocol by following the longest blockchain record, assuming the majority has agreed on this. That means most of the network's computational power is used to take the longest blockchain version and is also how changed blocks are rejected, simply because that block is now not chained to the longest blockchain. As we know, all the transactions and wallet balances on the Bitcoin blockchain are public, and anyone can see any wallet or any transaction that ever happened. However, while we can see anyone's wallet, we cannot know the wallet owners. Their privacy remains secure. What about Other Cryptocurrencies? All other cryptocurrencies are based on Bitcoin and are merely a form of the first digital currency that has been altered. Most have their own blockchain protocols, which may not share the same rules as the Bitcoin blockchain. Bitcoin was designed as a currency, meaning it has only one purpose - to function as money. Monero is another cryptocurrency that shares that function, but there are additional rules on the blockchain protocol that ensure Monero is far more private and transactions cannot be easily traced. However, any cryptocurrency can have any value, dependent entirely on the crypt issuer. Some are called tokens that allow their owners to do something - for example, getting a gaming license, accessing a social media site, accessing water, electricity, etc. No matter what it is, every token transaction is recorded on a blockchain, and a cryptocurrency exchange can be used to exchange them online. In short, they are the internet's money. One example of an industry that cryptocurrency could disrupt is the stock market. In time, shares and other company property rights will likely be stored on a blockchain as tokens. However, the blockchain is not limited to this. They can also be used to store medical data, history records, identities, tax records, and more. That is why blockchain technology is revolutionary and will have a huge impact on the world as we know it. The next chapter will look at how cryptocurrencies work. 

Chapter 4 How Do Cryptocurrencies Work? ![](image_rsrc1NY.jpg)In the last chapter, we looked at the blockchain and how it works, so it makes sense to move on to how cryptocurrencies work. It makes even more sense to start with some of the basic terms you will likely encounter in your cryptocurrency investing journey. ![](image_rsrc1P6.jpg)Basic Terms As you already know, cryptocurrencies are usually called digital coins, but they are nothing like the physical coins you carry with you every day. The major difference is that digital coins are not tied to an authority party, such as a country, government, or bank, and are not regulated. Let's see this in an example. You want to purchase the latest best-selling novel, so you head to your local book shop. Here's how it works when you pay with a debit card: 

1.  You pass your card to the store cashier or use the contactless facility most stores operate these days 
2.  Your bank details are checked, and the store is essentially asking if you have sufficient money to pay for your purchase 
3.  The bank says yes or no after checking its current records 
4.  If you have enough, the bank allows your transaction to go through and updates its own records to show the money has moved from your account to another 
5.  Because your bank is the middleman in the transaction, they take a cut from the transaction for their troubles 

Now, let's say you didn't want the bank involved. Who would keep your records for you? Who could you trust not to change them or cheat you out of any money? Most people can probably name one person they would trust to do this, but there is another alternative - trusting thousands, even millions of people on a network, to do it for you. As we saw in the last chapter, blockchain technology is based on decentralization, i.e., there is no middleman. When you use cryptocurrencies to make a transaction, the technology maintains its own central transactions record. Instead of the bank having a copy of your records and updating them, your transaction ledger goes to every person on the blockchain network. Every person who has a copy of your transaction ledger records your recent purchase as a transaction. Here's how your book purchase works when you use cryptocurrency: 

1.  Instead of passing your debit card over, you give the cashier your cryptocurrency details 
2.  The store will then ask all network members to see if you have sufficient digital coins for the purchase 
3.  Every network member will look at their records (network members are known as nodes - more about that later) 
4.  If you have sufficient coins, every network node agrees to the purchase 
5.  All the nodes will then update their own records 
6.  One random node will be rewarded 

All this means no one organization or person is tracking your digital coins, but it also means they are not investigating potential fraud. Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies could not exist if they did not have an entire network of nodes, essentially book-keepers, and a small but very important thing called cryptography. Cryptography Cryptography is where the crypto in cryptocurrency comes from, and, in this sense, it means "anonymous."  The term "cryptography" is derived from the Greek "krypto logos," which literally translates to "secret writing." It has long been used to send encoded messages - a key is used to encrypt a message, and only the receiver knows the key to decrypt it. For example, in 1799, the Rosetta Stone was found in Egypt, near Rosetta, by Napoleon's soldiers and 19th\-century scholars decrypted the Egyptian hieroglyphics on it. Today, we can send digitally encrypted messages, and they are decrypted using algorithms and cryptographic services. Cryptography is built-in to cryptocurrencies to ensure anonymity and security. Although digital coins are decentralized and unregulated, cryptography is one way they stay secure and protect against double-spending. There are three primary encryption methods used: 

1.  Hashing - in the last chapter on blockchain technology, I told you that a hash is similar to a signature. Hash functions take the input data, perform an operation, and return an output representative of the input data but of a smaller, fixed size. A cryptocurrency hash function is used to solve a mathematical problem akin to guessing a padlock combination. Hashing encodes account addresses, maintains the blockchain data structure, and ensures that block mining is possible. We'll talk more about mining in chapter 8. 
2.  Symmetric Encryption Cryptography - this is the simplest method of cryptographic encryption, with just a single secret key for both parties in a transaction. However, it does have a disadvantage - anyone involved in the transaction must exchange the key, so the sender uses it to encrypt the data, and the receiver uses the key to decrypt it. 
3.  Asymmetric Encryption Cryptography - this kind of encryption uses a public and private key. The receiver's public key can be used to decrypt data, but the sender can only use their private key to encrypt it. 

Nodes Nodes are the network members, the devices that run the network and keep all the transaction records. This is what makes it possible for a blockchain to be decentralized. That device may be a desktop or laptop computer, a tablet, smartphone, even a printer - the only requirement is that it has a fast internet connection and can access the blockchain. Mining The node owners help run the network by contributing electricity and computing resources to store transactions and validate them. In the process, they have the chance to get a reward and earn the transaction fees. This is called mining, and the owners are the miners. However, it's worth noting that only some cryptocurrencies can be mined. Bitcoin can and a few of the other bigger ones, but Ripple, and some others, don't partake in mining because they don't want their platforms dependent on vast amounts of electricity for mining the cryptocurrency. So, here's how the process works: 

*   Miners need to use software to solve mathematical (cryptographic) puzzles to add transaction blocks onto the ledger; they compete against one another to win bits of the relevant cryptocurrency as a reward. 
*   The reason it is known as mining is that it extracts cryptocurrency. 
*   Anyone can do it, so long as they have a device that connects to the internet and the right software. Your device needs to solve the blockchain-generated puzzle by guessing a random number. 
*   To do this, your device must calculate multiple 256-bit hashes or 64-character strings and make sure they have the right answer - the equation will confirm this. This is why a powerful device is needed - the more power it has, the more guesses it can take per second, which gives you a much better chance of winning. You earn the cryptocurrency reward and add the block of transactions to the chain if you win. 

Proof-Of-Work Miners who want their transactions and block added to the blockchain must provide an answer to a specific puzzle, but it isn't easy to provide, which is why so much computing power is needed. However, it is easy for the rest of the network to verify the correct answer in a process known as proof-of-work. For example, if you manage to guess a padlock's combination, it is proof of the challenge. It isn't easy to start with because you need to go through tons of combinations, but verification is easy - simply enter the code, and if the padlock unlocks, it's the correct one. The same applies to cryptocurrency mining. The miner who solves the problem before any of the others is rewarded. This is a way of incentivizing the miner to continue and providing healthy competition between them. Some cryptocurrencies use proof-of-work to ensure that the network hasn't been manipulated. However, this concept does have downsides where blockchain technology is concerned. One of the biggest is the sheer amount of electricity and computing power needed to make what amounts to random guesses. Many new cryptocurrencies have shunned proof-of-work in favor of a different concept called proof-of-stake. Proof-Of-Stake The proof-of-stake concept is different from proof-of-work because you must prove ownership of a specific stake or amount of money. That means those with the most amounts of crypto have the most mining power, thus getting rid of the requirement for vast amounts of computing power. It is easy to prove the calculations in this concept, so you have ownership of a percentage of all available cryptos. This system also differs in that block rewards are not offered. Instead, the "winning" miner is given the transaction fees. This is how the system is many times more effective than proof-of-work. However, proof-of-stake also has its downsides. You could say that the system rewards those who hoard coins. Each miner can only mine the transaction percentage related to their cryptocurrency stake. For example, if a proof-of-stake miner owns 20% of the total available cryptocurrency, they can mine 10% of the network blocks. This limitation provides nodes with an excellent reason to save rather than spend their coins. It could also lead to the rich continuing to get rich because they can mine a bigger percentage of the network blocks. Proof-Of-Importance The proof-of-importance concept first came about with the NEM platform in support of its cryptocurrency, XEM. It is similar to proof-of-stake in that nodes are only allowed to mine if they hold a specific amount of cryptocurrency. The network then scores all those nodes who meet the requirements, creating a block with a similar proportion to the score. However, nodes don't score higher just because they have more cryptocurrency. Instead, the score considers all sorts of variables to solve the problem of hoarding seen with proof-of-stake. The NEM community uses harvesting, which Investopedia defines as "Instead of each miner contributing its mining power in a cumulative manner to a computing node, a harvesting participant simply links his account to an existing supernode and uses that account's computing power to complete blocks on his behalf." Putting It All Together To summarize, this is how cryptocurrency works: 

1.  When you use cryptocurrency to purchase something, your network and wallet will check your transactions history to ensure you have sufficient crypto to make the purchase. This requires your public and private keys, which we'll discuss in chapter 6 when we talk about wallets. 
2.  The transaction gets encrypted, sent to the network, and added to a queue where it waits to go on the distributed ledger. 
3.  The mining process adds the transaction to the ledger. The addresses for the sender and receiver are hash values or IDs tied to a wallet. These are not linked to any particular user ID, allowing the user to remain anonymous. 
4.  In proof-of-work cryptocurrencies, a puzzle must be solved by the network miners before the transaction can go on the blockchain. In proof-of-stake, each node's mining power is directly related to the number of coins they hold, rather than actual computing power. This solves the issues raised with Bitcoin, where vast amounts of electricity and expensive computers were required to guess random numbers. Proof-of-information uses different variables to choose which nodes have mining power, which solves the hoarding issue raised by proof-of-stake.

Adaptive Scaling This is one of the major reasons people choose to invest in cryptocurrency, and it means that, as time passes, it gets more difficult to mine a cryptocurrency. This is why cryptocurrencies have a cap on supply, thus creating scarcity, and, as more coins enter public circulation, the mining reward is reduced too. Adaptive scaling ensures that mining difficulty rises and falls based on coin popularity and the blockchain, thus ensuring cryptocurrencies enjoy market longevity. Decentralization The biggest advantage to the blockchain is that the technology is decentralized, meaning no one organization or person controls it. Some say that Ripple, and other similar cryptocurrencies, are not really classed as decentralized, and their reasoning is that they don't follow the mining protocol Bitcoin uses. There is some debate over whether Ripple and any other cryptocurrency that isn't mined is considered a real cryptocurrency, but that doesn't stop you from investing them, and they can prove to be quite lucrative. Harvesting Harvesting is a method used in place of mining to maintain the network's integrity. The NEM platform designed it to generate XEM, which is their cryptocurrency. Harvesting works in this way: 

*   When a transaction is carried out, the first device that sees the transaction and verifies it notifies all nearby nodes of it. This creates an information cascade.

Open-Source Cryptocurrencies are classed as open-source, meaning that anyone can join in and participate in the network and the mining or harvesting process without paying a fee. Public Ledger Ledgers are a standard method of keeping records, i.e., recording data and other information. A ledger is still used with cryptocurrencies, but it is public. Smart Contracts Also known as digital, blockchain, or self-executing contracts, these are similar to a written contract but are entirely digital, removing the middleman you get in traditional contracts. Using a smart contract allows you to set up investment products, automatic payments, and other similar things without needing to use a bank or other central authority. Smart contracts are not written on paper; they are written into small computer programs stored on the blockchain and run from there. Every transaction through one of these contracts is distributed, and there is no central authority in control of any aspect of the transaction. And because the blockchain is used to store the program, it makes the smart contract immutable, which means it cannot be changed once entered onto the blockchain. However, immutability has disadvantages. Because the smart contract cannot be changed in any way, if there are any errors in the code, they cannot be fixed. This means that security surrounding smart contracts is quite difficult. Some companies choose to audit their smart contracts to get around this issue, but that can be expensive. In time, we expect the development life cycles to improve and better coding practice to help combat these security issues. At the end of the day, although successful so far, smart contracts are still very young and have plenty of time to prove themselves. Cryptocurrency Forks Cryptocurrency forks have the potential to be lucrative. Some of today's more popular cryptocurrencies came about as the result of a cryptocurrency fork or split, but what exactly is this? Sometimes, not all the developers on a network agree to a rule change or the direction a crypto is heading. Often, they decide to split away from the main blockchain and start a fork. Think about the fork you eat your meals with - one handle and a few breaches (the tines). That is what a cryptocurrency fork is. Some cryptocurrencies are built on open-source software, and they all have a protocol that all network members should follow. Some examples of the rules the network should follow include: 

*   The size of the transaction blocks
*   Rewards given to "winning" network participants, harvesters, or miners
*   How the transaction fees are calculated

However, it is unlikely that cryptocurrencies will ever finish being developed because they are nothing more than projects built on software. As with anything, there is always some space for improvement, and developers often release updates to increase their crypto's performance or fix issues. Some may be small updates, while others change how a cryptocurrency works - that same crypto they loved when they first developed it. In all relationships, we grow strong together, or we grow apart. When developers or network members strongly disagree on something, they have two options - shut up and stick with what's what, or move on and create a protocol of their own. Hard vs. Soft Forks A fork is never just a fork; it could be a soft fork or a hard one. The majority of cryptocurrencies comprise a protocol (the rules) and the blockchain. If some of the network decides they want their own protocol, they will copy the original code and then change it - this can only be done where a cryptocurrency is fully open-source. Once the changes have been put in place, the developers decide where they want their fork to start, which will be a specific block number. The network will divide when the original cryptocurrency hits that block number on the blockchain. Some of the network opt to stay with the original while the rest go to the new fork. Each separate network will then start to add new transaction blocks to their fork, rendering the two chains incompatible. That is a hard fork, and the two resulting blockchains will never interact again, not even to acknowledge the old blockchain's transactions or nodes. A soft fork is somewhat different. Think of a relationship that breaks up, but the partners stay friends - that's a soft fork. The developers or network may decide to split the blockchain while ensuring that their new protocol changes are compatible with the original. Let's say the developers choose block 800 for the soft fork. Most of the network may choose to follow the strong blockchain that follows both sets of rules. If a consensus is reached between the two sides, the newer rules are implemented across the entire network. Any nodes that choose not to follow and continue mining are simply wasting their time and resources. The community rejoins, and everyone is happy until the next disagreement rolls around. Free Money When a blockchain forks, the new one is based on the old one, so all the transactions previously recorded will also be on the fork. Simply put, when the chain forks, the developers take a "snapshot" of the ledger at the block they choose for the fork to happen, and the new chain is a duplicate of the old one up to that point. So, if you held 10 BTC in your wallet at the point the chain forks, you still have 10 BTC afterward. So how do you get free money? When a blockchain forks, you must hold cryptocurrency on a platform supported by the fork, and it must be before the chosen block number before the fork happens - this is classed as free money. However, its value depends entirely on the performance of the new fork and its subsequent popularity. Example - Bitcoin vs. Bitcoin Cash Bitcoin has been the subject of forks in the past, and one of the best-known occurred on 1st August 2017, Bitcoin's birthday. The developers were in disagreement over block sizes - some said it should be increased to 2 MB, while others said it should be 32MB. Some of the network fell for the big idea, but the rest just thought making the block size that big was crazy. In the end, the two groups split and went their own way. Bitcoin Cash was created and got a new symbol - BCH. Those who followed the fork and already had BTC holdings were given an identical amount in BCH placed in their wallets. Today, BCH is worth just over $287, while BTC is worth almost $40,000. It will take time to know if BCH will ever get past the value of the original protocol, but the network members who followed did, at least, get something for their troubles. Chapter 8 Cryptocurrency Mining Explained ![](image_rsrc1NY.jpg)Ask as many people as you like what they think cryptocurrency mining is. Some will look at you like you've grown an extra head, while many others will say it's just a way of getting more crypto coins. In a way, it is, but it involves so much more. Mining is also about validating transactions on the blockchain and adding them in blocks to the ledger. Perhaps its most important job, though, is to prevent double-spending. Think about the physical fiat currency you spend. When you make a purchase, your bank updates your account, showing the payment leaving your account and going into another one. It works the same way with cryptocurrencies. When a crypto purchase is made, the digital ledger is updated, showing the debit from one account and the credit to another. However, digital platforms are constantly being hacked or manipulated. The Bitcoin ledger overcame this challenge by creating a system where transactions could only be updated on the ledger by verified miners. This ensures those miners are responsible for ensuring double-spending cannot take place. While the miners keep the network secure, new crypto coins are being generated. These are given to the miners to reward them for their work. Because there is no central authority on a distributed ledger, mining is critical to ensure transactions are validated, hence the incentive for the miners to take part - if they validate the transaction, they win the reward.  A consensus protocol is in place to ensure that only those verified miners can do the work. This is known as a proof-of-work protocol, and it also keeps the network secured against outside attacks. Proof-of-Work You can think of cryptocurrency mining as akin to mining precious gems or metals. While these miners will uncover diamonds, gold, silver, etc., cryptocurrency miners unearth new coins and bring them into circulation. A miner needs to use a powerful machine to solve complicated mathematical problems - cryptographic hashes to earn the rewards on offer. A hash is a shortened digital signature representing the data in the transaction, generated to keep the data on public networks secure. Miners compete against one another to solve the hash value that each transaction generates - the first one to solve it adds the transaction block to the blockchain and gets the reward. The hash function for each transaction block contains a reference to the previous block. This continues right back to the first block, known as the genesis block, creating an unbroken chain. This is why each network node can verify and validate blocks and can verify whether the winning miner correctly solved the hash. As more miners join the network and more powerful computation resources are used, the equations become more difficult to solve. This increases the competition and adds to the cryptocurrency's scarcity. How to Start Mining To start mining on any cryptocurrency blockchain, you need a powerful computer with the right software designed to solve the cryptographic equations. In the early days of blockchain technology, you could mine simple cryptocurrencies with a home computer containing a simple CPU chip. However, as the difficulty levels have risen over the years, those CPU chips no longer do the job. Today, you need one of two things: 

*   An ASIC (application-specific integrated circuit) miner 
*   A specialized GPU 

The GPUs must also be connected to a fast, reliable internet connection 24/7. Each cryptocurrency miner must also be part of an online mining pool. Different Mining Methods The mining method you choose will dictate the length of time it takes. In the early days, CPU mining was the chosen method. However, it is incredibly slow and is no longer practical because of the length of time needed to accrue a tiny profit - it can take months, and mining rigs use a significant amount of electricity and cooling. The cost is often way beyond what the profits are. GPU mining is now a more common method, using a set of GPUs in a single mining rig to maximize the computational power. The rig also requires a motherboard and an effective cooling system. ASIC mining is also becoming popular. ASIC mining rigs are designed ONLY to mine cryptos, unlike GPU rigs that can be used as ordinary, albeit powerful, computers. Because of this, ASIC miners can produce more crypto units, but they are more expensive - the more difficult mining becomes, the more likely it is that these rigs will disappear. Cloud mining is slowly taking over, eliminating the rising costs of ASIC and GPU mining. People who do cloud mining can leverage power from dedicated mining facilities and large corporations. As an individual miner, you can find paid and free mining hosts and rent a rig for a set period, making it a hands-free mining method. Mining Pools While you are perfectly at liberty to mine alone, many people choose to join a mining pool. All the miners' resources are combined, providing more computational power to increase the chances of solving the equation and earning the reward. Should a pool succeed, the reward is split among all members, proportionate to the level of resources each contributes. Most cryptocurrency mining applications already have a mining pool, but you also have the option of creating a pool by joining other like-minded enthusiasts online. Once you join a pool, you are not tied to staying with it - you can move between pools as much as you want, and many people move because some pools earn higher rewards. However, many miners feel that the official pools are better - they are more reliable, offer technical support, and are updated regularly by the hosting companies. *   The hash rate produced by the mining rig 
*   Power consumption 
*   Overall costs

For example, a typical ASIC miner used to create one Bitcoin every 10 minutes will use roughly 72 terawatts of power - 1 terawatt is sufficient to power 10 billion 100-watt lightbulbs simultaneously, just to give you an idea of how much power that is! As blockchain technology gets better and the mining difficulty becomes harder, that power consumption figure will change. You also need to factor in the costs for keeping the rig cool, especially when using ASIC or GPU rigs. 2.  When they exchange or sell their reward tokens or coins

Chapter 13 A Word about Taxes ![](image_rsrc1NY.jpg)The last couple of years have been pretty wild for the cryptocurrency market. Over 10% of Americans traded, and more than a few wonder what the score is regarding taxes. Many new crypto investors think that they are exempt from tax, but this isn't the case, unfortunately. Anyone who trades in crypto must report conversions, sales, payments, and their income from crypto to the state tax authorities and IRS where applicable. What makes it harder is that each type of transaction is subject to different tax rules. This chapter will cover those rules for you, so you are in no doubt what you need to report. Before we move on, though, I must tell you that you should speak to a tax professional experienced in crypto taxes to ensure you get things right. ![](image_rsrc1PG.jpg)Do I Owe Taxes on My Crypto? Cryptocurrency is a digital asset and is considered as such in the USA. Because of this, it is treated in the same way the IRS treats bonds, stocks, and capital assets. This means that any money gained from your crypto is taxed at all different rates, dependent on the method used to obtain the crypto and the length of time you held it. So, how do you know if you owe any taxes? The most important thing is to look at how your crypto was used in the previous year, in this case, 2021. A crypto transaction where tax is due is called a "taxable event," while those where tax is not due are called "non-taxable events. Let's break down what each involves: Non-Taxable Events: 

*   Purchasing and Holding Crypto - you do not need to declare tax on cryptocurrency when you just purchase and hold it. That comes later when the crypto is sold and you "realize" the gains. 
*   Donations to Non-Profit or Tax-Exempt Charities - provided the charity is a registered 501(c)(3) organization, you can donate crypto without incurring taxes and may even be able to claim charitable deductions. 
*   Receiving Crypto as a Gift - if someone gifts you some crypto, you will not need to pay tax unless you sell it, stake it, or partake in any other taxable activity. 
*   Giving Crypto as a Gift - you can gift each recipient $15,000 or less per year without needing to pay tax - that amount is higher for a spouse. If you go over $15,000, a gift tax return must be filed. However, this doesn't usually generate a tax liability. Whenever you send crypto to another person, and it is not classed as paying for a service or goods, it may be considered a gift, even if it was intended that way. 
*   Transferring Between Your Own Wallets/Accounts \- this is not considered taxable. In preparation for an eventual trade or sale, it is possible to transfer the original cost basis and the date you acquired the asset so you can track your tax impact. 

Taxable Events - Capital Gains 

*   Selling Cryptocurrency for Cash - if you sell your crypto and receive U.S. dollars in exchange, you are liable for tax on any amount above what you paid for the asset. For example, if you purchased crypto for $1000 and sell it for $1500, you must pay tax on the additional $500. However, if you sell for less than you paid, you may be able to show it as a loss. 
*   Converting Crypto to Another Crypto - for example, you use Bitcoin to purchase Ether. Technically, your Bitcoin must be sold before purchasing Ether, and the IRS treats this as a taxable sale. If you sell for more than you originally paid, you must pay taxes on the price difference. 
*   Using Crypto to Buy Goods and Services - any transaction where crypto is used to buy a service or goods, tax is due. According to the IRS, there is little difference between spending and selling crypto - in these transactions, the crypto is sold before it can be exchanged for services or goods, making it a taxable transaction. 

Taxable Event - Income 

*   Receiving Payment in Crypto - if you receive your salary in crypto, you are liable for income tax as it is classed as compensation, and you are taxed per your tax bracket. 
*   Receiving Crypto as Payment for Services or Goods - this is taxable as it is treated the same way as a sale. It is classed as income, and you must report it as such. 
*   Mining Cryptocurrency - if you are a crypto miner and earn rewards/transaction frees, you may have to pay taxes. This is based on a fair market value of the coins when you receive them. If you are mining crypto as a business concern, it must be registered as self-employment income. 
*   Receiving Staking Rewards - in the same way as earnings from mining, these earnings are taxable, based on a fair market value on the day you receive the reward. 
*   Other Income - if you hold cryptos and earn a return from them, it is considered income and is taxable as such. Although this may be interest paid on your holdings, the IRS doesn't treat it in the same way as interest earned on your bank account. 
*   Receiving an Airdrop - crypto airdrops are when you receive them as part of a giveaway or marketing campaign. This is classed as taxable income and must be reported as such. 
*   Receiving Other Rewards or Incentives - this could be from incentives, friend referrals to certain exchanges, and so on - anything like this must be reported as taxable income. 

How Much Do I Owe? So, you've probably determined that you will owe taxes on some of your crypto activities. What do you do now? Well, you could estimate your taxes by working out your income, losses, and gains. Calculating Your Income As a U.S. taxpayer, you are used to seeing your state and federal income tax deducted straight from your pay. Any crypto assets earned as income must be declared as income and taxes paid. However, these are not treated the same way as your salaried income, i.e., the taxes won't be automatically deducted, and you must report them on your yearly income tax form. The taxes owed on this income is determined based on your tax bracket. However, be aware of one thing - if you have earned a high level of additional income from your crypto assets, you may be moved up a tax bracket or so and will have to pay higher taxes on your earnings. This will include your fiat money earnings, such as salary. Calculating Your Capital Gains and Losses Before calculating this, you must know the amount of crypto you had at the start of the tax year. This is known as your cost basis. When you purchase crypto, this is based on the amount you paid for the asset. However, if your crypto came from staking or mining, a fair market value is used to work out the cost basis based on when you receive the crypto. In terms of crypto you received as a gift, this depends on the cost basis of the person gifting it to you and a fair market value on the day you received it. If you sell your cryptocurrency, the cost basis is deducted from the sale price to determine if you have made a capital gain (if it is more than your cost basis) or a capital loss (when it is less). Short-Term vs. Long-Term Capital Gains These taxes are applied at the state level, where applicable, and federal level and may be short or long-term. The length of time you have held your assets will determine how much tax you must pay. If you have held them for at least 12 months, the tax rate is typically lower than if you held them for less. 

*   Long-Term Gains - these are typically taxed at a lower capital gains tax rate. At the federal level, these rates are 0%, 15%, and 20% and are based on your total income. Those taxpayers in the higher income tax brackets may also have to pay a Net Investment Income Tax on income and gains, currently 3.8%. 
*   Short-Term Gains - these are typically taxed at your current income tax rate, usually a higher rate, which makes it worthwhile holding onto your assets for at least a year before selling them. 

Don't forget; when you sell your crypto for cash, convert it into a different crypto asset, or purchase goods or services with it, it results in a taxable event because you have "realized" the losses or gains. If you continue to hold the original crypto, your gains or losses are "unrealized." Understanding Capital Losses If you sell a crypto asset for less than you originally bought it for, you have "realized" a capital loss. However, these aren't always bad news and can sometimes work to your advantage. Losses can be used to offset your capital gains, even when they come from stocks or other non-crypto holdings. This offset can be done on a dollar-for-dollar basis and can reduce your tax liability for the year. If your losses are far higher than your gains or you don't "realize" any gains at all, there is a limit to how much you can declare as losses to offset your liabilities. That limit is $3,000 per year - if your losses are higher, anything over $3,000 is carried over to the following year, and this will continue until the total loss amount has been applied. Investing is all about profit, so never allow your heart to rule your head - not too much anyway. Yes, gut feeling is good, but relying solely on it can result in financial disaster. 

10.  Use IDDA 

Lastly, regardless of the type of trader or investor you are, you should always use IDDA (see chapter 12) to help and guide you. You will need to understand the markets and have plenty of patience, but this strategy will pay off in spades if you put the time in. There's no winging it. Just because your favorite movie star promotes a crypto, it doesn't mean you should go ahead and plow all your money into it. Analysis is key. You may believe you have stumbled on a strong trend, but it is only on a single time frame - unless you look for more indicators, you cannot say you have done your technical analysis. Before you make an investment decision, ensure all the IDDA elements are pointing you the same way.
