
# Chapter 13 A Word about Taxes

The last couple of years have been pretty wild for the cryptocurrency market. Over 10% of Americans traded, and more than a few wonder what the score is regarding taxes. Many new crypto investors think that they are exempt from tax, but this isn't the case, unfortunately. Anyone who trades in crypto must report conversions, sales, payments, and their income from crypto to the state tax authorities and IRS where applicable. What makes it harder is that each type of transaction is subject to different tax rules. This chapter will cover those rules for you, so you are in no doubt what you need to report. Before we move on, though, I must tell you that you should speak to a tax professional experienced in crypto taxes to ensure you get things right.

## Do I Owe Taxes on My Crypto?

Cryptocurrency is a digital asset and is considered as such in the USA. Because of this, it is treated in the same way the IRS treats bonds, stocks, and capital assets. This means that any money gained from your crypto is taxed at all different rates, dependent on the method used to obtain the crypto and the length of time you held it. So, how do you know if you owe any taxes? The most important thing is to look at how your crypto was used in the previous year, in this case, 2021. A crypto transaction where tax is due is called a "taxable event," while those where tax is not due are called "non-taxable events. Let's break down what each involves:

### Non-Taxable Events

*   **Purchasing and Holding Crypto** - you do not need to declare tax on cryptocurrency when you just purchase and hold it. That comes later when the crypto is sold and you "realize" the gains.
*   **Donations to Non-Profit or Tax-Exempt Charities** - provided the charity is a registered 501(c)(3) organization, you can donate crypto without incurring taxes and may even be able to claim charitable deductions.
*   **Receiving Crypto as a Gift** - if someone gifts you some crypto, you will not need to pay tax unless you sell it, stake it, or partake in any other taxable activity.
*   **Giving Crypto as a Gift** - you can gift each recipient $15,000 or less per year without needing to pay tax - that amount is higher for a spouse. If you go over $15,000, a gift tax return must be filed. However, this doesn't usually generate a tax liability. Whenever you send crypto to another person, and it is not classed as paying for a service or goods, it may be considered a gift, even if it was intended that way.
*   **Transferring Between Your Own Wallets/Accounts** - this is not considered taxable. In preparation for an eventual trade or sale, it is possible to transfer the original cost basis and the date you acquired the asset so you can track your tax impact.

### Taxable Events - Capital Gains

*   **Selling Cryptocurrency for Cash** - if you sell your crypto and receive U.S. dollars in exchange, you are liable for tax on any amount above what you paid for the asset. For example, if you purchased crypto for $1000 and sell it for $1500, you must pay tax on the additional $500. However, if you sell for less than you paid, you may be able to show it as a loss.
*   **Converting Crypto to Another Crypto** - for example, you use Bitcoin to purchase Ether. Technically, your Bitcoin must be sold before purchasing Ether, and the IRS treats this as a taxable sale. If you sell for more than you originally paid, you must pay taxes on the price difference.
*   **Using Crypto to Buy Goods and Services** - any transaction where crypto is used to buy a service or goods, tax is due. According to the IRS, there is little difference between spending and selling crypto - in these transactions, the crypto is sold before it can be exchanged for services or goods, making it a taxable transaction.

### Taxable Event - Income

*   **Receiving Payment in Crypto** - if you receive your salary in crypto, you are liable for income tax as it is classed as compensation, and you are taxed per your tax bracket.
*   **Receiving Crypto as Payment for Services or Goods** - this is taxable as it is treated the same way as a sale. It is classed as income, and you must report it as such.
*   **Mining Cryptocurrency** - if you are a crypto miner and earn rewards/transaction frees, you may have to pay taxes. This is based on a fair market value of the coins when you receive them. If you are mining crypto as a business concern, it must be registered as self-employment income.
*   **Receiving Staking Rewards** - in the same way as earnings from mining, these earnings are taxable, based on a fair market value on the day you receive the reward.
*   **Other Income** - if you hold cryptos and earn a return from them, it is considered income and is taxable as such. Although this may be interest paid on your holdings, the IRS doesn't treat it in the same way as interest earned on your bank account.
*   **Receiving an Airdrop** - crypto airdrops are when you receive them as part of a giveaway or marketing campaign. This is classed as taxable income and must be reported as such.
*   **Receiving Other Rewards or Incentives** - this could be from incentives, friend referrals to certain exchanges, and so on - anything like this must be reported as taxable income.

## How Much Do I Owe?

So, you've probably determined that you will owe taxes on some of your crypto activities. What do you do now? Well, you could estimate your taxes by working out your income, losses, and gains.

### Calculating Your Income

As a U.S. taxpayer, you are used to seeing your state and federal income tax deducted straight from your pay. Any crypto assets earned as income must be declared as income and taxes paid. However, these are not treated the same way as your salaried income, i.e., the taxes won't be automatically deducted, and you must report them on your yearly income tax form. The taxes owed on this income is determined based on your tax bracket. However, be aware of one thing - if you have earned a high level of additional income from your crypto assets, you may be moved up a tax bracket or so and will have to pay higher taxes on your earnings. This will include your fiat money earnings, such as salary.

### Calculating Your Capital Gains and Losses

Before calculating this, you must know the amount of crypto you had at the start of the tax year. This is known as your cost basis. When you purchase crypto, this is based on the amount you paid for the asset. However, if your crypto came from staking or mining, a fair market value is used to work out the cost basis based on when you receive the crypto. In terms of crypto you received as a gift, this depends on the cost basis of the person gifting it to you and a fair market value on the day you received it. If you sell your cryptocurrency, the cost basis is deducted from the sale price to determine if you have made a capital gain (if it is more than your cost basis) or a capital loss (when it is less).

### Short-Term vs. Long-Term Capital Gains

These taxes are applied at the state level, where applicable, and federal level and may be short or long-term. The length of time you have held your assets will determine how much tax you must pay. If you have held them for at least 12 months, the tax rate is typically lower than if you held them for less.

*   **Long-Term Gains** - these are typically taxed at a lower capital gains tax rate. At the federal level, these rates are 0%, 15%, and 20% and are based on your total income. Those taxpayers in the higher income tax brackets may also have to pay a Net Investment Income Tax on income and gains, currently 3.8%.
*   **Short-Term Gains** - these are typically taxed at your current income tax rate, usually a higher rate, which makes it worthwhile holding onto your assets for at least a year before selling them.
