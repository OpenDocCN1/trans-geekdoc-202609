Previous studies found mixed results concerning the importance of online exposure to explain victimization in cyberspace. For instance, Holt and Bossler (2009) did not find a statistically relationship between loss of digital information by malware and online shopping, online chatting and online banking. Moreover, Leukfeldt (2014) did not observe a relationship between the activities performed online and the risk of victimization. On the other hand, Marcum et al. (2010) and Ngo and Pateroster (2011) found that the online exposure (e.g. number of hours per week that respondents engaged in instant messaging) increased the likelihood of experiencing online victimization. Furthermore, van Wilson (2011) observed that participation in forums and online shopping increased the likelihood of online fraud victimization and, in accordance, Reynolds (2013) found that making reservations online, online social networking and purchasing behaviors were related to phishing. Specifically in what concerns OIT and RAT, a set of important needs needs to be pointed out. For instance, Reynolds (2013) discovered that individuals who used online banking and/or e-mail/instant messaging were about 50% more likely to become victims of OIT.
