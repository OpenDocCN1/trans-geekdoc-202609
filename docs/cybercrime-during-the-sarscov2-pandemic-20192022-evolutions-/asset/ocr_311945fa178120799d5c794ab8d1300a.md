The “great scattering” of extremists had a twofold effect: first, it enabled online content to be distributed away from the mainstream; second, brought previously niche communities in contact with elements of the larger anti-government movement. It did largely through promoting “shared attitudes toward (doubting) the legitimacy of the pandemic, lockdown orders and the role of law enforcement and other government officials” [FIN 20]. By migrating to the alt-tech ecosystem, accelerationists interacted with unprecedented intensity with online communities of firearms enthusiasts, survivalists, QAnon adherents and others, comparing grievances and building a “big tent” [KIL 21] for a mass movement. Historically, an entanglement of such massive scale, involving varying political backgrounds and ideologies, would have prevented extremist researchers referring to “ideological coherence” [ISD 21]. Recent research, shows that the ideological antagonism that traditionally plagued the American far-right is eroding to some extent [STA 11]. This ideological convergence is not surprising, the invigorating effect that the coronavirus pandemic has on online communities – especially those on the anti-government spectrum [KRI 21]. Research conducted during the pandemic showed that far-right milit proliferated with unprecedented speed on social media, such as Telegram, with Boogaloo-related chatter nearly doubling during quarantine periods [ONG 20; DAV 21]. This may be the notable quickening in radicalization timelines – that is, the time it takes from initial exposure to extremist beliefs to participation in extremist violence [YAT 16] – which researchers warn has changed markedly since the advent of the Covid-19 pandemic. Research from early as 2016 shows that increasing interaction on social media tends to speed up the duration of radicalization. Over time, the duration has shrunk from 18 months in 2005, to as few as 7 months today [STA 11].

One is also confronted by the issue of numbers. Arguably, the most notable difference between the Covid-19 era and prior eras of violent extremism in the United States relates to the sheer size of radicalized populations. On January 20, 2021, Americans were given a first taste of the size of armed radicalization over Covid-19. On that day, the Virginia Citizens Defense League, a state-based gun advocacy group, organized Lobby Day, a pro-gun rally, at the Virginia State Capitol in Richmond, Virginia.
