# Hacker Crackdown: Law and Disorder on the Electronic Frontier (Sterling 1992). At that time, the Internet was far from what it is nowadays.

Nevertheless, these visionaries saw something coming. This subject was coming to stay in the public debate and in the legal literature. Harry Rubin, Leah Fraser, and Monica Smith wrote an article about the criminal potential over the Internet in 1995 (Rubin et al. 1995). They stated that the adaptation of the law to the Internet scenario was something hard to do and even that the 1984 (Counterfeit Access Device and Computer Fraud and Abuse Act) and the 1986 (Electronic Communications Privacy Act) federal amendments were unable to solve most of the legal issues. For Jo-Ann M. Adams, the solution was to amend some more of the 1984 Act, passing into law some other provisions, as in the case of the Communications Decency Act (CDA) of 1995, to effectively rule over “anarchy on the Internet” (Adams 1996).

Defining clear lines to separate common crimes from computer crimes was still very difficult for criminal law. One reason was that some crimes involve intellectual property and intangible goods. David Wall tried to classify cybercrime in parallel to actual crimes. He wrote about “cyber-trespass,” “cyber-theft,” “cyber obscurity” and “cyber-violence” (Wall 1999). His objective was to criticize both authors focused on the crimes themselves authors who tried to classify “new” social behaviors. Nonetheless, his classification at that time was too wide and lacking refinement. Another way to look at the hardware involved. Carl Benson, Andrew Jablon, Paul Kaplan, and Maria Elena Rosenthal proposed this. They define three possibilities to classify “computer crime” (Benson et al. 1997). Computers could be targets of criminal activities as someone could steal them. Also, a computer could be instruments of traditional crimes. We will see that this definition has more refined over the years. However, at some point, the logical basis of this classification is still valid. Also, the expansion of the Internet comes along with the international aspect of cybercrime.

One example is figuring out how to prosecute crimes outside the borders perpetrating intellectual property crimes (CHR 97, ZEV 97–98).
