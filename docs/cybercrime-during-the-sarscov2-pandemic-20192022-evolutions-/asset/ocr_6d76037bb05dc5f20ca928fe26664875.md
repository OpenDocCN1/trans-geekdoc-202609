European Commission (EC) assesses the data protection level of a destination country, territory, or organization at least every four years [Article 36(3)(4)(5)(6)]. It also publishes the results [Article 36(8)].

Evaluation of three significant aspects.
1 – Legal order that must comprise the rule of law, respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms, relevant legislation, including public security, defense, national security and criminal law and the access of authorities to personal data [Article 36(2)(a)].
2 – Protecting systems that grants and enforceable data protection rights by administrative and judicial redress, with an independent supervisory authority [Article 36(2)(a)].
3 – International commitments, conventions or instruments, and participation in multilateral or regional systems for data protection [Article 36(2)(c)].

EU member states must enforce the EC decision to assess whether transfers happen only to safe destinations [Article 36(1)]. Also, they must not discriminate transfers relying on other lawfully from Articles 37 and 38 [Article 36(7)].

Amadeo Santuososo and Alessandra Malerba explain legal interoperability as an alternative concept for having a new glaze on comparative law and international law (Santosuososo and Malerba 2014). The central aspect of the concept is its offer to look at dynamic interchanges between different legal contexts. They also highlight that the theoretical center of the debate lies in both linguistic and cultural understandings. They define three situations. In the first situation, there is a legal system with just language in use. It is the temporal transformation by which societies shape new legal concepts or modify their interpretation. In the second situation, different legal systems use the same language, like the United States and the United Kingdom. In the third situation, there are different legal systems with different languages. One could state that the core solution is to have a perfect translation. However, that is precisely the problem.
