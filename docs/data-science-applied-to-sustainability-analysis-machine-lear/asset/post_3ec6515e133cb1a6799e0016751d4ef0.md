## General methods for process LCA

A standard process LCA has four major components: 1) goal and scope definition, 2) inventory analysis, 3) impact assessment, and 4) interpretation and analysis (ISO 2006a). These components are often addressed sequentially and refined iteratively throughout the process of building an LCA model (Fig. 8.2).

### Goal and scope definition

The first step in LCA is to develop a clearly defined goal that specifies the system of interest, the intended use of model results, and the intended audience. For example, the goal of an LCA on beef may be “to quantify the environmental impacts of beef” to “identify areas of potential improvement in the process” or “enable consumers to compare the impacts of beef to those of other foods” (Cederberg et al. 2003; Dick et al. 2015). The scope of the LCA is then defined such that the goal can be accomplished. Defining the scope means setting the boundaries of the system to be modeled (e.g., from raw materials extraction to consumption or disposal by the consumer), specifying the level of detail required for the analysis, and choosing a functional unit. The level of detail required is defined by the scale of the unit processes selected. In a beef LCA, high-level unit processes include fertilizer production, crop and pasture farming, cattle raising, and slaughter (Fig. 8.3).

Defining a functional unit is a critical step in LCA. The functional unit is the quantified performance of the product system for use as a reference unit (ISO 2006a). An appropriately defined functional unit allows the impacts of a product to be compared fairly to those of other products with the same function. In a beef LCA, the appropriate functional unit could be kilograms of beef, or a unit that quantifies nutritional content, like calories or protein; in these cases, all environmental impacts in the beef LCA would be reported per kilogram beef, per calorie, or per protein. The reported impacts could then be compared to environmental impacts estimated for other food products, like beans or chicken.

### Inventory analysis

The inventory analysis step, also known as the life cycle inventory (LCI), involves identifying all the relevant inputs (e.g., energy and resources) and outputs (e.g., products created and emissions) of the system of interest. However, we often want to be able to quantify environmental impacts at a finer resolution than for the entire system of interest, so inputs and outputs are identified or estimated for each unit process within the system of interest. For a corn production unit process, inputs might include seeds, water, fertilizers, machinery, and energy. Outputs include the corn product, and might also include nutrient losses from the fertilizers, and emissions from burning fuel to power agricultural equipment. Taking inventory of all the inputs and outputs to every unit process can be a huge endeavor. Luckily for LCA practitioners, much work has already been done to build databases of common unit processes and their associated inputs and outputs. However, LCA practitioners should consider the scope and age of a process dataset, and the location for which it was developed when determining whether it is appropriate to use in any particular LCA. For more on tools and resources for LCA see the **Tools and Data Sources for LCA** section.

### Impact assessment

Also known as life cycle impact assessment (LCIA), the impact assessment step converts the inventory data into meaningful environmental impacts. LCIA is used to improve understanding of the environmental significance of output flows identified in the LCI. Typical environmental impact categories in LCA include global warming, acidification, eutrophication, ozone depletion, smog formation, ecotoxicity, and human health carcinogenics, human health non-carcinogenics, resource depletion, and respiratory effects. The LCIA calculations are performed using impact assessment tools, which utilize databases of characterization factors (CFs). CFs are based on environmental mechanisms and are applied to convert life cycle inventory analysis results to the standard units of each impact category. They can be derived such that they convert to midpoint or endpoint environmental impact categories (Fig. 8.4).

Each chemical or material included in an LCIA database has a separate CF for each environmental impact category in the database. The impact calculation method is shown in Eqs. (8.1) and (8.2), where c denotes a particular emitted compound (CO₂, etc.), and i denotes a particular impact category (global warming, etc.).

```
Impact_{c,i} = mass_c * CF_{c,i} (8.1)
```

```
Total_{impact_i} = Σ_{c} Impact_{c,i} (8.2)
```

As shown in Eq. (8.1), the impact of a particular compound (c) in a particular impact category (i) is the product of the mass of c emitted and the CF corresponding to c and i. The total impact in category i is the sum of all the compound-specific impacts within that category (Eq. (8.2)).

### Interpretation and analysis

In this step, the LCA practitioner summarizes and discusses the results of an LCI or LCIA, and provides recommendations based on these results. This might mean identifying “hotspots” of environmental impact in the product system and comparing results to other research; in an LCA of grassland beef production in Brazil by Dick et al. (2015), authors report that enteric fermentation is responsible for about 80 percent of total GHG emissions. Comparing these results to those of other beef LCAs, they note that

other studies show lower fractions of GHG contributions from enteric fermentation because of higher emissions related to manure management and concentrated feeds in animal diets.

LCA practitioners also often conduct sensitivity and scenario analyses. Sensitivity analyses are conducted to test assumptions and assess uncertainty in results. This is done by varying model inputs over a range of potential values and observing how this changes the model results. For example, if the forage to concentrate diet ratio in a beef cattle LCA is uncertain, environmental impact results should be produced over the range of realistic forage to concentrate ratios. In a scenario analysis, process inputs are adjusted to represent conditions different from those of the original system of interest in order to assess differences in environmental impacts. In the beef LCA example, scenario analysis could be used to predict effects of climate change on the environmental impacts associated with beef production.

## Uses and limitations of LCA

LCA is applied in product development and improvement, strategic planning, public policy making, marketing, and more (ISO 14040, 2006). LCA is generally only relevant for comparative analyses, such as assessing the environmental impacts of different designs for a particular product or multiple products with the same function. LCA is useful for determining relative, not absolute, environmental sustainability—it can help us figure out which decision is better but does not enable us to label things as “sustainable” or “unsustainable.”

## Tools and data sources for LCA

### Process LCA

The most popular comprehensive tools for process LCA are OpenLCA (http://www.openlca.org/), SimaPro (https://simapro.com/), and GaBi (www.gabi-software.com). Of these, OpenLCA is the only open source tool. Other comprehensive LCA tools include umberto (https://www.ifu.com/en/umberto/), and Sustainable Minds (http://www.sustainableminds.com/software).

There are also process LCA tools that are limited to specific types of analyses. For construction, these include BEES, Athena LCA, and One Click LCA. BEES, developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), and Athena, by the Athena Sustainable Materials Institute, are both free software. For energy system LCAs, the National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) hosts a free LCA of Energy Technology and Pathways database, which includes the Grid Mix Explorer, a tool that allows users to browse LCI and LCIA data for regional energy mixes and model customized energy mixes.
