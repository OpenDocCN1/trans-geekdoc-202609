Life cycle assessment (LCA) is a data-intensive approach to quantifying environmental sustainability that enables researchers to compare and innovate processes. Growing areas of data science like machine learning (ML) are already being used to fill data gaps in LCA and even develop surrogate LCA tools that use design-phase product descriptors to predict the environmental impacts of a new design. In this chapter, we introduce LCA methodologies and the tools used by LCA practitioners. Next, we explore different types of machine learning (ML)—supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement learning, shallow and deep learning—and discuss how to determine what types of algorithms are best to solve a specific problem or appropriate for certain datasets. We then look at how ML algorithms have been used and consider how they could be used in LCA for data screening and cleaning, generating inventory data, environmental impact characterization, and sensitivity analyses.

## Introduction to life cycle assessment (LCA)

Life cycle assessment (LCA), also called environmental LCA, is a systematic, standardized approach to quantifying the potential environmental impacts of a product or process that occur from raw materials extraction to end of life. The methodologies for LCA are defined by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14,040 series (ISO 2006a; ISO 2006b).

LCA results can help determine areas of improvement in production processes, aid strategic planning, and inform public policy. LCAs enable the comparison of different products, allowing practitioners to answer questions like, “How do the environmental impacts of beans compare to those of beef?” (Harwatt et al. 2017). LCAs are also used to improve product, process, or system design; an LCA can identify the areas of production with high environmental burden, enabling the design and comparison of alternative production pathways. For example, an LCA can show that replacing a deep straw manure management system with a slurry system reduces ammonia and nitrate emissions, thereby reducing the acidification and eutrophication impacts of beef production (Cederberg et al. 2003).

## LCA's role in sustainability

Together, environmental, social, and economic analyses can help us answer questions about sustainability (Fig. 8.1). Approaches similar to environmental LCA have been used to quantify social and economic impacts (Lauer 2008; UNEP 2009); these approaches are termed social LCA (S-LCA) and techno-economic assessment LCA (TEA-LCA), respectively. Standardized guidance for both S-LCA and TEA-LCA is limited in comparison to that which exists for environmental LCA (Landis et al. 2018).

## Types of LCA

Generally, there are three types of LCA: process LCA, input-output (IO) LCA, and hybrid LCA. International Standards Organization (ISO) 14,040 specifically describes process LCA methods, but IO-LCA models can be useful as screening or model validation tools in LCA, provide supplementary data for a process LCA model or, in some cases, fulfill both the inventory and impact assessment steps in an LCA. Hybrid LCA is the combination of process and IO-LCA methods. Surrogate LCAs, which are described with more detail in the ML in LCA section, are ML algorithms trained on process LCA data to predict LCA results.

## Process LCA

Process LCA, described with more detail in the General Methods for Process LCA section, involves modeling the production of a good or service as the sum of “unit processes,” which are the LCA-practitioner-defined subsystems that make up the system of interest. The guidelines and standards for process LCA practitioners are outlined in the International Standards Organization (ISO) 14,040 and 14,044 (ISO 2006a; ISO 2006b). For each unit process, the LCA practitioner must identify and quantify the necessary resource inputs to the process and any outputs generated, including products, byproducts, and environmental discharges. Although the impacts of services can theoretically be modeled with process LCA methods, process LCA generally does not account for the indirect inputs related to human labor (Junnila 2006).

Process LCA databases provide unit process data for a wide range of activities, from growing of crops to plastics production. There are many free and commercial software packages to support process LCA. This information is presented in more detail in the Tools and Data Sources for LCA section.

## IO-LCA

Input-Output LCA (IO-LCA) tools can provide quick, inexpensive, holistic estimates of resources needed per dollar of economic activity in a sector. Transactions between all sectors included in an IO model are represented in an N by N table or matrix, where N is the number of sectors. As a result, IO-LCA is capable of quantifying all transactions between modeled sectors, even indirect transactions such as those that occur through service industries (Hendrickson et al. 1997). IO transaction data is extended to produce environmental impact estimates using sector-level coefficients corresponding to non-renewable resource use and pollutant emissions. One of the weaknesses of IO-LCA is that analyses are limited to the sectors specified in the Input-Output tables of each tool, and IO sectors are generally aggregated to much larger than product-scale (Castellani et al. 2019).

For more information on IO-LCA, see Chapter 8 of Life Cycle Assessment: Quantitative Approaches for Decisions that Matter (Matthews et al. 2014). Discussion of available IO tables and software for IO-LCA is in the Tools and Data Sources for LCA section.

## Hybrid LCA

A hybrid LCA is generally a Process LCA in which an IO-LCA model provides input-output data or environmental impact data for one or more unit processes, usually because the process or processes are not included in LCA databases.

## Comparison of types

Both process LCA and IO-LCA have strengths and weaknesses. In general, process LCA's strength lies in its flexibility to assess product systems at any desired resolution; unfortunately, full process LCAs can be time consuming and expensive. In contrast, the resolution of IO-LCA is limited to the sectors defined in a corresponding IO table; however, once constructed, an IO table enables generation of LCA results for all included sectors and are able to account for impacts associated with indirect transactions between industries that are often outside the scope of process LCA models. Hybrid LCA models allow the LCA practitioner to take advantage of the strengths of both process and IO-LCA models. For more comparisons of process and IO-LCA methods, see publications from Hendrickson et al. (1997), Junnila (2006), and Castellani et al. (2019).
