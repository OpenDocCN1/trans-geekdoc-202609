
Despite the nonlinearity of input data, which favors the more complex learning algorithms, the study found that the different algorithms performed similarly. Support Vector Machine and Random Forest models’ prediction errors were only about 10–15 percent lower than those of multiple linear regression models for Total EUI, and linear regression models outperformed other models in prediction of Plug Load EUI. Tests of input variable importance also suggested that the CBECS input variables used have low predictive power to map actual energy consumption, at least for the ML techniques applied. This study underlines the importance of having sufficient input data that is well correlated with the value to be predicted; although more complex algorithms may be able to improve model performance where the relationships between input and output variables are nonlinear, they cannot fix problems related to data availability.

## ML in LCIA

ML has been used in LCIA to estimate values of ecotoxicity CFs for uncharacterized chemical compounds. In the future, ML could also help characterize the environmental mechanisms used to define CFs as it improves environmental data collection related to ecology and human health impacts. Data collection supported by ML might even support development of new LCIA categories.

### CF estimation

Slapnik et al. (2015) conducted an LCA of Slovenia to produce region-specific normalization factors for LCA practitioners. Of the 440 chemical flows in their LCI, 139 had no impact characterization factors. Using the Weka 3.6.10 Java package for ML, physical–chemical, toxicological, ecotoxicological, and existing impact characterization data at the intersection of three chemical databases (PPDB, ReCiPe 1.08, and FURS), they were able to train linear regression models for terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecotoxicity, and human toxicity. With limited CF availability in ReCiPe, they were able to set up 75 training instances for the ecotoxicity categories and 41 training for human toxicity. By training 5 models for each impact category that ranged from including the maximum number of available chemical attributes to only those attributes available for the most data limited compounds, the researchers were able to produce validation CF estimates that were well correlated with existing ReCiPe CFs (>0.70 on average). However, the average root relative squared error was pretty high—about 70 percent for model predictions—so the researchers note that results derived from certain estimated CFs should be taken into consideration with care.

### CF definition

While estimating CFs using the properties of chemical compounds may be useful in cases where environmental data are not available for these compounds, a more direct approach to the data gap would be to improve the ways in which we characterize environmental impacts, including impacts on human health and on ecosystems. The studies reviewed in this section demonstrate applications of ML in improving estimation of human health impacts driven by emissions of particulate matter, and in growing ecological databases by automating population studies.

To estimate hospital admissions for respiratory diseases in relationship to particulate matter (PM) concentrations and meteorological variables like humidity, researchers have used generalized linear models (GLMs), which are a type of regression (Pan et al. 2019), ANNs (Tadano et al. 2016), and ANNs with ensembles (Araujo et al. 2020). Results from these studies could contribute to characterization of human health effects for LCIA not only of PM emissions but also of emissions that contribute to climate change.

Dieterich (2009) discusses potential for ML in ecosystem informatics and sustainability, detailing a study using image recognition to identify different species of stoneflies. Standard dictionary image recognition methods were not sufficient to produce reliable, unsupervised classification of stoneflies with an error rate of 16.1 percent. Alternative methods using stacked random forests and boosted dictionaries performed much better, with error rates of 6.4 and 4.9 percent, respectively. Using ML to collect ecosystem data could improve characterization of ecosystem health effects that occur in relation to environmental conditions.

## ML in interpretation and analysis

ML has been used in the interpretation and analysis step of LCA for sensitivity analyses and could be used for scenario analyses to generate inventory data corresponding to new conditions or constraints on the system of interest.

### Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis in LCA requires varying model inputs, especially those with high uncertainty, to understand how the magnitude of each input affects the LCA results. This can be as simple as running the model with a particular input adjusted +/-50 percent, and as specific as a varying model input over a known distribution based on detailed input data. Another approach to sensitivity analysis in LCA used by Heath et al. (2009) is to assess the relationships between LCA inputs and outputs using regressions.

Heath et al. (2009) used simple linear regressions in the sensitivity analysis of an ethanol LCA. The data for each linear regression was generated by running models with a range of input parameter values; the range of input parameters is the feature set, and the corresponding outputs are the labels. As an extension of this application, multivariate regressions could be used to conduct multivariate sensitivity analyses for each environmental impact category. These would enable mathematical representation of the relationships between LCA inputs and outputs.

For example, a regression model of the relationship between the global warming potential (GWP) of ethanol, the efficiency of feedstock conversion, and the rate of ethanol feed coproduct utilization might produce a response surface such as the one shown in (Fig. 8.9). Generating mathematical representations of LCA models would enable characterization of changes in results with changes in multiple input variables at once and might, in some cases, indicate an optimized set of production conditions for minimizing an impact category of interest, like GWP.

### Scenario analysis

As previously discussed in the Estimating Inventory Flows section, supervised learning algorithms can be used to produce models that predict system performances, and these predictions can be useful in scenario analyses where performance data is not available. In the examined building energy consumption study (Deng et al. 2018), a supervised learning algorithm was trained to estimate system performance, in this case building energy consumption. For scenario analysis, system performance variables such as energy consumption or emissions could be predicted for modified input variables such as light bulb types or climate.

Complex optimization scenario analyses in LCA could be aided by both unsupervised and reinforcement learning algorithms. In order to evaluate the potential for near term scale-up of cellulosic fuel production, Mueller-Warrant et al. (2010) determined optimal placements of straw-to-energy conversion sites given the location and quantity data on straw production using a K-means algorithm, which is a type of unsupervised clustering algorithm. Reinforcement learning is often utilized to model scenarios with continuous decision making under specific optimization constraints, so reinforcement algorithms could also be applied to generate LCI data for optimized scenarios. As an example, Wu et al. (2018) use deep Q learning (DQL), a type of reinforcement learning, to model optimal energy management strategies for hybrid electric buses (HEBs).

## Conclusion

LCA relies on inventory and impact characterization data to quantify environmental impacts of products and processes. Inventory analysis requires data on a system's life-cycle inputs and outputs, and impact characterization requires us to understand how chemical emission and other changes in environmental condition affect human health and ecosystems. Where sufficient training data exist, and the more data, the better, ML algorithms can be used to model relationships and predict missing datapoints. Using ML to enhance environmental data collection and optimize systems might also improve availability of inventory and impact characterization data.

In order to enable future applications of ML in LCA, data collection efforts should be focused on system performance (e.g., energy use, emissions, or waste production) and the design-phase drivers of performance measures, and on quantifying human health and the health of ecosystems under changing environmental conditions. In particular, more data characterizing nascent technologies (e.g., space resources) will be needed to drive future LCA studies and any corresponding applications of ML for these systems.

## LCA of ML

Although the focus of this chapter is on ML applications for LCA, it may be of interest to data science and sustainability audiences to note that researchers have also been working to quantify the environmental impacts of training complex ML models, which can demand significant computational resources (Strubell et al. 2019). Strubell et al. (2019) estimated the greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions associated with training, tuning, and experimenting with a natural language processing (NLP) algorithm at about 35.7 tons CO₂ equivalents. This is greater than twice the yearly GHG emissions of an average American, about 16 tons CO₂ equivalents.
