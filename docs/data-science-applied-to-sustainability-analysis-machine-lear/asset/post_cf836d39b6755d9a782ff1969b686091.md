Other considerations might be model transparency and the level of human effort required to produce the model. The functions of models produced using shallow algorithms are completely transparent, but the neural networks that carry out feature learning in ANNs are essentially a “black box,” and there is often no way to mathematically represent the relationship between model inputs and outputs. This may be important to consider for applications like LCA where model transparency is highly valued. On the other hand, human effort required to produce a model using an ANN can be lower than that required to produce a model using shallow learning because feature learning in ANNs eliminates the need for manual expansion of the feature set. If a shallow algorithm seems like the right choice for your dataset, Scikit-learn’s “Choosing the right estimator” decision tree may be a good place to start: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/tutorial/machine_learning_map/.

As would be done with any data source, the effects of using ML methods on model reliability should be considered. Model statistics can provide a starting point for propagation of uncertainties into model results, but conservative modeling choices should be made when using ML so that overfitting, which may produce artificially low uncertainties in model validation and testing phases, is avoided. Useful interpretation of LCA relies on model transparency, so extra care should be taken to explain which ML techniques were used and why.

## Tools for ML

With an ever-expanding set of ML tools, it is not possible to provide a comprehensive list. Some of the most common freely available and open source tools for building ML models include python libraries such as PyTorch, Scikit-learn, and Theano; Google’s TensorFlow; and Microsoft’s Cognitive Toolkit (CNTK), Weka, and KNIME. For those who are just getting started using ML, some good resources are Andrew Ng’s Machine Learning course on Coursera, and the OCDevel Machine Learning Guide podcast.

## ML in LCA

Potential applications of ML in LCA are numerous (Fig. 8.8), but which algorithms are useful will depend on the goal, the type of data, and size of the dataset. In this review, we found that ML has been applied for LCA in three areas: surrogate LCA development using ANN algorithms (Park et al. 2003; Sousa et al. 2006); CF estimation with multivariate linear regression (Slapnik et al. 2015); and sensitivity analysis with linear regressions (Heath et al. 2009). Additionally, ML has been applied outside LCA in data cleaning, predicting system output flows or performance, ecosystem informatics, and system optimization. From these methods, ML algorithms could have new applications screening or cleaning datasets for LCI, estimating flow data for unit processes, improving the quality and quantity of data used to determine CFs, and generating optimized scenarios.

Model transparency will also be important for acceptance of ML methods within LCA methodology.

## ML for surrogate LCAs

Within this space, ML algorithms have been used by LCA practitioners to develop surrogate LCA models that use product design parameters to estimate LCA results for new products. The term “surrogate LCA” seems to be used exclusively to describe ML algorithms trained on process LCA data to predict LCA results. One of the main goals of the surrogate LCA is to enable product designers to predict the environmental impacts of a new product system or process. Several studies have used ML to develop surrogate LCA models using product descriptors and existing LCI or LCA results (Sousa et al. 2000; Park et al. 2003; Sousa et al. 2006).

Sousa et al. (2000) tested the ability of an ANN trained to predict life cycle energy consumption from a set of 18 carefully defined project concept descriptors. The project concept descriptors were chosen based not only on strength of correlation with LCI data, but also on survey results indicating what designers are likely to know about a product in the early phases of development. Noting that correlations between descriptors and LCI data improved when examined for narrower classes of products (e.g., durable products only), they trained two ANN models: one for products with high use-phase energy consumption (HUPEC), and another for products with low use-phase energy consumption (LUPEC). The recognition that different classes of products have distinct relationships between product descriptors and LCI data has prompted further research to define appropriate groupings for products (Park et al. 2003; Sousa et al. 2006).

The HUPEC and LUPEC models were trained on the product descriptors and life cycle energy consumption data from full LCAs of 103 and 55 products, respectively. These included vehicles, plastics, paper products, and more. The models were tested using six products with known LCA results that were not in the training dataset: an internal combustion vehicle, refrigerator, vacuum, chair, coffee filter, and antifreeze. The predicted life cycle energy consumptions of products in the HUPEC group were all within +/-40 percent of the life cycle energy consumption estimated in the full LCAs for these products. In the context of LCA, where differences of this magnitude are common between results for the same product, a maximum error of 40 percent is encouraging. Differences between predicted and full LCA results were larger for products in the LUPEC group, but still close enough that the ranking of all six products from greatest to least energy consumption is the same using the model predictions as it is with the full LCA results. These findings led to development of the Sustainable Minds tool, which uses design-phase product descriptor inputs to produce comprehensive LCA results (Sousa 2008).

## ML in LCI

The LCI step focuses on collecting data for the model. ML has not yet been applied to LCI, but applications of ML in cleaning sensor data and predicting the performance of buildings could inform future ML in LCI for data cleaning and estimating inventory flows.

### Data cleaning

Automated data collection is susceptible to errors that can be time consuming to find and correct. For LCA practitioners using automated data collection for their LCI or to inform impact CFs, deep belief networks (DBN) might be useful. Dietterich (2009) used a Gaussian DBN to clean temperature sensor network data. The Gaussian DBN acted as a filter, labeling incoming data from wireless sensors as “valid” or “faulty” using probabilistic inference based on learned correlations between sensors in the network and replaced “faulty” values with a predicted most likely value.

### Estimating inventory flows

ML has been used to predict performance of systems from system properties. Performance data, as it relates to material and energy demands or production of waste and emissions, is useful in the LCI step. Studies using ML to predict building energy use are common (Zhao et al. 2012; Robinson et al. 2017; Deng et al. 2018), and their results demonstrate that models developed using ML could be used with current, publicly available data to estimate the performance of new buildings in the design phase or the performance of existing buildings scenarios with modified input variables. It is likely that ML has been used to predict performance data for other systems as well.

Deng et al. (2018) used a variety of ML algorithms to build models to estimate energy use intensity (EUI) for US commercial office buildings. The models were calibrated with a subset of 2012 Commercial Building Energy Consumption Survey (CBECS) microdata, comprised of 19 continuous variables such as square footage, occupancy percentage, and daylight percentage, and 56 categorical variables such as window glass type, building owner type, and whether there are occupancy sensors. The algorithms used were linear regression, lasso regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), ANN, gradient boosting, and random forest.
