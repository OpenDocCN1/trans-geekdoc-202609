
Based on the results of these interviews, various organizational requirements that ensure the successful adoption of business intelligence can be categorized into two main groups:

- Requirements associated with establishing an operational business intelligence system, which focuses on providing data from internal activities.
- Requirements associated with management and control, aiming to provide comprehensive coordination between the parts of the organizational structure and achieve organizational effectiveness.

## Organizational Requirements Associated With Establishing an Operational Business Intelligence System

When adopting a business intelligence system, it will necessarily include all operational activities and processes and all managerial levels of the organization, including suppliers, customers, and public data for citizens outside the organizational structure but in continuous relation with it. (Howson, Successful Business Intelligence: unlock the value of BI and big data, 2014, p. 4) In line with this approach, the operational system of the organization consists of a set of internal activities and processes that transform available resources into products and services directed to the market. At each step of these processes, users enter a set of data into the operating systems, which are then captured and used in the business intelligence system. Operational systems, also known as transaction processing systems or source systems and also referred to as Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems, are the main source of quantitative data related to the organization. Various activities are practiced, involving the input of a considerable amount of data into these systems, covering many areas, such as:

- Manufacturing system: Entering a specific product production order into the manufacturing system, recording the quantity and quality of raw materials used, etc.
- Sales system: Entering customer purchase orders, along with all details.
- Supply Chain System: Executing orders once the product is available and entering shipping details.
- Accounting system: Recording all cash and financial transactions, such as customer invoices, etc. (Howson, Successful Business Intelligence: Secrets to making BI a killer app, 2008, p. 22)

These operational systems form the primary and confirmed source of quantitative data related to the enterprise. At each step of the operational process, a considerable volume of data is inputted, covering various areas.

As shown in Figure (4), Operational Business Intelligence (OBI) relies on real operational systems (where production activities are performed) to obtain data. This data is derived from the following subsystems: Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP), Supply Chain Management (SCM), Customer Relationship Management (CRM), and Accounting systems. Additionally, it incorporates data from other internal sources such as distributors providing information on sales and inventory, clickstream data from websites displaying the most viewed products, and data on e-commerce shopping carts. Subsequently, this data is extracted, transformed, and loaded (ETL) into the data warehouse. (Howson, Successful Business Intelligence: Secrets to making BI a killer app, 2008, p. 24) The team responsible for the Business Intelligence system considers its integration with the operational system as the link that ensures the continuous creation of data related to internal activities of the organization. This contributes to the horizontal and vertical flow of data and information across different parts of the organizational structure.

Therefore, due to the simplicity of the organizational structure and the operational systems of small and medium-sized industrial enterprises, increasing operational flexibility becomes an achievable advantage, provided that there is a greater reliance on modern technological platforms. These platforms are used to digitize their operational infrastructure and processes. (Rao-Graham, McNaughton, & Mansingh, 2020, p. 3) This simplification leads to the achievement of the following:

- Providing real-time operational-level data for the enterprise, contributing to instant feedback for all practiced activities and reducing the time and errors associated with work performance.
- Eliminating non-value-added operational activities, thus reducing associated costs.
- Completely eliminating the possibility of distorting important information transferred from one department to another, linked to production characteristics. This provides significant opportunities to improve product quality and potentially reduce costs.
- Making the enterprise more responsive to market requirements.

## The set of organizational requirements associated with management, control, and coordination

As an inevitable result of the radical changes that have affected the working environment of organizations, mentioned at the beginning of this chapter, organizational structures have shifted from hierarchical functional structures, which rely on precise work division, detailed specialization, and vertical organization as fundamental principles, to horizontal structures. These structures are centered around processes as their primary mode of operation. One of the main reasons for this transformation is an attempt to create greater value for consumers. Initially, organizations tried to improve the quality of their products and achieve faster responsiveness to market demands. They built an administrative and organizational model for their activities, illustrated in Figure (5).

In reality, organizations adopted this structure in the form of a cross-functional process due to the problems and drawbacks experienced by functional organizational structures. These drawbacks included slow responsiveness to market requirements, low levels of product and service quality, and cost inflation. The organization’s management attempted to improve internal coordination between multiple departmental and functional activities that contribute to the same process. For example, Figure (5) illustrates the innovation and product development process. Initially, individuals responsible for consumer and market studies identify consumer preferences, functional features performed by the developed product, and demand levels. This information is then passed on in a detailed report using the information system to another functional department, the Research and Development department. This department is responsible for arriving at desired product designs. The designs are then passed on to the production department, where those in charge design suitable production lines and initiate manufacturing processes. Afterward, sales and distribution operations, as well as general promotional campaigns, commence, followed by after-sales services. What actually occurred between these departments was the transfer of important data and information (in paper or electronically) crucial for decision-making regarding each step of the process as a whole. However, observations revealed limitations, delays, errors, and distortions in the transfer of this data and information, resulting from the incorrect transfer method. This often had a negative impact on production costs and quality.

During the 1990s, precisely in 1993, James Champy and Michael Hammer attempted to introduce the concept of Reengineering. They defined it as follows: Radical rethinking and radical redesign of the organizational structure of the enterprise and its operational processes to achieve significant improvements in performance and increase efficiency by reducing production costs, improving quality, satisfying consumers more, reducing response time to their desires, and enjoying greater flexibility towards external changes, as well as raising the level of coordination and integration for its activities and practices. (Hammer & Champy, 1993, pp. 41-45)

The main goal behind reengineering is to transform the organizational structure of the enterprise from a functional to a process-centered organization, based on the belief that the latter is the most evolved form of organization and is more in line with external environmental conditions. This involves a radical departure from functions and departments, replacing them with a set of horizontal business processes formed by individuals with multiple functional specialties, namely:

- Product Design and Workshop System Design Process: This process includes all activities related to the development of new products. Due to the significant link between product design and the design of the workshop system, all these activities fall under one process, which is the process of designing new products and the workshop system.
- Enterprise Resource Management Process: This encompasses all activities related to obtaining capital resources, human resources, and managing the technology within the organization.
- Product Supply Process: This process includes a set of activities related to obtaining raw materials and various supplies used as inputs for the production system, culminating in the delivery of products and services to customers.
