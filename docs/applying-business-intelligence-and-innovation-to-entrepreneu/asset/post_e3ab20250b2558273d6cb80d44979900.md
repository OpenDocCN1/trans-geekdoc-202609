
## MUSLIM HEALTH TOURISM

Competition often makes Muslim businesses overspend and overreact to innovate new products or services to attract customers. Sometimes, pursuing profits without thinking about the hereafter can be overboard, making them lose their true value because they are similar or no different from conventional businesses. At the same time, a competitive advantage sets a company apart from its competitors in the eyes of its consumers.

People often mistake innovation for something so mind-blowing that it will change the way business is run or to make cutting-edge and sophisticated products. However, this process can be something simple. Please do not rule out something that seems too easy or miss an opportunity right before us while we search for the next big thing. After all, innovation is ultimately about giving your customers what they need.

Islam does not disregard innovation. Some terms refer to innovation in Islam. Islah, tajdid and ijtihad are good innovations, while bidaah is an innovation in religion that can be good or bad. In line with that, before the Muslim health tourism business can return to accomplishment and compliance in its fundamental framework, it can return outside the market competition arena. It requires a back-to-basics approach to innovation, where Muslim health tourism businesses return to focusing on perfecting the fundamental areas of their frameworks, i.e. Sharia as Islamic law.

As an example of the application of innovation by returning to Sharia as a basis, there is the current development of Muslim health applications and devices, both medical and wellness, so there is a need for medical devices to be Halal certified, as in research by Sarisae et al. (2023).

When Islamic values are applied to tourism, there should not be any elements of disobedience to Allah ﷻ (The most glorified, the most-highest), even at the level of ideas and concepts up to the implementation. However, in Muslim-majority countries where religion focuses more on life, spirituality is not seen as an “optional extra” (Smith & Puchkó, 2013). The term “Muslim” refers to people who follow Islam. Etymologically, Muslim refers to a form of subject or doer of the deeds of Islam, and its definition depends on the understanding of Islam itself. Islam is derived from the word “Al-Slim” from the Arabic word which means peaceful, happy, obedient, and surrender to Allah ﷻ (Pal, 2011).

Unfortunately, the growing health tourism is seen following secularism’s building block and negatively impacts health tourism sustainability. If Muslims continue being involved in it, it will become a drawback. This is because, from the beginning until now, Islam does not separate religion and world affairs. These two are interrelated in Islam. Islam does not accept secularism, which separates religion from worldly affairs. Muslim health tourism emerges as a positive alternative to the interpretation of hedonistic and secular tourism. Islam regulates human life in various aspects, including health and medicine.

Preventing disease by maintaining health (preventive) and healing (curative) is the command of Allah ﷻ in the Holy Quran. Moreover, Allah ﷻ, through the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ (may the peace and blessing of Allah ﷻ be upon him), teaches men how to maintain health and treat disease through the Sunnah (the Way of the Prophet). There is a quote that refers to the Hadith At-Tirmidzi said that Had not the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ himself said, ‘O Servant of God, use the medicine because God hath not created a pain without a remedy for it.” Every Muslim is obliged to travel to find a cure for the disease or maintain health while hoping for the pleasure of Allah ﷻ to provide healing. Offering a different approach derived from the Holy Quran and Hadith, Muslim health tourism poses a proximity between religion, tourism, and health (Bafadhal, 2021). This means that although the travelling purpose of medical travellers is for health treatment, their primary concerns are similar to other Muslim tourists in the spiritual-related journey in which ritualised practice is incredibly significant (Teerakunpisut & Kongpiam, 2023).

Bafadhal (2022) defines Muslim health tourism as “a worship through sincere trips by Muslims to leave their origin place towards a certain health care provider as an effort to prevent or cure a disease, in order to find improvement in the quality of physical, psychological, psychosocial, and spiritual aspects according to the Quran and Hadith.” There are several combinations in this term, for example, “halal healthcare tourism” and “Islamic health tourism.”

Muslim health tourism consists of two principal subsets, namely Muslim wellness tourism and Muslim medical tourism, which have different subjects, purposes, and objects. Regarding tourists who travel, Muslim wellness tourism tends to be done by people who are in good condition or healthy. However, they want to prevent disease and improve their health (preventive) (Bafadhal, 2017). Muslim wellness tourism is “a worship in a sincere trip by Muslims, leaving their origin place to certain health care providers to prevent diseases (preventive) as to get the optimised well-being and resilience with the blessing of Allah ﷻ” (Bafadhal, 2017). This term is often used mutually with other terms, such as “Halal wellness and spa.”

The trend of anti-Muslim called “Islamophobia,” is so alarming, leading to an irrational fear of Muslims, hatred of Islam, and hostile actions toward individuals and Muslim communities in general; this has invited the concern of many researchers (Al Hamarneh & Steiner, 2004). The growing sentiment has brought what is called communitarianism, where the Muslim communities prefer to gather with their fellow Muslims to confront negative stereotypes (Stephenson & Ali, 2010). The Muslim communities are growing in numbers, so their consumption needs have also become attractive for businesses to create tourism products according to the requirements and expectations of the Muslim market (Prayag & Hosany, 2014). Moreover, Muslims have been recognised as a growing market and have now officially received labels as “consumers” (Kesseler, 2015), which, in this case, Muslims have a special place as Muslim tourists.

Then, the large number of Muslims in the world may be assumed that every Muslim has the potential to be facilitated as consumer needs. Although Muslim health tourism facilities, products, and services are almost the same as conventional ones, adjustment is still necessary for understanding markets’ demand, product development, segmentation, and marketing (Bafadhal, 2022). Therefore, health tourism must focus on sub-sectors or niches because health tourists do not seek the same service (Smith & Puczkó, 2014).

In addition, consumers of the halal industry are universal with shared values. Consumers of the halal industry are mainly Muslims but also include non-Muslims of the same values. However, business in Islam is essentially universal and can be enjoyed by Muslim and non-Muslim consumers. This is because Islam is raḥmatan lil alamin (a mercy to all creation), a religion that respects human beings. Muslim health tourism specialises in delivering healthcare products and services to Muslim customers. However, it is undeniable that Muslim health tourism also attracts non-Muslim tourists who want to enrich their health, knowledge, and travel experiences. This then leads to the term “borderless patient” for health tourists. Muslim health tourism is, therefore, not just an issue of ethics and equity at a personal level. More inclusive health marketing towards the Muslim community is a pivotal part of global environmental and public health (Bafadhal, 2022). Making the demand for halal products become a religious obligation and a powerful market force. It has been established that boosting Muslim travelers’ mental health and satisfaction is vital for minimizing the impact of inconvenient tourism experiences (Han et al., 2021).

Countries of non-Muslim majority are expected to maintain tolerance and to keep the faith of Muslim tourists visiting those countries by providing Muslim-friendly facilities, services, and accommodations. The availability of Islamic attributes will not cause inconvenience to non-Muslims to any extent. Managers of tourist destinations can provide services that meet the needs of Muslims by allocating unique places and facilities for Muslims. Thus, Islamic branding within a destination or tourist product would not become merely a name to attract the Muslim market (Battour & Ismail, 2015). Hence, both Muslim and non-Muslim tourists may decide not to travel to a healthcare provider if there are strict rules, specific attributes, and restrictions. Tourism operators face a more significant challenge, particularly those promoting halal tourism destinations (Rahman et al., 2020).
