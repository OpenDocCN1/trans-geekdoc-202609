Celina Olszak suggests that analyzing and discussing the concept of business intelligence should be done from two fundamental perspectives: the technical perspective and the managerial perspective. The technical view, being the older of the two, considers business intelligence as an integrated set of tools, technologies, and software used to collect heterogeneous data from various sources, integrating, analyzing, and sharing it. The managerial view focuses on the crucial importance of information and knowledge as strategic resources for enterprise management. Data analysis not only allows for faster decision-making but also contributes to discovering valuable opportunities for future business success and sustaining profit achievement. (Olszak, 2020, pp. 38, 39) Sharda, Delen, and Turban argue that before adopting and designing a business intelligence system for the enterprise, top management should consider it as a tool that will fundamentally change the entire management approach. The reasons for investing in business intelligence should align with the enterprise’s strategy. It should be considered a means to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of operations and activities. Planning for adoption is essential, involving the identification of organizational skills available to achieve business intelligence goals and a pre-assessment of the prevailing organizational culture’s willingness to undergo appropriate changes. Integration between the business intelligence system and the enterprise’s adopted information technology systems is also essential. (Sharda, Delen, & Turban, 2018, p. 20)

In fact, most executive managers link their decisions to allocate a budget for investing in business intelligence to the economic return expected over a specific time period following the adoption process. This economic return is strongly related to business processes that will improve their performance, known implicitly as Business Intelligence Opportunities (BIOS). BIOS are systematic approaches to identify optimal ways to use business intelligence to increase revenue, reduce costs, or both. These economic justifications depend on the business processes practiced and provide the economic basis for how a business intelligence system can create value for the enterprise. For example, the total investment amount, the time required to reach the break-even point, the nature and amount of risk associated with this investment, and the most critical factor, the factors and requirements for success and the extent to which the enterprise’s management can fulfill them. (Williams, 2016, pp. 69, 70)
