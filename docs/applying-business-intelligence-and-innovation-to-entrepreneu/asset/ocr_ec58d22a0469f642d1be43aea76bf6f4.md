# INTRODUCTION

The rapid development of health tourism has been seen to have created a “health and prevention boom era” predicted to be a “new economic power” (Sigrist, 2006). Health tourism has become one of the fastest growing industries in the world, despite the desire to improve the quality of life, health, as well as healing and relaxation, which has been the primary motivation of tourists since long ago (Kim et al., 2015). Health tourism combines two things, namely health and tourism, and the involvement of these two things makes health tourism a promising tourism niche. On the other hand, Muslim medical tourism is generally done by people who are sick and need treatment to regain their health (curative) (Smith & Puczkó, 2014). Medical care is available in health tourism, such as surgery or other specialist interventions, while wellness tourism provides pilgrimages, meditation, spas, and others (Smith & Puczkó, 2017).

Increased public awareness of the health aspects is responsibly responded to by the increasingly diverse commoditisation of medicines, medicals, and treatments worldwide, forming a “false trap” for those who worry excessively about their health. The negative impact of this is the emergence of massive exploration of businesspersons over the naturally unlimited human desire for health. This desire must be encouraged to change into a necessity, not essential, artificial, and even pragmatic.

Take an example of the high obsession with cosmetic surgery. The merely profit-oriented advertisements put by beauty clinics and surgical hospitals have made patients from the country and abroad take long trips to reconstruct their faces and bodies in the clinics. This has turned into the psychosocial disease of society where people become preoccupied with what they perceive as defects in their body or face or even when the physical anomalies are only slightly; the fears become real and exaggerated and encourage a solid need to perform cosmetic surgery constantly with the never-ending search for satisfaction. This psychological disorder is called Body Dysmorphic Disorder, and even cosmetic surgery has become a social and cultural phenomenon (Kim, 2009; Heyes, 2009).

Then, it is pretty often that patients also end up with various controversial, circumvention, and illegal treatments that disregard morality and even religious beliefs. A variety of other treatment methods evidences this as a trend in the world, like “vampire facelift,” “sex change tourism,” “sex tourism,” “suicide tourism,” “nude tourism,” “abortion tourism,” “vasectomy and tubectomy,” “transplant tourism,” “fertility tourism,” “surrogacy tourism,” and so forth which use human organs and blood and hazardous chemicals for aesthetic and medical businesses.
