## Historical Evolution of Business Intelligence

The term Business Intelligence was coined by the Gartner Group in the mid-1990s, but its historical roots trace back to Management Information Systems (MIS) in the 1970s and Executive Information Systems (EIS) in the 1980s. These systems improved computerized management support, with MIS offering static two-dimensional reports, and EIS introducing dynamic multidimensional reports and trend analysis. In the 1990s, BI emerged, providing executives with all the information they needed (Sharda, Delen, & Turban, 2018, p. 16). Celina Olszak identifies three generations of BI systems: BI 1.0, BI 2.0, and BI 3.0. BI 1.0, which emerged in the 1970s and 1980s, and was closely tied to MIS, EIS, and Decision Support Systems (DSS). BI 2.0, which emerged from 1990 to 2005, had witnessed a continuous development in advanced data warehouses, OLAP techniques, and data extraction methods. It enabled enterprises to highlight their work online, aligning with market requirements. The latest generation, BI 3.0, introduced proactive solutions available to a large number of individuals within the enterprise, which was integrated with enterprise operations and offering cloud-based BI services (Olszak, 2020, pp. 42, 43).

The adoption of decision-making computing in enterprises began in the 1970s, experiencing five key stages to the present day.

As shown in figure (1), we can say that Business Intelligence (BI) systems have emerged and evolved through five chronological stages since the early seventies of the twentieth century, with the emergence of Decision Support Systems. The seventies were characterized by relying on Management Information Systems (MIS) in decision-making. This involved preparing reports concerning various operational, organizational, and strategic variables over past time periods. The executive management relied on these reports for making future decisions. Additionally, Operations Research and Expert Systems techniques were used to solve complex problems faced by enterprise management. The second stage extended through the eighties and witnessed significant changes in how enterprise management obtained data related to its activities and fields. Traditional systems used to capture data from various units, such as marketing, accounting, and manufacturing, were integrated into a unified system at the enterprise level, known as Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP). During this stage, Relational Database Management Systems (RDBMS) were also introduced to improve data capture, storage, and computerized access. In the nineties, due to the need for more diverse reports for top management, Executive Information Systems (EISs) and Decision Support Systems (DSSs) were introduced. These systems were specifically designed for executive managers to meet their decision-making needs. They included visual information dashboards with performance metrics and essential indicators, relying on data warehouses to facilitate and expedite decision-making (Sharda, Delen, & Turban, 2018, pp. 13, 14, 15).

## Definition Of Business Intelligence in Industrial Small and Medium Sized Enterprises

In our search for the conceptual roots of the term Business Intelligence, we found the first definition of H.P Luhn in 1958 where he defined it as the ability to understand the interrelationships among presented facts and how they lead to directing work towards desired goals. In the business field, Business Intelligence is an automatic system created and developed to disseminate information across various departments and parts of the organizational structure (Grossmann & Rinderle-ma, 2015, p. 1). Laura Zapata-Cantú, Teresa Treviño, and Flor Morton see Business Intelligence as referring to a wide range of methodologies, processes, infrastructures, architectures, and technologies that work together to provide solutions for managers to make sound and quick decisions. Eventually, it relies on the real-time analysis and aggregation of data, transforming it into useful information (Zapata-Cantu, Trevino, & Morton, 2020, p. 15). It is also known as a comprehensive concept for data structuring, analysis tools and software which is embedded in a specific way to enable enterprise managers for instantaneous reach of data and information simultaneously. Therefore, decision-maker can have crystal clear visions for a sound administrative decision (Sharda, Delen, & Turban, 2018, p. 16). Business Intelligence comprises different activities, analysis means, works, and the necessary techniques to transform data into useful information, which in turn becomes knowledge. Thus, this knowledge is used in the enterprise to find plans which lead eventually to the adoption of operational and managerial procedures for economic profits. (Loshin, 2013, p. 7)

Accordingly, Business Intelligence is a large structure of computerized media devices, means, technologies, computer software, and infrastructures. These elements are integrated according to a specific configuration, aligning with the activities and operations of the organization and its managerial levels and adopted organizational structure. The main purpose is to obtain vast data, in various forms and from different sources, both internal and external to the enterprise environment, associated with its field of activity. This data is then organized, analyzed, processed, and transformed into useful information in the management process and the making of correct decisions, thus creating knowledge. The following sequential stages are followed:

- Data collection and integration from various available sources, relying on ETL, data warehouses, and databases.
- Data analysis, organization, information production, and the preparation of various reports related to the enterprise's activities and operations. This relies on OLAP, query languages, and ad hoc analyses.
- Knowledge acquisition through data exploration, utilizing various data mining techniques.
- Making the right decisions based on the previous stages, ultimately achieving organizational success. (Olszak, 2020, p. 41)

As it is known, the large enterprise differs significantly from the small and medium-sized enterprise in terms of its organizational structure, financial and human resources, expertise levels, and production and technological capabilities. Consequently, the factors influencing the definition and adoption of Business Intelligence (BI) systems in SMEs differ radically from those in large enterprises. Therefore, it is crucial to establish a specific conceptual framework for BI, accurately encompassing the key factors driving the definition and adoption of BI in SMEs.

In Algeria, an industrial SME is defined as an economic entity with a workforce ranging from 01 to 250 employees and an annual turnover not exceeding four (04) billion Algerian dinars or a total annual turnover of one (01) billion dinars, meeting the conditions of independence, and engaging in productive activities by transforming raw materials and supplies into semi-finished or finished products. (People’s Democratic Republic of Algeria, 2017, p. 5)

According to this definition, the characteristics of industrial SMEs in Algeria do not differ globally due to the convergence of classification criteria, including the number of employees, annual turnover, and conditions of independence. Unlike large enterprises, which largely rely on extensive capital, technological resources, and human capital, SMEs are quite the opposite. Their owners, managers, and employees typically possess low levels of awareness and training in effectively utilizing information and communication technology (ICT), which is a fundamental pillar in defining and adopting BI systems and activating their role in value creation and overall performance improvement. Additionally, their ability to finance IT and BI projects is limited due to financial constraints.

Another key factor in defining BI systems suitable for industrial SMEs is the low levels of organizational and human knowledge, which determine their ability to collect, integrate, analyze, and organize necessary data and information. Furthermore, their capacity to design and manage operational processes horizontally and correctly integrate them with BI systems to effectively improve value creation is crucial.
