Little, a member of the Shanghai branch, wrote a manual titled China's Income and the New Treaty: Protests against Tariff Increasing, from a Merchant's Perspective to promote Public opinion against the plan (Little 1902). The editorial department of the North China Herald printed and published this manual, which not only affected the attitudes of many members at the Shanghai branch but also sparked widespread discussion among the British public. Many opponents who were directly involved in China's trade used media such as The North China Daily News to raise a large number of persuasive objections. After the first general meeting of the Shanghai branch, due to excessive abstention votes, to gain the support of those who remained silent in the meeting, Edward S. Little published a speech in the North China Daily News to the Business Counselor of the British Consulate General in China, accuses the British government of inadequate regulation of the clause in Tianjin Treaty ( 天津条约) which said transit pass charge of 2.5% for goods sent into the interior.

He questioned the feasibility of the plan of canceling likin and raising the tariff (Little 1902 March 5). During this process, the British government and supporters of the plan also used public opinion to retaliate. J. O. P. Bland, a correspondent for The Times, published an article refuting Little's interpretation of the Tianjin Treaty (天津条约) and calling on British businessmen in China to support the plan in exchange for a true open door (Bland 1902). On the other hand, the branch used various means to guide public opinion. The Shanghai Mercury launched an attack on the identity of Bland, who had been a customs employee for many years, implying that the tariff increase was due to Sir Robert Hart, the chief tariff officer, who was willing to harm the interests of the British for collecting wealth for the Chinese (The National Archives 1902). Mackay closely followed the debate and sent all the newspaper clippings to the Marquess of Lansdowne, regretfully stating that except for Bland's article, most of the other comments were against the plan (Mackay 1902b). Because almost all English newspapers in China represented the interests of British businessmen, the British government had almost failed in the public opinion arena. It had to make concessions on the amount of tariff increases that the Shanghai branch could accept.

In terms of diplomacy, the Shanghai branch requested the British government to force the Chinese government to expand the scope of the abolition of likin barriers to smooth the mainland Chinese market. The abolishing likin barriers plan originally proposed by Mackay did not include salt barriers, local opium barriers, etc. The Chinese government had emphasized the differences between the above and likin barriers, as well as the importance of investigating illegal activities and collecting taxes on the mainland. The Shanghai branch insisted these barriers were as same as likin barriers which must be included in the scope of abolition.
