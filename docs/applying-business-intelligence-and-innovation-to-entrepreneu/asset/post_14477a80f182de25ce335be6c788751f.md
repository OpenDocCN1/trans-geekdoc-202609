This chapter will attempt to study the nature of Business Intelligence (BI) systems that are compatible with small and medium-sized industrial enterprises, considering the distinctive characteristics of this type of institution compared to large enterprises. It will identify and analyze the various organizational, technical, and human requirements necessary for the successful adoption of BI systems by these enterprises, making it an effective tool for creating value, improving performance, and making the right decisions.

## CHAPTER BACKGROUND

Field experiments have shown that small and medium-sized industrial enterprises tend to use various technologies encompassed within BI, seeking to acquire a level of flexibility towards market requirements and to respond to continuous changes in their external business environment. The use of these technologies enables them to have organizational capabilities that lead to a constant sensing of available opportunities and threats. Consequently, they have begun to support the idea of acquiring and exploiting data and information assets (internal and external) as factors that effectively contribute to creating and strengthening competitive advantages in a sustainable manner. (Rao-Graham, McNaughton, & Mansingh, 2020, p. XIII) As is known, small and medium-sized industrial enterprises possess a nature characterized by agility and mobility, primarily resulting from the simplicity of their organizational structure due to their small size. This makes it easier for them to adopt process-centered organization, which is more adaptable to external environmental conditions over time. Additionally, small and medium-sized industrial enterprises mostly adopt one of three types of manufacturing systems: the job shop, the flow shop, and the Cellular shop. These manufacturing systems endow them with many characteristics associated with the strategic triangle: low cost, high quality, and timeliness. For example, the job shop, due to its technical characteristics and structural composition, enables small and medium-sized industrial enterprises to easily and quickly modify and change the type, number, and characteristics of their products, thus giving them an extremely organizational agility and the ability to adjust their competitive positioning in a short period of time. These are features entirely lacking in large enterprises.

The nature of BI systems in small and medium-sized industrial enterprises differs significantly from that in large enterprises. Rao, Mansingh, and McNaughton argue that in the recent past, most researchers and specialists had a misconception that BI was only suitable for large resource-intensive enterprises. However, this has completely changed at present, as small and medium-sized industrial enterprises possess inherent organizational agility, which can only be enhanced by successfully adopting BI. (Rao-Graham, McNaughton, & Mansingh, 2020, p. XIII) This constitutes a considerable research gap, necessitating deeper theoretical and field research into the requirements for the successful adoption of BI by small and medium-sized industrial enterprises, taking into account their distinctive characteristics. That is, the need for methodical specification and detailing of the requirements for the successful adoption of BI, primarily associated with the requirements for establishing an operational BI system, the technical integration requirements of the BI system with operational processes, in addition to the level of maturity of information and communication technology (ICT) practices and the technical requirements for establishing a BI system. Also, the human requirements related to the training and qualification of individuals responsible for establishing and operating BI.

## BASIC CONCEPTS OF BUSINESS INTELLIGENCE IN INDUSTRIAL SMALL AND MEDIUM SIZED ENTERPRISES

The current business environment is characterized by its complexity and instability. To achieve a quick response to consumer demands, economic institutions face the inevitability of using information technology and various decision support systems. This necessitates acquiring technological communication tools and methods to gather a variety of data from different sources, analyze it, and transform it into new and useful knowledge for decision-making processes. Business Intelligence (BI) systems play a crucial role in this regard, serving as a set of practices, methods, tools, and applications that assist enterprise management in collecting, integrating, analyzing, and presenting data in an understandable and usable format, enhancing overall performance and decision-making (Olszak, 2020, p. 35). Therefore, BI includes practices and tools such as data warehousing, data mining, and reporting and querying tools contribute to informed decision-making (Deepmala, Anurag, Amizan, & S.B, 2023, pp. 32, 33). Therefore, Lila Rao-Graham, Maurice McNaughton, and Gunjan Mansingh argue that a business intelligence system provides small and medium-sized industrial enterprises with high organizational flexibility, in addition to their inherent organizational flexibility, derived from their small size. These enterprises completely lack the formal and bureaucratic organizational structures that largely constrain large enterprises. (Rao-Graham, McNaughton, & Mansingh, 2020, p. 4)
