Celina Olszak suggests that analyzing and discussing the concept of business intelligence should be done from two fundamental perspectives: the technical perspective and the managerial perspective. The technical view, being the older of the two, considers business intelligence as an integrated set of tools, technologies, and software used to collect heterogeneous data from various sources, integrating, analyzing, and sharing it. The managerial view focuses on the crucial importance of information and knowledge as strategic resources for enterprise management. Data analysis not only allows for faster decision-making but also contributes to discovering valuable opportunities for future business success and sustaining profit achievement. (Olszak, 2020, pp. 38, 39) Sharda, Delen, and Turban argue that before adopting and designing a business intelligence system for the enterprise, top management should consider it as a tool that will fundamentally change the entire management approach. The reasons for investing in business intelligence should align with the enterprise’s strategy. It should be considered a means to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of operations and activities. Planning for adoption is essential, involving the identification of organizational skills available to achieve business intelligence goals and a pre-assessment of the prevailing organizational culture’s willingness to undergo appropriate changes. Integration between the business intelligence system and the enterprise’s adopted information technology systems is also essential. (Sharda, Delen, & Turban, 2018, p. 20)

In fact, most executive managers link their decisions to allocate a budget for investing in business intelligence to the economic return expected over a specific time period following the adoption process. This economic return is strongly related to business processes that will improve their performance, known implicitly as Business Intelligence Opportunities (BIOS). BIOS are systematic approaches to identify optimal ways to use business intelligence to increase revenue, reduce costs, or both. These economic justifications depend on the business processes practiced and provide the economic basis for how a business intelligence system can create value for the enterprise. For example, the total investment amount, the time required to reach the break-even point, the nature and amount of risk associated with this investment, and the most critical factor, the factors and requirements for success and the extent to which the enterprise’s management can fulfill them. (Williams, 2016, pp. 69, 70)

In the past, simultaneous with the emergence and initial use of business intelligence, it was believed that the top management’s support for the idea of the enterprise adopting business intelligence systems was almost the only condition for the success of this initiative. However, successive radical transformations in the working environment of enterprises have imposed the inevitability of adopting a more integrated approach in this regard. Attention is paid to all branching and interconnected aspects of the idea of adopting business intelligence systems, known as Business Intelligence Opportunities. The distinctive features of small and medium-sized industrial enterprises compared to larger ones also give a special character to the idea of adoption. Therefore, an analytical framework will be adopted in this regard, dividing adoption requirements into three main groups: technological requirements, organizational requirements, and human requirements. Each aspect complements the other two, ultimately integrating business intelligence into the structure of small and medium-sized industrial enterprises to ensure maximum benefit, making this economic entity a stakeholder in what is currently known as the strategic triangle: cost, quality, and timeliness.

## Organizational Requirements for Adopting Business Intelligence

According to the literature of management science and as per the thinker Peter Drucker, organization is considered the second stage of practice within the framework of any managerial process, following planning. Organizing is, therefore, an activity that involves designing the organizational structure, in addition to achieving coordination and harmony among its various parts and separate administrative units. This is to make the organization fully adaptable to the continuous changes occurring in its environment, providing it with organizational agility. Consequently, organizational requirements refer to the compatibility, harmony, and coordination that must be achieved between business intelligence as a system and the organizational structure of the entire institution. This ensures the smooth flow and proper distribution of data and information. Managers and executives must receive real, useful, detailed, and timely information concerning all practiced activities and external environmental conditions to achieve high performance and organizational efficiency. Thus, the operational business intelligence system is integrated with the overall operational system of the organization. (Howson, Successful Business Intelligence: Secrets of making BI a killer app, 2008, p. 23) Among the prominent reasons leading to the emergence of these organizational requirements are the modern organizational structures adopted by industrial enterprises, known as flat structures, horizontal structures, or structures centered around operations. Additionally, the new distribution of decision-making authority, by enabling individuals responsible for business operations to make various decisions, played a crucial role. (Olszak, 2020, p. 3) The urgent transformations in the work environment, the emergence of the digital economy, and competition based on quality and innovative competition played a decisive role in the need for enterprises to achieve high levels of internal coordination. Business intelligence systems can play a crucial role in this regard. For example, some of the organizational requirements that a business intelligence system should meet include:

- Identifying business decisions to be supported by business intelligence.
- Identifying business processes to be improved using the data and information provided by the business intelligence system.
- Assessing the economic impact resulting from the potential improvements in business operations.
- Determining the level and extent of change required in the organization’s operations and its employees.
- Providing an accurate description of the information needed by the management of the organization. (Williams, 2016, pp. 70, 71)

During field visits to many small and medium-sized industrial enterprises in the industrial area of Setif, Algeria, extensive interviews were conducted with 32 managers and executives (CIO, CFO, COO, and CEO). Over a period of two months, 14 detailed questions were asked about activities that could be significantly improved using business intelligence systems. These questions were selected based on their connection to the enterprise’s strategy, as well as their contribution to creating benefit. The interviews led to the following results:

- Using business intelligence contributes to predicting sales trends and discovering new markets.
- Relying on business intelligence leads to significant improvements in the distribution process to stores.
- Using business intelligence facilitates the measurement and improvement of customer satisfaction.
- Business intelligence can be used to achieve substantial improvements in supply chain management.
- Using business intelligence helps improve productivity across the enterprise.
- Business intelligence contributes to eliminating non-value-added activities.
- Business intelligence can be used to improve indirect cost allocation on products.
- Business intelligence can be used to improve inventory management.
- Business intelligence can be used to improve coordination between different functional departments.
- Business intelligence can be used to maintain a high level of competitive awareness.
