## ABSTRACT

This chapter examines the role of technology business incubators (TBIs) in improving the effectiveness, viability, and overall development of startups. The chapter attempts to establish a connection between the support offered by these TBIs and its effects on the overall operational effectiveness of these startups by referring to various available relevant literature. Further, the chapter investigates the assistance offered by these TBIs as well as the challenges encountered throughout the process from the ideation and commercialization of funded ideas. Through the empirical analysis of Gujarat’s entrepreneurial ecosystem, a comprehensive framework is given that can be used to improve the existing support system.

## INTRODUCTION

The term entrepreneurship ecosystem refers to a complex network of highly interrelated activities. There is a paucity of knowledge on how ecosystems interact and contribute to the growth of interconnected components that support entrepreneurship. The interconnectedness of its constituent parts, which reinforce and maintain one another, is often referred to as an ecosystem. Examining these interactions is crucial to fully comprehend how the environment influences the development and expansion of new employment in the residential environment (Acs et al., 2012; Ács et al., 2014, 2016; Alvedalen & Boschma, 2017). These components are knitted together in a way to empowers the entrepreneur. Van De Ven, (1993) states that several components interconnectedly contribute to the development of the ecosystem, some of which primarily relate to the role entrepreneurs play.

Certain characteristics of the entrepreneurial ecosystem make it an attractive setting for a variety of supporting activities. These include the promotion of present and future knowledge, the intense attention of investment firms, institutions for skill improvement and training, governance, access to legal and financial resources, and the desire to invent and create. Recent studies, particularly theoretical contributions, provide a useful framework for identifying qualities essential to the success of a startup environment. Van De Ven, (1993) is one of the prominent researchers who has extensively worked with business enterprises and advocates that industrial infrastructure can help to produce an enabling environment that contributes to the entrepreneurial ecosystem. This infrastructure consists of functional arrangements for technology management and implementation, the provision of basic scientific information services, the estimation of the amount of financing, skilled labor, research facilities, and marketing and logistics support that are readily available. The university, the government, the professionals, support services, capital services, and the talent team are all cited as important elements of the business environment (Cohen, 2006; Neck et al., 2004).

## DEFINING ENTREPRENEURIAL ENVIRONMENT AND ENTREPRENEURSHIP ECOSYSTEM

The entrepreneurial environment, as defined by Spiegel, (2017), consists of eleven traits that give entrepreneurs security, encouragement, and access to resources. Aspects include supporting culture, entrepreneurial history, investor talent, networks, advisers, and successful businesses, as well as academics, virtual infrastructure, and market support (Spiegel, 2017; Spiegel & Harrison, 2018). Researchers have put forth various frameworks for explaining the role of the entrepreneurial journey which should be a combination of different elements including affirmative culture, streamlined policies and leadership, access to exclusive funding, fair utilization of labor, user-friendly product markets, and a variety of institutional and infrastructure support (Isenberg, 2014). Further, the interaction of stakeholders in the physical environment as well as access to a variety of intangible resources, such as skill set, services, and capital, interconnected in cutting-edge settings further contribute to the ecosystem (Feld, 2012).

In one of its investigations, Oxford Global Innovation Consultancy recommends a simple method for identifying ecosystem gaps. The business ecosystem consists mostly of academic and research institutions, government agencies, business and industrial groups, and other non-profit organizations. A framework for ecosystem support based on the Babson Entrepreneurship Ecosystem Project seeks to develop world-class entrepreneurs (Isenberg, 2010) Babson Business Entrepreneurship focuses on six key areas that are essential for creating a strong business case at the national level, including a corporate governance culture characterized by social trends and success stories, a financial infrastructure that is accessible to entrepreneurs, and a high level of staff due to the quality of the educational systems used and the character of the staff. Each modern business environment is as distinct as the environment we are in because hundreds of ecosystems interact in complicated and polarising ways (Isenberg, 2011).

Because the stakeholders in these areas view entrepreneurs and startups as distinct entities with a variety of skills and resources and the capacity to function in social and commercial contexts, the approach to the entrepreneurship ecosystem is unique to industrial districts, suburbs, and regions with high concentrations of tech clusters. Recent publications on the business environment. Feld, (2012), Isenberg, (2011), and Spiegel (2017), have suggested effective company success and its impact on technological entrepreneurship without clearly articulating and demonstrating the origins and impacts of these effects.

## GUJARAT ENTREPRENEURIAL ECOSYSTEM

Gujarat is one of those ecosystems where the state government has pledged to an enormous number of startups right from the seed stage by providing support with early-stage grants. Through student startup interventions at several technology and non-technology universities, Gujarat made systematic efforts at its pre-incubation layer. Gujarat’s ecosystem has been significantly shaped by ecosystem actors from the non-government sector, particularly from the perspectives of mentoring, early investment, and incubation. In the most recent Gujarat Budget, more than 200 crores were distributed to several departments of the Government of Gujarat to stimulate entrepreneurship. The state government has launched several programs to direct these financial resources. Some of these prominent programs are, (a) a scheme for availing assistance to startups/ innovations under Gujarat Industrial Policy (2015), which emphasizes recognizing Nodal Institutions (NIs) in the state for extending startup support, (b) the Student Startup and Innovation Policy, governed by Gujarat Knowledge Society, Directorate of Technical Education, and (c) Electronics & IT/ITeS Start-up Policy for the state of Gujarat (2016-21) governed by Department of Science & Technology (DST), Govt. of Gujarat (GoG).

Along with initiatives and support from the government, various corporate institutions, trade associations, and academia have come together for the same. It is very interesting to note that many states have scaled up institutional mechanisms based on the Gujarat ecosystem’s lessons learned. Pre-incubation and university startup ideas in Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, and across the nation through AICTE have drawn a lot of inspiration from Gujarat-driven academic projects. While the Gujarat startup ecosystem is envisioning modern mechanisms and institutions to cater to knowledge-driven startup needs as well, it has already acted quite early in the 1980s by institutionalizing its processes through entities like the Centre for Entrepreneurship Development (CED), Industrial Extension Bureau (iNDEXTb), and others.
