
Little, a member of the Shanghai branch, wrote a manual titled China's Income and the New Treaty: Protests against Tariff Increasing, from a Merchant's Perspective to promote public opinion against the plan (Little 1902). The editorial department of the North China Herald printed and published this manual, which not only affected the attitudes of many members at the Shanghai branch but also sparked widespread discussion among the British public. Many opponents who were directly involved in China's trade used media such as The North China Daily News to raise a large number of persuasive objections. After the first general meeting of the Shanghai branch, due to excessive abstention votes, to gain the support of those who remained silent in the meeting, Edward S. Little published a speech in the North China Daily News to the Business Counselor of the British Consulate General in China, accusing the British government of inadequate regulation of the clause in Tianjin Treaty (天津条约) which said transit pass charge of 2.5% for goods sent into the interior.

He questioned the feasibility of the plan of canceling likin and raising the tariff (Little 1902 March 5). During this process, the British government and supporters of the plan also used public opinion to retaliate. J. O. P. Bland, a correspondent for The Times, published an article refuting Little's interpretation of the Tianjin Treaty (天津条约) and calling on British businessmen in China to support the plan in exchange for a true open door (Bland 1902). On the other hand, the branch used various means to guide public opinion. The Shanghai Mercury launched an attack on the identity of Bland, who had been a customs employee for many years, implying that the tariff increase was due to Sir Robert Hart, the chief tariff officer, who was willing to harm the interests of the British for collecting wealth for the Chinese (The National Archives 1902). Mackay closely followed the debate and sent all the newspaper clippings to the Marquess of Lansdowne, regretfully stating that except for Bland's article, most of the other comments were against the plan (Mackay 1902b). Because almost all English newspapers in China represented the interests of British businessmen, the British government had almost failed in the public opinion arena. It had to make concessions on the amount of tariff increases that the Shanghai branch could accept.

In terms of diplomacy, the Shanghai branch requested the British government to force the Chinese government to expand the scope of the abolition of likin barriers to smooth the mainland Chinese market. The abolishing likin barriers plan originally proposed by Mackay did not include salt barriers, local opium barriers, etc. The Chinese government had emphasized the differences between the above and likin barriers, as well as the importance of investigating illegal activities and collecting taxes on the mainland. The Shanghai branch insisted these barriers were as same as likin barriers which must be included in the scope of abolition. The Shanghai branch used newspapers to criticize fiercely by publishing a series of articles. The article pointed out that the shackles hindering the development of mainland trade were not only the likin barriers. If the salt barriers and local opium barriers were not abolished, the extortion of goods circulation would continue to exist (Little 1902 June 25). Under the pressure of public opinion from British businessmen in China, Mackay proposed the abolition of salt barriers and local opium barriers at the Treaty Amendment Conference.

Sheng Xuanhuai (盛宣怀), who was a Chinese treaty envoy, expressed confusion and opposition to the sudden expansion of the scope (China, 1994). The local governors Liu Kunyi (刘坤一) and Zhang Zhidong (张之洞), who participated in the negotiation, also firmly resisted (Chen 2018; Zhao 2008). The British government compromised by renaming the salt barriers to Salt Reporting Stations, renaming the local opium barriers to Opium Stations, and establishing a monitoring system. Without appreciating it, the Shanghai branch launched another public opinion offensive. In China, British businessmen pointed out that salt barriers were only nominal extinction. (Little 1902 October 5). However, salt tax and opium tax were both important sources of fiscal revenue for the Chinese government, especially used to offset foreign debts, including those of Britain. The British government was unable to support the Shanghai branch's demand. If the actors have specific fundamental interests, they strive to maximize their utility. The most desirable policy for these two is a policy choice that maximizes their fundamental interests in the field of the issue (Milner 1997). In the end, the British government set an ex-factory tax in exchange for recognition of the plan by local British businessmen. The Shanghai branch was forced to accept the result.

All studies on the evolution of public and private power have shown that both types of power have gone to extremes while both have provided lasting benefits that do not conflict with each other (Rothkopf 2012). In the issue of cancelling likin and rising tariff, the British government (public/government actors) engaged in a fierce game with the Shanghai branch (private/non-governmental actors), regarding management of public affairs. The Shanghai branch exerted pressure on the British government through collective motions, public opinion attacks, and other means to achieve its interests, which was a manifestation of private actors attempting to extend their power to the public space field. The British government, on the other hand, adopted administrative coercive orders, differentiated organizational structures, and other methods to maintain its authority in public affairs. Among them, non-governmental actors have significant shortcomings due to their loose internal structure, inconsistent member interests, susceptibility to external influences, and even difficulty in achieving internal power. However, their leverage can't be ignored.

However, the ultimate goal of the game between the Shanghai branch and the British government was still to pursue the maximum common interest. Although the British government used various means in this issue to try to suppress the claims of the Shanghai branch and force it to cooperate with overseas strategies. The Shanghai branch also used various methods to pressure the government to change policies based on the opinions of British businessmen in China. However, the British government has always provided a platform for communication and cooperation with British businessmen in China. The various demands of the Shanghai branch had not deviated from this platform. It was obvious that the British government fully understood that under a market-oriented system, public affairs were in the hands of two leading groups, the government and the industry. They must do their best to cooperate (Lindblom 1997).

Based on a mutual compromise, the two bodies reached an agreement on the issue of canceling likin and raising tariffs, which provided convenience for British business in China. On the other hand, the Shanghai branch also had to accept an additional ex-factory tax on machinery products in China, to safeguard the interests of local British businessmen and win support from local British businessmen for the plan of cancelling likin and rising tariffs.

## Philippine Chinese Charitable Association Inc.

## The Issue of Establishing a Consulate of China in the Philippines: The Informal Colonialism of the Philippine Chinese Charitable Association Inc.

In terms of economy, the Philippine Chinese Charitable Association Inc. requested the Chinese government to establish a consulate to promote the development of Chinese business in the Philippines and protect industrial security. During the Spanish rule, colonizers formulated various harsh regulations restricting overseas Chinese (You 1957). Industry tax and store tax were imposed to restrict overseas Chinese business (Huang & He 2009). Faced with such unfair treatment, the Philippine Chinese Charitable Association Inc. turned its attention to its home state. They attempted to seek support from the Chinese government.
