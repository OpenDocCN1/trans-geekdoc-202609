### ABSTRACT

The fast growth of health tourism has been attributed to creating a health and prevention boom era expected to usher in new economic power. Offering a different approach derived from the Sharia, Muslim health tourism poses a proximity between religion, tourism, and health. Amid the urgency to understand Muslim tourists as a potential market with such unique characteristics and specific needs, it is unfortunate that no model describes the decision-making behaviour when selecting a Muslim-friendly health destination. As a result, the author suggests a Muslim health tourist behaviour model. This study also highlighted Sharia salons and spas as one of the forms of Muslim health tourism business. It’s becoming increasingly clear that for the Muslim health tourism business to truly understand its market, going back to basics is the most innovative approach.

### INTRODUCTION

The rapid development of health tourism has been seen to have created a “health and prevention boom era” predicted to be a “new economic power” (Sigrist, 2006). Health tourism has become one of the fastest growing industries in the world, despite the desire to improve the quality of life, health, as well as healing and relaxation, which has been the primary motivation of tourists since long ago (Kim et al., 2015). Health tourism combines two things, namely health and tourism, and the involvement of these two things makes health tourism a promising tourism niche. On the other hand, Muslim medical tourism is generally done by people who are sick and need treatment to regain their health (curative) (Smith & Puczkó, 2014). Medical care is available in health tourism, such as surgery or other specialist interventions, while wellness tourism provides pilgrimages, meditation, spas, and others (Smith & Puczkó, 2017).

Increased public awareness of the health aspects is responsibly responded to by the increasingly diverse commoditisation of medicines, medicals, and treatments worldwide, forming a “false trap” for those who worry excessively about their health. The negative impact of this is the emergence of massive exploration of businesspersons over the naturally unlimited human desire for health. This desire must be encouraged to change into a necessity, not essential, artificial, and even pragmatic.

Take an example of the high obsession with cosmetic surgery. The merely profit-oriented advertisements put by beauty clinics and surgical hospitals have made patients from the country and abroad take long trips to reconstruct their faces and bodies in the clinics. This has turned into the psychosocial disease of society where people become preoccupied with what they perceive as defects in their body or face or even when the physical anomalies are only slightly; the fears become real and exaggerated and encourage a solid need to perform cosmetic surgery constantly with the never-ending search for satisfaction. This psychological disorder is called Body Dysmorphic Disorder, and even cosmetic surgery has become a social and cultural phenomenon (Kim, 2009; Heyes, 2009).

Then, it is pretty often that patients also end up with various controversial, circumvention, and illegal treatments that disregard morality and even religious beliefs. A variety of other treatment methods evidences this as a trend in the world, like “vampire facelift,” “sex change tourism,” “sex tourism,” “suicide tourism,” “nude tourism,” “abortion tourism,” “vasectomy and tubectomy,” “transplant tourism,” “fertility tourism,” “surrogacy tourism,” and so forth which use human organs and blood and hazardous chemicals for aesthetic and medical businesses.

Correspondingly, Cohen (1998) asserts that mass tourism hurts the religiosity or level of spirituality of people who live in tourist destinations and events for tourists itself; the impact is increasing secularisation, weakening compliance toward religion and belief, as well as waning of the sanctity of holy places, rituals, and customs. If this continues, tourism can be a coherent process against religiosity. Joyce (2001) calls this phenomenon the myth of morality. In the view of the myth of morality, there is no relationship between business and morality, and it is a mistake if business activity is run under morality and ethics. The famous phrase “business is business” or “it is not personal, it is strictly business” in business practices.

Western or mainstream medicine is ineffective in meeting the consumers’ body–mind–and spirit needs (Bomar, 2013). Even though spirituality is viewed as an essential dimension of health, it tends to be more and more secular (Smith & Puczkó, 2013). In times like these, business requires more than just executing or embedded in interconnected, nested local and global system. They can select and establish rules and a new purpose in their formal structure, more dignified, virtuous, and moral respect, including spirituality, to control harmful excesses from tourism.

On the other hand, the spiritual motif has long been an integral part of the travel activity and can be regarded as the world’s oldest non-economic form of travel (Jackowski & Smith, 1992). Studies have examined the relationship between religious aspects and tourism (Veiga et al., 2017). Wahab et al. (1976) assert that a tourist is a rational decision maker (homo economicus) trying to maximise the utility and, therefore, assesses the costs and benefits of actions before making the trips. Meanwhile, modifying the afore-mentioned term, according to the author, a bt is a rational and spiritual decision maker (homo Islamicus) who needs to limit the decisions of doing reasonable and spiritual travel with the principles of Islam. What defines Muslim tourists’ decisions is the Maslahah concept (something that brings goodness, safety, or benefit). Not all destinations that offer utility contain Maslahah in them, meaning that not all destinations can be visited and are worth a visit by Muslim tourists. Thus, the objective is not to maximise the utility of Muslim tourists but to maximise Maslahah.
