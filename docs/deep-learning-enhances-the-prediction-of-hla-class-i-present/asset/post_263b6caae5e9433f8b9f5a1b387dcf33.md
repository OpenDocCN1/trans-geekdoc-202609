Accurate silico detection of CD8+ T cell epitopes would greatly enhance cell-based vaccine development, but current prediction models are not reliably successful. Here, motivated by recent successes using machine learning to decode complex biology, we curated a dataset of 61,237 unique leukocyte antigen class I (HLA-1) ligands and developed MUNIS, a deep learning model that identifies peptides presented by HLA-1 ligands. MUNIS showed improved performance over existing models in predicting peptide presentation and CD8+ T epitope immunodominance hierarchies. Moreover, application of MUNIS to proteins from Epstein–Barr virus led to successful identification of both known and novel HLA-1 epitopes which were experimentally validated by vitro HLA-peptide stability and T immunogenicity assays. MUNIS performs comparably to an experimental assay in immun prediction, suggesting that deep learning can reduce experimental burden and accelerate detection of CD8+ T cell epitopes for rapid vaccine development.

Although numerous methods exist to predict CD8+ T cell epitopes, their accuracy varies across HLA-I alleles. Moreover, to understand whether these models can identify immunogenic epitopes, the lack of unbiased datasets is a major bottleneck. Frequently predicted peptides are of uncertain affinity or selected binding binders⁶⁷ Several tools can then characterize the characteristics of immunogenic peptides⁸⁹¹⁰ they are, given the remarkable successes of deep learning to improve model generalization.

# Deep learning model for CD8+ T cell epitope identification

To develop a robust model of CD8+ T cell epitope prediction, we constructed a dataset composed of mass spectrometry data from immunopeptide datasets including MHCPre2.2, MHCNet4.1, and MHCII2.0 training sets and from the ImmunePeptide Database (IEDB: ied.org?). We used these HLA-B binding peptides with non-binding peptides from SwisProt at a ratio of 1:1. Importantly, all peptides in model evaluation were independent of training sets of HLA-B restriction. This is a filter strategy to address data leakage in previous methods where overlapping HLA-B peptides were present between training and test sets. We show the results of HLA-B in 1a.

The architecture of MUNIS is composed of two modules (Fig. 2). The first module learns the binding affinity and a vector representation of the HLA-peptide binding. The second module aggregates the features from the N and C-terminal to produce a prediction.

Previous work has highlighted the area under receiver characteristic (AUC-ROC) and precision as important metrics for evaluating performance. However, the disadvantage of accuracy is that for a given threshold, the proportion of positives is low. We use the area under the precision-recall curve to evaluate performance. This score ranges from 0 to 1, with higher being better.

# MUNIS performing better in classifying HLA-B

We evaluated the performance of our model on a test set containing 37,215 HLA-B peptides and 2065 randomly sampled non-binding peptides. compared with tools like MHCPre2.2, MHCNet4.1, MHCII2.0, and MUNIS (including MHC-EL) and immunopeptidomics (BiMHC-IG). In contrast, our model achieved an average precision of 0.922, which corresponds to a 21% increase in comparison with tools, with MHCPre2.2 scoring 0.768, MHCNet4.1 scoring 0.795, MHCII2.0 scoring 0.862. The ROC-AUC of our model was 0.939, which corresponds to a 33% increase in comparison with MHCII2.0 at 0.971, MHCNet4.1 scoring 0.927, MUNIS scoring 0.895, and MHCPre2.2 scoring 0.836.

Evaluation of the model on the test sets using epitopes in the evaluation dataset showed promising results, excluding models where we have overlap. For 22/24 HLA-B alleles we detected high performance in precision. This performance could be due to the model's ability to encode peptide length.

# MHC predicts epitope identity and immunodominance

Given that only HLA-presented peptides are CD8+ T cell epitopes, we measured the ability of MHC to predict immunopeptide identity. To avoid bias from using peptide sets for prediction, we selected a test set of immunopeptidomics data from viruses A and B that bind to HLA-B-A02:01, HLA-A11:01 and HLA-A*24:02. We de novo constructed a list of HLA-B-presented peptides identified via mass spectrometry that were subsequently evaluated for immunodominance using gamma (IFN-γ) immunospot (ELISpot) assays, where 32 of the peptides were tested. We ranked them as positive (immunodominant) or negative in the viral proteome. When comparing the set of datasets, we only considered peptides with at least one immunogenic peptide to prevent preventing bias by high levels of protein immunity.
