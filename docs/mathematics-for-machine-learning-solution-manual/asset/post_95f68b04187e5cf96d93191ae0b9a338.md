## Express Beta distribution as an exponential family distribution:

Recall the Beta distribution from Example 6.11

p(μ|α,β) = Γ(α + β) / [Γ(α)Γ(β)] μ^{α−1} (1 − μ)^{(β−1)}

= 1 / B(α,β) μ^{α−1} (1 − μ)^{(β−1)} .

This can be written in exponential family form

p(μ|α,β) = exp[ (α − 1) log μ + (β − 1) log(1 − μ) − log B(α,β) ] .

This can be identified as being in exponential family form by observing that

h(μ) = 1

θ|(α,β) = [α − 1   β − 1]ᵀ

φ(μ) = [log μ   log(1 − μ)]

A(θ) = log B(α,β) .

## Show the product of the Beta and Binomial distribution is also a member of exponential family:

We treat μ as random variable here, x as an known integer between 0 and N. For x = 0, …, N, we get

p(x|N,μ)p(μ|α,β) = C(N,x) μ^{x} (1 − μ)^{(N−x)} 1/B(α,β) μ^{α−1} (1 − μ)^{(β−1)}

= C(N,x) exp[ x log μ + (N - x) log(1 - μ) ] · exp[ (α−1) log μ + (β−1) log(1−μ) − log B(α,β) ]

= exp[ (x + α − 1) log μ + (N − x + β − 1) log(1 − μ) + log C(N,x) − log B(α,β) ] .

The last line can be identified as being in exponential family form by observing that

h(μ) = 1

θ = [x + α − 1   N − x + β − 1]ᵀ

φ(μ) = [log μ   log(1 − μ)]

A(θ) = log C(N,x) + log B(α,β) .

## 6.10 Derive the relationship in Section 6.5.2 in two ways:

- a. By completing the square
- b. By expressing the Gaussian in its exponential family form

The *product* of two Gaussians $\mathcal{N}(x\mid a,A)\mathcal{N}(x\mid b,B)$ is an unnormalized Gaussian distribution $c\mathcal{N}(x\mid c,C)$ with

$$C=(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1}$$
$$c=C(A^{-1}a+B^{-1}b)$$
$$c=(2\pi)^{-D/2}|A+B|^{-1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(a-b)^\top(A+B)^{-1}(a-b)\right).$$

Note that the normalizing constant $c$ itself can be considered a (normalized) Gaussian distribution either in $a$ or in $b$ with an “inflated” covariance matrix $A+B$, i.e., $c=\mathcal{N}(a\mid b,A+B)=\mathcal{N}(b\mid a,A+B)$.

### (i) By completing the square, we obtain

$$\mathcal{N}(x\mid a,A)=(2\pi)^{-D/2}|A|^{-1/2}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(x-a)^\top A^{-1}(x-a)\right],$$

$$\mathcal{N}(x\mid b,B)=(2\pi)^{-D/2}|B|^{-1/2}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(x-b)^\top B^{-1}(x-b)\right].$$

For convenience, we consider the log of the product:

$$\log\left(\mathcal{N}(x\mid a,A)\mathcal{N}(x\mid b,B)\right)$$
$$=\log\mathcal{N}(x\mid a,A)+\log\mathcal{N}(x\mid b,B)$$
$$=-\frac{1}{2}\left[(x-a)^\top A^{-1}(x-a)+(x-b)^\top B^{-1}(x-b)\right]+\text{const}$$
$$=-\frac{1}{2}\left[x^\top A^{-1}x-2x^\top A^{-1}a+a^\top A^{-1}a+x^\top B^{-1}x-2x^\top B^{-1}b+b^\top B^{-1}b\right]+\text{const}$$
$$=-\frac{1}{2}\left[x^\top(A^{-1}+B^{-1})x-2x^\top(A^{-1}a+B^{-1}b)+a^\top A^{-1}a+b^\top B^{-1}b\right]+\text{const}$$
$$+\text{const}$$
$$=-\frac{1}{2}\left[(x-(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1}(A^{-1}a+B^{-1}b))^\top(A^{-1}+B^{-1})\right.$$
$$\left.\cdot(x-(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1}(A^{-1}a+B^{-1}b))\right]+\text{const}.$$

Thus, the corresponding product is $\mathcal{N}(x\mid c,C)$ with

$$C=(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1} \tag{6.9}$$
$$c=(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1}(A^{-1}a+B^{-1}b)=C(A^{-1}a+B^{-1}b). \tag{6.10}$$

### (ii) By expressing Gaussian in its exponential family form:

Recall from the Example 6.13 we get the exponential form of univariate Gaussian distribution, similarly we can get the exponential form of multivariate Gaussian distribution as

$$p(x\mid a,A)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x^\top A^{-1}x+x^\top A^{-1}a-\frac{1}{2}a^\top A^{-1}a\right)$$

### b. The distribution $p(y)=\int p(y\mid x)p(x)\,dx$ is Gaussian. Compute the mean $\mu_y$ and the covariance $\Sigma_y$. Derive your result in detail.

$p(y)$ is Gaussian distributed (affine transformation of the Gaussian random variable $x$) with mean $\mu_y$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma_y$. We obtain

$$\begin{aligned}
\mu_y &= \mathbb{E}_y[y] = \mathbb{E}_{X,W}[Ax+b+w] = A\mathbb{E}_X[x]+b+\mathbb{E}_W[w]\\
&= A\mu_x+b, \qquad \text{and}\\[4pt]
\Sigma_y &= \mathbb{E}_y[yy^\mathsf{T}]-\mathbb{E}_y[y]\mathbb{E}_y[y]^\mathsf{T}\\
&= \mathbb{E}_{X,W}[(Ax+b+w)(Ax+b+w)^\mathsf{T}]-\mu_y\mu_y^\mathsf{T}\\
&= \mathbb{E}_{X,W}[Axx^\mathsf{T}A^\mathsf{T}+Axb^\mathsf{T}+Axw^\mathsf{T}+bx^\mathsf{T}A^\mathsf{T}+bb^\mathsf{T}+bw^\mathsf{T}\\
&\qquad +w(Ax+b)^\mathsf{T}+ww^\mathsf{T}]-\mu_y\mu_y^\mathsf{T}.
\end{aligned}$$

We use the linearity of the expected value, move all constants out of the expected value, and exploit the independence of $w$ and $x$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\Sigma_y &= A\mathbb{E}_X[xx^\mathsf{T}]A^\mathsf{T}+A\mathbb{E}_X[x]b^\mathsf{T}+b\mathbb{E}_X[x]^\mathsf{T}A^\mathsf{T}+bb^\mathsf{T}+\mathbb{E}_W[ww^\mathsf{T}]\\
&\qquad -(A\mu_x+b)(A\mu_x+b)^\mathsf{T},
\end{aligned}$$

where we used our previous result for $\mu_y$. Note that $\mathbb{E}_W[w]=0$. We continue as follows:

$$\begin{aligned}
\Sigma_y &= A\mathbb{E}_X[xx^\mathsf{T}]A^\mathsf{T}+A\mu_xb^\mathsf{T}+b\mu_x^\mathsf{T}A^\mathsf{T}+bb^\mathsf{T}+Q\\
&\qquad -A\mu_x\mu_x^\mathsf{T}A^\mathsf{T}-A\mu_xb^\mathsf{T}-b\mu_x^\mathsf{T}A^\mathsf{T}-bb^\mathsf{T}\\
&= A\left(\mathbb{E}_X[xx^\mathsf{T}]-\mu_x\mu_x^\mathsf{T}\right)A^\mathsf{T}+Q\\
&= A\Sigma_xA^\mathsf{T}+Q.
\end{aligned}$$

Alternatively, we could have exploited

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{V}_y[y] &= \mathbb{V}_{x,w}[Ax+b+w]\mathrel{\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{=}}\mathbb{V}_x[Ax+b]+\mathbb{V}_w[w]\\
&= A\mathbb{V}_xA^\mathsf{T}+Q=A\Sigma_xA^\mathsf{T}+Q.
\end{aligned}$$

### c. The random variable $y$ is being transformed according to the measurement mapping

$$z=Cy+v,$$

where $z\in\mathbb{R}^F$, $C\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times E}$, and $v\sim\mathcal{N}(v\mid 0,R)$ is independent Gaussian (measurement) noise.

- Write down $p(z\mid y)$.

$$\begin{aligned}
p(z\mid y)&=\mathcal{N}(z\mid Cy,R)\\
&=(2\pi)^{-F/2}|R|^{-1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(z-Cy)^\mathsf{T}R^{-1}(z-Cy)\right).
\end{aligned}$$

- Compute $p(\mathbf{z})$, i.e., the mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_z$ and the covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z$. Derive your result in detail.

Remark: Since $\boldsymbol{y}$ is Gaussian and $\boldsymbol{z}$ is a linear transformation of $\boldsymbol{y}$, $p(\boldsymbol{z})$ is Gaussian, too. Let's compute its moments (similar to the “time update”):

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_z = \mathbb{E}_Z[\mathbf{z}] = \mathbb{E}_{Y,V}[\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{v}] = \mathbf{C}\mathbb{E}_Y[\mathbf{y}] + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_V[\mathbf{v}]}_{=0} = \mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\mu}_y.$$

For the covariance matrix, we compute $$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z = \mathbb{E}_Z[\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^\top] - \mathbb{E}_Z[\mathbf{z}]\mathbb{E}_Z[\mathbf{z}]^\top = \mathbb{E}_{Y,V}[(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{v})(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{v})^\top] - \boldsymbol{\mu}_z\boldsymbol{\mu}_z^\top = \mathbb{E}_{Y,V}[\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{C}^\top + \mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}\mathbf{v}^\top + \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y})^\top + \mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^\top] - \boldsymbol{\mu}_z\boldsymbol{\mu}_z^\top.$$

We use the linearity of the expected value, move all constants out of the expected value, and exploit the independence of $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{y}$: $$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z = \mathbf{C}\mathbb{E}_Y[\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^\top]\mathbf{C}^\top + \mathbb{E}_V[\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^\top] - \mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\mu}_y\boldsymbol{\mu}_y^\top\mathbf{C}^\top,$$ where we used our previous result for $\boldsymbol{\mu}_z$. Note that $\mathbb{E}_V[\mathbf{v}] = \mathbf{0}$. We continue as follows: $$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z = \mathbf{C}\left(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_Y[\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^\top] - \boldsymbol{\mu}_y\boldsymbol{\mu}_y^\top}_{=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_y}\right)\mathbf{C}^\top + \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_y\mathbf{C}^\top + \mathbf{R}.$$

### d. Now, a value $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is measured. Compute the posterior distribution $p(\mathbf{x} \mid \hat{\mathbf{y}})$. Hint for solution: This posterior is also Gaussian, i.e., we need to determine only its mean and covariance matrix. Start by explicitly computing the joint Gaussian $p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$. This also requires us to compute the cross-covariances $\mathrm{Cov}_{x,y}[\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}]$ and $\mathrm{Cov}_{y,x}[\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{x}]$. Then apply the rules for Gaussian conditioning.

We derive the posterior distribution following the second hint since we do not have to worry about normalization constants: Assume, we know the joint Gaussian distribution $$p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\ \mathbf{y}\end{pmatrix} \bigg| \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_x\\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_y\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_x & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xy}\\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yx} & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_y\end{pmatrix}\right), \tag{6.11}$$ where we defined $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xy} := \mathrm{Cov}_{x,y}[\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}]$. Now, we apply the rules for Gaussian conditioning to obtain $$p(\mathbf{x} \mid \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_{x \mid y}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{x \mid y})$$ $$\boldsymbol{\mu}_{x \mid y} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_x + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xy}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_y^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_y)$$ $$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{x \mid y} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_x - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{xy}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_y^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yx}.$$
