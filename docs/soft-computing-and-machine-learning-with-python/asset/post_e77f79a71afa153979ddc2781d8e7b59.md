# Predicting Academic Achievement of High-School Students Using

The tree-based models are also more suitable to non-linear interaction effects from classical techniques. When several trees are ensemble to perform a prediction it generally leads to a high accuracy (Flach, 2012; Geurts et al., 2009), decreasing the chance of overfitting and diminishing the variance between datasets. The focus of the current paper was the application of this relatively new method in the educational psychology field, taking advantage of the tree-based models in a broad set of applications (Baca-Garcia et al., 2007; Eloyan et al., 2012; Gibbons et al., 2013; Kuroki & Tiley, 2012; Scott, Jackson & Bergman, 2011; Skogli et al., 2013; Tian et al., 2014; van der Wal & Kowalczyk, 2013). Within education, Blanch and Aluja (2013), Cortes and Silva (2008) and Golino and Gomes (2014) applied the tree-based models to predict the academic achievement of students from the secondary and tertiary levels using a set of psychological and socio-demographic variables. The discussion of their methods and results are beyond the scope of the current paper, since we focused on the methodological aspects of machine learning.

In the present paper we showed Rasch scores of the tests and scales significantly differentiated the high achievement from low achievement 10th grade students. Inductive reasoning presented a large effect size, while the deep learning approach, metacognitive control and self-appraisal moderate effect sizes. The random forest prediction led to a total accuracy of 75%, a sensitivity of 68% and a specificity of 73.69% in the training set. The testing result was a bit worse, with a total accuracy of 68.18%, a sensitivity of 72.72% and a specificity of 63.63%. The most important variable in the prediction was inductive reasoning that was associated with a mean accuracy of 66.22% when left out of the prediction bag. The deep learning approach was the second most important variable (mean accuracy of 28.45%), followed by metacognitive control (19.68%), TDRI self-appraisal (19.50%) and TCM self-appraisal (5.78%). This reinforces previous findings that showed incremental validity of the learning approaches in the explanation of academic performance beyond intelligence, using traditional techniques (Chamorro-Pruzmic & Furnham, 2008; Furnham Monsen & Ahmetoglu, 2009; Goses & Golino, 2012). It also reinforces the incremental validity of metacognition over intelligence, in the explanation of academic achievement (van der Stel & Veenman, 2008; Veenman & Bishizen, 2004).

## CONCLUSION

The application of machine learning models in the prediction of academic achievement/performance, especially the tree-based models, represents an innovative complement to the traditional techniques such as linear and logistic regression, as well as structural equation modelling (Blanch & Aluja, 2013). More than the advantages pointed earlier, the tree-based models can help us understand the non-linear interactions between psycho-educational variables in the prediction of academic outcomes. These machine learning models not only represent an advance in terms of prediction accuracy, but also represent an advance in terms of inference. Future studies could benefit from employing a larger and broader sample, involving students from different schools. It would also be interesting to investigate, in the future, the impact of varying the tuning parameters of the random forest model in the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and variability of the prediction.
