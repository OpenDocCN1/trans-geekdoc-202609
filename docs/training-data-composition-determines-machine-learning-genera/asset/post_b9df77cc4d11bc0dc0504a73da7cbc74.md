The influence of negative datasets for antibody binding prediction has been studied not only at the sequence level but also structurally, using simulated27 and experimental data25. Here we explored the impact of training set composition on prediction accuracy and generalizability, with an emphasis on the discovery of binding rules (Fig. 1b). We define the learning of binding rules as the identification of sequence-level binding energies and residue-level contributions to binding (energies).

The binding of immune receptors to their corresponding antigen is mainly determined by sequentially diverse complementarity-determining regions (CDRs)32,33. In particular, the CDR3 regions present in the heavy chain (CDRH3) of antibodies or beta chain (CDR3β) of TCRs contribute substantially to immune receptor–antigen binding32–34. Therefore, in the current study, CDRH3 regions were used as a proxy for full-length immune receptor sequences to train the ML models. Existing state-of-the-art immune receptor–antigen binding databases contain <10⁴ data points35–40 with known sequence/structures but, mostly, without the binding affinity. To address this gap, synthetic data have emerged as a viable solution to benchmark ML methodologies14,41,42. Synthetic data enable unconstrained access to ground-truth data, enabling an arbitrary composition of training datasets and thus, a more controlled analysis of generalization and rule discovery. We previously developed the antibody–antigen binding simulation framework Absolut!27, which enables the large-scale generation of synthetic antibody–antigen complex structures annotated with affinity. In earlier works, it was shown that the accuracy-based rankings of ML methods trained on experimental data are preserved when the same methods are trained on data generated by Absolut!, for both sequence- and structure-based prediction tasks43–45.

In this work, we used the Absolut! framework27 to generate synthetic training datasets with varying negative data compositions, reflecting common practices in immune receptor–antigen prediction (Fig. 1c). We trained ML models on these datasets and assessed how training data composition affects both generalization and biological rule recovery (Fig. 1d,e). Rule recovery was performed using explainability methods46–48, which aim to uncover the reasoning behind model predictions by identifying data features that most influence the output. We validated our findings using a large-scale experimental dataset of HER2 affinity-labelled antibody sequences from ref. 49. Our results show that both generalization and biological rule discovery depend critically on the training dataset composition – including the choice of negative examples (Fig. 1f).

## Results

### Training dataset sequence composition influences prediction performance in ID and OOD binary classification tasks

ML setup on synthetic and experimental data. We first investigated how the composition of negative data affects the performance and generalization of antibody–antigen binding predictors in supervised binary classification. Using the Absolut!27 framework, we simulated the binding of a large set of CDRH3 sequences to ten antigens. For each antigen, we defined four classification tasks (Table 1 and Fig. 2a), all sharing the same positive data (top 1% affinity sequences) but having a different type of negative:

1. ‘vs non-binder’ (bottom 95%)
2. ‘vs weak’ (1%–5% range)
3. ‘vs 1’ (top 1% binders to another antigen)
4. ‘vs 9’ (top 1% binders to any of the nine other antigens).

Each dataset contained 20,000 positive and 20,000 negative sequences, with 10,000 of each held out for testing. ML tasks were named according to the antigen and dataset type.

We trained a simple fully connected neural network with ten hidden neurons (SN10) to allow explainability. To ensure robustness, we repeated training with multiple random train–test splits and weight initializations, and monitored performance on the validation set to avoid overfitting (Supplementary Fig. 3f). This architecture and procedure were used by default for all analyses involving the SN10 model. We also compared SN10 with logistic regression (LR) and transformer models (Supplementary Fig. 3c,e and Supplementary Table 4), which showed no performance advantage, consistent with previous findings in similar settings27.

Finally, we replicated the in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) performance analyses using an experimental dataset of 24,790 CDRH3 sequences labelled with binding affinity to the antigen HER2 previously published in ref. 49, which mirrors the structure of the synthetic data.

### ID prediction accuracy is a function of training dataset composition

To assess the models’ ability to learn generalizable rules, we evaluated their performance on ID testing sets that matched the training data distribution (Fig. 2a). In this setting, models achieved high median accuracies across all tasks (>0.85), with a clear performance trend: highest on ‘vs non-binder’ (range: 0.97–1.00; median: 0.99), followed by ‘vs 1’ (0.94–1.00; median: 0.98) and ‘vs 9’ (0.91–0.98; median: 0.94), and lowest on ‘vs weak’ (0.85–0.98; median: 0.92; Fig. 2b), identifying ‘vs weak’ as the most challenging task.

### Negative data influence rule learning

We found that the composition of the negative dataset influenced the model’s ability to learn binding rules at both the sequence and residue levels (Fig. 3). Correlations between model logits and antibody binding energy were the strongest in the ‘vs weak’ and ‘vs non-binder’ tasks, as were correlations between per-residue energy contributions and model attributions. In contrast, rule learning varied more in ‘vs 1’ and ‘vs 9’, where distorted attributions suggested interference from confounding negatives (Fig. 3d). This suggests that although positive data alone may suffice for learning, negative data can either support or impair rule discovery.

A fundamental challenge is to fully decipher the information captured by model attributions. We did not observe attribution–energy correlations beyond −0.6 to −0.7, probably due to limitations in attribution methods, incomplete energy modelling (for example, CDRH3 only) or the intrinsic complexity of residue-level effects. Interestingly, attributions obtained with DeepLIFT exhibited varying degrees of correlation with LR weights (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Attributions from the ‘vs non-binder’ task correlated more strongly with LR weights, whereas those from the ‘vs weak’ task showed mixed correlations across antigens. This suggests that DeepLIFT captures some non-additive rules but lacks the explicit interpretability of feature interactions. Further complexity emerged when analysing epitope-based datasets, where positives bind a specific epitope rather than an entire antigen (Supplementary Text 2 and Supplementary Fig. 9). While these improved rule discovery at the logit level, they showed no consistent advantage in attribution-based analysis. Thus, although binding rules can be learned at both levels, per-residue rules remain more difficult to disentangle.

### ML rule learning and prediction performance

We found no clear dependence between rule learning and high prediction performance. For instance, models trained on ‘vs 9’ and ‘vs 1’ generally achieve higher ID performance than those trained on ‘vs weak’, despite the latter learning binding rules more effectively across all antigens. This presents a counterexample to a hypothesis from a TCR field study suggesting that models with higher performance are more likely to rely on biological rules37. Our findings suggest that while ID performance may be necessary for rule discovery, it is not sufficient on its own. Instead, a model’s ability to learn detailed binding information represents an additional dimension of its overall performance.

A slight refinement to this interpretation is that models with higher OOD performance are more likely to rely on robust biological rules. Despite our results showing that models trained on ‘vs non-binder’ and ‘vs weak’ exhibit similar levels of binding rule learning, OOD performance is the lowest for ‘vs non-binder’ and the highest for ‘vs weak’ among all the tasks (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3d). This discrepancy arises from differences in how each task captures feature interactions; as discussed before, the ‘vs weak’ task permits a greater consideration of interactions between amino acid contributions. We suggest that OOD performance should serve as an orthogonal metric for benchmarking rule recovery methods, where a successful rule set and model interpretability approach will capture OOD robustness.

## Synthetic antibody–antigen binding data enable a detailed study of the impact of training dataset composition on ML performance

Synthetic data enabled us to systematically investigate how the composition of the training dataset affects ML performance, particularly in tasks constructed based on affinity. Single-sequence affinity-annotated experimental datasets, as used in this work, have only started to become available49,58. The available experimental dataset size is one to two orders of magnitude lower (our synthetic dataset has ~700k sequences compared with ~25k for experimental), and the synthetic dataset covers more antigens.

Absolut! was previously shown to guide ML development on experimental data27 and can be viewed as a lower bound for the complexity of antibody–antigen datasets. It retains biological relevance by preserving the surface amino acid composition of real proteins and explores multiple structural conformations of CDRH3, thereby incorporating nonlinearity into sequence-to-affinity correspondence. The used CDRH3 data are derived from the murine repertoire and exhibit a physiological amino acid composition and positional dependencies.

Furthermore, synthetic data have the advantage of avoiding confirmation bias in explainability studies41,59,60 as ground truth is available. Indeed, data are distributed over an array of features, some relevant and others irrelevant for a given prediction task. These features may be structural properties, affinity, k-mer distributions, amino acid composition and CDRH3 length. It is generally not known in advance which features are irrelevant and, thus, which should be balanced in the training data (both positive and negative). Once identified, the irrelevant features could be balanced between the positive and negative datasets to prevent the model from learning spurious correlations. As the first step towards this goal, we explored the impact of negative class on positive data learning.

## ML considerations

In this study, we focused on classification tasks that reflect the most common immune receptor datasets, which are typically binary or semi-quantitative22,61,62 (that is, divided into categories based on binding strength). Moreover, the negatives in the ‘vs 1’ and ‘vs 9’ datasets are often not annotated by affinity to a positive class target12,29,30. We, therefore, binarized the continuous affinity values to create semi-quantitative categories and assessed whether these were sufficient for learning binding rules such as affinity properties. However, when affinity-annotated data are available, it may be feasible to take a two-step approach: initial classification followed by fine tuning on continuous labels63,64.

Within the classification framework, we evaluated several ML models. We focused on SN10 due to its straightforward explainability and prior knowledge of its performance67. We also tested a transformer-based model and models based on protein language model embeddings, but these did not improve performance. SN10, as a neural network, can capture interpositional dependencies, which is illustrated by improved performance over LR (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). Thus, for our tasks, SN10 offered a practical balance of model complexity and explainability.

Future work could explore how rule learning is influenced by class balance and the type of negative class simultaneously, by input representation (sequence versus structure), and by model architecture. Recent studies65,66 suggest that simpler models often capture binding interactions, whereas more complex models tend to reflect broader biophysical properties, such as protein folding.

## Conclusion

When constructing negative data, two main practical approaches exist: (1) subsampling existing non-specific (negative) data and (2) generating negative data experimentally. On the basis of our findings, we recommend using methods14,67 that select ‘hard’ or ‘semi-hard’ negatives from unlabelled data when applying the subsampling strategy. However, our work primarily advances the second approach—constructing negative data when there is control over the data generation process. In such cases, we recommend collecting categorical or continuous data that are close in terms of the metric of interest (for example, binding affinity) to create a more challenging training set. In this scenario, positive and negative data originate from the same experiment and the same sequence distribution.
