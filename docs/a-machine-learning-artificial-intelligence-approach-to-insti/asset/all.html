Author Biography

John Moye is a native of Jacksonville, FL, where he attended Jacksonville University. During his undergraduate and master’s degree experience, he studied with a series of forward-looking thought leaders in education from which he developed an interest and belief in the science of learning and the power and importance of education for all learners. These interests have accompanied him throughout his career and led to a focus on the performance, effectiveness, and responsibilities of higher education.

Dr Moye continued his pursuit of the science of learning through his Ph.D. studies at Florida State University, where he focused his research in the field of psychophysics. Heavily impacted by the burgeoning field of neuroscience, he examined the response of the human perceptual systems to sensory stimuli as a model for understanding learning as a psychophysical process in individuals and organizations. The conceptual frameworks contained in this text are based on the evidence of the psychophysics of learning that are still emerging in the academic learning literature.

Dr. Moye has held effectiveness positions with numerous institutions of higher learning in the United States, including Saint Mary’s University of Minnesota, Capella University, and De Paul University, Chicago, IL, in which he has researched, developed, and applied these approaches to the development of relevant, innovative, and effective learning environments. In addition, he has contributed to a wide array of other institutions of higher learning as a consultant, which has provided a comprehensive perspective on the science of measurement and assessment in complex organizations.

Dr Moye believes the leverage point in the system of institutional improvement to be the availability of authentic, credible, and trustworthy information to make sense of institutional performance for effectiveness improvement efforts. The research and development of systematic assessments to measure the effectiveness of unique institutions is a focus of his ongoing professional efforts.

 

Foreword

This work seeks to catalyze discussion and thinking about the information required to measure, assess, and make sense of institutional performance with credible and trustworthy data. To those who believe it is possible to improve the performance of our institutions this work offers a method to improve service to students and society through data-informed problem-solving and decision-making.

To achieve this outcome requires data that objectively describe the “actual” performance of the institution, which the faculty and staff use to understand current performance and improve the future performance of their programs and institutions (Tadesse, Manathynga, & Gillies, 2018). The result is a system in which the principles of machine learning define the data processing functions and create a credible and trustworthy artificial intelligence for institutional effectiveness (Yousef, Allmer, Baştanlar, Özuysal, & Walker, 2013). The purpose of this work is to offer a fully aligned system of authentic assessments, which provide faculty and staff with credible and trustworthy information to monitor, demonstrate, and enhance institutional performance (Swaggerty & Broemmel, 2017).

The processes and procedures in this work adapt recent and current strategies of performance measurement, assessment, and sensemaking in the discipline of organizational effectiveness into a science-based approach to the assessment and sensemaking of institutional effectiveness in higher education (Cameron & Whetten, 2013). The principles of organizational assessment and the sciences of educational and psychological measurement and assessment define the content and structure of the information collected in this system (Knight, McLaughlin, & Howard, 2012). As such, this approach is a “best science” approach to institutional assessment and effectiveness. In this work, the goal is to present a fully-aligned system of assessments for institutional effectiveness, which are disciplined by appropriate technologies.

The methods and instruments employed in this assessment system emerged from research, design, development, and testing the results of their use as institutional effectiveness assessments for a cross-section of higher education institutions. These instruments have consistently yielded stable, statistically powerful, credible, and trustworthy data about the performance of the institution. These data inform authentic assessments, data modeling, and sensemaking functions to evaluate the effectiveness of the institution (Swaggerty & Broemmel, 2017).

The principles of machine learning and artificial intelligence frame the data modeling and sensemaking strategies to visualize actual institutional performance from multiple perspectives. The output of this approach is a system that provides credible, trustworthy, and meaningful data for the evaluation of effectiveness, which the human intelligence in the institution evaluates.

Chapter 1

Defining, Measuring, and Assessing Effectiveness

Overview

The purpose of this discussion is to articulate the assumptions and clarify the terminology used to design a systematic assessment method to measure (monitor), assess (demonstrate), and make sense of institutional effectiveness. This approach synthesizes the sciences of assessment and related concepts into the design and development of each part of a practical and powerful assessment system, which produces authentic, reliable, and valid data for institutional sensemaking.

Extended empirical discussions of assessment theory and practice are beyond the scope of this work and only employed to create an understanding of the assumptions that underpin the design decisions. The Reference List of this work includes an extensive collection of research, which the reader may consult to pursue these theories and the research that underpins these design decisions in greater detail.

Defining Effectiveness Versus Ineffectiveness

In organizational terms, an effective process produces the intended result (Daft, 2006; Harrison & Shirom, 1999). Institutional effectiveness is the ability of the institution to do what it says it does, in the way it says it does it. Effectiveness includes the assessment of both products (what) and processes (how) to understand the performance of an institution.

A process or procedure that produces some outcome other than the intended outcome is ineffective. In organizational terms, ineffectiveness is a lack of effectiveness. In other words, the system is producing a different outcome than desired. From a systems perspective, a system always produces the result its design produces, and human interventions have negligible impact. Converting an ineffective system into an effective system needs a change to the design of the system. Hence, this work focuses on measuring and improving the design of the institutional systems instead of people.

Assessing Effectiveness

In the model of systematic assessment under discussion, institutional effectiveness is the synergistic interaction of all functional processes to produce an outcome that is greater than the sum of the functional processes. To evaluate effectiveness, the measurements (data) of functional performance are collected, analyzed, and modeled to show these functional interactions. However, individual measurement data are insufficient to understand institutional effectiveness (Badia, 2014; Glidden, 1996; Macfadyen & Dawson, 2012).

The credible assessment of institutional effectiveness measures the performance of each function as defined by the mission, vision, and values (MVVs) of the institution (Camelia & Marius, 2013; Mouritsen, 1986). These organizational statements define the intended outcomes of each functional system and the measurements collected describe the degree to which the process is delivering its intended outcome, which is the traditional definition of effectiveness: the system produces the intended outcome. The data collected to measure the performance of each function describe the interactions between the stakeholders and their interactions with the functional system (Sullivan & Wilds, 2001).

Authentic interactional data exist in the environment in which they occur and not a laboratory, which controls for threats to trustworthiness and credibility. These data contain measurement error and imprecision that the measurement, assessment, and sensemaking processes in this systematic process respect (Bowman, Kibria & Banik, 2013).

Terminology

Assessment is a well-defined and adaptable group of sciences codified through many years of application and testing (Banta, 2009). These sciences articulate the methods adapted in this work to design, develop, and administer authentic measurement instruments, summarize, and aggregate those data into meaningful assessments of functional performance, and model those same data into tools to facilitate sensemaking of institutional effectiveness. However, the adaptability and multiple applications of research methods have created an inconsistent use of terminology by assessment scientists as well as practitioners.

It is the responsibility of each assessment designer to articulate the terminology in use and to employ that terminology consistently to communicate with diverse populations and engage the diversity of those populations into the creation of a synergistic result. The discussions in this work follow the definitions and explanations articulated below without suggesting that this usage is the “correct” definition.

Measurement

For purposes of these discussions, measurement refers to the process of collecting data from multiple individual sources to quantify functional performance. Measurement data describe the performance between institutional processes and procedures (functions) and the interactions with the users of the system. They measure the performance of institutional functions with individuals (Harrison, 2015).

The size of the correlations in the resulting individual data reveals the key performance indicators (KPI) (Badawy, El-Aziz, Idress, Hefny, & Hossam, 2016; Chan, 2015). It is critical that these measurements produce credible and trustworthy data, which objectively describe the performance of each function with each user and enable the modeling of institutional effectiveness from these same data. These data describe the intended performance framed by the intentions articulated in the MVVs.

Assessment

Assessments summarize the individual responses for the functional performance indicator they measure. These performance indicator summaries aggregate the data into the constructs that they define. This aggregation changes the focus of the indicators from the measurement of individual performance to the demonstration of the performance of the function.

The process of assessment aggregates multiple measures for each indicator into the constructs of the assessment, which demonstrate the performance of the function. The assessment function is another descriptive process meant to present a credible and trustworthy representation of performance.

Analysis

Analysis is the process of examining data to confirm the mathematical structure and relationships within it. In this work, analysis always refers to discovering the emergent “qualities” of the data through mathematical or statistical methods, rather than inferring the quality of the data. These methods reveal the underlying interdependencies in the data, which create practical models of actual performance (Tadesse, Manathynga, & Gillies, 2018). Actual performance describes what is occurring in the system without comparison with intentions or other assumptions (machine learning).

Evaluation

Evaluation is a process of interpreting the data in the assessments to understand the relationship between intended and actual performance. In this application, the comparison determines the degree to which each function is performing as intended. In institutional research, the lack of precision in the data dictates the use of descriptive methods instead of inferential methods (Heinze, Shapira, Rogers, & Senker, 2009; Knight, McLaughlin, & Howard, 2012). In this system of assessments, the evaluation phase of the process is objective (observational) and descriptive. It describes the actual performance of the functions within an institution.

Sensemaking

Institutional sensemaking facilitates “looking beyond the assessment data” to quantify the interactions (synergies) between the functions, which generate institutional effectiveness (Birnbau, 1991; Macfadyen & Dawson, 2012). Teresa Amabile (1998) has described this intellectual process as “scanning the perceptual space” between and beyond the data. In institutional effectiveness research, sensemaking is a collaborative process that integrates a diversity of perspectives to generate innovative and plausible interpretations (Gokhale, 1995; Perry, 1999). The differences and disagreements that emerge from the amalgamation of these divergent perspectives drive the sensemaking process into new and innovative directions (Binswanger & Oechslin, 2015; Snehal & Kremer 2010; Woerkom & Sanders, 2010; Young, Alibali, & Kalish, 2012). By engaging and integrating the value of difference into institutional decision making, the sensemaking process creates a foundation for a culture of improvement and effectiveness (Drouin, Stewart, & Van Gorder, 2015; Goldman, 1999).

According to Weick (1995), the intellectual process of sensemaking in organizations has seven procedural characteristics. These characteristics are:

  1. Grounded in Identity Construction – accomplished in this method by using the shared MVVs of the organization to collect and organize the data.

  2. Retrospective – accomplished in this model by focusing on the objective measurement of real events that have occurred in the natural environment.

  3. Enactment of sensible environments – accomplished in this method by assuming a connection between the conditions experienced and the outcomes of the participants, such as assuming the causal relationships between the components of the instructional system and student performance.

  4. Social – the realization that sensemaking needs the collective intelligence of the organization to make sense of the data. The data give clues and hints, which the creative intelligence of the leaders synthesizes into a complete picture of performance and effectiveness (Fourie-Malherbe, 2015; Leithwood & Mascall, 2008).

  5. Ongoing – the embedding and acculturalization of sensemaking into continuous activities and operations, accomplished in this method by the design of the systematic approach to assessment which monitors performance with credible and trustworthy data.

  6. Focused on and by extracted clues – accomplished in this method by clustering the key performance indicators into models of actual performance and using those models as clues or hints (indicators) of institutional effectiveness.

  7. Driven by plausibility rather than precision – accomplished in this model through the conceptualization and treatment of institutional data as indicators instead of data with absolute precision. That is, respecting the attributes of the data as they “are” rather than assigning or assuming attributes they do not demonstrate (i.e., parametric vs. non-parametric data). The search for plausibility is a distinguishing characteristic of this assessment process and one that distinguishes practical research from empirical research.

Collectively, the sensemaking activities create a systematic process for evaluating the effectiveness of institutional performance. The data from the measurements monitor performance at the individual level, the assessments aggregate those data into constructs to demonstrate performance at the program or functional level, and the sensemaking process uses the data to evaluate performance at the institutional level, all within the limits of confidence (power) contained in the data.

Valid Data – Credibility

The term validity applies to the collected data and not the instrument. The content of the data demonstrates its validity, which is identical to the definition of the function to be valid. Data that are valid to one situation are only valid in another situation if both situations are identical, not similar.

The discussions in this work focus on design validity, which demonstrate construct (concepts) and content (processes) validity through the design assumptions for each instrument. Validity, however, is an attribute of the collected data, and not the instrument. Since institutional data contain copious amounts of variance and error, the term that describes valid data in this work is credibility, which assumes the data to be more qualitative than quantitative and respects its lack of precision.

Reliable Data – Trustworthiness

It is the structure of the instrument that generates the trustworthiness of the collected data as confirmed (calculated) in the collected data. Trustworthiness refers to the degree to which the data reveal the meaning that the respondents intended. For these purposes, a statistical power test measures the trustworthiness of the collected data (Fosnacht, Sarraf, Howe, & Peck, 2017). The power test, discussed in detail later in this chapter, directly measures the attributes of the collected data to determine its trustworthiness.

Authentic Data

Another characteristic of the data that is important to institutional assessment is the authenticity of the data. Authenticity describes data collected from an activity that aligns with the performance objective. If the objective is to demonstrate knowledge, then the data measure knowledge. If it demonstrates performance, then the data measure performance. For institutional research, the “best evidence” of performance is direct measurements of authentic activities. A distinguishing characteristic of this work is the attention to authenticity in the data collection phase.

Functional Data

Functional data are those data that affect and influence the performance of a specific institutional function. In the case of higher education, the functional data measure the components of teaching, learning, and support services. They measure the results of the functional system’s interaction with each student or stakeholder.

Institutional Assessment of Effectiveness

Educational Institutions are complex organizations that employ complex functions to serve the needs of stakeholders living and performing in the twenty-first century. There is little that is simple or dualistic about the MVVs of higher education. The assessment and evaluation of institutional effectiveness need systems of assessment that reflect and accommodate this complexity and employ methods that study complex phenomena. Such systems use multiple scales to measure the dimensions of functional performance and aggregate those measures into the constructs of performance as defined in the MVVs of the institution.

There are at least two broad models of information needed to assess institutional effectiveness comprehensively: political models and performance-based models. A political model of effectiveness measures the construct of satisfaction. It asks stakeholders to decide whether they are satisfied with the performance of the organization based on their opinions of satisfaction. Political models appropriately measure some of the more personal functions in higher education that are too complex to parse into practical assessments (Charteris-Black, 2005; Charteris-Black & Palgrave Connect, 2011). Basing the evaluation of the effectiveness of the entire organization on a political model does not give a complete demonstration of institutional effectiveness and may assess the characteristics of the students more than the institution.

A performance-based model of effectiveness collects objective measurements of the performance of institutional functions and compiles them into the constructs of functional performance to show institutional effectiveness. These data reveal the drivers and constraints within the functional data to describe institutional performance.

These models conceive the institution as a complex, multidimensional phenomenon, created by the synergistic interaction of the parts of the institution, its functions. This work assumes the performance of the institution to be a quantum or synergistic result of the interactions of those parts. Therefore, single performance indicators do not fully demonstrate institutional performance, and it is necessary to “look” beyond the performance indicators to make sense of institutional effectiveness (Bazeley, 2015; Bedeian, 2015). In this application, the sensemaking phase enables looking beyond the data for meaning (Sullivan & Wilds, 2001).

Machine Learning – Artificial Intelligence

Machine learning (ML) is the use of algorithms to model data to reveal the “actual” performance of a complex system (Alpaydin, 2014; Burger, 2018). ML uses algorithms to define the attributes of data and build relevant performance models, which describe current performance and predict the effects of changes on future performance (George, Osinga, Lavie, & Scott, 2016; Hastie, Tibshirani, & Friedman, 2009). The measurement of the internal interactions or processes within the system (machine) constitutes the ML data (Domingos, 2015). This approach creates the ability to infer causation between the data points and predict the results of changes to the system, a process known as predictive analytics (White & Breckenridge, 2014; Witten, 2017).

This systematic approach to institutional effectiveness applies the principles of ML to design the measurement, assessment, and data modeling processes (Alpaydin, 2014). The following ML strategies are employed to construct this systematic assessment.

Authentic Data

The data collected in an ML process authentically measure the performance of the system. In an institutional effectiveness (IE) system, this is accomplished by measuring the characteristics of the individual interactions between the system and the user and modeling those data for evaluation (Mohammed, Khan, & Bashier, 2017).

Algorithms as Measurement Strategies

The measurements of the individual interactions are framed by definitions of performance, which are extracted from theories of organizational effectiveness and adapted to institutional systems. These algorithms define and frame the interactions between the machine (institutional system) and the human being (user, constituent) to measure performance (Domingos, 2015; MacKay, 2003).

Internal Data

To reveal the performance of a system, the data describe the performance of the system. Data external to and outside the control of the system are not valid measures of system performance in an ML environment and interfere with the ability of the ML process to describe actual performance through a coherent systematic assessment process.

Acyclical Data

The measurement of the performance of an institution aggregates data from multiple performance cycles (sessions, semesters, quarters, years) to comprehensively understand the performance of the institution over time. Acyclical data increase the statistical power of the data and allow outliers and other anomalous data to emerge above the threshold of discrimination (Lerma, 2012; Liu, Yue, & Li, 2011; Wanner, Hajicová, Gerdes, & IOS Press, 2013).

Classification

In ML and statistics, classification is the process of categorizing or grouping data into interconnected subsets. In other words, all data that impact a function are grouped into a description of that function, even if they may be applied to more than one function (Medler, 1998). These classifications result from the summarization of the data (Anitha & Deisy, 2015; Balyan, McCarthy, & McNamara, 2018; Medler, 1998; Salles et al., 2017).

Statistical Modeling

The data summarization, aggregation, and modeling strategies employ valid statistical methods that are appropriate to the attributes of the data to analyze and model the data. An ML process aggregates, describes, and organizes the raw measurement data into assessments that construct institutional performance (Hastie et al., 2009; Yan, Wang, & Lu, 2018).

Dimension Reduction and Modeling

Dimension reduction refers to the process of reducing the number of actions under evaluation by calculating the statistical strength of each interaction contained in the raw data. This process organizes the data into a smaller collection of data points to enhance human information processing limits (Goodfellow, McDaniel, & Papernot, 2018; Saraswati, Nguyen, Hagenbuchner, & Tsoi, 2018; Tangkaratt, Morimoto, & Sugiyama, 2016).

Association Learning

Association learning is an ML method for discovering interesting relations between variables in large databases. It reveals strong rules (relationships) that exist in the data using measures of interestingness (causality). As the data set grows, new rules emerge from the increased power within the data (acyclical), which reveal hidden “interests” (Piatetsky-Shapiro, 1991).

Outlier Detection

In ML, outlier (anomaly) detection is the process of identifying rare data, events, or observations, which differ significantly (interestingness) from the majority of the data (Piatetsky-Shapiro & Matheus, 1994). Typically, the anomalous items represent an attribute of the system that exists in a small sample of the population and, as a result, does not contain enough power to rise to the system level of performance (Angiulli, Ben-Eliyahu-Zohary, & Palopoli, 2014; Ha, Seok, & Lee, 2015; Yan, Wang, & Lu, 2018; Zimek & Schubert, 2017).

Structured Prediction

Structured prediction is an ML technique that involves predicting the structure of systems conceived as complex phenomena, as differentiated from discrete or single (linear) values. Structured prediction employs the interconnectedness in the data to determine the effects of a change in one data point on others (Du, 2017; Jiuqing, Xu, & Xianhang, 2017).

Artificial Intelligence

Organizing the ML data outputs into models that describe institutional performance as the human intelligence has defined it creates an artificial intelligence (AI) (Duda, Stork, & Hart, 2001; Gopnik, 2017; MacKay, 2003). There are many models of AI that create a comprehensive collection of flexible conceptual frameworks to model the attributes and interconnectedness of the data (Boden, 2015; Ngai, Tao, & Moon, 2015; Russell & Norvig, 2014). To visualize data as a series of interconnected and causal relationships (Marcus, 2001), AI often uses an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) to organize and display the data. The ANN accommodates multiple definitions of causality and interconnectedness to make sense of complex institutional data (Bishop, 1995; Chernoded, Dudko, Myagkov, & Volkov, 2017).

AI does not preclude or replace the human intelligence in the administration of organizations and institutions: it informs the human intelligence with objective information about institutional performance (Birnbaum, 1991), which is as accurate as possible and formatted to reveal the interconnectedness within the data. These interconnections represent the synergies that produce the phenomenon of institutional performance. AI is the input to a data-informed, evidence-based, sensemaking process, which includes all data, balanced by the size of the contribution to performance, as well as anomalous or outlier information. It makes no a priori judgments about the importance of any data.

The human intelligence evaluates the AI, determines the plausibility of its content, and decides how to use that information to advance the institution or improve performance. The AI provides the “hints” and “clues” of actual performance; it is the human intelligence that fills in the gaps between the “hints” and “clues” to make sense of the information. Like mathematics, AI is a representational language; the human intelligence decides what the AI represents. The strategies and techniques of this decision-making process are a component of a systematic assessment of IE (Birnbaum, 1991).

A Mixed Methods Approach to Institutional Research

The systematic assessment process presented in this work follows the structure of a mixed methods research approach (Cresswell, 2006, 2015; Cresswell & Poth, 2017; Tashakkori & Tedlie, 1998). The assumptions that underly this approach to research and decision making are consistent with the attributes of the data and the goals of institutional effectiveness assessment. Mixed methods research designs are infinitely flexible due to their multidimensional, non-linear design assumptions, and the incorporation of both qualitative and quantitative data into credible research design and sensemaking process (Galliott & Graham, 2016). This flexibility uncovers the synergies of institutional performance from the interrelationships between multidimensional functional performance indicators (Vinyard, Mullally, & Colvin, 2017).

Data collected from and in the natural environment give authentic best evidence of the effect of a system on the human experience. Naturalistic data collect the opinions, perceptions, or judgments of “an observable event” or experiences from the perspective of the individual. The resulting variance or error in measurement results from the differences in the way that humans conceive of and label their experience (Bowman & Herzo, 2011). The variance reveals perspectives on functional and institutional performance that might otherwise escape notice (Cameron, 1978, 1986; Kale, 2015; Mikelsone & Liela, 2016). In the study of institutional effectiveness, these “outlier” data may have clues or hints of some innovative and dynamic discoveries (Sokolova & Krishna, 2016).

The mixed methods approach employs a “constructivist/phenomenological” process to analyze research data, which “constructs” meaning from multiple data sources. This constructed approach accommodates the vagaries and complexities (clues and hints) of “human dynamic adaptive” systems data (phenomena), which hold powerful information about functional performance and institutional effectiveness. Data collected from and about these systems data contain copious amounts of variance making them imprecise measures of events (Museus & Griffin, 2011).

In organizational research, these measures are “indicators” of performance to reflect this imprecision. This imprecision precludes the use of statistical measures to analyze the data. Therefore, institutional research uses a sensemaking approach to find plausible meaning in the data instead of certainty. The sensemaking process is consistent with the constructivist/phenomenological approach of the mixed methods research method.

Assessment methods in institutional research often seek to limit variance through attempts to dismiss or eliminate variance in the data (i.e., halo effect) and ignore the data that contain the unique information, for example, “outliers.” If all the responses in the data are identical, there is 0 variance in the data. While this result may be self-affirming, it produces data that have no statistical power or trustworthy meaning. Variance (average difference) in the aggregated data is a critical factor in the generation of statistical power, which shows the trustworthiness of the data for sensemaking. In this system of assessment, the goal is to generate data that have enough variance to produce acceptable statistical power in the collected data. This strategy is primarily a function of the design of the measurement instruments and the structure of the questions asked, but it is also a function of the research design and its ability to accommodate multidimensional data.

A Systematic Approach to Effectiveness

The mixed methods approach organizes the research process into a system of measurements and assessments that give the data necessary for institutional sensemaking (Museus & Griffin, 2011). The use of local measurements aggregated into assessments and clustered by the institutional definitions creates a coherent system of measurements and assessments of functional performance, which aligns the data with the content of the institutional MVVs (Banta, 2009; Esi, 2013). This alignment creates a conceptual framework between the local measurements, the functional assessments, and the global sensemaking.

An overview of the phases in a systematic assessment approach helps conceive the total system and the processes that create it. These definitions synthesize the work of Popham (1990, 2011), Daft (2006), and Harrison and Shirom (1999), as well as the citations included for specific items. The phases in the process are:

  1. Definition. Define the conceptual framework for the institution and all functions within it. The MVVs’ statements articulate this institutional definition (Bart & Baetz, 1998; Bart, Bontis, & Taggar, 2001; Mouritsen, 1986).

  2. Measure. Design instruments to collect data that measure the performance of the functions as defined by the MVVs of the institution as translated into the performance of each functional area. In this phase, the data describe the performance of each function with individual stakeholders (Popham, 1990, 2011).

  3. Assess. Aggregate the collected data into assessments of each function, which describe the performance of the function with data obtained from its interactions with users (Cook, Tone, & Zhu, 2014; Cook & Zhu, 2014).

  4. Analyze. Using the raw functional data, calculate relationships contained within the data. Analysis is an objective process driven by the need to understand the structure and “metadata” contained within the data (Maté, Trujillo, & Mylopoulos, 2016; Peral, Maté, & Marco, 2017).

  5. Functional Models. Cluster the strongest KPIs into a data model based on the interdependencies in the data, which represent the synergies between the functions and generates institutional effectiveness (Drouin, Stewart, & Van Gorder, 2015).

  6. Institutional Models. Reorganize the KPIs into data models differentiated by the approaches to organizational effectiveness (Cameron, 1978, 1986; Cameron & Whetten, 2013), which translates the functional data into a visual representation of institutional effectiveness. These effectiveness approaches reveal the performance attributes of each function and organize the KPIs into authentic models of institutional effectiveness (Daft, 2006; Harmon, Kowalski, & Kowalski, 2018; Harrison & Shirom, 1999).

  7. Sensemaking. Evaluate the effectiveness models with the collective intelligence of the organization to develop a comprehensive understanding of institutional performance (Birnbaum, 1991; Macfadyen & Dawson, 2012; Sullivan & Wilds, 2001; Weick, 1995).

  8. Use Organizational Learning for Improvement. Create action plans for the institution using a data-informed strategic planning process, which feeds back the conclusions of the sensemaking process into the design or redesign of the functions (Junqueira, Dutra, Filho, & Gonzaga, 2016; Lunenburg, 2010).

Parts 1 and 2 involve the effort of the academic faculty and staff. The institutional research staff performs parts 3–5. Parts 6 and 7 engage the entire community in the process of evaluation and action planning. The total process lends itself well to automation.

Diversity of Thinking

It is crucial to the dynamics of this process to point out the importance of diversity, differences, and disagreement with the outcomes of the sensemaking process. The ability to engage diverse thinking in the sensemaking processes is a critical leadership skill in the evaluation and sensemaking of assessment data (Altaf, 2011; Binswanger & Oechslin, 2015; Woerkom & Sanders, 2010) as well as to create a culture of performance and improvement (Kataria, Rastogi, & Garg, 2013). Diverse thinking generates differences in understanding, which generate new, dynamic, and unexpected learning (Perry,1999). The recognition and integration of difference into an affirmative (inclusive) evaluation of the available information is critical to the effectiveness of the assessment process. This systematic assessment design embeds the power of difference (variance) into the sensemaking of institutional effectiveness (Uebersax, 1987).

Power and Sample Size

As mentioned, it is the responsibility of an assessment designer to demonstrate the credibility and trustworthiness of the collected data in an assessment process. While many statistical procedures measure these attributes of instruments the Statistical Power Test generates direct evidence of the trustworthiness of the data collected for institutional assessment. The statistical power test is a four-factor calculation, which is easy to execute and interpret with current statistical software packages. The test provides researchers with the information to show the trustworthiness of the information.

In general, institutional research does not sample subjects due to the ethical concerns the process engenders. Therefore, the inferential generalizability of research findings to a larger population, which needs a random sample of subjects, is not possible for most institutional research. The use of samples, which excludes the input of some students, is outside the ethical standards of institutional and educational research (American Educational Research Association, 2014; Fuller, 2017). However, the process that calculates a sample size also calculates the statistical power contained in a set of collected data and “measures” the data’s trustworthiness for decision making. For this purpose, the critical consideration is not the sample size or its characteristics (response rate), but the power in the collected data revealed by the power test.

The statistical power test analyzes the results of a statistical calculation (in this application it is a correlation coefficient) to determine the reliability of the data to support decision making. Two errors occur in the decision-making (hypothesis testing) process: a Type I error and a Type II error. A Type I error is reporting a positive result when it is negative: a false-positive error. A Type II error is reporting a negative result when it is positive; a false-negative error. The statistical power test measures the likelihood of both errors in a set of data, which are always present in all research analyses. In this system of assessment, the researcher uses the results of the power test to show the trustworthiness of the data to support sensemaking and the search for plausibility.

Jacob Cohen (2013) codified the study of statistical power and sample size. Throughout many years, Cohen defined and refined a systematic process for calculating the power in the data to help researchers understand the limits of the interpretation of their research results. He found that power is a function of four attributes of the data:

  1. Significance level

  2. Statistical power

  3. Effect size (variance)

  4. Sample size

Significance level (α or alpha) refers to the likelihood that the results occurred by chance. For educational research, it is usually 0.1 (10% possibility of chance) or 0.05 (5% possibility of chance). The significance level is less important for institutional data than the statistical power.

Statistical power is the measure of the data to support conclusions drawn from it accurately. Calculated power levels between 0.80 and 1.00 are strong. Less than 0.8 and there are too few data to trust meaning without a strong possibility of a Type I error in decision making: a false-positive error. Larger than 1.00 and there are too many data, and the possibility of a Type II error is strong (Cohen, 2013).

Effect size is the average difference (disagreement, variance) in the responses. If all responses are identical, there is no variance or 0 effect size. If the number of responses varies for each category of response, there is maximum effect size. As effect size increases, the necessary sample size decreases.

In this systematic assessment process, the driver for the design decisions is to create the ability for the respondents to discriminate levels of performance based on their experience and to report it in an equivalently scaled manner. The role of effect size quantifies the diverse responses, which include all data, especially outliers. The variance in institutional data has essential information (meta-information, differences, disagreements) for the assessment of institutional effectiveness (Binswanger & Oechslin, 2015; Snehal & Kremer, 2010; Woerkom & Sanders, 2010; Young, Alibali, & Kalish, 2012). If an indicator has little or no variance, it gives little trustworthy information for sensemaking.

The sample size is the number of cases (responses) in the data. In general, larger sample sizes are more useful, if they are random, have enough variance, and are valid measurements of the indicator. A non-random sample only gives information about the sample, which is also the population and not a sample. As noted earlier, sampling in institutional research is problematic and used only in situations where it does not limit the input of students into their experience (Kalkan & Kelecioglu, 2016).

The name “statistical power test” is something of a misnomer. If any three of these variables are known or predicted, this procedure calculates the fourth variable. In other words, the statistical power test is also a test of effect size, significance level, or sample size, which is the most common application (Kalkan & Kelecioglu, 2016). For instance, the researcher assumes the significance level, the desired power, and effect size; the power test calculates the approximate sample size needed to produce trustworthy data in planning a research project. After the data collection process is complete, the sample size (population) is known, the effect size calculated from the statistical analysis of the data, the desired significance level is known, and the power test calculates the statistical power of the data. A power of 0.8–1.0 shows that there is enough power in the data to support conclusions. A researcher performs a power test for each indicator in a data set as the variance, and therefore power varies in the responses to each question.

Creating Shared Mission, Vision, and Values

Mission, Vision, and Values

To monitor, demonstrate, and enhance the effectiveness of an institution a conceptual framework is necessary to design measurements, compile assessments, and make sense of the collected data to institutional performance. The conceptual framework articulates the intentions, strategies, and reasons an institution exists, which are the constructs and content of institutional performance the assessments of effectiveness used to ensure the data are credible, trustworthy, and authentic. For most institutions, the institutionally shared statements of mission, vision, and values (MVV) articulate the institutional conceptual framework.

To be most effective, these concepts are communicated through well-crafted statements that express the high-level commitments, intentions, and aspirations of the institution to internal and external stakeholders as well as the community at large (Bart & Baetz, 1998; Bart et al., 2001; Mouritsen, 1986).

Organizationally, MVV statements frame institutional performance (Gurley, Peters, Collins, & Fifolt, 2015) by:

  • Creating a shared understanding of the purpose of the institution to align individual as well as institutional performance.

  • Creating stakeholder buy-in to the institution by ensuring that the institution reflects the interests and contributions of all stakeholders.

  • Focusing attention of the stakeholders on institutional performance.

From an effectiveness perspective, MVV statements articulate the conceptual framework (Mission statements Harrison & Shirom, 1999) that:

  • Validates data collected in the measurements process.

  • Defines the clusters of data in the assessment process.

  • Defines institutional effectiveness for the sensemaking process.

The definitions and organization of these statements vary in the management literature. For purposes of this work, the components of an institutional conceptual framework are defined as:

  • A statement of Mission that articulates “what” the organization intends to do.

  • A statement of Vision that identifies “how” the organization intends to accomplish its mission.

  • Values statements that explain “why” the organization performs this mission, its “reason for being.” While the mission and vision statements are practical and predictable, the values statements are aspirational.

Effective MVV statements are the outcome of a collaborative process, which integrates the collective thinking of all stakeholders into the development of the statements. The results of an environmental scan inform the collaborations to ensure that the institution is aware of and responsive to the external environment (Fisher et al., 2013). However, the statements represent the latent interests and commitments of the members of the institution to create shared outcomes, processes, and performance. MVV statements in some form are critical to the assessment of institutional effectiveness and, like a research question, clearly express the institution’s focus.

At the institutional level, MVV statements are broad enough to encompass the entire operation of the institution yet specific enough to communicate the intended outcomes. To serve this purpose well, the MVV statements are unambiguous. For this application, broadness and clarity are synergistic values.

A standard to model all kinds of organizational statements is known by the acronym SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, and Time-bound. The specifics of this acronym may vary when used by different authors, but the attributes they define are consistent and generate a useful checklist to design MVV statements. For these purposes, the attributes of effective MVV statements are:

  • Specific: the terminology gives a clear and unambiguous understanding of the concepts contained in the statement.

  • Measurable: the concept is directly measured in some meaningful way with reliable and valid data or credible and trustworthy information.

  • Achievable: the concepts are accomplished within the infrastructure of the institution (internally focused). Since educational institutions have limited resources, it is critical to ensure that all concepts are achievable within the resources and institutional capabilities.

  • Realistic: the concept is achievable within the contingencies of the environment in which the institution is situated (externally focused).

  • Time-bound: the processes in the statement are observable within a reasonable amount of time; usually one calendar cycle (calendar year, academic year, etc.).

This wording convention generates a reasonable opportunity for all stakeholders to operationalize the institutional constructs into their specific responsibilities and performance. From an institutional performance perspective, the MVV statements build the institutional metaphors that create the culture of the institution (Grant & Oswick, 1996; Rukh & Qadeer, 2018). In both instances, the MVV statements articulate the functional organization (design) of the institution.

Mission Statement

A mission statement describes the intended contribution the institution provides to the environment in which it is situated. Mission statements answer the question “what do we do?” for the community and larger society served. Organizationally, the mission statement identifies the outcomes the institution seeks to achieve. Operationally, the mission statement defines the institutional “product” (Camelia & Marius, 2013).

At the institutional level, the mission statement encompasses all efforts within the institution. The size of the institution and the number of functions within it determine how broad the statement is, but regardless of how broad it is, it must be SMART to communicate the concepts effectively to a diverse group of stakeholders. From a strategic perspective, the mission statement articulates both the focus and the limits (the drivers and constraints) of the institution’s performance (Bart & Baetz, 1998; Goldratt, 1990; Junqueira, Dutra, Filho, & Gonzaga, 2016).

Vision Statement

The vision statement delineates the processes that deliver the mission. While the mission statement articulates the outcomes, the vision statement outlines the internal processes or procedures to achieve those outcomes. Organizationally, the vision statement articulates the institutional behavior expected of the internal stakeholders in pursuit of its mission. Operationally, it articulates the climate and experience stakeholders expect when interacting with the institution.

The vision statement is broad enough to inform all processes within an institution and flexible enough to allow a diverse array of strategies to accomplish each outcome. The vision statement embeds the social commitments of an institution (diversity, ethics, etc.) into institutional performance. As the field of education develops complex behaviors, the vision statement is a place to embed those behaviors into institutional performance.

Values Statements

The values statements explain “why the institution does what it does.” Since most institutions have many complex values to which it is committed, the values statement is often a series of statements or a bulleted list of the high-level constructs the institution strives to achieve. The values statements articulate the institutional beliefs, which create the moral and ethical framework for the institution.

The values statements are comprehensive enough to include those ideas that are critical to the entirety of the institution. While the list of values may be extensive, the statement cannot become unfocused and ineffective. Therefore, it is best to have no more than five to seven concepts included in the statement at the institutional level, which are broad enough to encompass the activities of all functions (Alan, 1994; Linhares, Chada, & Aranha, 2011; Miller, 1956; Shiffrin & Nosofsky, 1994).

The contents of these three organizational statements are unique and specific to each institution to reflect the diversity of the people within each institution. One of the most effective techniques to create these statements is to begin by developing a list of the constructs to include, without necessarily defining them (Camelia, & Marius, 2013). Once that list is complete, then a statement may be crafted to include these constructs and articulate the intent of the stakeholders. Functional MVVs’ statements translate the performance of the institutional into functional performance. The functional statements articulate the way each function (department, service, program, etc.) contributes to the institutional MVVs. If the institutional statements are broad enough, each functional area parses the institutional statements into their functional statements. The importance of these statements to institutional performance cannot be overstated. For an organization to perform as intended, the people within the organization must understand what they contribute to the success of the institution and how their function expects to produce those contributions. The MVV statements provide individuals at all levels of the institution with the information they need to perform their tasks in alignment with the expectations of the institution (Lunenburg, 2010). Measuring the External Environment

The first component of a systematic assessment of institutional effectiveness is a scan of the external environment, which informs the process of defining the MVVs of the institution. The environmental scan identifies the needs, expectations, and desires of the external environment that influence the external perceptions of institutional success and the situation of the institution in the environment (Morrison, 1993). The purpose of the environmental scan is to develop an objective and realistic understanding of the external environment, which inform the definition of the MVVs and design of multiple assessments of institutional effectiveness (Christiansen & Lechman, 2016).

The external environment is a complex and dynamic social, political, and cultural milieu, which both drives and constrains all aspects of institutional design. The environmental scan informs a systematic analysis of the external environment for determining the drivers and constraints on institutional performance, which are generated by the contingencies of the external environment (Goldratt, 1990; Volberda, Weerdt, Verwaal, Stienstra, & Verdu, 2012).

In organization theory, an organization that has a strong and synergistic relationship with its environment is an “open systems” model (Daft, 2006). Open systems do not and cannot function effectively without a symbiotic relationship with and knowledge of the external environment. Successful organizations continually scan the environment to identify and anticipate external changes in the contingencies the institution must address if it is to survive and thrive. The environmental scan provides the information to maintain this awareness and inform institutional responsiveness.

Environmental scanning is a systematic measurement of the events, patterns, trends, and relationships that currently exist outside the institution and over which the institution has little control or influence, but impact its MVVs (Wissam, Harik, & Khoury, 2014). The information scan gathers environmental information from two sources: the external contingencies or expectations of the institution by society-at-large and the institutional strategies and efforts the institution employs to respond to those expectations. This response is either affirmative, through direct incorporation into strategy (if-then response), or adversely, through indirect or a differentiated strategy (if not, then what response). In a differentiated response, the contingency is acknowledged, and a strategy to respond to the contingency without agreeing with it, usually by leading or facilitating an environmental change (e.g., social justice).

The environmental scan for any institution begins with the development of a relevant framework to define an authentic measurement strategy, which demonstrates the credibility and trustworthiness of the data to the specifics of the institution. The approach presented in this assessment system analyzes the following environmental factors of institutional interaction, which become the constructs to measure the environmental conditions:

  • Social factors – include the status of and perceived need for higher education, the status of the higher education profession, the perceived need of higher education as a prerequisite for life success as articulated by society and other global contingencies (Johnson & Peifer, 2017).

  • Economic factors – include the cost of education, the return on investment based on salary expectations with or without the education, availability of financial aid, time to complete the program, etc. (Christiansen & Lechman, 2016; Douglas-Gabriel, 2018).

  • Employment/Profession factors – include the expectations of the professions and the ability of graduates to find relevant employment or continue the pursuit of education upon completion of a program of study (Parris & Saville, 2011).

  • Political factors – include the standing of the institution in its marketplace, the value of the education provided to the community as perceived by the community, the respect of the external environment toward the education and services provided to the community by the institution (Foxlee, 2014; James, 2015b).

  • Technological factors – include the delivery method, advanced instructional technologies, and other methods of interacting with institutional systems in a manner that is consistent with student sophistication and their changing expectations (Chambers & Poore, 1975).

  • Legal factors – include those factors that control the operations of the institution. All institutions have regulatory expectations to meet such as the Americans with Disabilities Act and the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, etc., and these expectations understood and embedded into institutional performance (Fuller, 2017).

This information reveals the global concerns faced by the institution. The information is a factor in the Program Viability and Feasibility assessment, which examines similar information specific to each program offered (James, 2015a, 2015b). The Program Viability and Feasibility assessment is discussed in the next chapter.

Measuring the External Environment

Data for each of these constructs are extracted from credible external sources of information, which is the “best evidence” of the external expectations. Often this is quantitative information; at other times it is qualitative (meaning “qualities,” not the judgment of “quality”). The purpose of this exercise is to provide an accurate and realistic analysis of the contingencies in the external environment as the external environment defines them.

For each of the constructs in the measurement, three to five contingencies are identified and reported through a concise description describing that specific contingency in general detail. The statements must authentically, credibly, and reliably describe the conditions that exist in the external environment, which are the components of each construct.

For the internal analysis, the institutional leadership and management create a shared understanding of the strategies and efforts that specifically respond to each of the external contingencies. These strategies inform the MVVs’ statements the institution creates to guide and articulate its performance.

This model of assessment is often characterized as a “competing-values” assessment in which the relationship between the expectations of the external environment is compared with the latent values (response) of the institution. It is a process to identify and explain the situation of the institution to provide a clear perception the institution faces.

Measuring and Assessing Program Structure: Intended Performance

Overview

This chapter presents instruments and methods to measure and assess the design of the academic programs to follow established standards and optimize learning. Functional design creates many institutional performance issues and the credible and trustworthy measurements of the performance of each aspect of design informs the processes of institutional improvement. These instruments aim to collect authentic data with which to monitor the design of the components, processes, and procedures (Institutional Vision) that deliver the institutional mission. The resulting data describe the attributes of functional design and measure their contribution to institutional performance and effectiveness.

This assessment process measures three dimensions of program design: the design of the curriculum and courses, the delivery of the program learning, and the viability of a program to perform in the external environment (Ali, 2018). Together, these assessments measure the ability of the program design structures to achieve the mission (Casarejos, Gustavson, & Frota, 2017).

Measuring Curriculum

The process of delivering the institutional mission, vision, and values begins with the design of the curricula and the courses that deliver those curricula (Mouritsen, 1986). The curriculum and the courses engineer the instructional experience to allow learners to achieve the learning effectively and efficiently the institution advertises, and each student seeks (Rubenstein & Ridgley, 2017). A curriculum contributes two influences on the success of the students: a prescribed path to follow and “guardrails” to prevent failure. From an instructional systems design perspective, the curriculum creates the drivers and the constraints for the learning process. The availability of trustworthy and credible data to describe the attributes of the curriculum creates the opportunity to understand how the attributes of curriculum design contribute to student success, persistence, retention, and other meaningful indicators of instructional performance (Duque, 2016; Holder, 2007; Macfadyen & Dawson, 2012). In this assessment system, the purpose of the curriculum design is to promote and enhance the learning needs of the students enrolled in a program and the institution (Barnett, Coate, & Society for Research into Higher Education, 2005; Curry & Wergin, 1993, 1997; Diamond, 2008) and includes any discrete unit of learning: a program, specialization, major, minor, general education, etc.

While there is limited research on the performance of the design attributes of curriculum in higher education, there is literature that articulates a theoretical series of attributes that presume to organize the learning in a manner that creates an effective learning experience (Diamond, 2008; Ewell, 1997; Ferren, 1997; Leow, Neo, & Hew, 2016; Marsh & Willis, 2003). These attributes form a conceptual framework to measure and differentiate curricular design and measure its ability to enable the learning of the students within an institution. In this measurement process, the comparison of the attributes of effective curriculum design with an articulated standard constitutes a direct measurement of a curriculum’s coherence and contribution to institutional performance.

The structure of a curriculum is the primary consideration in the learners’ ability to engage with the intended content and learning processes (Rubenstein & Ridgley, 2017). The student’s ability to grasp and process the disciplinary content in a manner that makes intellectual sense to them is the curricular attribute that makes learning engagement possible. Hence, measurements of the design attributes of the curriculum are a critical consideration in the assessment of student engagement. The measurement data monitor the performance of the curriculum at the transactional level where the learners interact with and “experience” the learning the institution intends. Additionally, correlations between the design of the curriculum and the performance of other functions reveal the synergies of the instructional process for the institution (Sullivan & Wilds, 2001).

An instrument is presented, which uses criteria extracted from the research literature to measure the “qualities” of a curriculum (Diamond, 2008; Ewell, 1997; Ferren, 1997; Marsh & Willis, 2003) based on the following standards.

Externally valid content. This standard describes the consistency of the content of the curriculum with expectations of the external environment. These expectations, published in the literature in the discipline, professional employers, licensing boards, or accrediting agencies (Brown, 2017), build a conceptual framework for the curriculum. This dimension determines the relevancy of curriculum content, which promotes learning engagement through the generation of immediacy for the learners. Externally valid content represents the organization of the discipline as conceived by the collective intelligence of the professionals within each discipline and is more likely to be accessible to a diverse population of learners since a diverse population of professionals, which is theoretically comparable to the student population, created the content. Externally valid curriculum ensures that the learning is relevant to the profession and the discipline and meaningful to a diverse population of students (Thompson, Griffith, & Armon, 2014).

Coverage. This standard examines the breadth of the program outcomes that define the program. This dimension determines the ability of the curriculum to deliver a comprehensive conception of the discipline (Mena Report, 2015; Oprandi, 2014) as well as the educational goals of the institution (Oaks, 2015).

Coherence. Coherence is a standard often articulated as a criterion for curriculum without definition. The alignment of the curriculum with the psychophysical processes of learning, as articulated in the empirical theories of learning, engineers a coherent learning experience (Marcus, 2001). Designated as a “conceptual framework” (Fortuin, Koppen, & Leemans, 2011), this dimension facilitates the ability of the students to engage with the instructional system. Applying the theories of learning to design the structure of the learning process applies the best evidence available to design the best opportunity to access and learn the material in an efficient manner (Blackmore & Kandiko, 2012; Imam, 2018; Pietarinen, Pyhältö, & Soini, 2017; Pietarinen, Soini, & Pyhältö, 2014). Curriculum coherence is a critical factor in the assessment of performance and improvement in higher education (Hurst, 2016; Kress & Selander, 2012; Pautler, 1989).

Interconnectedness and Alignment. This standard determines if the program learning objectives appropriately construct the program outcomes. The most effective learning objectives parse the program outcomes into a constructed learning experience for the students (Ellis, 1998; Gurvitch & Metzler, 2013; Marcus, 2011). This dimension determines the ability of a curriculum to deliver an effective learning experience (Rubenstein & Ridgley, 2017; Swaggerty & Broemmel, 2017).

Complexity. This dimension analyzes the curriculum to determine if the depth of learning articulated in the learning objectives is appropriate for the level of learning the curriculum delivers. This dimension determines if the learning objectives create appropriate rigor or depth of learning to produce a comprehensive understanding of the program learning outcomes (Fortuin et al., 2011; Soleimani & Alaee, 2014).

Outcomes Demonstration. This standard examines the curriculum to determine if the curriculum presents adequate opportunities for learners to demonstrate program outcomes. This dimension measures the opportunities to demonstrate the program outcomes designed into the curriculum.

Measuring Courses

The courses in an academic program deliver the learning in the curriculum as articulated by the design of the curriculum. The collective design (synergy) of the courses provides another useful dimension to monitor the contribution of the course design to the learning experience. This information describes the interrelationships of the courses (group) as a factor in the learning process.

According to the educational literature (Diamond, 2008; Ewell, 1997; Ferren, 1997; Marsh & Willis, 2003), the following attributes demonstrate the ability of courses to deliver a curriculum efficiently:

Structure. Structure refers to the collective ability of the courses to deliver the curriculum effectively and efficiently. While the structure of the curriculum impacts learning engagement, the collective structure of the courses generates the learning experience (Alt, 2015; Borges, Vittori, Nabak, & Lewis, 1998; Clifton, Baldwin, & Wei, 2012; Freeman, Haak, & Wenderoth, 2011).

Integration. Integration refers to the distribution of the program learning objectives across courses. There are several ways to do this, which is the decision of the faculty and driven by their conceptualization of the discipline (Bock & Randall, 2014; Islam, Talukder, Taheri, & Blanchard, 2016; Walker & Freeze, 2011). The learning objectives are grouped by outcome, characteristics of the learning, problem-solving, etc., or some other strategy to ensure an optimal learning experience is provided by the courses. An optimal model presents each learning objective the minimum number of occurrences to learn the objective effectively. Addressing a learning objective more than necessary reduces the efficiency of the learning experience (Dirks-Naylor, Wright, & Alston, 2015).

Sequence. Sequencing refers to the order in which the learners pursue the courses. The courses are expected to facilitate learning through a well-articulated flow within and across courses, to ensure that the students have the information they need to participate in each course (readiness) (Bock & Randall, 2014; Dirks-Naylor, Wright, & Alston, 2015; Swaggerty & Broemmel, 2017).

Consistency. Consistency is a measure of the groupings of the learning objective within courses that is consistent with the structure and thinking systems of the discipline (Gardner, 1999). In other words, the distribution of the learning objectives reflects the structure of each discipline. This consistency between the delivery of the learning and the structure of the discipline optimizes learning and enhances the student learning experience.

Designing an Optimal Rubric

In the measurement and assessment of program learning outcomes, rubrics guide and structure the decision-making process for the evaluators and facilitate the collection of trustworthy and credible data (Mertler, 2001). This discussion explains the structure of the rubrics used in this process and the systematic thinking that went into these designs. The objective of this discussion is to explain the characteristics of the rubrics proposed in this systematic process.

The research process identified standards to guide the design process and serve as the criteria to evaluate the results (Mertler, 2001; Smit & Birri, 2014; Tierney & Simon, 2004). The initial goal was to create rubrics for the measurement of program learning outcomes demonstration and quickly morphed into a strategy to evaluate student learning products for multiple purposes. The consistency between the designs of the rubrics created at the course, program, and institutional level provides positive and constructive benefits for student and institutional learning (Diamond, 2008; Tierney & Simon, 2004).

The criteria established the following standards for the design of rubrics for program learning measurements.

Standard 1: The content of the rubric is the program learning outcomes and the program learning objectives. The outcomes constitute the constructs to measure, and the learning objectives are the criteria for measurement.

Standard 2: The rubrics measure the outcomes and objectives through the evaluation of student work products that provide an opportunity for the student to demonstrate the outcome or objective authentically.

Standard 3: The learning objectives serve as the performance criteria.

Standard 4: The criteria are listed in the first and second vertical columns on the left side of the rubric so that all criteria are easily discernible (and differentiated) to the evaluator.

Standard 5: For each criterion, a four-part conceptual framework defines the performance categories demonstrable in the work product using a nominal scale. This scale may follow many models of learning. Included in the model below is an example using Bloom’s Cognitive and Affective Domains, Perry’s Model of Intellectual and Ethical Development, a competency development model, and a performance model (King & Howard-Hamilton, 2000).

The rows of the rubric contain the criteria ordered from left to right. Global descriptors such as acceptable, minimal, exceptional, superior, etc., are not used to label or describe the performance categories as this leads the evaluators to make a subjective evaluation instead of applying the criteria to the assignments. The rubric design passively limits the measurements to the criteria contained in the rubric.

Standard 6: The scaling model is translated into the rows to evaluate each criterion. The scale may be the same for all criteria or unique to each criterion. In this example, all performance criteria used the same performance scale.

Standard 7: No more than five discrete, non-overlapping performance categories are used for each criterion as the ability to discriminate categories of performance become less precise and more indefinite, which creates a threat to the reliability of the data.

Standard 8: The rubric is laid out in a grid pattern, with the low point of the scale on the left moving toward the high point on the right. Most cultures represent time (progress, development, learning) in this manner.

Standard 9: The rubric contains no more than seven criteria and four performance categories, although one of these performance categories might be a “non-performance” category, as used in the Program Outcomes/Objectives Assessment Rubrics.

Measuring Program Feasibility and Viability

As an open system organizational model, the contingencies of the external environment drive and constrain both programs and institutions. Institutional effectiveness is determined by the institution’s ability to address these contingencies in the design of its programs. It is possible for a program to be performing effectively internally but irrelevant or unnecessary from the perspective of the external environment. Without assessing the viability of the program to address the needs of the external environment, program performance improvement strategies can prove ineffective (Pritchard, Pausits, & Williams, 2016).

An institutional environmental scan identifies the contingencies at work in the external environment at the institutional level. These contingencies impact the design and performance of academic programs and requires measurement and assessment in the program design (Freeman, Chambers, & King, 2016).

The measurement and assessment of program viability compares two sets of data to describe the performance of each program: the contingencies of the external environment that are relevant to the program (from the environmental scan) and the response of the program design to those contingencies (from the mission, vision, and values of the institution) (Freeman, 2017). The relationship between these two perspectives systematically examines the feasibility of new programs or the viability of existing programs (Grosemans et al., 2017).

A GoF analysis is a research model that achieves such an analysis (Bozeman & Feeney, 2008; Demir, 2016; Shayan, Elahi, Ghazinoory, & Hoseini, 2018). The GoF analysis is a statistical method, which compares two sets of quantitative data to determine the extent to which those two sets are comparable.

When applied to program feasibility or viability, the analysis describes the degree to which the structure of an academic program aligns with the mission, vision, and values of an institution (Harrison, 2015). The GoF methodology provides a relevant conceptual framework to monitor the congruence between the program, the external environment, and the institution. These data are also compared with other functional data to uncover correlations between functional performance and the contingencies of the external environment.

Measuring and Assessing Instruction: Intended Performance

The Instructional System

An instructional system includes all aspects of the learning environment with which a student interacts to obtain the learning articulated by the program. This system includes the content and structure of the curriculum, the design of the course components, instructional strategies, learning resources, as well as instructional support (Clouder, Cureton, & Steventon, 2016; Rubenstein & Ridgley, 2017). The assessment of the instructional system requires the measurement of the students’ interactions with all components of the instructional system to determine their ability to deliver the intended learning (Lecca, 2015). The best evidence of this effectiveness is student perceptions of the extent to which the components of the instructional system helped them learn (Allen & Nichols, 2017; Bell & Aldge, 2014; Macfadyen & Dawson, 2012).

The instructional system includes four distinct categories of interactions: learning engagement, learning experience, learning achievement, and learning support. These categories interact synergistically to produce a dynamic learning system and measuring each of these constructs makes it possible to study the interactions between these categories. This section examines the direct measurement of the instructional system. The next section details the measurement and assessment of the Instructional Support Services. A systematic assessment measures each of these functions separately and aggregates those measurements into an assessment of the entire instructional system. This approach generates authentic, credible, and trustworthy data that are correlated with functional and operational data to uncover relationships between institutional performance and program attributes.

For purposes of this demonstration, it is necessary to develop a conceptual framework to guide the definition of the instruments and resulting measurements. Instruction is a process, which is framed at a high level in the Institutional Vision Statement and more specifically in an Instructional Vision statement, which may exist at the institutional level, or, more appropriately for each discipline.

Instructional Vision

An instructional vision articulates the categories of methods and strategies that the faculty employs to facilitate learning. These strategies (processes) create the learning engagement, learning experience, and the learning environment (support). The instructional vision provides the faculty with a palette of differentiated instructional strategies that are adapted to serve the learning needs of diverse cognitive styles.

The Instructional Vision statement focuses on those specific characteristics of the instructional system that translate the instructional vision into instructional performance. The hypothetical Instructional Vision Statement upon which the instruments presented in this section are based is:

Instruction in this institution differentiates the design, delivery, and measurement of learning to accommodate the cognitive styles of the learners. The instructional staff identifies and employs strategies to engage learners, facilitate an effective learning experience, and create an environment that is supportive of the learner’s efforts to learn.

This statement provides a broad conceptual framework that is adaptable to each discipline to design and develop instruments to measure the effectiveness of the components of the instructional system.

Measuring Learning Engagement (Classroom Observations)

Learning engagement is defined as the interactions between faculty, course components, and other students in the process of acquiring or accessing information. These interactions determine the ability of the students to acquire the information they need to achieve the expected learning. One of the principle ways students engage with the learning process is through their interactions with the instructor, both inside and outside the classroom (Scheerens & Blömeke, 2016; Taut & Rakoczy, 2016). The structure and format of the course components, textbooks, media, etc., impact the ability of the students to engage with learning (Heikonen, Pietarinen, Pyhältö, Toom, & Soini, 2017).

To measure these interactions requires several measurements, beginning with a method to objectively measure the interactions between faculty and students during an instructional experience. The purpose of this measurement process is to collect trustworthy and credible measurements that describe the type and frequency of the interactions during an instructor-led instructional event (Kuh et al., 2000).

There are two aspects of this information that it is vital to collect: the qualities or modes of the interactions and the distribution of those modes over the learning experience. These data are correlated with institutional data to uncover correlations between performance and the attributes of classroom interactions.

The interactions between faculty and students fall into at least six discrete and generic categories of engagement: the instructor is presenting information, the instructor is asking a question, the instructor is answering a question from the students, the students are presenting information, the students are asking a question, the students are answering a question. These categories provide the constructs to differentiate and record the strategies, distribution, and rate of changes over time in instructor interactions.

Measuring Learning Achievement (Portfolio, Prior Learning Measurement)

The final factor measured to study the effectiveness of the instructional system is a measure of the learning achievement generated by that instructional system. The learning that results from the student’s interactions with the instructional system measure the ability of the students to demonstrate the intended outcomes in their work products. To measure learning achievement requires data that measure both the program outcomes and program (course) learning objectives (Penn, 2011). The measurement of these outcomes is a separate process from a course grading and conducted with a random sample of work products, to minimize faculty workload through generalizability.

The faculty evaluates the sample of work products using a well-constructed rubric to determine the extent to which students demonstrate the learning outcomes of the program and the learning objectives in the courses. These measurements focus on the results of the instructional system and not the performance of individuals.

The measurement of learning outcomes is conducted effectively with selected student work products compiled into a developmental portfolio. The learning artifacts are either compiled during the program coursework or generation by a unique project course, such as a capstone or topics course. As part of the measurement process, students choose the artifacts they think demonstrate each outcome.

Measuring Satisfaction

In these instruments, student perceptions provide the measures of satisfaction through questions that model one or more of the empirical constructs of satisfaction. While “are you satisfied with” is an acceptable question, for institutional effectiveness, it produces inadequate information to improve institutional performance.

Questionnaires are instruments that collect personal and individualized information in a structured or framed manner (Sheu, Mejia, Rigali-Oiler, Prime, & Chong, 2016). A questionnaire asks convergent questions that focus on the effect of an external stimulus to produce a desired internal response, unlike surveys that tend to ask divergent questions about an internal result (how happy are you with …). In this measurement process, the objective is to measure the effectiveness of the strategies by the services staff, whatever those were, to achieve the results the student expects and needs to succeed, without specifying them (Dumitrescu & Martinsson, 2016).

The assessment of job satisfaction has confirmed the measurement of satisfaction (Dawis, 2004). The job satisfaction models provide a systematic, science-based approach to the measurement of satisfaction that is adapted to use for student support services (Bowling, Wagner, & Beehr, 2018; Forero & Gómez, 2017; Sheu et al., 2016).

There are five empirical models of satisfaction that are adapted to scale and measure satisfaction in student support services functions (Dawis, 2004). These are:

  1. The fulfillment of needs – did the strategies and methods provide the guidance needed to succeed, for example, instructional support services.

  2. The fulfillment of values – did the experiences reflect and model the stated values, for example, social experiences.

  3. The fulfillment of expectations – did the experiences deliver the expectations created for it, for example, transition support services.

  4. The perception of equity – did the experiences assist all students, cultures, and genders with equal respect and assistance, for example, diversity and intercultural services.

  5. Social information processing – did the experiences create an opportunity for group or experiential learning, for example, community learning experiences.

Questions framed by these models of satisfaction measure the effectiveness of the student support services. The questions in each instrument include examples from all five models of satisfaction to match the intended contribution of the service to the success of the students. Each of the instruments uses the same “Likert-type” scale to help the respondents measure their responses (Uebersax, 2006).

Functional Data Modeling: Identifying the Drivers and Constraints of Actual Performance

Overview

In this phase of systematic assessment, the functional data are analyzed to identify the KPIs for each function. These KPIs are grouped into causal triads with their two strongest cross-functional correlates to identify the drivers and constraints of functional performance (Goldratt, 1990). The strongest of these KPIs demonstrate the performance of the functions of the institution. This procedure is the first of two phases of the data modeling process in which the purpose is to create multi-level models of institutional performance (Antony & Lott, 2012) from a comprehensive set of credible and trustworthy functional data (Ledder, 2016).

Functional Performance Data

In the previous chapters, instruments collect trustworthy, credible, and authentic data to measure the performance of functional systems at the level of the transaction, or point of service, between the institutional functions and the stakeholders. These data describe the results of the individual processes and procedures the function uses to accomplish its work and provide the best evidence of the performance of the functional systems to serve the needs of its constituents.

Over 90 performance indicators (measurements) are measured. Individually, these measurements describe the performance of the function with individuals and not the collective performance of the function. The individual functional data are aggregated for each indicator to demonstrate the intended performance of each function.

Functional Data Modeling

Data modeling is a multidimensional process of searching for and uncovering the mathematical relationships that exist within large data sets (Antony, & Lott, 2012; He, Levine, Fan, Beemer, & Stronach, 2018). In this application, the data models reveal the emergent interrelationships in the functional performance data and translate that information into meaningful models of actual performance unframed by the MVVs (intended performance). The MVVs define what the function does, that is, intended performance. The data modeling uncovers what the system is doing, that is, actual performance. The interrelationships revealed in the modeling process between the indicators (correlations) represent the “synergies” of cross-functional performance that construct institutional performance.

In the measurement phase, performance data were summarized to monitor the performance of each function with the stakeholders. In the assessment phase, these individual data were aggregated to demonstrate the performance of the function at multiple levels of the institution. The data modeling processes transform the functional data into models of institutional performance that demonstrate the effectiveness of the institution.

In this first phase of data modeling, these interrelationships are calculated by using correlation coefficients to quantify their size and relationship (Cook & Zhu, 2014; Cook et al., 2014). The correlations are calculated between all indicators in the measurement data (Peral et al., 2017). The second phase of the data modeling groups these interrelationships into authentic models of institutional performance by applying the organizational effectiveness approaches as a conceptual framework for each model (Cameron & Whetten, 2013).

An exploratory factor analysis is conducted with the functional data set (all responses to all indicators) to analyze the functional data for its interrelationships by calculating the correlations between all indicators. While it is possible to use many data modeling methods and statistics in this phase, the exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses are particularly useful approaches because they identify the plausible relationships within the functional data and combine those correlations into descriptive models. The factor analysis reports a matrix of correlations between all indicators (variables) as well as using the variance in the data to calculate models of factors, which explain performance with the fewest indicators.

The total functional data set contains the individual responses to each of the 90 performance indicators measured in the functional systems. Each variable (curricular structure, course efficiency, etc.) is conceived only as a performance “indicator” (not KPIs) at this phase of the process. Since these are categorical data, the factor analysis use a Spearman Rho coefficient of correlation (Kalkan & Kelecioglu, 2016; Plewis & Raab, 1999), which measures the size of the correlations (synergies) between each indicator and every other indicator as well as the direction of the correlation. The result of this analysis is a 90 × 90 correlation matrix, which contains a total of 8,100 correlations. This matrix is too large to evaluate performance in a meaningful way, and it is necessary to reduce (transform) these 8,100 calculations into models of the data that most completely explain the performance of the institution. The factor modeling function reduces these correlations to the minimum set of indicators that fully define institutional performance.

The correlation matrix identifies the performance indicators with the strongest correlations, either positive or negative, within the functional data. The strongest correlations become the KPIs (Kucukaltan, Irani, & Aktas, 2016). Positive KPI correlations indicate a driver of performance and negative KPI correlations indicate a constraint on performance (Goldratt, 1990, 2009; Goldratt & Weiss, 2005; Techt, 2015).

Aggregating Functional Data into Causal Triads

As has been discussed, single indicators are inadequate to demonstrate the complexities of institutional performance. In this example, there are 90 performance indicators, suggesting that any single indicator only explains 1/90th of the total performance information. The indicators are triangulated into meaningful groups to provide practical meaning. In this assessment system, the KPIs are clustered into triads to demonstrate performance. The correlations between the KPIs define these triads based on the strength of the correlations. These triads demonstrate the interrelationships between the indicators, which represent the synergies of functional performance (Nagarajan & Maluk Mohamed, 2017).

A subset of the data is analyzed to calculate the KPIs between indicators and demonstrate the procedure with all 90 indicators. Table 65 presents a correlation matrix reported from a factor analysis conducted with the subset of 14 indicators selected from the 90 indicators contained in the original functional data set.

Table 65 presents the correlations between each of the 14 indicators and each of the other 14s indicator. A correlation of or around zero indicates no correlation. The strongest correlations become the KPIs of institutional performance because they represent the most powerful drivers of institutional performance.

Each KPI with its two strongest correlates (positive or negative) triangulates into clusters of three KPIs or KPI triads. First-, Second-, and Third-Order Effects of Functional Changes

The clustering process also provides a method to anticipate the consequences of changes made to a system to enhance or correct performance. In the field of organizational learning, these are first-, second-, and third-order effects of a change in strategy (Aminbeidokhti, Jamshidi, & Hoseini, 2016; Börjesson Rivera, Håkansson, Svenfelt, & Finnveden, 2014; Noumair & Shani, 2018; Pinelli & Maiolini, 2017; Robinson, 2001).

The first-order effects are those that occur at the level of the change. In Table 66, columns 2 and 3 identify the plausible first-order effects anticipated for changes in the indicators in column 1. That is, if the curricular structure–external validity produces a positive change, a first-order effect would be the changes expected on the indicators that correlated most strongly with external validity: curricular structure–coherence and disabilities services. Since both correlations are negative, a positive change in the curricular structure–external validity indicator plausibly produces a negative change in the curricular structure–coherence and disabilities services indicators.

Second-order effects of a change in the curricular structure–external validity would vary with the KPIs associated with disabilities services, the strongest KPI associated with changes in the curricular structure–external validity. The disabilities services indicator negatively correlates with curricular structure–external validity, which is a first-order effect, and with the course effectiveness indicator. Therefore, a second-order effect of a positive change in the curricular structure–external validity is anticipated for the course effectiveness indicator, in the negative direction, since the correlation with the primary indicator, curricular structure–external validity, is negative and the correlation between disabilities and course effectiveness is positive. The size of the correlations predicts the likelihood of such a consequence.

There are also effects on the indicators associated with each of the indicators included in the second-order effects; these are third-order effects. The third-order structure–effects of a change in the curricular structure–external validity would be those expected in the indicators associated with the second strongest correlation. In this example, the third-order effects would be curricular structure–coherence, which positively correlates with program learning objective and tutoring performance. Since the correlation between curricular structure–external validity and curricular structure–coherence is negative, a positive change in curricular structure external validity indicator produces a negative impact on the curricular structure–coherence indicator as well as the program learning objective and tutoring performance indicators since they correlate positively with curricular structure–coherence.

This method provides the staff and managers with information to anticipate the consequences of changes to the functional systems they are considering, which informs the action-planning process. Whether there is a real effect is determined after the change is implemented and measured with the same instruments that identified it (Harmon et al., 2018; Noumair & Shani, 2018). In the systematic assessment of complex, nonlinear, and dynamic processes, these calculations intend to provide plausible contingencies to consider when identifying the first, second, and third order consequences of a change in strategy.

When implementing a change strategy, the immediate response to the change is usually negative. If the change strategy produces a positive change, that change emerges over the long term.

A scorecard is most easily comprehended when it employs a maximum of three categories of analysis and no more than seven KPIs for each category (Fooladvand, Yarmohammadian, & Shahtalebi, 2015). This design standard is supported by the principles of absolute judgment in the field of neuroscience, which studies the characteristics and abilities of the human information processing (sensemaking) system (Alan, 1994; Linhares et al., 2011; Miller, 1956; Shiffrin & Nosofsky, 1994) to make sense of these indicators. Institutional Data Modeling

In this second phase of data modeling, the KPIs that emerged from the first phase of functional data modeling are reorganized into organizational effectiveness models to develop an institutional view of functional performance. The data modeling approach reorganizes the information into the institutional effectiveness approaches to demonstrate institutional performance from multiple perspectives (Brown, 2017; Harrison & Shirom, 1998; McClure, 2017; Tadesse, Manathynga, & Gillies, 2018). These models group the KPIs of actual functional performance into models of institutional performance, differentiated by the characteristics of the processes each function uses to perform its mission (Dey, Hassanien, Bhatt, Ashour, & Satapathy, 2018). This second facilitate of data modeling provides visual data models to facilitate the sensemaking process for institutional effectiveness (Morgan, 1996).

The effectiveness models facilitate the process of “looking beyond the functional performance data” to see the actual performance of the institution and uncover implicit strategies to consider for performance enhancement (La Salle & Johnson, 2016; Vinyard et al., 2017; Macfadyen & Dawson, 2012).

To fully understand the performance of the institution in its environment, dimensions of operational performance (efficiency) are also useful to include in the institutional performance models. These data are collected by the systems that monitor the daily transactions outside the functions of the institution.

The sensemaking process may combines functional and operational data into models of institutional performance using the same data modeling methods used the functional data. The resulting KPIs reveal the drivers and constraints of the actual performance at the institutional level (Goldratt, 1990).

Five organizational effectiveness approaches have been researched, tested, and confirmed as appropriate models to demonstrate the contribution of functional educational systems and processes to institutional performance. These approaches are the goal-path approach (often identified as the goal-attainment approach), the systems approach, the strategic-constituencies approach, the strategic contingencies approach (Hickson, Hinings, Lee, Schneck, & Pennings, 1971), and the competing-values approach (Rukh & Qadeer, 2018). Each of these approaches assumes a distinctly different functional process and reorganizes the functional KPIs into data models, which demonstrate institutional effectiveness for institutional sensemaking.

Goal-path approach. The goal-path approach assumes effectiveness as the achievement of a clearly defined goal, or goals, which produces identical outcomes that demonstrate specific criteria. This approach is most credible to functions or institutions that are deliberate, rational, goal-seeking efforts using predictable processes to produce identical results from consistent inputs. The goals may fall into short- or long-term categories and must be specific, measurable, and shared.

Designed for manufacturing applications where the result is identical products, such as the manufacturing of electronic instruments, it is a useful approach for those aspects of institutional performance that require uniformity. In this systematic assessment of educational institutions, this approach is applied to the effectiveness of the design of program curricula and courses.

Systems approach. The systems approach assumes effectiveness as generated by establishing and maintaining processes and procedures that produce a consistent result. Effectiveness in this approach is demonstrated by producing outcomes that demonstrate specific, yet complex criteria in ways that are unique (within limits) to each product; in the case of education, individualized products. This approach explains the performance of an organization by demonstrating the integrity of three functional processes: inputs, throughputs, and outputs.

The system processes are interactive, interdependent, and causal (synergistic); therefore, the KPIs require a degree of interconnectedness produced in this system by calculating the KPIs directly from the correlations between the indicators. Commonly employed in process-oriented functions, this approach is consistent with the characteristics of a functional component that obtains resources with varied characteristics from the external environment, treats or processes those resources in a structured manner, and returns those resources to the external environment with new or improved characteristics. It is the process that is measured and assessed.

In this systematic assessment, the systems approach is used to make sense of the performance of each academic program to institutional performance.

Strategic constituencies approach. The strategic constituencies approach assumes that stakeholders’ satisfaction with the efforts of the institution to serve their individual needs is evidence of effectiveness (Alfirević, Pavičić, & Mihanović, 2007; Börjesson et al., 2014; Holbeche, 2017; Nellesen, Birnbaum, & Greenberg, 2010). The strategic constituencies approach measures the satisfaction of the stakeholder groups that are large enough to generate sufficient statistical power to be a discrete group. Stakeholder groups that are not large enough are combined with other groups until the power is sufficient and the analysis includes all stakeholder groups, no matter how small.

This approach ensures that the efforts (design assumptions) of the institution are serving the needs (real performance) of all stakeholders by evaluating the results with diverse constituencies. In this systematic assessment process, the strategic constituencies’ model informs the design of the measurements and assessments of the student support services function.

Strategic contingencies approach. The strategic contingencies approach to organizational effectiveness assumes that the effectiveness of the institution is produced by effectively delivering the outcomes or results from each function in the institution (Junqueira et al., 2016; McAdam, Miller, & McSorley, 2016; Qiu, Luo, Jackson, & Sanders, 2017). It makes sense of the performance of an institution on its strategic plan. In this systematic process, the strategic contingencies approach was used to design the measurements and assessments of the instructional system to deliver the program learning outcomes and program learning objectives. In this application, the strategic contingencies are the program outcomes the institution intends to deliver. The program learning objectives provide the tactics and strategies that deliver those outcomes. The strategic contingency approach is consistent with the processes employed by the instructional system to deliver the learning for the institution (Doyle & Seagren, 1984).

While this approach is like the goal-path approach, it is strategically different. The goal-path approach evaluates a product (curriculum); the strategic contingencies approach evaluates a process (learning).

Competing-values approach. The competing-values approach combines many of the attributes of the previous approaches formulated in a much more flexible and less precise manner. This approach assumes that institutional effectiveness is determined by how well the institution manages competing interests and forces with broad and flexible outcomes (Grabowski, Neher, Crim, & Mathiassen, 2015; Rukh & Qadeer, 2018). Effectiveness measures the response to a condition without seeking to change the condition.

The competing-values approach makes sense of institutional performance by modeling the KPIs into a stimulus–response format. In this systematic model of assessment, the competing-values approach used to design the environmental scan, which measures the expectations of the external environment.

These approaches apply to organizational effectiveness in functions that demonstrate the same assumptions as each effectiveness approach. Any approach may be applied to any aspect of institutional performance to match the institution’s conception of itself and the way it wishes to assess its performance. The use of these approaches as data models facilitates the process of “looking beyond” the functional data systematically to make sense of institutional effectiveness and provides a collection of methods to analyze and organize functional data to make sense of institutional effectiveness.

Conceptual Underpinnings

These approaches differentiate by the assumptions and thinking systems that underpin them (Cresswell, 2006). Since these models engage the collective intelligence of the institution into the review, evaluation, and sensemaking process, it is useful to articulate the differences in the ways these approaches conceive of performance.

The goal-path model and the strategic contingencies approach are both dualistic thinking models. They assume that for every problem, there is one correct solution. Both conceive of effectiveness as a dichotomous event; it is an either/or assessment. This approach is most useful to an institution or function that views its performance dualistically or mechanically.

The systems approach assumes that for every problem there are a few viable solutions. This approach represents multiplistic thinking and presents a more flexible approach to institutional effectiveness. This approach works well for those institutions that view their performance as process- or procedure-driven with slight variations needed to accommodate unique situations with slight variations.

The strategic constituencies approach reflects a relativistic thought process. In this approach, for every problem, many solutions work, depending on the circumstances. This approach applies a conceptual framework to a highly varied and unpredictable operational system, to limit its performance options. It is most appropriate to organic institutions in which functional performance is adapted to the individual needs of the stakeholders (Duque, 2016).

The competing-values model accommodates flexibility and variation on both the stimulus (problem) side and the solution side of the process. The competing-values approach assumes that institutional performance is complex and composed of a series of changing circumstances to which the institution responds in complex ways. The complexity exists on both sides of the equation, and this approach is appropriate to organic systems, which exist in complex, unpredictable, and dynamic external and internal environments. In this conception of effectiveness, the performance of the institutional response is measured and assessed for effectiveness using the functional data.

These differentiated models make sense of institutional effectiveness as the institution conceives itself to be. Each model applies to multiple situations depending upon the conception the institutional leaders choose to assume.

Making Sense of Functional KPIs Through the Goal-Path Approach

The goal-path approach to organizational effectiveness (also known as the goal-attainment approach) configures the KPIs to facilitate sensemaking for functions in which the contribution is to produce clearly defined outcomes with identical attributes. The result of a goal-path approach is to produce an identical product with limited variance from the criteria. The degree to which the product complies with the standards established by the institution determines the effectiveness.

The goal-path approach has two components: a specific goal or outcome that accomplishes and the processes and protocols which produce that outcome. The evaluation criteria define a specific model, standard, and criterion to measure the completed product. The goal-path approach presumes linear, stable, predictable, and uniform contributions of the function to institutional effectiveness. The assumption that underlies a goal-path approach is that performance is a dualistic event: the goal was achieved/not achieved; the agreed upon strategy, criteria, or method was followed/not followed (Casarejos, Gustavson, & Frota, 2017; Moran, 2016).

Making Sense of Assessment KPIs Through the Competing-Values Approach

The competing-values approach to organizational effectiveness makes sense of environmental scan results. In this competing-values approach, the KPIs are selected to demonstrate the effectiveness of the internal response to the external contingencies (Chidambaranathan & Regha, 2016; Pounder, 2000; Rizvi & Lingard, 2011). Most often, this approach evaluates the interactions between large complex institutions and the external environment in which they exist (Kroeger & Weber, 2015; Qiu et al., 2017). The number of contingencies within each category of environmental concern is limited to a maximum of seven contingencies (Alan, 1994; Linhares et al., 2011; Miller, 1956; Shiffrin & Nosofsky, 1994).

Half of the KPIs collected for a competing-values assessment is measured internally (program feasibility and viability assessments) and the other half is collected externally (institutional environmental scan) (Volberda et al., 2012). The external KPIs objectively describe the conditions in the external environment, which impact institutional performance. The internal KPIs describe the response of the institution to those external contingencies.

The sensemaking process organizes the KPIs to demonstrate the effectiveness of the institutional response to address the external contingencies and expectations. In the competing-values approach, the presence of a reasonable and effective strategy to balance the competing interests of the external environment with the commitments of the institution determines effectiveness. In this competing-values approach, there is no presumption of right/wrong, appropriate/inappropriate, or any other linear paradigm. Instead, the presumption is that the external environment creates conditions that the institution attempts to address, which may be successful or unsuccessful.

In practice, it may be that the institution is unable or unwilling to address these concerns due to institutional commitments, that is, the mission, vision, and values. This condition creates a cognitive dissonance between the institution and the external environment, which is recognized and articulated, but unavoidable. However, this cognitive difference constructively uncovers strategies that advance the interests of both sides (Woerkom & Sanders, 2010; Snehal, & Kremer, 2010). Conducted in this manner, the competing-values model can significantly advance the position of an institution in a conflicted environment (Qiu et al., 2017).

Continuous Qualities Improvement

The final phase of the institutional effectiveness process is to use the conclusions drawn from these data-based collaborations to enhance or improve institutional effectiveness. One of the names given to this process in organizational management is continuous “quality” improvement. As a performance-based model of institutional effectiveness, the current process seeks to focus attention on the specific attributes and performance of the institution instead of the performance of the humans laboring to cause those systems to perform as intended (Kang, Zhao, Li, & Horst, 2016). This approach moves beyond functional performance as an outcome to conceive of functional performance as a “means to an end,” which is institutional effectiveness. The “qualities” or design of the system produces effectiveness (O’Connor & McDermott, 1997; Martin, 2016) of the functional systems. Doing otherwise has been identified as a significant cause of failure in institutional effectiveness initiatives in higher education (Kataria et al., 2013; Thompson, 2004).

Sensemaking

Sensemaking is the critical and culminating component of the assessment of institutional effectiveness and continuous quality improvement, which is a product of the diverse human intelligence of the institution engaging in the sensemaking process. The sensemaking process requires the collective intuition, judgment, agile thinking, and creativity of the humans in the organization to envision effectiveness and plan innovative improvements (Leber, 2005; Sofo, 2013). However, sensemaking requires the availability of authentic, credible, and trustworthy indicators upon which to base decisions (Khezrimotlagh, Zhu, Cook, & Toloo, 2019).

The purpose of this chapter is to demonstrate the application of indicator modeling to sensemaking (Pîrlog & Balint, 2016). None of these discussions seeks to provide an absolute interpretation of the meaning of the indicators but to demonstrate a search for meaning and effectiveness within the data. The possibilities to explain performance are many, and for clarity, this discussion includes only a few. In practice, the search is for plausibility and all plausible meanings, including those that are anomalous or outliers, are included. The scanning for plausibilities generates thorough, dynamic, and creative sensemaking.

Sensemaking in Action

In the system of assessment under discussion, sensemaking for institutional effectiveness employs at least two similar analytic approaches. The first analysis scans the Institutional Effectiveness Report to explore anomalies in intended performance. In this evaluation, intended performance is defined by the MVVs and the KPIs demonstrate actual performance. The sensemaking process “looks beyond the KPIs” to evaluate the effectiveness of institutional performance indicated by the MVVs. From this evaluation process, institutional change strategies emerge for performance improvement (Kulkarni, 2016).

The second analysis evaluates the indicator clusters to confirm causal interrelationships between indicators, which become the drivers and constraints of performance. The drivers and constraints of performance represent the synergies within the functional indicators that produce institutional performance. Each of the triads in the clustering analysis is evaluated to determine the drivers and constraints impacting the effectiveness of each KPI.

In neither of these analytical discussions is the purpose of the search for truth or absolute meaning. Instead, the purpose of the collaborations is to collectively recognize plausible explanations and solutions that are addressed through improvement strategies, tested in practice, assessed with authentic performance assessments, and evaluated for effectiveness (Ioannidis, Fanelli, Dunne, & Goodman, 2015). Sensemaking is a process of formulating questions to investigate further by separating functional performance into its constituent parts (clusters) and reassembling it into a model of institutional effectiveness, which is based upon the relationships within the KPIs (Chan, 2015).

Sensemaking of the Institutional Effectiveness Report

The report tells a story of institutional effectiveness on all dimensions of performance, which the sensemaking process seeks to uncover and articulate. The sensemaking process begins with the examination of this report for performance anomalies that deserve further exploration. This phase of sensemaking is exploratory and seeks to identify all possible, plausible performance synergies.

The most concerning performance indicator in this report is the average program completion percentage of 22%, which is an operational indicator. However, the functional interactions or transactions produce that indicator. In this instance, the number of people who start a program divided by the number of people who complete the program not necessarily graduating, separates program completion from student success.

When examined for interrelationships with other indicators, the program completion percentage and the course completion rates covary theoretically and are equivalent. The course completion percentage for major courses is 89%. Projected over eight quarters, the 89% course completion percentage predicts a program completion percentage of 44% for eight semesters. This inconsistency suggests that something is constraining or interfering with program completion other than the course completion.

The average GPA of graduates is 3.80, and the average GPA of those who withdraw from the institution is 1.80. The students who graduate seem to be doing so with a high degree of academic success and the withdrawing students are not failing academically (GPA< 1.0), although their GPA is lower than those who complete the program. The students are completing the major courses, with a 5% failure rate, which is at the level of chance. Why then is the program completion percentage only 22%?

The indicators suggest that the courses are well-designed and delivered but that the total student experience is not maintaining their interest. The component of institutional performance that is responsible for creating a relevant and coherent experience is the curriculum. To confirm this assumption, the program outcomes assessments, which were not a KPI at the institutional level, are evaluated for additional information. Also, evaluating indicator 17, which reports the percentage of students who are either employed in their field after graduation or successfully passed a certification examination for their degree, which is 63% of the students who completed the program, excluding those who pursued further education. This percentage supports the conclusion that the curriculum may not have external validity as only 63% of the students are finding employment in their field of study. These are only some of the possibilities that might emerge from this approach to sensemaking of these data. This first method of sensemaking explores a problem with a single indicator and searches for relationships within the indicators to explain or further understand the performance of that indicator.

The second method of sensemaking from the systems report involves the search to understand the collective performance of the three areas of institutional effectiveness: student acquisition, student experience, or student success. This demonstration evaluates the student acquisition function for sensemaking.

This case poses a “research” question: Are the recruitment and admission processes providing qualified students (inputs) whose needs the institution can serve? This question is a qualitative inquiry about the effectiveness of the admissions processes. The evidence of effectiveness for this function comes from the interactions of several indicators across all three functions.

The average GPA of graduates is 3.80, indicating that the students who are graduating from the program are academically successful. The average GPA of withdrawn students is 1.80, indicating that they are not failing, but having performance difficulties, which is supported by the persistence rate of developmental students at 11%. The average time to withdraw from the program is 43 weeks, indicating that students are resilient and not giving up quickly. The student success number is 14%, which is the percentage of students who meet three conditions: they start a program, graduate, and find a position in the field for which they prepared. The failure rate of the general education courses is 8%, which is slightly higher than the major course failure rate of 5% suggesting that the admitted students may not be as academically prepared as expected but are more adept in their major or professional education. A question that emerges from this data is “are the students adequately prepared to pursue the education the institution provides?”

From an admissions perspective, this may indicate that the admitted students have needs the institution cannot serve, which would suggest a review of the student support services function to determine if some variety of support is missing or if the functions are not performing effectively.

A third variety of sensemaking is to examine a cross-functional process to discover its indicated performance. The processes of the instructional system, which involve student acquisition, experience, and success, is selected for discussion (Dick, Carey, & Carey, 2014). In this instance, the indicators in the student experience section of the report are of prime interest with secondary considerations coming from indicators in the other two areas.

The major course completion percentage averages 92% over five years, indicating that the major courses are delivering the learning. The general education course completion percentage averages 89%, which is consistent with and slightly lower than the major course completion percentages. The major course time to withdrawal average is 2.4 weeks and the general education courses average length of time to withdraw is 2.0 weeks, again demonstrating consistency between those that persist and those that do not persist. From these indicators, it appears that the instructional system is effectively delivering learning. The projected program completion % from this performance factor over eight semesters is 56%. Whatever the problem is with the program completion percentage does not seem to be related to the delivery of the program or the preparation of the students. The question that emerges is “what is the functional problem that is creating the low completion percentages?”

The correlation matrix reveals several strong correlations for program completion percentage, both positive and negative. However, these are correlative and not causative.
