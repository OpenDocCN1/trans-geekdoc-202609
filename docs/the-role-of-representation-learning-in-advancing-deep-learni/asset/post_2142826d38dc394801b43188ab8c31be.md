The measurements are evenly spaced between \(\lambda \in [\pi/2, -\pi/2]\) and \(\phi \in [0, 2\pi)\) with step-sizes \([0.25^\circ, 0.25^\circ]\). We down-sampled the grid from \(721 \times 1440\) to \(180 \times 360\) using bi-linear interpolation. We treat each time-step as a different sample. The train-, validation- and test-split is done at 10 000, 2200 and 2200 respectively. Similar work transforms the polar coordinates to cartesian coordinates by letting \(\mathbf{x} = (\cos \lambda \cos \phi, \cos \lambda \sin \phi, \sin \phi)\) [73]. However, we leave ours in polar form as we already have the shape data modality with 3D Cartesian coordinates. In addition we min-max normalize the data to the range [0, 1] using the all-time minimum- and maximum temperatures measured in the dataset.

### 3D Shapes.

For our work with 3D shapes, we utilized the ShapeNet Core [38] dataset, which is a curated and richly annotated subset of the comprehensive ShapeNet dataset. ShapeNet Core comprises 52 456 3D shapes, representing a total of 55 object categories. The models are provided in three formats: mesh, voxelized hollow, and voxelized solid. For our purposes, we use the voxelized solid representation. We only use the first 4 categories, namely Tables, Cars, Chairs and Airplanes. With 8443, 7497, 6778 and 4045 samples respectively. The dataset is split into training, validation, and test sets with 45 000, 3728, and 3728 samples, respectively. To optimize storage and ensure compatibility with our processing requirements, we downsample the voxel grid resolution from \(128 \times 128 \times 128\) to \(64 \times 64 \times 64\). This downsampling makes the data mod 12 divisible and reduces disk space usage. We employed trilinear interpolation for downsampling using the `torch.nn.functional.interpolate` function with trilinear mode. We also evaluated `scipy.ndimage.zoom` as done in [73], but observed no significant visual differences compared to the PyTorch method. However, the PyTorch implementation proved computationally more efficient, as it avoids polynomial fitting. After interpolation, voxels with a value > 0.05 are considered as occupied.

### Audio.

For the audio data, we utilize a subset of the LibriSpeech [204] dataset, specifically the 100-hour clean dataset. LibriSpeech is a large-scale corpus of read English speech derived from audiobooks, sampled at 16 kHz. The selected subset includes numerous audio files ranging from 1.4 to 25 seconds in duration. From this dataset, we extract the first 12k samples, trimming each sample to 5 seconds and discarding those shorter than this length. The audio files are then resampled from 16 kHz to 12 kHz to reduce data size and computational load. This resampling preserves all critical speech frequencies up to 6 kHz, ensuring that the intelligibility and quality of the speech are maintained. To optimize memory usage and processing efficiency, the audio signals are converted to float32 format, with amplitude values normalized to the range 0.0 to 1.0. The processed data is then divided into training, validation, and test sets, consisting of 10k, 1k, and 1k samples, respectively.

### Video.

We employ the Adobe240 dataset [254], comprising 133 high-frame-rate videos captured at a resolution of 720p and a frame rate of 240 frames per second. Each video has a duration ranging from 1 to 10 seconds. For data pre-processing, the videos are divided into 25-frame segments, and any portrait-oriented videos are rotated to landscape orientation. Of the 133 videos, the first 100 are designated for training, 16 for validation, and 17 for testing. Following the approach in [178], we apply data augmentation by randomly cropping 512 × 512 patches. During 7-skip frame and x4 spatial interpolation, we use 4 input frames, each separated by 7 frames, providing a total of 25 frames as ground truth. Each spatial axis is further downscaled by a factor of x4.

### LiDAR.

To illustrate the adaptability of our framework, we further train our model on LiDAR data from the KITTI-360 dataset [164]. We disregard the global scene context and consider each vehicle at each timeframe as an independent sample. Following the methodology in [298], we define training, validation, and test splits of 20,000, 2,500, and 2,500 samples, respectively, by temporally down-sampling frames in the driving sequences. The point clouds are then converted into distance maps with a resolution of 64 × 1024. This data was captured using a Velodyne HDL-64E sensor, which provides a vertical field of view (FoV) of 26.8°, spanning from -24.8° to +2°, and a horizontal FoV of 360°. For consistency, the distance map is normalized based on the sensor’s maximum range of 120 meters.

### Motivation for downsampling.

Each of our dataset is mod 12 divisible. We motivate this to have the possibility to further downsample our data by a factor of x2, x3 and x4. Many Single Image Super Resolution Benchmarks degradations are compared in that range [65, 163, 124]. However, we did not test 8x degradation. Furthermore, we zero-pad each data that are not divisible by the spatial Functa dimensions as well as our FunctaFormer’s attention window. In return before comparison and evaluation, each data point is again reduced to its original size.
