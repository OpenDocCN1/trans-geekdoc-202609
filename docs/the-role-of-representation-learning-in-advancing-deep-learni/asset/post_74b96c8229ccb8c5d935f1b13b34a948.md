
## 5.4 Experiments

In this study, we conduct a comprehensive series of five distinct experiments. First, we aim to identify the optimal spatial dimension for each data modality by determining the most effective slicing window for our SIREN model. Second, we seek to establish the ideal Functa sizes corresponding to these optimal slicing windows. Third, we create a baseline for all data modalities using simple interpolation algorithms. Fourth, we perform an ablation study to investigate the correlation between the SIREN slicing dimensions and our FunctaFormers window size. Finally, leveraging the insights gained from the ablation study, we train models on ×2, ×3, and ×4 degraded data and compare their performance against state-of-the-art methods, where applicable.

### 5.4.1 Datasets

To evaluate the multimodality of our framework, we train and test our models on six distinct data modalities: images, data on a manifold, 3D-shapes, audio, video, and LiDAR. Despite the inherent differences among these diverse data modalities, our model varies only in the number of input and output neurons. We provide an overview of all datasets, with additional details in Section C.3.

**Images.** We train on DF2K, which combines the DIV2K [6] and FlickR2K [165] datasets. For our training set, we use the 800 training images from DIV2K along with the 2560 training images from FlickR2K. For ablation studies, we utilize the 100 validation images from DIV2K. For comparison against current state-of-the-art methods on super-resolution tasks, we utilized the SET5 [19], SET14 [299], Urban100 [126], Manga109 [186], and BSD100 [184] image sets.

**Manifolds.** We train on the hourly 2m surface temperature measurements in Kelvin from 1971 to 2020, a subset of the ERA5 [118] climate reanalysis. The measurements are then monthly averaged by the hour of day, giving a total of 14.4k temporal measurements in polar coordinates. The measurements are evenly spaced between \(\lambda \in [\pi/2, -\pi/2]\) and \(\phi \in [0, 2\pi)\) with step-sizes \([0.25^\circ, 0.25^\circ]\). The train, validation and test split contain 10k, 2.2k, and 2.2k samples, respectively.

**3D-Shapes.** For 3D shape super-resolution, we utilize a subset of the ShapeNet Core [38] dataset. We use the voxelized solid representation of the first 4 categories, namely Tables, Cars, Chairs and Airplanes. The dataset is split into training, validation, and test sets with 45k, 3728, and 3728 samples, respectively.

**Audio.** For the audio data, we use 100-hour clean subset of the LibriSpeech [204] dataset. From this dataset, we extract the first 12k samples, trimming each sample to 5 seconds and discarding those shorter than this length. The processed data is divided into training, validation, and test sets, consisting of 10k, 1k, and 1k samples, respectively.

**Video.** We use the Adobe240 [254] dataset containing 133 videos recorded at 720p resolution and 240 frames per second, with video durations between 1 and 10 seconds. For data preprocessing, we split the videos into 1-second segments and rotate any portrait-oriented videos to landscape. The first 100 videos are allocated for training, 16 for validation, and 17 for testing.

**LiDAR.** We train on LiDAR data from the KITTI-360 dataset [164]. We disregard the global scene context and consider each vehicle at each timeframe as an independent sample. Following the methodology in [298], we define training, validation, and test splits of 20k, 2.5k, and 2.5k samples, respectively, by temporally down-sampling frames in the driving sequences. We retrieve our spatial Functa by dividing the scans into angular sectors.

### 5.4.2 Model Ablations

We implemented all models in Python using PyTorch [209]. For all experiments, we empirically determined the optimal scale factor \(\omega_0\) to be \(\omega_0 = 30\) for images, videos, LiDAR and data on manifolds, \(\omega_0 = 50\) for audio, and \(\omega_0 = 10\) for 3D-shapes. Training was conducted on 8 RTX 3090 GPUs with 24 GB of VRAM each, with training times ranging from 10 to 48 hours. The random seed is 42.

**Spatial Functa.** We first determine the optimal slicing dimensions for the spatial Functa across all data modalities. We want to optimize the slicing for the spatial Functa to fit the data as well as possible. This presents an upper bound for the performance of FunctaFormer, its quality directly depends on how well the spatial Functa are able to fit the data. To achieve this, we conduct seven runs per data modality, adjusting the slicing-window dimension to fractions of {1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6, 1/12, 1/30, 1/60} along each spatial axis. Individual SIREN models consist of 3 layers with 64 hidden units. The Functa size is set equal to the dimension of a slice, meaning for example a 3-dimensional cube of shape 5 × 5 × 5 corresponds to a Functa size of 125.

All runs are trained for 1 × 10^5 steps. The learning rate starts at 3.00 × 10^-6 and is halved every 2 × 10^4 steps. For optimization, we use the Adam optimizer [144] with parameters β1 = 0.9 and β2 = 0.999. The batch size is set to 32. During training, a single slice per data point is randomly cropped to the specified slicing dimensions, and the model is trained on that specific resolution only.

In Figure 5.6 we can see a clear correlation between spatial size and reconstruction quality, with smaller spatial windows giving better results. Although this could be expected, since more features need to be represented by the same size network of 3 layers with 64 hidden units. However, every Functa is proportional to the window size, giving every data point the same representational capacity. If the base network is just a mapping between Functa and Features there should be no correlation between spatial size and reconstruction quality. Thus, we conclude that the base network plays an important role in the representation and distillation of the data and that it is able to perform better on lower window sizes. One can also see that the increase in reconstruction quality towards lower window sizes is less significant in Audio than Image and Manifold data. We explain this difference by the spatial differences between the data modalities. 1/60 in audio represents 1000 features or 83ms of data, whereas for images this represents only 12 features or a patch of shape 2 × 2 × 3. The numerical results of the ablation are provided in Section C.4.

**Functa Size Ablation.** Next, we investigate the correlation between Functa size and reconstruction quality across different base network sizes. For this purpose, we define two base models: a Large (denoted by L) and a Small (denoted by S) version. The large version contains 10 layers with 128 hidden units each, and the small version contains 3 layers with 64 hidden units. We ensure that the slice used for each data modality represents the same amount of underlying values, despite the different dimensions. Based on the results from the previous ablation study, we choose 48 as the number of underlying values. This corresponds to a slicing window of 4 × 4 × 3 × 1 for shapes, 4 × 4 × 3 for images, 6 × 8 × 1 for manifolds, and 48 × 1 for audio. We sweep over Functa sizes in the range [2, 256], keeping the learning rate, batch size, optimizer, and number of steps the same as in the spatial dimension ablation study.

With results in Figure 5.7, we observe that with small slicing dimensions, the network can achieve a near-zero reconstruction error across all data modalities, with larger Functa sizes strongly correlating with better reconstruction quality. In particular, larger models start to diverge compared to smaller models once the Functa size exceeds the represented window size of 48. Most data modalities exhibit an S-curve, with reconstruction quality saturating beyond a Functa size of 256, except for images in the small model.
