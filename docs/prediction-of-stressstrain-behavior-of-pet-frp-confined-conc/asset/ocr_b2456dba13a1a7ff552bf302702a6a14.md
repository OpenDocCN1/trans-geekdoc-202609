The schematic representation of the stress–strain behavior of PET FRP-confined concrete is illustrated in Fig. 2. Based on the findings of several empirical and theoretical investigations [8, 16, 29, 57, 66], the stress–strain behavior of PET FRP-confined concrete is impacted by multiple constituents, depending on the shape of the specimens. Circular specimens typically encompass peak compressive stress (f_c1), peak compressive strain (ε_c1), compressive strength (f_c2), compressive strain (ε_c2), ultimate compressive strength (f_cu), and ultimate compressive strain (ε_cu). The stress–strain curve, as seen in Fig. 2, exhibits a downward slope shift in the noncircular case, indicating the influence of strength (f_cs) and strain (ε_cs), associated with the same constituents observed in circular PET FRP-confined concrete. The structural integrity of the confined PET FRP concrete section is primarily impacted by factors such as the cross-sectional shape of the specimens, the properties of the concrete material, the corner radius of the noncircular specimens, and the number of PET FRP layers used for confinement.

# 2 Research Methodology

The primary focus of the present study is to develop a co-driven model based on data and comparison with mechanism models for the prediction of the stress–strain curve of PET FRP-confined concrete. Subsequently, the performance assessment and interpretation analysis of the predicted results are conducted. As seen in Fig. 1, the initial step is the establishment of the experimental database, encompassing the processes of feature selection and data collecting for both PET FRP-confined circular and noncircular specimens. The determination of the input characteristics was done by using feature selection separately for both PET FRP-confined circular and noncircular specimens. According to the facts in the database, the subsequent objective entails the acquisition of the model that exhibits the utmost precision in prediction. This will be accomplished by means of training and assessing three conventional ML models. Further, the performance of the optimal model acquired in this work is contrasted with that of the empirical model proposed by Pimanmas et al. [57]. The empirical model in their study outperformed previously proposed models in predicting the stress–strain mechanism of PET-FRP-confined concrete.

The analysis in the present study aims to predict the stress–strain mechanism of PET-FRP-confined concrete using ML and showcase the effectiveness of the ML model in terms of its predictive accuracy, generalizability, and the impact of crucial input characteristics. Lastly, the study involves verification of the decision-making foundation and predicted values of the acquired model align with the stress–strain mechanism, as determined by empirical analysis in earlier studies. The qualitative discussion of the resulting model’s outcomes is based on the SHAP feature significance and dependency. The present study employs an interpretable technique in conjunction with the stress–strain mechanism to quantitatively examine the model’s contribution allocation to the stress–strain curve of PET FRP-confined concrete. # 3 Data Collection and Preprocessing

# 3.1 Data Collection

In this study, to predict the stress–strain behavior of PET FRP-confined concrete, 21 specimens with a height of 300 mm were tested under monotonic axial compression. Several key variables were considered during the testing phase. These variables included the cross-sectional shape of the specimens, which could either be circular or noncircular (square or rectangular). Additionally, the corner radius of the noncircular specimens was varied, with values of 0, 13, and 26 mm. The number of PET FRP layers used for confinement was also varied, with choices of one, two, and three layers. The specific details of each specimen, including its geometry, dimensions, and mechanical properties, are provided in Table 1. A comprehensive description of the specimen design, the materials utilized, the process of wrapping concrete with PET FRP, the instrumentation used to collect data during testing, and the testing procedures can be found in [17].

Furthermore, to ensure the comprehensiveness and reliability of the ML methods used in this study, a substantial amount of existing data related to PET FRP-confined concrete was collected from [8, 10, 16, 20, 29, 30, 66–68]. In total, a collection of 154 data points was obtained in its entirety. Out of these data points, 103 were associated with circular specimens, while the remaining 51 data points pertained to noncircular specimens.
