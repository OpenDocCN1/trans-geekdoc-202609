This implies that using LRS FRP composites offers more benefits in terms of promoting a more ductile response in the restricted concrete [12–15]. Furthermore, LRS FRP composites have shown promise in enhancing the deformation capacity of high- and ultra-high-strength concrete, particularly in applications exposed to substantial deformations such as impact, blast, and earthquakes [16, 17]. This makes them favorable for use in specialized constructions like protective and subterranean facilities that require high ductility [10, 18].

Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) FRP, a type of LRS FRP composite made from recycled PET materials, has shown promise with a tensile rupture strain of over 7% and a relatively low elastic modulus. It offers a cost-effective solution for retrofitting existing reinforced concrete columns, especially when high deformation capacity is required [16, 18–20]. Extensive research has been conducted on the compressive behavior of FRP-confined concrete columns, both circular and noncircular, taking advantage of FRP’s high strength-to-weight ratio and shape flexibility [21–28]. Circular columns benefit significantly from FRP confinement, while noncircular columns exhibit half-effective confinement, with square columns showing smaller strength enhancements compared to circular ones [23, 25, 29]. The corner radius in noncircular columns plays a crucial role in the effectiveness of FRP confinement, as a larger corner radius leads to a more uniform confining pressure and enhanced compressive strength [7, 25, 29–31].

Various predictive models have been developed to estimate the mechanical response of concrete under FRP confinement, categorized into design-oriented [32–41] and complete analysis models [42–50]. Ozbakkaloglu et al. [51] conducted a comprehensive assessment whereby they methodically analyzed a total of 88 stress–strain models specifically designed for circular cross-section FRP-confined concrete specimens. The models provided by Al-Salloum [52] and Lam and Teng [53] performed well on limited specimens with varying corner radius ratios. Wu and Wei’s model [54] accurately predicted the material’s ultimate strength. In another study, Dong et al. [55] calculated circular section ultimate strains. Shehata et al. [56] model predicted final strength well. However, it tended to overstate strength in specimens with low confinement. In another study, a number of mechanical models were used and compared to estimate the stress–strain properties of PET FRP-confined concrete [57]. The predictive model by Pimanmas et al. [57] outperformed others. It accurately predicted the ultimate strength of specimens with sharp edges and correlates with stress–strain responses. This model performed well on PET and PEN FRP-confined circular concrete samples. These design-oriented and analysis-oriented models anticipate material behavior using mathematical models

These approaches depend on detailed models, involve assumptions, and take a long time to produce elaborate composites. Integrating heterogeneous material behavior into a single model is difficult. There is no model that can accurately anticipate the ultimate circumstances and stress–strain response for FRP-confined composites, despite much study [1].

The application of artificial intelligence (AI) has become increasingly popular in civil engineering for predicting concrete’s mechanical properties, resulting in time, effort, and cost savings. Several researchers have utilized machine learning (ML) methods to predict the compressive strength of geopolymer concrete [58–60]. Several other studies employed ML models to predict the shear strength of RC deep beams [61–63]. In their study, Keshtegar et al. [64] utilized a hybrid regression and ML model to accurately predict the ultimate condition of FRP-confined concrete. In another study, Zhang et al. [65] utilized AI techniques to forecast stress–strain curves of FRP materials. Jiang et al. [1] conducted a study that demonstrated the superior performance of data-driven ML models compared to traditional mathematical models in predicting stress–strain curves for FRP-confined concrete. This finding emphasizes the potential of ML models in accurately predicting the stress–strain behavior of PET FRP-confined concrete, particularly in the context of structural design and response prediction.

Therefore, in this study, a novel approach for accurately predicting the stress–strain curve of PET FRP-confined concrete using ML techniques was presented. The study mainly focused on predicting the key parameters in the stress–strain curve, including peak, slope change, end of transition, and maximum values. At first, a complete dataset including 154 axial compression test specimens of PET FRP-confined concrete was compiled and systematically arranged. The dataset consisted of 103 circular specimens and 51 noncircular specimens, which were utilized for the purpose of model training and testing. Three advanced ML models, namely extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), random forest regression (RFR), and k-nearest neighbors (KNN), were employed in this study to predict the mechanical properties of PET FRP-confined concrete for both circular and noncircular specimens. To evaluate the evaluation performance of three ML models, four key evaluation metrics, coefficient of determination (R²), mean square error (MSE), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE), were utilized. Based on the evaluation metrics, the XGBoost model was found to be the most accurate in predicting mechanical properties compared to RFR and KNN.
