##### 2.3.2.1 Brain lesion segmentation

In an emergency, precise localization of a brain lesion from injury is necessary for successful treatment and saving lives. However, several complexities make accurate estimation of a brain lesion’s location and size difficult, such as multiple sites, varying shape and size, and an intensity profile matching unaffected brain regions. Different conventional models have been developed but show limited performance. To address this, a two-pathway deep learning (DL) model was proposed for accurate lesion estimation and segmentation \[[46](#Chapter_2.xhtml#bk978-0-7503-2244-7ch2bib46)\]. In this model, two separate CNN architectures were designed for each pathway. Each pathway processed different dimensions and scales of the same input images, which were later combined to provide an accurate boundary and size estimate of the lesion. The initial pathway received the original 3D images, while the second pathway received down‑sampled images. The initial pathway captured the detailed shape of the lesion, whereas the second pathway captured its location. The output feature maps of the second pathway were up‑sampled to the original size and combined with the first pathway’s output to achieve overall lesion segmentation. The joint output was then fed into a fully connected conditional random field (CRF) network for final estimation.

The input images of traumatic brain injuries were collected from 66 patients and participated in the ISLES‑SISS challenge. The Dice similarity coefficient was 0.59. The method outperformed the random forest method by a large margin. Although it performed better than previous methods, the DL model’s performance is considered low for clinical significance. ##### 2.3.2.2 Brain tumor segmentation

Several classification methods exist for brain tumors. Among brain tumors, gliomas have the highest fatality rate. Gliomas are further divided into low‑grade and high‑grade (LGG and HGG). The high‑grade type is more aggressive. Accurate segmentation and classification of gliomas into LGGs and HGGs are critical for treatment planning, follow‑up, and monitoring. Due to its invasive nature, biopsy is risky and dangerous, so non‑invasive imaging is preferred. However, manual glioma segmentation is error‑prone and tedious. MR images present challenges such as intensity variation, inhomogeneity, and scanner artifacts. Consequently, automated brain tumor segmentation techniques are preferred. In \[[47](#Chapter_2.xhtml#bk978-0-7503-2244-7ch2bib47)\] a deep learning–based CNN model was proposed for tumor segmentation from MR images. Experts categorized the tumors into necrosis, edema, non‑enhancing, and enhancing classes. Before training the CNN, the pre‑processed images were used for dataset normalization.

Two CNN models of different depths were trained separately for LGGs and HGGs. The LGG model consisted of four convolutional layers, two max‑pooling layers, and three fully connected layers. The HGG model had six convolutional layers, two max‑pooling layers, and three fully connected layers. The shallower LGG model did not improve by increasing depth. Two datasets were used: the first contained 65 MR scans, and the second contained 274 MR scans. Patch‑based segmentation was employed, yielding 335,000 patches for LGG and 450,000 patches for HGG, which were used to train and test the respective CNN models. Three segmentation schemes were evaluated: complete, core, and enhancing. The Dice similarity scores were 0.88, 0.83, and 0.77, respectively.
