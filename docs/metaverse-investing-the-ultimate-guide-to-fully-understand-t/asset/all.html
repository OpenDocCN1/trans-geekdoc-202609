Introduction

 

The metaverse concept was already living and fast-developing even before Facebook was renamed Meta, and CEO Mark Zuckerberg talked about "the metaverse" at length. There's no getting around it: the metaverse has here, and it's most likely here to stay.

So, what exactly is the metaverse? Is it as significant as some corporations claim, or is it merely a fleeting fad that will fade away in a few months? Do you need to know everything there is to know about the metaverse, and should you get engaged before it explodes anymore? This book goes into the metaverse concept, discussing its past, present, and, most importantly, future.

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

Chapter One

The True Meaning of the Metaverse

 

As an illustration of how confusing and confounding the concept "metaverse" may be, consider the following experiment: In a sentence, replace the term "the metaverse" with the word "cyberspace." Ninety percent of the time, the meaning will remain unchanged. Rather than referring to a single piece of technology, the term refers to a broad change in the way we engage with it as a result of technological advancement. The phrase may become outdated even if the technology that it originally specified becomes widely available, which is a distinct possibility.

It is made up of two technologies: virtual reality, which is defined by permanent virtual surroundings that remain even when you are not playing, and augmented reality, which blends elements of the physical and digital worlds to create a more realistic experience. However, it is not required that certain places be solely accessible via virtual reality or augmented reality. A virtual world that is available via PCs, gaming consoles, and even phones, such as Fortnite, may be considered metaversal in its accessibility.

Also referred to as the "digital economy," it is a platform that allows users to create, purchase, and sell things. Interoperability allows you to move virtual objects like as clothes or automobiles from one platform to another in more idealized metaverse settings. In real life, you may purchase a shirt at the mall and then wear it to the movies that night. The majority of platforms already restrict users' virtual identities, avatars, and inventories to a single platform; however, a metaverse may allow you to create a persona that you can carry with you wherever you go as easily as transferring your profile image from one social networking site to another.

When you hear descriptions like the ones above, it might be tough to comprehend what all of this entails since you might think, "Wait, doesn't that already exist?" when you hear them. For example, the World of Warcraft's World of Warcraft is a permanent virtual environment where users may purchase and sell items. Rick Sanchez may get an understanding of Martin Luther King Jr. via virtual encounters like as concerts and a Fortnite exhibition. You may enter your virtual world by putting on an Oculus Rift virtual reality headset. What is the definition of the term "metaverse"? Is it only a few new forms of video games that have sprung up?

In a nutshell, the answer is yes and no. To refer to Fortnite as "the metaverse" is like to referring to Google as "the internet." Even if you spend a significant amount of time in Fortnite conversing, purchasing, studying, and playing games, this does not guarantee that you will understand all there is to know about the metaverse.

The Metaverse has its roots in science fiction, which is where the majority of commonly held beliefs about it come from. As shown in films such as Ready Player One and The Matrix, the Metaverse is often portrayed as a form of digital "jacked-in" internet — a depiction of genuine reality but based in a simulated (sometimes theme park-like) environment comparable to that seen in Ready Player One and The Matrix While it is conceivable that such experiences will exist in the Metaverse, they will be restricted in the same way that films like Tron presented the Internet as a real digital "information superhighway" made up of bits.

When it comes to the Metaverse, we are at a loss for words, just as it was hard to imagine the Internet of 2020 in 1982 — and much more difficult to explain it to individuals who have never "logged" into it. Core characteristics, on the other hand, may be identified.

Persistence is defined as the fact that it never "resets,""pauses," or "terminates," but rather continues without interruption.

In addition to pre-planned and self-contained events, the Metaverse will be a live experience that exists in real time for everyone, just as it does in "real life."

Allow for an endless number of concurrent users while yet keeping each user's feeling of "presence" — everyone may be a member of the Metaverse and participate in a certain event/place/activity at the same time and with their own agency if the Metaverse supports it.

The economy will work completely - people and corporations will be able to develop, own and invest in a huge variety of "labor" that creates "value" that is prized by others, and they will be able to sell and be reimbursed for this labor.

Be a blend of the digital and physical worlds, private and public networks/experiences, open and closed platforms, and open and closed systems.

As a result of this integration, you will be able to share unparalleled data, digital items/assets, content, and so on across each of these experiences – for example, your Counter-Strike rifle skin may be utilized in Fortnite or given to a friend on/through Facebook. In the same way that a vehicle produced for Rocket League (or even Porsche's website) can be transferred to function in Roblox, so can a vehicle made for Roblox. Today's digital world operates in a similar manner to a shopping mall, with each store having its own unique ID cards, money, proprietary units of measurement for goods such as shoes or calories, and specific dress rules, among other things, to differentiate itself from the others.

Be populated by "content" and "experiences" that have been generated and run by a diverse group of contributors, some of whom are self-employed individuals, while others may be informally structured groups or commercially focused corporations, among others.

There are a few of more notions that might be crucial to the Metaverse, albeit they aren't commonly recognized. One of these worries is whether or not individuals will be able to use a single digital identity (or "avatar") across all of their contacts with other people. However, although this might be advantageous, it is doubtful since each of the leaders of the "Metaverse era" will still need their own identifying systems in the near future. There are a few major account systems available today, but none of them is accessible throughout the whole web, and they often stack on top of one another with limited data exchange and access capabilities (e.g., your iPhone is based around an iOS account, then you might log into an application using your Facebook ID, which itself is your Gmail account).

Another point of contention is the degree to which interoperability is required for the Metaverse to be considered "the Metaverse," rather than just a development of the Internet as it now exists. Many people also ask whether there can be just one operator in a genuine Metaverse, as opposed to a virtual one (as is the case in Ready Player One). The definition (and success) of a Metaverse, according to some, requires a strongly decentralized platform built primarily on community-driven standards and protocols (similar to the open web) and a "open source" Metaverse operating system or platform (though this does not rule out the presence of dominant closed platforms in the Metaverse).

Another idea to consider is the fundamental communications architecture of the Metaverse. While the Internet is now built on individual servers "talking" to one another as required, others argue that the Metaverse should be "wired" and "managed" on constant many-to-many connections, rather than on individual servers "talking." A agreement has yet to be reached on how this would function or the degree to which decentralization would be necessary.

It's also good to think about what the Metaverse is commonly likened to, even if the comparison is incorrect. While these parallels are most likely a component of the Metaverse, they are not the Metaverse in and of their own right. The Metaverse, on the other hand, isn't...

A "virtual world" is an universe that exists only in the mind of the user. It has been decades since virtual worlds and games featuring AI-driven characters, as well as those filled with "genuine" individuals in real time, first appeared on the scene. A synthetic and fictitious world, rather than a "meta" (Greek for "beyond") cosmos with a single objective, is being explored (a game).

Virtual worlds, such as Second Life, are frequently referred to as "proto-Metaverses" because they (A) do not have game-like goals or skill systems; (B) are persistent virtual hangouts; (C) provide near-synchronous content updates; and (D) feature real people who are represented by digital avatars in the virtual world. But they are inadequate properties for the Metaverse, as will be discussed later.

A virtual reality (VR) experience is a means of immersing oneself in a virtual environment or region without really being there. To qualify as a Metaverse, one must have a sensation of being present in a digital environment. Essentially, it's the equivalent of claiming to live in an affluent city just because you can see and stroll about it.

Another kind of economy - the "digital and virtual economy" - is already in existence. Individual games, like as World of Warcraft, have had functioning economies for a long time, in which real people exchange virtual goods for real money or do virtual labor for real money for a lengthy period of time. Furthermore, platforms such as Amazon's Mechanical Turk and technologies such as Bitcoin are based on the use of people, corporations, and computer power to do virtual and digital labor, which is why they exist. As of right now, we are doing large-scale transactions via entirely digital markets for purely digital goods and services for purely digital activities.

Fortnite is a "game," yet it features various Metaverse components. For example, it (A) mashes together IP; (B) has a consistent identity that spans numerous closed platforms; (C) is a doorway to a varied variety of experiences, some of which are fully social; and (D) pays content providers. While it has improved since the release of Ready Player One, it still falls short in terms of what it can accomplish, how far it can go, and what "work" can be done (at least for now). However, although the Metaverse may have certain game-like objectives, may include games, and may make use of gamification, it is not a game in and of itself, nor is it focused on achieving particular goals.

A "virtual Disneyland or theme park" is a kind of virtual theme park. The number of "attractions" will be limitless, and they will not be "planned" or "programmed" in the same way that Disneyland is, nor will they all be about fun or entertainment in the same way that Disneyland is. Furthermore, the distribution of engagement will have a very long tail, as would the distribution of engagement.

Nobody requires a new way to access applications, and doing so "in VR" (for example) would not unlock/enable the types of value promised by a successor Internet. However, there will be a "new app store." The Metaverse's architecture, paradigms, and aims are vastly different from those of today's Internet/mobile architecture, paradigms, and objectives.

In other words, the Metaverse isn't simply another YouTube or Facebook-like platform where numerous individuals may create, share, and monetise material, with the most popular content accounting for just a tiny part of overall consumption. The Metaverse is a "new user-generated content platform." This will be a place where genuine empires will be built, and where these well-capitalized businesses will be able to totally possess a consumer, manage APIs and data, and govern unit economics, among other things, in the Metaverse. Furthermore, as is the case with the web, a dozen or so platforms are likely to dominate a significant percentage of user time, experiences, material, and other aspects of their lives.

With this in mind, imagine being in The Nightmare Before Christmas, where you may stroll into any event or activity and possibly satisfy nearly any of your desires, all from a single starting point or planet that is home to everyone you know. It is for this reason why hypertext is so vital. However, it's important to remember that the Metaverse is not a game, a piece of technology, or an online experience in the traditional sense. It's the equivalent of stating that World of Warcraft, the iPhone, or Google are all representations of the Internet. Digital environments, gadgets, services, and websites, among other things, are included in this category. The Internet is comprised of a diverse range of protocols, technologies, tubes, and languages, as well as access devices, information, and communication experiences of varying kinds. And the Metaverse will be included.

 

 

Chapter Two

Why the Metaverse is Important?

 

While it is expected to fall short of the grandiose predictions of science fiction writers, the Metaverse as a new computer platform or content medium is expected to generate billions of dollars in value. As it is currently envisioned, the Metaverse will serve as a doorway to the majority of digital experiences, as well as a critical component of all physical experiences and the next major labor platform.

Although there is no "owner" of the Internet, the benefit of being a big participant in such a system is self-evident – although there is no "owner," practically all of the largest Internet corporations are among the world's top ten most valuable public companies. There will unquestionably be substantially bigger economic advantages if the Metaverse serves as a functioning "successor" to the web, with far wider reach, time spent, and commercial activity than the web now provides. It doesn't matter how you look at it, the Metaverse should offer a similar range of opportunities as the web, with new companies, products, and services handling everything from payment processing to identity verification to hiring to ad distribution to content production to security, among other things. Many incumbents will undoubtedly lose their seats as a consequence of this outcome.

As a whole, the Metaverse has the potential to alter the way in which we distribute and market our current natural resources. For millennia, the developed economies have altered as labor, and real estate scarcity, have risen and fallen in demand. The Metaverse will provide opportunities for would-be workers living outside of cities to participate in the "high value" economy via virtual labor. As more consumer expenditures are directed toward virtual products, services, and experiences, we will witness more changes in where we live, the infrastructure that is built, and who performs certain responsibilities in the future. Take, for example, the notion of "Gold Farming." Many "players," many of whom were employed by a bigger firm and many of whom were from lower-income countries, would spend their workdays accumulating digital resources for sale outside or within the game not long after in-game trade economies were established. These sales were typical of higher-income players in the Western hemisphere. The diversity and worth of this "work" will increase in parallel with the Metaverse, despite the fact that most "labor" is menial, repetitive, and confined to a few applications.

Creating the Metaverse is a huge undertaking.

When it comes to operating properly, the Metaverse will need an abundance of new technologies, protocols and businesses as well as technological advances and scientific discoveries. In addition, it won't arise out of nowhere; there won't be an obvious line between "Before Metaverse" and "After Metaverse." As numerous items, services, and capacities link and meld with one another, the Internet of Things will eventually develop. In any case, it's important to think about three critical variables that must be in place.

To think about these three categories procedurally, I think about them in terms of the Book of Genesis - first, one must establish the underlying universe ("concurrency infrastructure"), then describe its laws of physics and regulations ("standards and protocols"), and finally, one must fill it with valuable content ("Content") that evolves and iterates in response to selection pressures. This means God does not create and construct the world as if it were a miniature model, but rather enables one to grow over a mostly empty tableau, and so on). Infrastructure for Concurrency

To put it simply, at its most basic level, the technology does not currently exist to support hundreds, much alone millions, of people all participating in the same synchronized experience. Take, for example, the Marshmello concert in Fortnite, which took place in 2019. In all, 11 million people tuned in to see the action in real time. They did not, however, do so in a coordinated fashion. It is estimated that almost 100,000 occurrences of the Marshmello concert took place, each of which was slightly out of sync and restricted to 100 participants. Epic can probably handle more now, but not in the hundreds of thousands or even millions, as it did in the early days.

Not only does the Metaverse demand infrastructure that does not already exist, but the Internet was never intended to be used for anything even somewhat close to what is now being offered in the Metaverse. After all, it was established for the purpose of transferring data from one computer to another. As a result, the majority of the Internet's underlying systems are built around a single server that communicates with another server or an end-user device over the Internet. It is still in use today, despite its age. Despite the fact that there are billions of people on Facebook today, each user has their own connection to the Facebook server and does not share this connection with anybody else. The effect is that whenever you access material from another user, you are only receiving the most recent information available from Facebook. Despite the fact that text dialogues were the first pseudo-synchronous apps, you are still giving largely static data to a server and retrieving the most up-to-date data from it as, when, where, and how you need it. When it comes to persistent (as opposed to continuous) communication, the Internet was never meant to be used for it, much alone persistent communication that was synced in real time with many other individuals.

To work properly, the Metaverse necessitates the use of technology more comparable to video gaming and video conferencing. These meetings are made possible by permanent connections that allow the participants to keep each other up to date in real time and with a degree of accuracy that is not required by other programs. However, they are seldom used by a big number of people at the same time: most video chat systems have a capacity of just a few people, and once you reach 50 people, you'll have to "live stream" a broadcast to your viewers rather than sharing a two-way connection. These interactions are not need to be genuine, and they are not in fact genuine.

Towards this aim, one of the reasons why the battle royale genre has only recently gained popularity in video games is because it is only now feasible to play live with a large number of other players. The most popular games, such as Second Life and World of Warcraft, have been available for almost two decades, but they have largely mimicked the experience by "sharding" and splitting players into separate "worlds" and servers. For example, Eve Online may have more than 100,000 players "in the same game" at the same time, despite the fact that they are distributed over numerous realities (i.e., server nodes). Consequently, a player is only ever exposed to a small number of other players and has very limited interaction with them at any one moment. Furthermore, switching to a different galaxy demands the termination of one server and the loading of another (which the game manages to "conceal" narratively by forcing players to travel at light speed to span the immensity of space). The system came to a grinding stop if/when Eve Online reached combat with hundreds of people, as it did many times. Because the game's overall gameplay dynamic was predicated mostly on large-scale, well designed ship-based fighting, it was a success.

Especially if it had been a "fast-twitch" game like Rocket League or Call of Duty, it would have made the game completely unplayable.

Several firms, like the aptly named Improbable, are putting up significant effort to solve this obstacle. This tremendous computing problem, on the other hand, runs counter to the Internet's original architecture and goal.

Standards, Protocols, and the Adoption of These Norms The Internet as we know it today is made possible by standards and protocols for visual display, communication, graphics, file loading, data, and many more functions. The range of products covered covers anything from well-known to obscure companies. In addition, the WebSocket protocol, which supports practically every kind of real-time communication between a browser and other servers on the internet, has been assigned to the GIF filetype. In the Metaverse, S&Ps will need to be more wider, more complicated, and more robust than they already are. Because interoperability and live synchronous experiences are important, we'll have to prune some existing standards and "standardize" around a smaller set of standards per function—for example, today's image file formats include.GIF,.JPEG (and variations),.PNG (and variations),.BMP (and variations), TIFF (and variations), and WEBP (and variations). Despite the fact that today's web is built on open standards, most of it is closed and proprietary in nature. A comparable set of technologies is used by Amazon, Facebook, and Google; but, they are not meant to function together in the same way that Ford's wheels are not designed to fit into a General Motors vehicle frame. Moreover, many organizations are adamant about avoiding linking their systems or exchanging their data with one another. However, although such activities may improve the total worth of the "digital economy," they also undermine the hyper-valuable network effects that are associated with it, making it simpler for people to shift their digital lives elsewhere.

This will be a very tough and time-consuming task to do. The greater the value and interoperability of the Metaverse, the more difficult it will be to obtain an industry-wide consensus on concerns such as data security, data permanence, forward-compatible code evolution, and transactional functionality. As a result, new regulations for censorship, communication control, regulatory enforcement, tax reporting, and the prevention of online radicalization will be required, in addition to existing rules for a plethora of other challenges with which we are now wrestling today.

The Metaverse's standards will not be created in advance, in contrast to the traditional method of standard-setting, which involves face-to-face discussions, negotiations, and arguments. Unlike the standard process, which is considerably messier and more organic, meetings and views alter on an ad hoc basis in the standard process.

Consider SimCity to be a Metaverse equivalent in terms of gameplay. To get the best results, the "Mayor" (i.e., the player) would first design their mega-metropolis before building it from the ground up. You cannot, however, just "build" a city with 10MM inhabitants in the game, just as you cannot in real life. You begin by concentrating on a small town and optimizing it for maximum efficiency (e.g., where the roads are, schools are, utility capacity, etc.). Construction occurs everywhere over the town as it expands, with some "old" parts being demolished and new ones being built in their place on occasion but only when a problem (such as a power outage) or tragedy happens (a fire). However, in contrast to SimCity, there will be numerous mayors rather than just one, and their desires and motivations will regularly collide with one another.

We don't know precisely what the Metaverse will demand, much alone how, when, or via which applications and organizations current standards will be transmitted to the new environment. Consequently, it is critical to analyze how the Metaverse grows rather than just the technical standard that it adheres to in order to be effective.

The 'On-Ramp' Experiential Learning.

In the same way that Metaverse standards cannot be "announced," consumers and companies will not accept a would-be proto-Metaverse merely because it is provided.

Take a look at the facts of life. It is not necessary to build a mall big enough to accommodate a hundred thousand people or a hundred businesses in order to attract a single client or a single company. When existing infrastructure and activities are not sufficient to fulfill current civilian or business needs, "town squares" organically grow around them. Last but not least, any meeting area — whether it's a bar, basement, park, museum, or amusement park — is frequented mostly due to the people or things that are already there, rather than because it's a goal in and of itself.

In the case of digital interactions, the same may be stated. Facebook, the world's biggest social network, did not become so successful because it announced itself to be a "social network," but rather because it started as a collegiate hot-or-not site, grew into a digital yearbook, and eventually became a photo-sharing messaging service with messaging. The Metaverse, similar to Facebook, must be "populated," not merely "populated," and this population must then fill in the spaces in this digital environment with activities to do and things to consume, rather than simply "populate."

The fact that Fortnite is a video game or an interactive experience is much too limiting and hurried when considering it in these terms. Fortnite started off as a video game, but it quickly evolved into a social media platform with millions of followers. Teenagers would come home from school and talk on the phone for three hours every day from the 1970s to the 2010s. They are now talking about Fortnite with their friends, but not about Fortnite themselves. Instead, they talk about other things such as school, movies, sports, the news, guys and girls, and so on. Indeed, Fortnite does not have a narrative or an IP; rather, the storyline focuses on what occurs on the island and who is there to experience it.

In addition, Fortnite is quickly becoming a platform for other businesses, intellectual property, and stories to express themselves through gameplay and storytelling. This contains, most notably, Marshmello's live performance from last year. However, the number of similar examples has exploded in the intervening period. During a larger in-game audience-interactive event in December 2019, which included a live mocap interview with director J.J. Abrams, Star Wars: The Rise of Skywalker released a clip from the highly anticipated film that could only be accessed through the game's Fortnite mode. Furthermore, this occurrence was explicitly mentioned in the film's opening sequences. Weezer has set up a unique island where fans can receive a first listen to their next album before anybody else (while dancing with other "players"). In addition, Fortnite has developed a number of themed "limited-time modes" based on popular brands such as Nike's Air Jordan and Lionsgate's John Wick movie series. Sometimes, these "LTMs" transform a section of the Fortnite map into a mini-virtual world that, when accessed, alters the visuals, items, and playstyle of the game to mimic the appearance and gameplay of another game. Some of these settings have included the Borderlands world, Gotham, Batman's home city, as well as the old west.

Thus, Fortnite is one of the only places where the IPs of Marvel and DC may be found in the same game. In Gotham City, you may literally dress up as a Marvel character and chat with others who are wearing NFL jerseys that have been legally sanctioned by the league. This is the first time that anything like this has occurred in history. It will, however, be critical to the Metaverse's development.

A more general statement may be made that Fortnite has developed a new sub-economy in which "players" can produce (and sell) their own content. Skins (digital clothes) and dances (emotes) are examples of this kind of content. Although it began as a small group of games, it has swiftly expanded to embrace any and all new games and experiences that make use of the Fortnite engine, assets, or aesthetics. Every kind of gamer will find something to their liking, from simple treasure hunts to complex mash-ups of the Brothers Grimm with parkour culture to a 10-hour sci-fi epic that spans numerous universes and timeframes. For one thing, Fortnite's Creative Mode is already starting to seem like a prequel to the Metaverse. Once inside, players may choose among thousands of "doors" (i.e., space-time rifts) that will take them to one of the thousands of separate worlds, where they can play with up to 99 other players at the same time.

As creative director Donald Mustard has made more obvious, the game's long-term ambitions are at the heart of the matter.

Epic Games.

One of the most telling indicators of Fortnite's potential is its ability to persuade a large number of seeming rivals to collaborate (or to demonstrate early "interoperability" with one another). Currently available on every major entertainment platform – including mobile devices such as iOS and Android as well as consoles such as PlayStation, Nintendo, PC, and Xbox – Fortnite allows for seamless cross-platform play across multiple identity/account systems, payment methods, social graphs, and generally locked ecosystems. Long have the main gaming platforms battled against such an experience, arguing that enabling such an experience would undermine their network effects and reduce the need to purchase their proprietary equipment. This resulted in the inability of a buddy with Call of Duty on PlayStation to play with a friend who had Call of Duty on Xbox, despite the fact that both Sony and Microsoft knew they wanted to.

As a matter of fact, IP owners are reluctant to allow their characters and tales to be incorporated into other IPs. This occurs from time to time (e.g., several Marvel v DC comic book crossovers and video games). However, it is particularly rare to see it in an experience that they do not editorially control, much alone one that is centered on unpredictability (not even the Fortnite creative team knows what will happen in 2021), and with such a vast spectrum of intellectual property.

No amount of emphasis can be placed on the significance of organic evolution. If you "announced" your intention to construct a Metaverse, these parties would never accept interoperability or commit their intellectual property to you. However, just as P&G cannot declare, "Oh, Facebook isn't for us," the vast majority of counterparties have no option except to join in conversation. They're almost certainly eager to become a part of the "game" Because Fortnite has grown so popular and unique that most opponents have no option but to take part in the competition. Fortnite is a platform that is highly valuable.

Fortnite is the name of the game. Please, however, re-ask the question in a year or two.

Epic is bringing much more to its Metaverse-building efforts than simply a visually appealing on-ramp to the world. Additionally, Epic Games is the owner of Unreal Engine 2, the second-largest independent gaming engine, and is responsible for the operation of Fortnite, which was allegedly a side project for the company. Thousands of games (to keep things simple) presently make use of its "stack" of tools and software, which makes it simple to interchange assets, integrate experiences, and share user profiles with other games and developers. Epic's game engine has evolved over time to the point where it now supports a variety of conventional media experiences in addition to video games. Using Unreal for the filming and rendering of Disney's The Mandalorian, director Jon Favreau was able to virtually visit the digital sets to frame shots and arrange actors. If Disney so desires, audiences will be able to freely tour most of these sets since the majority of the locations and materials are already in place. Increasingly, Unreal is being utilized for live events outside of the realms of film and television, such as Fox Sports' NASCAR coverage.

Even While this may be true, the Metaverse requires that everyone, not only well-staffed corporations and technologically competent people, be able to create and exchange "content" and "experiential" material and experiences. Epic purchased Twinmotion in April of last year for the express aim of achieving this goal. However, rather than VFX engineers or game designers, the company was/is focused on providing straightforward, icon-based software that allows "architecture, construction, urban planning, and landscaping professionals" to create realistic, immersive digital landscapes based on Unreal "in seconds," rather than complex procedural animation software. The CEO and co-founder of Epic Games, Tim Sweeney, has stated that this means there are now three ways to create in Unreal: the traditional "coding" engine itself, the more simple and "visual" Twinmotion, and the Fortnite Creative Mode, which is intended for users who have no prior experience in programming or design. Over time, it is probable that each option will grow more competent, user-friendly, and well-integrated. It is becoming more important for Epic to have a "Online Services" suite, which enables developers to offer cross-platform play across platforms such as Sony, Microsoft, Nintendo, PC, iOS, and Android while using Epic's account systems and social network (which has 1.6B player connections). While this is exceptional, Microsoft paid $400 million for PlayFab and spent millions more to maintain Xbox Live, while Amazon paid $1.5 billion for both GameSparks and GameLift, which are both service providers for game producers that need a significant number of servers and tools to operate their online games. Valve does not supply server infrastructure, but its Steamworks solution does so by offering free matchmaking and account services to developers — but only for Valve's primary business, the Steam Store. Valve does not provide server infrastructure. Essentially, this is how Epic's approach to Online Services is exposed. Epic does not demand a subscription fee, in contrast to today's industry leaders. Additionally, it is completely free to use with any engine, platform, or game. As a bonus, since it can expand up to the same size as Fortnite's player network, it allows any game to take use of the world's biggest player graph to help restart their user populations. Epic believes that, despite the apparent benefit of such a service, it is "more valuable if provided for free" because it expands the company's already massive social graph, makes it much easier for additional games to "speak" to one another, and allows players to transition more smoothly from one experience to another. As a result of all of this, Epic's reliance on Fortnite to develop the Metaverse comes into doubt. While Epic Online Services is currently in private beta testing, the company has claimed that it will be offered to the general public in Q2 2020 and will support "hundreds or thousands" of games when it becomes available. In addition, it's important to note that all of this reduces Epic's dependence on Fortnite in terms of long-term Metaverse-building initiatives.
