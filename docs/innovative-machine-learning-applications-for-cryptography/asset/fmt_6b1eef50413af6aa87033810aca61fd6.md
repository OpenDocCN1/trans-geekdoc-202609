
## Suggested Method for Selecting a Sparse Wrapper Head

In the context of a suggested method for selecting a sparse wrapper head, a vector $\bar{v}$ is constructed, which contains $N$ high-dimensional points $x_i$. The Euclidean distances between any two points $x_i$ and $x_j$ inside the vector $\bar{v}$ are calculated to determine a conditional probability $P_{j|i}$. The conditional probability functions as a metric of similarity between the point $x_i$ and the point $x_j$. The conditional probability $P_{j|i}$ represents the likelihood of point $x_i$ selecting $x_j$ as its neighbour, given that the probability density of the characteristics follows a normal distribution (Gaussian) centred at $x_i$. Hence, the conditional probability experiences an augmentation when taking into account adjacent data points, but for data points that are far, $P_{j|i}$ becomes almost insignificant. The conditional probability $P_{j|i}$ may be represented mathematically as follows:

$$P_{j|i} = \frac{\exp(-||x_i - x_j||^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(-||x_i - x_k||^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)} \tag{5}$$

where $\sigma_i$ is the mean of the Gaussian distribution centred at the position $x_i$. The likelihood of a point being next to itself is 0, since the approach primarily aims to depict pairwise similarities $P_{i|i}$.

A conditional probability, $Q_{j|i}$, may be derived to characterise the similarities between the map feature points $y_i$ and $y_j$, which are the low-dimensional analogues of the high-dimensional $x_i$ and $x_j$, respectively.

$$Q_{j|i} = \frac{(1 + ||y_i - y_j||^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq i} (1 + ||y_i - y_k||^2)^{-1}} \tag{6}$$

Since this method is solely interested in simulating pairwise similarities, the conditional probability $Q_{i|i}$ is similarly zero ($Q_{i|i}=0$).

In order to obtain a low-dimensional representation of the data that minimises the discrepancies between $P_{j|i}$ and $Q_{j|i}$, the dimensionality reduction mapping is performed. The gradient descent technique is used frequently in sparse wrapper to achieve this for a certain cost function $C$, such that:

$$C = \sum_i KL(P_i || Q_i) \tag{7}$$

The Kullback-Leibler divergence function of $P_i$ is denoted by $KL(P_i || Q_i)$. The Kullback-Leibler divergence, denoted as $KL(P || Q)$, is the distance between two discrete probability distributions $P$ and $Q$, and it is defined as,

$$KL(P || Q) = \sum_i P(i) \log \frac{P(i)}{Q(i)} \tag{8}$$

The expectation of the logarithmic difference between $P$ and $Q$ is given by Equation (9) above. Any random continuous variable $x$ in $P$ and $Q$ may be treated in the same way.

$$KL(P || Q) = \int p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} dx \tag{9}$$

where $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ are the distributions of likelihoods of $P$ and $Q$.

The Kullback-Leibler divergence function may be rewritten as when $P$ and $Q$ are evaluated over continuous sets $X$ and $P$.

$$KL(P || Q) = \int p(x) \log \frac{dP}{dQ} dx \tag{10}$$

where $\frac{dP}{dQ}$ in (7) is a derivative of Radon and Nikolayevich Nikodym of $P$ with respect to $Q$.

By using the chain rule for factorization, we can rewrite (10) as:

$$KL(P || Q) = \int p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} dx \tag{11}$$

It is commonly agreed that the entropy of $P$ with respect to $Q$ is given by the preceding equation.

If we have two absolutely continuous probability densities $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ such that $P \ll Q$ and $Q \ll P$, then the Kullback-Leibler divergence from $P$ to $Q$ is given by for every measure on the set $z$.

$$KL(P || Q) = \int p(z) \log \frac{p(z)}{q(z)} dz \tag{12}$$

To minimise the cost function in equation (13), a recursive gradient descent process is used using the following form:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial y_i} = 4 \sum_j (P_{j|i} - Q_{j|i} + P_{i|j} - Q_{i|j}) (y_i - y_j) (1 + ||y_i - y_j||^2)^{-1} \tag{13}$$

$y_i$ and $y_j$ are located at different coordinates on a map.

To continuously update the coordinates of the map at each iteration, the factorised gradient is combined with an exponentially declining sum of previous gradients. This update is governed by the provided formula.

$$Y^{(t)} = Y^{(t-1)} + \alpha \frac{\partial C}{\partial Y} + \beta(t) (Y^{(t-1)} - Y^{(t-2)}) \tag{14}$$

where $\frac{\partial C}{\partial Y}$ represents the gradient value at iteration $t$ is denoted as $\beta$, the learning rate is represented by $\alpha$, and $\alpha(t)$ Momentum is a significant element that is added to the gradient to improve the detection of local minima.

It is important to emphasise that the computational cost of Non-Negative Matrix Factorization is $O(N^2)$. However, the use of the cost function for selecting a sparse wrapper head is limited since it increases quadratically as the number of objects $N$ increases. Hence, it is only appropriate for datasets that consist of a limited number of input items, usually ranging from a few thousand. As datasets get larger, it is expected that the learning process would slow down, while the memory requirements will rise.
