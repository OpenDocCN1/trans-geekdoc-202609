In addition, since only 2 mg samples are needed for each collecting of spectra, less requirement of quantity of sample is possibly a typical superiority of Raman techniques in the specialized application of large-scale tree genetics and breeding programs. Raman spectroscopy combined with regularization algorithms have been successfully applied to the task of predicting holocellulose content and lignin content of poplar clones (Gao et al., 2021; Gao, Shu, et al., 2022). Even predicting results of these models are acceptable yet, it is still wondering whether the predicting accuracy and efficiency of the models can be improved further or not.

Various classification and prediction works related to lignocellulosic materials and its chemical components were successful accomplished owing to the application of classical machine learning (ML) algorithms which include support vector machine regression (SVR), decision tree (DT), and random forest (RF) (Daassi-Gnaba et al., 2018; Ehrhart et al., 2021; Gao, Zhou, Liu, et al., 2022; Gu & Henrik Andersson, 2010; Souza et al., 2020; Xing et al., 2019). Meanwhile, it was reported that advanced gradient boosting machine (GBM) algorithms, such as Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), are able to handle complex mathematical problems, such as classification, regression, and others (Chen & Guestrin, 2016; Ke et al., 2017; Prokhorenkova et al., 2018). Based on these meaningful works, lignin content predictive models by ML algorithms based on Raman features have been recently established (Gao, Zhou, Liu, et al., 2022). The result indicated that ML algorithms achieved a more accurate and efficient prediction of lignin content than regularization algorithms (Gao, Zhou, Liu, et al., 2022). Therefore, the significant improvement in predictive accuracy of holocellulose content is expected if the machine learning (ML) algorithms is going to be implemented instead of the regularization algorithms. However, it should be noticed that the position and intensity of lignin-related peaks are quite different from that of the holocellulose-related ones, and then the useful features extracted from Raman spectra in establishing holocellulose content predictive model (HCP-Model) will be another data matrix. It will certainly bring question to how transferred the methodology of predicting lignin content by the ML algorithms to the new target, namely holocellulose. Furthermore, it also needs to be verified that the comparative result among different ML algorithms is consistent or not between HCP-Model and lignin content predictive model.

Therefore, the objective of the present work is to apply the ML algorithms to develop the HCP-Models. the holocellulose-related Raman features extracted from Raman spectra were applied as the input variables and the measured holocellulose content was applied as the output variable. Meanwhile, the regularization algorithms (RR, LR, and ENR) were also adopted to make a comparison with those ML algorithms. The accuracy of each predictive model is going to be examined and compared, and then the optimal model will be selected. Furthermore, after progressively tuning hyper-parameters, the HCP-Models with high predictive accuracy will be anticipated. It is hoped that the optimal predictive model is favorable of quick and efficient estimating holocellulose content in large-scale tree genetics and breeding programs.

# 2. Materials and methods

# 2.1. Materials

125 groups of poplar samples were harvested from ten poplar clones. The information of these ten poplar clones is depicted elaborately in the published study (Gao, Shu, et al., 2022; Gao, Zhou, Liu, et al., 2022). All the samples were processed into 40–60 mesh and 100–120 mesh wood powder, for measuring the holocellulose content by WCM s and collecting the Fourier-transform Raman (FT-Raman) spectra, respectively. 80% of the samples were randomly divided into training set, and the

remaining 20% of the samples were used as the test set.

# 2.2. Determination of holocellulose

The sodium chlorite method was applied to measure the holocellulose content, referring to the China national standard of GB/T 2677.10–1995. First, introduce a distilled water, glacial acetic acid, and sodium chloride into a container with the extracts-free sample and soak in water at 75 °C for one hour. Then, 0.5 mL of acetic acid and 0.6 g of sodium chlorite were added to the solution, and the water bath was continued for one hour. Repeat the above operation of adding sodium chlorite and acetic acid for sufficient and successive cycles of chlorination (four times in this study). After the adequate chlorination, the solution was filtered and washed. The residue was measured by gravimetrically, namely the measured holocellulose content (Liang et al., 2020; Rabemanolontsoa & Saka, 2012).

# 2.3. Collection of Raman spectra

Poplar samples used to collect FT-Raman spectra were processed into pellets from ~2 mg of 100–120 mesh sample, in triplicate. Then, all the FT-Raman spectra were collected from 64 scans with a 4 cm−1 spectral resolution by using a FT-Raman spectroscopy from Bruker (Vertex 70RAMII). The fluorescence was exited at 1064 nm and the Raman shift ranged from 200 to 3600 cm−1 was collected with the laser power of 500 mW. And the range of 800 to 3600 cm−1 was applied for analysis.

# 2.4. Spectral processes and Raman features extracted from Raman spectra

The extraction of Raman features is challenging because of the spectral noise, baseline drift, and signal overlap, which can be alleviated by spectral processes of denoise, baseline correction, and the calculation of deconvolution. Refer to the previous works (Gao et al., 2021; Gao, Shu, et al., 2022; Gao, Zhou, et al., 2022), Savitzky-Golay and adaptive iteratively reweighted penalized least squares (airPLS) can be carried out to smooth the spectra and correct the spectral baseline, respectively. Then, Gaussian and Lorentzian deconvolution was carried out to overcome the signal overlap for calculating the single signal intensity of each specific peak. Therefore, information of peaks located at 1095, 1122, 1149, 1331, 1378, 1462, and 2895 cm−1 was extracted successfully, which were all related to holocellulose (Agarwal, 2009; Agarwal & Ralph, 1997; Wiley & Atalla, 1987). Refer to the previous work, the peak intensity of 2945 cm−1 was qualified to normalize the intensities of holocellulose-related peaks (1095, 1122, 1149, 1331, 1378, 1462, and 2895 cm−3) (Gao, Shu, et al., 2022). Therefore, the normalized peak intensities of holocellulose-related peaks (I1095, I1122, I1149, I1331, I1378, I1462, and I2895), which were called Raman features in this study, were obtained and applied as the predictor variables of the HCP-Models.
