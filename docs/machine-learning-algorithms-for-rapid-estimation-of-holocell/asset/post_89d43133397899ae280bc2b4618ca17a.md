## ABSTRACT

In this study, regularization algorithms (RR, LR, and ENR), classical ML algorithms (SVR, DT, and RF), and advanced GBM algorithms (LightGBM, CatBoost, and XGBoost) were applied to build the holocellulose content predictive models of poplar based on features extracted from Raman spectra. Evaluation results of models indicate that classical ML algorithms show higher predictive accuracy than regularization algorithms, and the advanced GBM algorithms better than the classical ML algorithms. Furthermore, models built by CatBoost and XGBoost can estimate holocellulose content with high predictive accuracy of test R² above 0.93 and test RMSE less than 0.29%. It provides the heretofore best precision of holocellulose content predictive model based on Raman spectroscopy so far for our knowledge. Therefore, it is suggested that Raman spectroscopy coupled with ML algorithms is a promising tool for predicting the holocellulose content in poplar which can be applied in large-scale tree genetic and breeding programs.

## 1. Introduction

Holocellulose, the sum of cellulose and hemicellulose, is a water-insoluble carbohydrate fraction which can be extracted from wood materials through the chlorination method by removing the lignin (Basu, 2018). It is an important fraction because its hydrolyzed products were composed by various monomeric sugars, which are able to convert into useful compounds or to ethanol via fermentation (Isikgor & Becer, 2015; Ma et al., 2012). Due to the large differences in holocellulose content among different types of lignocellulosic biomass, species, or even genetic resources, the selection of resources with expected holocellulose content becomes an essential task. Meanwhile, the variation also provides a good opportunity to scheme a large-scale genetic breeding program for selecting and finding the suitable germplasm with the expected holocellulose content. However, for doing so, intensive labor and tedious testing are required when the traditional wet chemical methods (WCM) are used since there are plenty of samples that need to be measured. Besides that, because each genetic sample is prepared from small seedling or growth cone of living tree, the spendable quantity of the sample sometimes fails to meet the required replica when using WCMs. Moreover, the WCMs are also criticized for the huge usage of toxic reagents during the determination. Therefore, a rapid, accurate, eco-friendly, and low sample quantities requirement method is needed.

Several spectroscopic methods, such as near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy, middle-infrared (MIR) spectroscopy (Tucker et al., 2001) and Raman spectroscopy, combined with suitable algorithms have been successfully predicted the content of chemical components of lignocellulosic materials instead of traditional WCMs. NIR has been used to predict the lignin content in wood (Liang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2019), lignin content in pears (Wu et al., 2021) and holocellulose content in wood (Liang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2019). However, it is reported that both the position and intensity of water-related peaks of NIR spectra will be changed by the sample moisture and testing ambient humidity (Gordobil et al., 2021). So, it possibly results a disturbance on the quantitative results if the sample moisture and testing atmosphere is out of rigid control. Conversely, Raman spectroscopy, which is insensitive to the sample moisture, provides a great deal of spectral information with high chemical information content, which is characterized by a series of peaks related to specific chemical bonds (Jermyn et al., 2016). These valuable information brings high sensitivity and specificity, such as, to analyze the differences in the amounts of major chemical components (Agarwal & Ralph, 2012; Gao, Shu, et al., 2022; Gao, Zhou, et al., 2022; Gao, Zhou, Liu, et al., 2022), and crystallinity of cellulose (Agarwal et al., 2010, 2018). In addition, since only 2 mg samples are needed for each collecting of spectra, less requirement of quantity of sample is possibly a typical superiority of Raman techniques in the specialized application of large-scale tree genetics and breeding programs. Raman spectroscopy combined with regularization algorithms have been successfully applied to the task of predicting holocellulose content and lignin content of poplar clones (Gao et al., 2021; Gao, Shu, et al., 2022). Even predicting results of these models are acceptable yet, it is still wondering whether the predicting accuracy and efficiency of the models can be improved further or not.

Various classification and prediction works related to lignocellulosic materials and its chemical components were successfully accomplished owing to the application of classical machine learning (ML) algorithms which include support vector machine regression (SVR), decision tree (DT), and random forest (RF) (Daassi-Gnaba et al., 2018; Ehrhart et al., 2021; Gao, Zhou, Liu, et al., 2022; Gu & Henrik Andersson, 2010; Souza et al., 2020; Xing et al., 2019). Meanwhile, it was reported that advanced gradient boosting machine (GBM) algorithms, such as Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), are able to handle complex mathematical problems, such as classification, regression, and others (Chen & Guestrin, 2016; Ke et al., 2017; Prokhorenkova et al., 2018). Based on these meaningful works, lignin content predictive models by ML algorithms based on Raman features have been recently established (Gao, Zhou, Liu, et al., 2022). The result indicated that ML algorithms achieved a more accurate and efficient prediction of lignin content than regularization algorithms (Gao, Zhou, Liu, et al., 2022). Therefore, the significant improvement in predictive accuracy of holocellulose content is expected if the machine learning (ML) algorithms is going to be implemented instead of the regularization algorithms. However, it should be noticed that the position and intensity of lignin-related peaks are quite different from that of the holocellulose-related ones, and then the useful features extracted from Raman spectra in establishing holocellulose content predictive model (HCP-Model) will be another data matrix. It will certainly bring question to how transferred the methodology of predicting lignin content by the ML algorithms to the new target, namely holocellulose. Furthermore, it also needs to be verified that the comparative result among different ML algorithms is consistent or not between HCP-Model and lignin content predictive model.

Therefore, the objective of the present work is to apply the ML algorithms to develop the HCP-Models. The holocellulose-related Raman features extracted from Raman spectra were applied as the input variables and the measured holocellulose content was applied as the output variable. Meanwhile, the regularization algorithms (RR, LR, and ENR) were also adopted to make a comparison with those ML algorithms. The accuracy of each predictive model is going to be examined and compared, and then the optimal model will be selected. Furthermore, after progressively tuning hyper-parameters, the HCP-Models with high predictive accuracy will be anticipated. It is hoped that the optimal predictive model is favorable of quick and efficient estimating holocellulose content in large-scale tree genetics and breeding programs.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Materials

125 groups of poplar samples were harvested from ten poplar clones. The information of these ten poplar clones is depicted elaborately in the published study (Gao, Shu, et al., 2022; Gao, Zhou, Liu, et al., 2022). All the samples were processed into 40–60 mesh and 100–120 mesh wood powder, for measuring the holocellulose content by WCM and collecting the Fourier-transform Raman (FT-Raman) spectra, respectively. 80% of the samples were randomly divided into training set, and the remaining 20% of the samples were used as the test set.

### 2.2. Determination of holocellulose

The sodium chlorite method was applied to measure the holocellulose content, referring to the China national standard of GB/T 2677.10–1995. First, introduce a distilled water, glacial acetic acid, and sodium chloride into a container with the extracts-free sample and soak in water at 75 °C for one hour. Then, 0.5 mL of acetic acid and 0.6 g of sodium chlorite were added to the solution, and the water bath was continued for one hour. Repeat the above operation of adding sodium chlorite and acetic acid for sufficient and successive cycles of chlorination (four times in this study). After the adequate chlorination, the solution was filtered and washed. The residue was measured by gravimetrically, namely the measured holocellulose content (Liang et al., 2020; Rabemanolontsoa & Saka, 2012).

### 2.3. Collection of Raman spectra

Poplar samples used to collect FT-Raman spectra were processed into pellets from ~2 mg of 100–120 mesh sample, in triplicate. Then, all the FT-Raman spectra were collected from 64 scans with a 4 cm−1 spectral resolution by using a FT-Raman spectroscopy from Bruker (Vertex 70RAMII). The fluorescence was exited at 1064 nm and the Raman shift ranged from 200 to 3600 cm−1 was collected with the laser power of 500 mW. And the range of 800 to 3600 cm−1 was applied for analysis.

### 2.4. Spectral processes and Raman features extracted from Raman spectra

The extraction of Raman features is challenging because of the spectral noise, baseline drift, and signal overlap, which can be alleviated by spectral processes of denoise, baseline correction, and the calculation of deconvolution. Refer to the previous works (Gao et al., 2021; Gao, Shu, et al., 2022; Gao, Zhou, et al., 2022), Savitzky-Golay and adaptive iteratively reweighted penalized least squares (airPLS) can be carried out to smooth the spectra and correct the spectral baseline, respectively. Then, Gaussian and Lorentzian deconvolution was carried out to overcome the signal overlap for calculating the single signal intensity of each specific peak. Therefore, information of peaks located at 1095, 1122, 1149, 1331, 1378, 1462, and 2895 cm−1 was extracted successfully, which were all related to holocellulose (Agarwal, 2009; Agarwal & Ralph, 1997; Wiley & Atalla, 1987). Refer to the previous work, the peak intensity of 2945 cm−1 was qualified to normalize the intensities of holocellulose-related peaks (1095, 1122, 1149, 1331, 1378, 1462, and 2895 cm−1) (Gao, Shu, et al., 2022). Therefore, the normalized peak intensities of holocellulose-related peaks (I1095, I1122, I1149, I1331, I1378, I1462, and I2895), which were called Raman features in this study, were obtained and applied as the predictor variables of the HCP-Models.
