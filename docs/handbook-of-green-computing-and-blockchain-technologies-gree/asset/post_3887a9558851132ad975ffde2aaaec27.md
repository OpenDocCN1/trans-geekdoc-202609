The most standard alternative way to achieve consensus in blockchain is Proof of Stake (PoS). It depends on the fact that clients who own more coins are more committed to the persistence and proper operation of the system, and thus are the most suitable to bear the responsibility of securing the system. Essentially, the idea behind using PoS is to shift opportunity costs from outside the system to inside the system.

The algorithm randomly selects the node responsible for the creation of each block, based on the number of coins they have. A common criticism is that this method doesn't offer incentives to choose the right block (known as the nothing at stake issue). In addition, it is negative as it promotes the rich getting richer. PoS was initially used by Peercoin and later in Nextcoin, Nxt [65], Pine for and Ethereum. A variant of PoS is Delegated PoS (DPoS) of BitShares, Monax, Lisk, or Tendermint. In BitShares [66], elected witnesses verify checks and timestamps of transactions by including them in blocks. The election is performed via a voting system, and each time a witness successfully creates a block it is reviewed. This methodology allows witnesses to set block latency, block rate and demand transactions per second.

The Leased Proof of Stake (LPoS) allows clients to rent stakes to different nodes, with the goal that they will more likely be selected for block creation, increasing the number of eligible participants, and thus reducing the likelihood of the network being controlled by a single group of nodes. Rewards are mostly shared. The Proof of Burn (PoB) [67] proposes burning coins, that is, sending them to an obvious unspendable address, to create a new block. Like PoW, PoB is hard to do and easy to verify, however, oddly requires no energy consumption. Additionally, PoB has some economic outcomes that contribute to a steadier environment. Nem's [68] approach to managed consensus, called Proof of Importance (PoI), relates an importance score to each account, thus building a reputation system in the network. The possibility of being selected to create a block depends on this value; its calculation also considers the amount of coins and the number of transactions performed. All in all, profitable network activity is also rewarded, not just the amount, encouraging cooperative behavior of users.

Other extended varieties are Proof of Activity (PoA), a hybrid approach that combines both PoW and PoS, and the Elapsed Time Test (PoET) made by IBM that uses a random selection of leader to mine each block based on runtimes inside secure execution environments. The Proof of Capacity (PoC) [69], also called proof of capacity or space, uses available hard drive space instead of computing resources. This approach is used in Permacoin, SpaceMint, and Burstcoin. Private blockchains have specific features, since the number of participants is generally lower than public blockchains and are semi-trusted. They are generally based on a design with a predefined set of endorsements. These systems, therefore, require specific consensus modules that fit these attributes. Raft [72] which separates key parts of consensus, for example, leader election, log replication and security, and enforces a higher level of consistency to reduce the number of states that need to be considered. Sifter [73], treats the blockchain as a replica, executing operations and comparing the output of each copy. If there are divergences between versions, the transaction is not supported. Another variant of PBFT is the Byzantine agreement Federated Byzantine Agreement (FBA). In FBA each node keeps a list of trusted people and monitors things for these people to accept a transaction before being considered committed. It is used in Ripple [74]. FaB [75] is another variant that uses majority and partial majority concept. The majority is a set of nodes enough to agree, the partial majority is a subset of a majority with the ability to convince another given community about the strategy. HDAC [76] is a system, currently being implemented, which proposes an IoT Contract and M2M Transaction Platform based on Multichain.

Finally, the HC Consensus, proposed in Hydrachain, depends on a quick overview of validators of which nearly 33% are malicious. To summarize, consensus mechanisms in public blockchains have been completely proposed but insufficiently formally analyzed. It is necessary to understand the guarantees they offer and their weaknesses before using them [77].
