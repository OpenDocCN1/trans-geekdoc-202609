## 11.6 Smart Contracts

In 1993, Nick Szabo described the smart contract as “An electronic protocol that executes the terms of an agreement.” One of the essential features of a smart contract is that it has a way to facilitate or self-execute contractual terms. Until the advent of blockchain technology, this was not viable. Blockchain has ended up being the ideal technology to help smart contracts. Also, smart contracts have contributed significantly to the evolution of blockchain, moreover this coupling has led to a second generation of blockchains, commonly known as Blockchain 2.0. The combination of self-executing contracts in a trusted environment without centralized control promises to change the way current business is conducted. Fundamentally, the smart contract code is dealt with on the blockchain, likewise, each contract is identified by a unique address, and for clients to work with it, they simply send a transaction to this address.

The correct execution of the contract is realized by the blockchain execution model. Smart contracts present a ton of advantages, for instance, cost reduction, speed, precision, ability, and straightforwardness that have strengthened the presence of different new applications in a wide range of fields. Although Bitcoin offers a basic scripting language, it has proven insufficient, which has led to the rise of new blockchain platforms with enhanced smart contract support. The most prominent smart contract platform is Ethereum [8]. Ethereum is a blockchain with a Turing-complete programming language that permits the definition of smart contracts and decentralized applications. The code in Ethereum’s smart contracts is written in “Ethereum virtual machine code,” a low-level, stack-based bytecode language. Sometimes, financial smart contracts require that the relevant parties be informed about specific states and events. This information is provided by so-called oracles. These components are fundamental for the effective operation of smart contracts in the real world, yet authentication, security and trust in oracles must be provided [45].

The expected benefits of smart contracts do not come without cost, as they are vulnerable to a range of attacks [46–48] that bring new challenges. Delegating contract execution to computers brings with it several issues, as it makes them susceptible to specific problems such as hacking, bugs, corruption, or communication failures. Bugs in contract coding are particularly critical as a result of the irreversible and unchanging nature of the design. Systems to verify and ensure the correct operation of smart contracts are critical for them to be widely adopted and trusted by customers and suppliers. The formal specification of contract logic, and its correctness, are research areas where efforts need to be made in the years to come [49]. Essentially, smart contracts traditionally have clauses or conditions that are not quantifiable. In this sense, there is still much work to be done to express the conditions of contracts in smart contracts, so that they are representable and quantifiable for a machine to execute them. Furthermore, mechanisms are needed to enable users to select and understand smart contracts [50].

## 11.7 Legal Issues

The absence of a central authority, the non-existent issuer, and thus the complete lack of oversight in Bitcoin is an attractive and at the same time hazardous phenomenon. Bitcoin users are often accused of using the system for illegal purposes, and thus the technology is associated with promoting or facilitating illicit activities. Bitcoin, as the first decentralized cryptocurrency, has faced many challenges [51]. From one perspective, regarding its value, some experts claim it is a bubble [52] and that it will collapse completely [53], while others estimate that its value will reach $100,000 in 10 years [54]. The European Central Bank has abandoned its potential as a monetary innovation [55]. However, regarding the lack of regulation, various countries are developing new laws, attempting to regulate the use of virtual currencies (a map of Bitcoin regulatory status is available at [56]).

The current situation has many shortcomings and is clearly the reason for its recent decline [57]. Real-world effects on monetary systems are a major concern, as they can directly and contradictingly affect blockchain applications based on that currency. Different private and permissioned blockchain applications have definitely arisen. These are blockchains that grant access permissions to a predefined group or set of participants. This can bring several advantages to support and audit components – for example, key recovery or transaction recovery, and can similarly help improve security and reduce transaction latency. In fact, attractive business opportunities have emerged in transaction confirmation for bitcoins that would soon no longer be critical if regulators managed this responsibility. The risk of mining pools controlling the network, along with other deficiencies, favors the development of such blockchains, and governments are clearly encouraged by a regulated and controlled use of this technology in various applications. However, this means that the trustless network will lose trust in an external trusted third party, losing part of its essence.

Moreover, this can create bottlenecks if contracts incorporate centralized components. The features of these blockchains are closer to those of distributed databases. Obviously, the best way to expand trust in this technology could be the responsibility of governments or possibly large consortia of organizations in their development. Promoting activities in this direction will be useful [58, 59]. Currently, specific data is scattered across various sectors: government, schools, organizations, etc. The data is spread across different sectors and accessing it is redundant, however, when these sectors report to the same authority, e.g., the government. This hinders access to data despite the absence of a reliable service that ensures the data. A reliable and universal digital identity associated with every individual's data would be impractical at present. After all, every country has its own laws and rules. Initiatives such as Alastria [60] aim to involve various entities in the operation of a public federated blockchain, from public notaries to schools and private companies. They aim to create a public and legal wallet for every individual. Organizations can also be part of the federation. In this context, each digital wallet could be an electronic proof of assets, organizations where he/she has worked, academic degrees, etc. This data could be used in a reliable, secure way for specific organizations. For example, in a coordinated recruitment process, candidates could share their academic degrees and work experience data with recruiters. This data is reliable and verifiable by definition. These initiatives are the best way to expand blockchain within government institutions, as well as the first step in creating a standardized and regulatory framework for blockchain systems. Moreover, this could likewise support individuals' rightful access to their data, data mobility between countries, the reduction of sensitive data, and an expected balance between individuals, organizations, government, and universities. However, this also enables a fundamental process to obtain essentially private data, so the privacy and security discussed in the remainder of the paper should essentially align with these initiatives.
