A different classification based on the number of subdivisions can be made resulting in recursive and non-recursive subdomaining. In recursive partitioning a model is divided into 2<sup>n</sup> subgraphs, while in the non-recursive method the number of subdivisions is arbitrary. Non-recursive graph theoretical methods have been developed by Farhat and Lesoinne [3] and Kaveh and Roosta [4]. The algebraic graph theoretical method of recursive spectral bisection has been suggested by Simon and Pothen et al. [5]. In mathematical optimization methods are represented in the form of an objective function [6]. The non-deterministic optimization methods such as simulated annealing and genetic algorithms are used to minimize the considered objective function. Other methods which have been used for decomposition are based on either graph theoretical optimization or algebraic graph theoretical optimization. These methods consist of two stages. In the first stage, the domain is partitioned into several subdomains, and in the second stage the primary partitioning is altered until the optimality criteria is satisfied to a required degree.

Recently many algorithms have been developed for domain decomposition. An efficient algorithm should be general purpose, i.e. applicable to arbitrary domains, and should be efficient enough to decompose large domains with a reasonable computational effort. The latter is dealt with in parallel in order to reduce the time and the effort of decomposition.

This chapter presents a hybrid method for domain decomposition of finite element meshes employing graph theoretical algorithms and neural network methods. The presented approach uses graph theory for partial decomposition, followed by a neural network approach to complete the bisection. Examples are included to illustrate the efficiency of the present method.

# 17.2 Definitions from Graph Theory

A graph G consists of a set elements called nodes (vertices) N(G) and a set of elements called members (edges) M(G) together with a relation of incidence which associates two distinct nodes with each member, known as its ends. Two nodes of a graph are called adjacent if these nodes are the end nodes of a member. A member is called incident with a node if it is an end node of the member. The degree of a node is the number of members incident with that node.

A subgraph G<sub>i</sub> of a graph G, is a graph for which N(G<sub>i</sub>) ⊆ N(G) and M(G<sub>i</sub>) ⊆ M(G), and each member of G<sub>i</sub> has the same ends as in G. A node n<sub>i</sub> is called adjacent to a subgraph G<sub>j</sub>, if it is adjacent to a node of G<sub>j</sub>.

A path is a finite sequence P<sub>k</sub> = {n<sub>0</sub>, m<sub>1</sub>, n<sub>1</sub>, ..., m<sub>p</sub>, n<sub>p</sub>} whose terms are alternately distinct nodes n<sub>i</sub> and distinct members m<sub>i</sub> for 1 ≤ i ≤ p and n<sub>i-1</sub> and n<sub>i</sub> are two end nodes of m<sub>i</sub>.
