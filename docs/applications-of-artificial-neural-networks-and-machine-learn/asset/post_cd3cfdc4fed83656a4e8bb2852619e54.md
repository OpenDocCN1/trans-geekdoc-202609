## Chapter 22 Development of Predictive Models for Shear Strength of HSC Slender Beams Without Web Reinforcement Using Machine-Learning-Based Techniques

### Abstract
The present chapter presents two robust methods to predict bond strength between FRP reinforcement and masonry units. The M5’ algorithm as a rule based method is used to develop practical equations for estimating bond strength. The MARS algorithm besides its high predictive ability is used to determine the most important parameters in predicting bond strength. To develop the models, a comprehensive database including 575 test series is collected from different sources in literature for the first time. The reinforcement width, ratio between widths of FRP reinforcement and masonry unit, tensile strength of support, axial strength of reinforcement and bond length are the five influential parameters considered to estimate the maximum bond strength.

### 22.1 Introduction

This chapter is based on the predictive models for shear strength of HSC slender beams without web reinforcement using machine-learning based techniques developed by Ref. [1]. In the last 30 years, the application of high strength concrete (HSC) have been remarkably increased in field due to significant development in building and materials technology. Several advantages including having better mechanical properties, structural efficiency and economically beneficial in terms of both structural and aesthetic aspects made the HSC as a widely used material in the construction of high-rise buildings and long-span bridges in all over the world. The definition of HSC has been changed parallel to the developments of its properties in recent years. HSC can be defined as a concrete with compressive strength significantly beyond and more than double from what is used in normal practice (normal strength concrete, NSC). Different design codes and researchers stated different limits for compressive strength of concrete for demarcating between NSC and HSC materials. In the present study, the definition of HSC by ACI 363R-10 [2], which is defined as a concrete with compressive strength more than 40 MPa, has been followed.

One of the most crucial failures in reinforced structures made of HSC or NSC materials is the shear failure. Failure due to the shear in beams with HSC is brittle and occurs suddenly without warning. Several researchers have investigated experimentally and analytically to evaluate shear carrying capacity of reinforced concrete members. It has been found that several mechanisms including the shear transfer in the compression zone, aggregate interlock across crack face, stirrups crossing the shear crack, and dowel action of longitudinal reinforcing bars crossing the crack can be involved to provide shear resistance of RC beams [3]. In beams without web reinforcing stirrups, crossing the shear crack mechanism cannot be considered as internal contributing forces to shear resistance. It has been found that the shear failure mechanism in concrete members reinforced longitudinally and but without transverse reinforcement under bending varies significantly and the size of member and shear span-to-depth ratio are the two main parameters that influence on the shear failure mechanism. In fact, the shear span to depth ratio (a/d) determines the failure modes of RC beams and divides them into deep beams (<2.5) and slender beams (>2.5). For deep beams with a/d approximately less than 2.5, the arch action occurs [4]. For the slender beams with a/d greater than 2.5, the shear strength of reinforced member with longitudinal steel bars is a function of a/d as well. For these members, a/d represents the interacting effect of the moment and shear at a section on the shear strength. Accurate prediction of the shear behavior of reinforced concrete (RC) beams unlike to flexural behavior, which can generally be predicted well, is a challenging work due to the complexity of the shear transfer mechanism.

Furthermore, there are remarkable differences between NSC and HSC beams without web reinforcing [5–10]. It has been reported that the fracture surface in HSC beams is smooth and develops along the transition zone between the matrix and aggregates whereas it is rough in NSC beams. Also internal shear transferring mechanisms show different contributions for different concrete strengths. At higher concrete strengths, the aggregate interlocking does not contribute greatly because of the smooth fracture planes and the straight cracks, which do not go around the aggregate particles [11, 12].

Shear strength prediction of HSC slender beams without web reinforcement is still contentious. There is no unified rational theory explaining the interaction of the three internal contributing forces to shear resistance, especially for HSC. Various design equations are available to estimate shear strength of reinforced concrete beams without stirrups. Their accuracy, however, seems limited for slender reinforced HSC beams as these equations were empirically developed using predefined forms and with experimental data mainly generated for a limited number of influential parameters.

Recently, machine learning approaches have been successfully employed to overcome these limitations in many different problems of civil engineering (e.g. [13–23]). In this regard, particularly, Elsanadedy et al. [12] very recently applied regression models and neural networks for predicting the shear strength of HSC slender RC beams without stirrups. Their results showed that ANN approach generally provides better predictions than regression approach in term of accuracy. However, ANN implementation suffers from several drawbacks. The major disadvantage of this approach is that the ANN training process is achieved through a gradient descent algorithm on the error space, which can be very complex and may contain many local minima. Furthermore, trial and error processes are required to determine the network structure. In addition, ANN method does not give enough insight into the generated models and are not as easy to use as the empirical formulas.

Considering these drawbacks and the unsatisfactory of existing equations, this chapter proposed two alternative approaches for predicting shear strength of HSC slender beams without web reinforcement. Among soft computing approaches, the GMDH network and MARS algorithms are known as self-organized and non-parametric methods to model and discover the behaviors of unknown or complicated systems based on given input–output data points [24–26]. The main advantage of GMDH and MARS methods in comparison with ANN method is that the dependencies between input parameters and output parameter are represented in parametric form as an equation while these dependencies are hidden within neural network structures in ANN method.

To develop new predictive models based on GMDH and MARS models, a comprehensive existing database very recently used by Elsanadedy et al. [12] containing 250 experimental tests was employed. The developed GMDH model related the shear capacity to the longitudinal steel ratio, the shear span-to-depth ratio, compressive strength of concrete, the size of the beam specimens, and the size of coarse aggregate. The results of the developed GMDH and MARS were compared to the most common existing equations and also to multiple nonlinear regression (MNLR) approach developed in this study through statistical error indicators. Results confirmed that the developed GMDH and MARS models outperformed the existing equations and the developed regression-based equations in terms of both accuracy and safety aspects. The relative importance of significant parameters dealing with shear strength is also investigated through sensitivity analysis. The robustness of proposed models was also verified through a parametric analysis.

This chapter outlines as follows: the existing design equations and their limitations are given in the following section. The GMDH and MARS algorithms are described next in the Sect. 22.3. The applied data set and modeling process are presented in Sect. 22.4 which is followed by the results and discussion section. Finally, the summary and conclusion are presented in Sect. 22.6.
