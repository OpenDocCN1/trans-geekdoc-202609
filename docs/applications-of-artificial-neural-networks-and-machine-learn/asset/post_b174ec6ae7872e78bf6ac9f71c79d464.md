
## Chapter 6
BP and RBF Neural Networks for Predicting Displacements and Design of Schwedler Domes

**Abstract** In this chapter, efficient neural networks are trained for predicting the displacements and design of Schwedler domes using the Radial Basis Functions and Backpropagation neural networks. Programs are developed for accurate distribution of applied forces and wind load to the nodal points of these domes.

### 6.1 Introduction

In this chapter, BP and RBF neural networks for predicting displacements and design of Schwedler domes [1]. Space structures are often used for covering large spans. Sport stadiums, assembly halls, exhibition centers, swimming pools, shopping arcades and industrial buildings are typical examples of structures where large unobstructed areas are essential and where minimum interference from internal support is required. Space structures are often classified as grids, domes and barrel vaults.

Configuration processing and data generation for space structures can be simplified using the concepts from formex algebra, Nooshin [2]. Though an optimal analysis can be carried out using usual matrix displacement method [3], however, for large-scale problems, the analysis is usually time consuming due to a large number of equations being involved. Therefore approximate methods are beneficial in the process of nonlinear analysis and optimal design.

Neural networks provide a powerful tool for approximate analysis and design of space structures. Such networks are trained using backpropagation [4–7] and counterpropagation networks [8–10].

In the present chapter, efficient neural networks are trained for predicting the displacements and design of Schwedler domes using NeuralWorks software [11]. Single layer domes with varying the spans between 42.5 and 65 m, with an increment of 2.5 m, are considered. Radial Basis Functions (RBF) and Backpropagation (BP) networks are employed for training and testing these nets. For accurate distribution of the applied forces and wind load on the nodal points, additional programs are developed. Analysis and design are carried out employing ANSYS software [12] for preparing the training and testing pairs.

### 6.2 Structural Model

The model selected for the study of this chapter is a single layer Schwedler dome with ribbed members are considered as beam elements joined together rigidly and diagonal members are taken as bar elements Fig. 6.1. The spans are varied between 42.5 and 65 m with the steps of 2.5 m. The opening angle of the domes is chosen from the angles between 45 and 67.5° A typical Schwedler dome used in this study is shown in Fig. 6.1a. Simple supports are considered for the exterior ring of the dome. The number of intervals between the ribs is considered as 20 and for rings this number is taken as 8.

In order to simplify the design, and due to symmetry, 8 types of cross sections are considered for the members of a typical rib. The span and deflection at the apex of the dome are illustrated in Fig. 6.1b.

### 6.3 Analysis and Design

The analysis and design of structures are performed by *ANSYS* [11], and programs are written to evaluate the nodal forces under the dead and live loads, and the wind load. The following data is used:

- All the nodes are considered as rigid.
- Elements are selected from the tube sections available in European profile list.
- Linear analysis is performed.
- Design is carried out according to AISC-89 code.

The dead load is taken as 0.5 kN/m² and the sum of dead load and live load is considered as 2.5 kN/m² applied at the nodes.

In order to calculate the external wind pressure on a dome, the diagram suggested by the European Conventional Committee for Steel (ECCS) is used, see also Makowski [13]. In this method, the external pressure is imposed in the form of a non-linear diagram. Here a program is developed for calculating the equivalent nodal forces. These forces are calculated as

F<sub>i</sub> = A<sub>i</sub>P<sub>ei</sub> (6.1)

where A<sub>i</sub> is the area associated with node i and P<sub>ei</sub> is the average value of the external wind pressure on the area associated with node i. The wind pressure is calculated as

P<sub>e</sub> = C<sub>e</sub>C<sub>g</sub>C<sub>pe</sub>q, (6.2)

where C<sub>e</sub> is the coefficient of exposure, C<sub>g</sub> is the gust coefficient, C<sub>pe</sub> is the external pressure coefficient and q is the base pressure. In this research, C<sub>e</sub> = 1, C<sub>g</sub> = 2 and q = 50 kg/m² are adopted for calculating the wind pressure.

A total number of 100 structures are analyzed and designed from which 80 structures are used for training and 20 pairs are employed for testing the networks.

### 6.4 BP and RBF Neural Networks

In the last decade neural networks are extensively employed in structural mechanics, Refs. [4–10]. In the following, some basic concepts of neural networks is summarized, however, the interested reader may also refer to excellent textbooks on this subject [14, 15].

#### 6.4.1 Basic Concepts

Artificial neural networks (ANNs) try to simulate the functions of the brain through mathematical and computational models. ANNs are structured from a set of artificial neurons or processing units, which are arranged on a set of layers. A processing element of an ANN is a simple device that approximates the function of a biological neuron. The topology of ANNs may be in the form of feed-forward or recurrent. Learning may be supervised or unsupervised depending on the topology. Feed-forward neural networks are usually trained using supervised training procedures.

In a processing element, the input signals are translated to an output signal by an appropriate transfer function. Weights, representing the synapses, are given to input signals according to their importance. These weights w_st are the strength of connections between processing elements. After a processing element receives all of its input signals, through its input path, it computes the net sum of input according to:

net_{pt} = \sum_{s} w_{st} o_{ps}, \tag{6.3}

where w_st is the connecting weight from the source “s” layer to the target “t” layer and o_ps is the output produced from pattern p as the result of the input o_pt(s-1).

The net sum is applied to an activation function. The most commonly used activation function is the sigmoid function defined as

o_{pt} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\frac{(net_{pt} + bias)}{\sigma}}} \tag{6.4}

where bias serves as a fictitious weight of a unit value which always fires (o_pt = 1). This function is bounded within the range (0, 1) and has a continuous first derivative. The output of a unit is obtained by passing the value of the net-sum through the activation function as

o_{pt} = f_t(net_{pt}). \tag{6.5}

#### 6.4.2 Backpropagation Algorithm

The backpropagation learning algorithm was discovered by Parker [16], and it was popularized and developed into a workable process by Rumelhart et al. [17]. Numerous applications have been found for backpropagation since 1986.

Here the backpropagation (BP) neural network is briefly described. The BP consists of two types of elements: neurons (nodes) and weights (connections). Neurons are organized in layers: the first layer is called input layer, which receives stimulus from the outside environment; the last layer called output layer, which provides output or answers from the network. In between the input and output layers, there is at least one hidden layer, Fig. 6.2. The weights represent the strength of the connections, which decide the distribution of data flow across layers. Between any two neighboring layers, the neurons are fully connected; for two layers with m and n neurons, there are m x n connections. Therefore the weights between two adjacent layers can be represented as an m x n matrix.
