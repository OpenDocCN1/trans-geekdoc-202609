## Statistical analysis

Both the fCBT and the iCBT registries also recorded overall anxiety severity and impairment scale scores, allowing us to use this continuous-valued score as a baseline predictor of the outcome.²³ Other predicting variables were drawn from the multiple linked administrative registries (figure 2B). For privacy and variance reduction, we only included categorical covariates that resulted in cell sizes ten observations or above when cross-tabulated with treatment allocation.

We arrived at our sample by taking all eligible patients from the register. Our aim was to estimate the ATE between fCBT and guided iCBT. An estimate with a 95% CI no wider than 1.7 PHQ-9 points can be considered accurate in the sense of falling below a previously estimated detection threshold between “feeling better” versus “feeling the same” as before treatment.²²

For TMLE, we implemented super learners with a broad set of base learners (examples 6 and 7 in Philips and colleagues’ article⁴), including generalised linear models, Bayesian generalised linear models, generalised additive models, lasso, elastic net and ridge regressions, polynomial splines, multivariate regression splines, extreme gradient boosting, Bayesian additive regression trees, and highly adaptive lasso.²⁴⁻²⁷ TMLE requires two super learners, one predicting treatment allocation and another predicting treatment outcome.³¹⁴ Our super learners used cross-validation to automatically assign weight to the base learners with the non-negative least squares regression algorithm, with a guarantee of asymptotically better performance compared with the base learners.¹⁵,²⁴ We used the super learners to construct an estimate for ATE, using TMLE to optimise the bias-variance trade-off for the ATE parameter.¹³,²⁶ Thus, TMLE controlled for confounding by variables and their machine-learned, non-linear combinations and interactions. We followed the online and published guidance by Philips and colleagues²⁴ and van der Laan and colleagues,²⁶ using their tlverse R environment, including packages sl3 (version 1.4.4), tmle3 (version 0.2.0), and cvAUC (version 1.1.4). ## Results

A total of 32 343 registry entries were assessed for eligibility, with 8417 coming from the FPQR and 23 926 from the HUS-iCBT registry. 8025 entries from the FPQR and 18 459 from the HUS-iCBT registry were excluded, resulting in a total of 392 patients from the FPQR, forming the fCBT group, and 5467 patients from the HUS-iCBT, forming the iCBT group, for naturalistic analyses (figure 2A). 25 patients were missing baseline data and were removed from counterfactual analyses for the primary outcome, resulting in 388 (99 [26%] male and 289 [74%] female) patients in the fCBT group and 5446 (1634 [30%] male and 3812 [70%] female) in the iCBT group (figure 2A). Four (<0.01%) of 40 263 patients whose data we accessed had objected to the use of their registry data by Findata (present inclusion criteria unknown; some of the accessed registers were not included in this study but could have contained the objecting four patients).
Four (1%) of 392 patients from the fCBT group and 21 (<1%) of 5467 patients from the iCBT group were missing mostly demographic baseline data. Table 1 summarises treatment group characteristics at baseline, after removing these 25 patients (other missingness pertaining to treatment dropout did not affect table 1). The mean age of patients in the final analytic fCBT group was 40 years (SD 16; median 36 years [IQR 28–51]) and 363 (94%) of 388 spoke Finnish as their native language. In the final guided iCBT group, the mean age was 34 years (SD 12; median 31 years [IQR 25–42]) with 5092 (93%) of 5446 speaking Finnish as their native language.
A mean of 9.4 weeks (SD 3.5) passed between baseline and the last follow-up assessment in guided iCBT for those who completed the last session and 7.7 weeks (4.4) for all patients (including patients whose first and last measurements were the same one). We estimated a mean interval of 35 weeks (22.9) between the reported start and end dates of the fCBT therapy period; this estimate was based on less accurate data reported to the outsourcing registry owner (in contrast, the iCBT programme automatically collects and dates data).
In the analytic iCBT group, there was a mean of 5.5 (SD 2.1; median 7.0 [IQR 4.0–7.0]) treatment sessions. In the fCBT group, reporting the number of sessions was not obligatory due to a coupon system that predefined the number of HUS-paid sessions; therefore, these data were only available for 352 (91%) of 388 patients (mean 12.3 [SD 9.8]; median 14.0 [IQR 2.0–20.0]).
