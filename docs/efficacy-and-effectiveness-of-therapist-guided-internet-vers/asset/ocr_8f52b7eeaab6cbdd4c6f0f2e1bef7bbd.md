# Research in context

# Evidence before this study

Multiple meta-analyses indicate that cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is equally efficacious in treating depressive disorders when implemented as guided internet-delivered CBT (iCBT) compared with when delivered as the usual face-to-face CBT (fCBT). Because guided iCBT programmes use automated videos and exercises, online therapists can spend much less time per patient compared with face-to-face therapists, which can lead to significant costs savings. However, the meta-analyses rely on randomised controlled trials (RCTs), and the possibility remains that the trials do not represent the full routine-treatment population, or adequately account for the large differences in treatment adherence or other differences between fCBT and guided iCBT. Over the past few decades, the use of complementary sources of causal information has been increasingly recommended for all of medicine. One of the state-of-the-art methods for such causal triangulation is the targeted maximum likelihood estimation with machine learning. We searched PubMed (MEDLINE), with no language restrictions, for papers published from database inception to Sept 16, 2024, using the search terms: “targeted maximum likelihood” AND depression AND (“internet” OR “digital”) AND (“cognitive behavioural therapy” OR “cognitive behavioural therapy”), yielding no results. A second search, replacing terms “targeted maximum likelihood” with “causal triangulation” or with “causality”, or with “quasi-experiment”, also gave no results. Overall, causal triangulation of the fCBT-iCBT efficacy differential in depression treatment is important

given the substantial societal and monetary costs associated with depressive disorders and their treatment, which jointly imply a need to comprehensively understand the health effects of seemingly low-cost treatment policies.

# Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate quantitatively the efficacy differential between fCBT and guided iCBT in a format that is not an RCT or a meta-analysis of RCTs. We used counterfactual causal inference using targeted maximum likelihood machine learning and a unique linked registry from five distinct registry owners, combined using Finnish laws and regulations on secondary use of health and social registries. This targeting method can integrate features from both parametric and non-parametric methods to provide precise and reliable estimates of causal effects in situations in which the data are complex and could contain multiple confounding factors. We observed that the results from RCTs held true both in routinely collected data and in a posteriori-balanced (ie, causally informative) data: the guided iCBT programme was as effective and efficacious as fCBT in terms of depression symptom decline from before to after treatment (or to the last available measurement).

The risk is that clinicians might inadvertently impart their attitudes to patients, potentially leading to diminished treatment efficacy.4 RCTs are not always representative of routine practice, which might inflate effectiveness estimates.7,8 For example, people who object to digital treatments might not enrol in an RCT offering iCBT. Furthermore, samples in RCTs tend to be small relative to registries. Although meta-analyses combine RCTs to achieve larger samples, they often lack commensurate and comprehensive data on the complexity of people’s conditions and other background factors that could affect representativeness. Thus, whether therapist-guided iCBT truly is as effective as fCBT in real-world settings remains unclear. The central counterfactual question is: would the average treatment effect (ATE) still be similar if we could allocate exact replicas of the same patients simultaneously to both guided iCBT and fCBT and compare their efficacies? Although considered a gold standard, RCTs are neither the only nor a perfect method for balancing intervention group composition to avoid confounding. Researchers in medicine, psychology, and statistics argue that reliable

causal inference often requires multiple sources of evidence rather than RCTs exclusively.9–11 Many methods make assumptions that reduce bias from confounding (Figure 1). For example, estimates weighted by known inverse probabilities of treatment are free from confounding.12,13 Alternatively, if the outcome can be accurately modelled as a function of confounders and treatment allocation, an unbiased estimate of treatment effect is achievable via G-computation.14 If models for both outcome and treatment-assignment probability exist, models can be combined to achieve an augmented inverse-probability-of-treatment weighted (AIPTW) estimate that is doubly robust. This method ensures that a consistent estimate of the ATE is obtained if at least one of the models is correctly specified.14 Finally, an ensemble of machine learners (ie, super learners) maximise the probability of at least one model being correct.13,14 In our case, the aforementioned methods make the working assumption that treatment outcome depends on the treatment (ie, delivery method).
