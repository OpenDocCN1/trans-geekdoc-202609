This conservative approach effectively penalises treatments with high dropout rates compared with complete-data or imputation analysis.

# Statistical analysis

Both the fCBT and the iCBT registries also recorded overall anxiety severity and impairment scale scores, allowing us to use this continuous-valued score as a baseline predictor of the outcome.²³ Other predicting variables were drawn from the multiple linked administrative registries (figure 2B). For privacy and variance reduction, we only included categorical covariates that resulted in cell sizes ten observations or above when cross-tabulated with treatment allocation.

We arrived at our sample by taking all eligible patients from the register. Our aim was to estimate the ATE between fCBT and guided iCBT. An estimate with a 95% CI no wider than 1·7 PHQ-9 points can be considered accurate in the sense of falling below a previously estimated detection threshold between “feeling better” versus “feeling the same” as before treatment.²²

For TMLE, we implemented super learners with a broad set of base learners (examples 6 and 7 in Philips and colleagues’ article⁴), including generalised linear models, Bayesian generalised linear models, generalised additive models, lasso, elastic net and ridge regressions, polynomial splines, multivariate regression splines, extreme gradient boosting, Bayesian additive regression trees, and highly adaptive lasso.²⁴⁻²⁷ TMLE requires two super learners, one predicting treatment allocation and another predicting treatment outcome.³¹⁴ Our super learners used cross-validation to automatically assign weight to the base learners with the non-negative least squares regression algorithm, with a guarantee of asymptotically better performance compared with the base learners.¹⁵,²⁴ We used the super learners to construct an estimate for ATE, using TMLE to optimise the bias-variance trade-off for the ATE parameter.¹³,²⁶ Thus, TMLE controlled for confounding by variables and their machine-learned, non-linear combinations and interactions.
