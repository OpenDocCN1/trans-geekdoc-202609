If we were to include, as null changes, the 819 patients with only baseline PHQ-9 and the 1800 patients who were excluded due to being prescribed the programme but who never submitted a single PHQ-9 report (figure 2a), the PHQ-9 decline would have been 2·7 points (4·6; d=–0·58).

Of the 392 patients given fCBT, those who completed the PHQ-9 after treatment (316 [81%]) reported a mean PHQ-9 score decline of 3·0 points (SD 5·2; d=–0·59). For 72 (23% of 316, symptoms increased: a greater proportion than the iCBT group (p<0·0001, χ²=16·96). The full-data change in PHQ-9 was 2·4 points (4·8; d=–0·51). The raw iCBT–fCBT difference in this primary outcome measure in the total sample was statistically significant (1·1 points in favour of iCBT; t=4·55, df 455·04, Hedge's g=0·228 [95% CI –0·331 to –0·126]; p<0·0001). When including patients without the baseline PHQ-9 as not having changed score (413 patients for fCBT and 7194 for iCBT), the difference between the treatments was 0·4 points and not significant (g=–0·083 [–0·182 to 0·016]). In patients given iCBT, mean PHQ-9 scores declined less for male patients (3·1 points [SD 4·9]) compared with female patients (3·8 [5·0]; p<0·0001), but there was no gender difference among patients who completed iCBT treatment (4·5 points [5·4] in male patients vs 4·9 points [5·3] in female patients; p=0·095) or in patients who completed fCBT treatment (2·4 [4·5] vs 2·5 [4·9]; p=0·99).

The mean PHQ-9 score for both treatment groups was within the point range for moderate depression (table 1). Overall, patients with depression who were directed to fCBT differed from those directed to iCBT (third column of table 1). Thus, we considered what the treatment effect would be if all patients were given fCBT compared with giving them all iCBT by computing ATE estimates.

The super learners for treatment outcome and treatment assignment weighted partly distinct base learners (ie, algorithms) and designated as important only partly overlapping sets of variables (appendix 2 pp 13, 17). The cross-validated area-under-the-curve (AUC) performance measure for the model predicting treatment assignment to fCBT or iCBT was 0·77 (95% CI 0·74–0·79), meaning that the model was informative (a value of 0·5 indicates chance performance and 1·0 indicates perfect performance). The cross-validated coefficient of determination (R2) for the outcome prediction model was 0·031 (–0·06 without cross-validation, or r=0·24). When we targeted the outcome and treatment-assignment models to ATE between fCBT versus iCBT, the result was close to the naturalistic treatment differential (table 2). In a scenario in which all

patients were given iCBT the PHQ-9 change outcome would have been nearly the same, but slightly better, as it would have been if all were given fCBT; PHQ-9 score declined 0·745 points (95% CI 0·156–1·334) more in the therapist-guided iCBT group than in the fCBT group (table 2). The result was robust when restricting iCBT treatments to an fCBT delivery region (ie, restricting to residents of Uusimaa) and for alternative counterfactual definitions of dropout (table 2).

Recognising a particular black-box nature (appendix 2 pp 2–3) in the TMLE, we computed simpler estimates for ATE as sensitivity analyses. We took the ten most important predictors based on the super learners (appendix 2 p 17) and instead fitted a logistic regression model predicting treatment assignment and a linear regression model predicting change in PHQ-9 (appendix 2 pp 14–15). The assignment model performance was well above chance (cross-validated AUC 0·70 [95% CI 0·67 to 0·73]), although inferior to the super learner. The adjusted R2 for the outcome prediction model was 0·03. After the removal of predictors that were non-significant in both the models (appendix 2 pp 14–15), their performance remained similar (cross-validated AUC 0·70 [0·67 to 0·73], adjusted R2=0·03). Weighting by modelled inverse probabilities of treatment assignment resulted in an improved covariate balance across fCBT–iCBT samples (appendix 2 p 19).
