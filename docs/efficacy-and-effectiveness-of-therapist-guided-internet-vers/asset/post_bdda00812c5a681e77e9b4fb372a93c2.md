A targeted maximum likelihood estimator (TMLE) based on super learners has the asymptotic double-robustness property, but it is additionally efficient, well defined, and well behaved in finite samples. A TMLE integrates features from modern statistics, machine learning, and causal inference. A TMLE can optimise the bias-variance trade-off for a target parameter (e.g., ATE) instead of the treatment-prediction and outcome-prediction models. The primary motivation to use a TMLE is its ability to combine multiple desirable properties, perhaps representing the state of the art in covariate-based counterfactual causal inference. The availability of such procedures enhances the value of administrative, population-based registries, which often contain abundant aetiological information.

Developments in Nordic countries enable the linking of psychotherapy data with information in national registries. In this study, we used TMLE on these rich information sources to estimate the efficacy difference between therapist-guided iCBT and fCBT in a causally informative design that is not an RCT, thus circumventing typical challenges of RCTs while providing evidence on the same causal question. Our naturalistic data inform about effectiveness, and the counterfactual analysis informs about both effectiveness and efficacy if all real patients would receive fCBT versus guided iCBT. The new gold standard for evidence will then consist of combined inferences from RCT meta-analyses and TMLE applied to naturalistic datasets. Our investigation serves as a pragmatic proof of concept for the use of TMLE and related methods to address the question of the ATE between fCBT and therapist-guided iCBT.

Patient gender, referring to biological sex unless the individual has requested a legal change of gender, was retrieved from data available from the civil registry of Finland, with the options of male or female. We selected treatments of patients whose primary diagnosis for the treatment was depression (ICD-10 codes F32, F33, or F34.1) and when the therapist was trained in the CBT framework. All FPQR therapists have a 60-credit, 3-year training in their framework alongside their clinical work. The fCBT treatments typically contained up to 20 sessions altogether, but, in rare instances, could maximally contain 40 sessions per year, for up to 3 years.16 A session was 45 min, and a conservative estimate of work time for each session would be 60 min, including preparation and documentation. With an observed mean number of 14 sessions in short FPQR treatments,19 we estimated an 840-min average work-time expenditure per patient. Weekly sessions were recommended by the HUS Helsinki University Hospital. The people given therapist-guided iCBT were listed in the HUS-iCBT registry. Patients entered iCBT with a doctor’s prescription, with inclusion criteria for treatment being mild to moderate depression (F32.0 or F32.1), age over 15 years, and Finnish or Swedish language proficiency with motivation and technical means to work online independently. Exclusion criteria for receiving either fCBT or therapist-guided iCBT were acute suicide risk and current substance use. Further exclusion criteria for receiving iCBT were treatment-interfering psychotic or neurological behaviours, personality disorder, or chronic or bipolar depression. The suitability for either treatment was assessed by the attending physician. All guided iCBTs were delivered with the same online programme that aligns with CBT principles (including third-wave techniques such as mindfulness): the patient proceeds at their own pace while asynchronously talking with a trained therapist (e.g., a psychologist or a nurse), who also provides unprompted guidance and an occasional telephone call (ideally one per patient per programme). The time spent by a therapist per patient as recommended by the HUS Helsinki University Hospital is 11 min per week, or 77 min in total for the seven weekly sessions. Patients were recommended by the HUS Helsinki University Hospital to complete one session per week and they could proceed more slowly or faster, up to one session per day. Not progressing in 2 weeks led to a call from the therapist and, ultimately, cessation of treatment if progress did not ensue despite encouragement.

In both treatment groups, we included treatments that had started 3 months before the end of our full sampling of the registries (Dec 31, 2021), thus giving time for the completion of therapy without completion being necessary. We only included patients for whom a baseline Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)-9 score was available. Both iCBT and fCBT were free to the patient, offered by the national public health-care system.
