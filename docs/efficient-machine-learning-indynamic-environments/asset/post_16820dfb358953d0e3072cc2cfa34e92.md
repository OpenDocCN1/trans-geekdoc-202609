### 1.2 Why Static Optimization Falls Short

A rich literature narrows the efficiency gap by *static* means. Broadly applicable methods such as model compression and distillation [21, 48, 50, 166], federated learning [66, 126], and efficient architecture design [52, 76, 87] complement rapid advances in hardware and software, including mixed-precision formats [67], tensor cores [205], and high-bandwidth interconnects [110]. These techniques yield large wins, but they share a common premise: the target deployment environment (dataset, resource budget, available hardware, query complexity) is fixed *a priori*. Once the model or approach is tuned, it is frozen and deployed unchanged. In practice, however, operating conditions and available resources in ML systems are rarely fixed:

- **Resource volatility.** On edge devices, battery level, thermal headroom, compute cycles, and wireless bandwidth can swing between successive inference calls. In the cloud, ephemeral GPU leases may be preempted with little warning, serverless functions incur cold-start delays, and backbone congestion leads to network delays and throttling. Any rigid deployment must therefore over-provision resources to accommodate these variances or risk timeouts.

- **Query heterogeneity.** Interactive services—web search, recommendation, dialogue—receive a highly skewed mix of easy and hard requests. A keyword query like “weather in Pittsburgh” is correctly answered by a tiny model, whereas an open-ended prompt—“What color is your raindrop?”—may need a much larger LLM with chain-of-thought reasoning. Using a single model for both wastes compute on the median case or sacrifices quality on the tail.

- **Cluster churn.** Multi-tenant GPU clusters never sit still: new training jobs arrive, spot instances are reclaimed, nodes fail or enter maintenance, and resource demand shifts. Job schedules and resource allocations computed offline grow stale within minutes, forcing either slack capacity or frequent re-solving, limiting the scale of problems they are applicable to.

Beyond a point, static tuning hits a ceiling as we demonstrate: further pruning or hardware acceleration degrades accuracy faster than it saves resources. Additional gains, therefore, require adapting to problem-specific structure—for example, reducing latency in sequential tasks by predicting on shorter subsequences [70] or exploiting temporal similarity in streaming inputs [87]. As conditions drift further during deployment, tuning must be *repeated* or resources must be over-provisioned—motivating the approaches developed in the chapters that follow.

### 1.3 Central Thesis: Variability as a Lever for Efficiency

Deep learning's remarkable progress has been fueled by steadily increasing model capacity, data volume, and compute availability. Yet at the point of deployment—whether on a battery-powered sensor or across a shared GPU cluster—resources fluctuate, constraints tighten, and *brute-force scaling* quickly becomes impractical. This raises the guiding question of this dissertation:

> > How can learning systems convert environmental variability into efficiency gains without sacrificing performance?

Our answer is to treat those fluctuations not as nuisances to be masked by worst-case provisioning, but as exploitable structure. By aligning computation with the temporal and structural variability of its environment, a learning system can deliver equivalent—or superior—utility at a fraction of the cost. The argument unfolds across two complementary regimes:

**Arbitrary environments:** In many real-world systems external conditions—battery drain, network congestion, input difficulty—may swing unpredictably from one point in time to the next. Each inference or optimization step must therefore remain memoryless or **stateless**, making no assumptions about the past and no promises about the future. Our goal in this regime is **elasticity**: adapt the computation to the budget and difficulty of the *current* instance, opportunistically saving work while degrading gracefully to a static method when the world behaves truly arbitrarily.

Gradually evolving environments: Many real systems are more benign and evolve gradually. The job queue on a GPU cluster at minute t resembles the queue at minute t−1; network throughput in minute five is close to that in minute four. Such slow drift makes yesterday’s solution a valuable head start on today’s problem. Algorithms for this regime are therefore stateful: they cache intermediate results, apply differential updates, and amortize effort across rounds instead of recomputing from scratch.

This dissertation is organized into two parts based on the type of environment they focus on: Part I designs elastic models for ML inference in arbitrary environments, and Part II discusses continual optimization for gradually evolving environments.

### 1.4 Contributions and Roadmap

By fusing ideas from machine learning, systems, and optimization, this work delivers practical contributions to support our thesis, organized across two parts:

## Part I – Adaptive Inference in Arbitrary Environments

- Chapter 2 – Constructing Elastic ML Models. We introduce progressive ensemble distillation, a principled way to decompose a large pretrained model into an elastic ensemble whose inference cost can be dialed up or down at runtime. The resulting B-DISTIL algorithm uses a boosting-like training procedure to build the ensemble and delivers competitive accuracy under tight resource budgets. B-DISTIL shows improvements in resource utilization across tasks in vision, speech, and sensor benchmarks and admits a O(1/√T) convergence guarantee for the distillation procedure.

- Chapter 3 – Dynamically Determining Elasticity. Shifting focus from fluctuating budgets to fluctuating query difficulty, ABC leverages ensemble agreement as a difficulty signal to construct elastic models that adapt to query difficulty changes. Because it relies solely on forward passes for the agreement checks, ABC is completely training-free and can be deployed on black-box APIs. Across four language, vision, and multimodal tasks, ABC cuts communication cost by up to 14× in edge-to-cloud inference, reduces GPU rental cost by up to 3× in heterogeneous model-serving scenarios, and lowers average API cost per token by 25× when accessed as black-box services.

## Part II – Continual Optimization in Gradually Evolving Environments

- Chapter 4 – Continually Adapting to Gradually Evolving Environments. Formalizing “slow evolution” in large-scale resource allocation problems, this chapter introduces the continual optimization paradigm and instantiates it with the COPTER algorithm for GPU cluster scheduling. By reusing prior solver work and warm-starting on each new round, COPTER achieves up to 100× reductions in solver time while maintaining near-optimal job completion times.

- Chapter 5 – Gradual Evolution Beyond Machine Learning. To show generality beyond ML training, we apply continual optimization to two further domains—shard load balancing and WAN traffic engineering. Using COPTER, both MILP and LP formulations solve 10–100× faster than state-of-the-art baselines, with negligible loss in allocation quality.

### 1.5 Broader Impact and Future Outlook

The evidence we collect across Part I and Part II shows that adapting computation to its deployment environment can unlock order-of-magnitude resource efficiency savings while remaining competitive. The gains translate into longer battery life on edge devices, lower carbon footprints for data center workloads, and wider access to high-quality machine learning in resource-constrained settings. Looking forward, the adaptive lens opens several research frontiers:

- Edge-to-Cloud Continuum. Current elastic models end at the device boundary, while continual optimizers start inside the data center. An end-to-end pipeline that jointly tunes on-device inference and backend scheduling will minimize total cost for hybrid applications such as real-time video analytics.

- Privacy-Aware Adaptivity. Benign workloads often arise in federated or privacy-constrained settings, where data cannot leave the device. Extending continual optimization to respect differential privacy or secure aggregation constraints would enable low-cost personalization without compromising data.

- Theory of Continual Optimization. Chapters 4 and 5 provide empirical evidence and formalizations that show how continual optimization can accelerate benign workloads. Extending these to other notions of gradual changes, considering cases where even the warm-started solver gets only a few iterations, and providing tighter regret/runtime bounds are interesting open problems.

By treating environmental variability—whether wild or mild—as an opportunity rather than a threat, this dissertation charts a path toward machine learning systems that are not only more efficient, but also more sustainable, privacy-preserving, and universally deployable.
