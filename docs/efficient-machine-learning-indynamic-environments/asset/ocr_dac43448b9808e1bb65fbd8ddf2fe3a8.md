Gavel [113] supports time-slicing DNN jobs across heterogeneous GPUs using LP formulations for many scheduling objectives, but runs into solver bottlenecks if jobs request space-sharing leading to missed scheduler deadlines in large clusters. Rebalancer [193] is a framework to express and compile scheduling policies into MILPs. The MILPs are then solved using off-the-shelf solvers at small scales, and use a faster, suboptimal local search algorithm on a graph representation at larger scales. COPTER complements Rebalancer in two ways – (a) potentially extending the size of problems directly solvable as MILPs, and (b) using COPTER’s outputs as initial guesses to initialize the local-search algorithm at scale for better quality solutions.

# Solving Optimization Problems

**Offline optimization.** Offline optimization deals with problems that are fully specified before optimization starts. State-of-the-art LP and MILP solvers (e.g., CPLEX [154], Gurobi [202], GLPK [23]) often use simplex or interior-point (barrier) methods that rely on solving linear systems iteratively, and employ optimizations like matrix factorizations (LU, QR, LDL) [23, 154]. These methods scale poorly to large problems due to high per-iteration costs. Additionally, factorization often destroys sparsity patterns in the constraint matrices and must be recomputed if the problem changes, rendering them inefficient for continual optimization where problems change frequently. First-order methods, such as OSQP [116], ADMM [28], ALCD [54, 68], PGD [2] emerged to address these limitations, trading lower per-iteration costs for slower convergence. For instance, to scale solvers to large-scale problems in seismic reflection tomography applications, Delbos and Gilbert [19] eschew simplex and interior point based methods, and instead develop a first order method based on the Augmented Lagrangian and conjugate gradients. This approach was further adapted to large-scale sparse linear programs in [54] with further improved analysis and runtime improvements coming from [68]. Stochastic versions of these algorithms, such as Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) are also popular, further trading convergence (and feasibility) for simpler per-iteration complexity.

**Online optimization.** The approaches discussed thus far are fundamentally offline approaches — they construct a linear program offline and employ a solver to solve it. In contrast, online optimization [57] methods instead optimize problems that are sequentially revealed and introduce a notion of time steps. Algorithms for online optimization often have some notion of uncertainty, and typically attempt to minimize regret against a fixed optimum [35, 57, 125]. The goal of an online optimization algorithm is to guarantee that the regret is sublinear in the number of iterations of the algorithm; so called no-regret algorithms.
