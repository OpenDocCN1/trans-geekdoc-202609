# Cascading using Score-Based Deferrals

Traditional cascading methods often rely on simple score-based metrics for deferral decisions. We compare to the recent Wisdom-of-Committees method of Wang et al. [143] as a general, representative method in this category but note that specialized instantiations of this approach have been applied across various domains in prior work, for instance, in object detection [8, 18, 30, 44, 45, 78], image classification [79, 143], and text classification [135, 156, 161, 174]. In these settings, deferrals within cascading systems use metrics such as confidence scores, entropy, and probabilities [91, 128, 158]. While using confidence scores of existing models is inexpensive, these scores are required to be well-calibrated, which is less common for off-the-shelf models [63, 127]. Recent methods have thus considered a variety of techniques to enhance score-based deferrals, such as explicitly adding deferrals as a prediction [79], difficulty-aware regularization [135], temperature scaling [181], and calibration mechanisms [195].

Recently, Jitkrittum et al. [171] explored the specific practical settings where confidence-based deferral in cascades could suffer; these settings include: scenarios where downstream models are specialists (the error probability of the downstream model is highly nonuniform across samples), when samples are subject to label noise, and in the presence of a distribution shift between the train and test set. Notably, in all these failure modes, where confidence-based cascades can be inadequate, our approach is likely to offer improvements, as ensembles help induce diversity, enable noise-robustness, and mitigate issues of distribution shift [5, 11, 17, 152].

# Cascades with Routing Procedures

Other methods avoid confidence scores entirely by training procedures independent of the model-set to route instances as needed. This trend has gained prominence with the rise of black-box model API services for LLMs, where prediction scores are often unavailable. Guan et al. [71] developed a selection module trained to determine the best-fit classifiers for data instances. Yue et al. [201] (MoT LLM Cascade) used sampling and consistency checking to determine when to defer to a high-cost model. AutoMix [177] proposed a few-shot self-verification mechanism, similar to Yue et al. [201], but also introduced a Markov-based meta-verifier for cascading in context-grounded tasks. FrugalGPT [165] leveraged a cascade strategy that triages incoming queries using a DistilBERT router and scoring function. HybridLLM [186] used a fine-tuned DeBERTa [106] to route queries to models based on the predicted query difficulty and the desired quality level. On the same note, OrchestraLLM [175]—using hand-labeled data to create expert model pools—selects model to query based on embedding distances between the pools’ instances and the test instances. Other methods also used some form of trained router, including RouteLLM [196], ‘Fly-swat or cannon’ [198], and Shnitzer et al. [180].
