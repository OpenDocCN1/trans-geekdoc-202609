## Chapter 1

## Introduction

Machine learning systems now operate everywhere—from battery-powered sensors at the edge to warehouse-scale GPU clusters in the cloud—making efficiency paramount. Even after deployment, hardware, data, and workload patterns continue to evolve, further shifting the landscape in which a system must operate. This dissertation argues that harnessing such environmental variability—whether sudden spikes or slow drift—can deliver order-of-magnitude reductions in inference cost and large-scale online optimization runtimes. The remainder of this chapter motivates that claim, states the thesis, and previews the contributions.

### 1.1 Motivation: The ML Boom and Efficiency Gap

The past half a decade of machine learning (ML) has been defined by an unprecedented scaling boom. Between 2019 and 2025, state-of-the-art language and vision models grew from billions to hundreds of billions of parameters (Figure 1.1), with single training runs consuming petaflop-days of compute and terabytes of curated data. Similar trends hold in computer vision, speech synthesis, and multimodal generation. As a result, deep learning can now generate high-definition images from text, translate paragraph-length passages in real time, simulate protein folding with astounding accuracy, and answer sophisticated questions requiring in-depth reasoning.

Yet the same trajectory exposes a mounting efficiency gap. Training a modern LLM routinely requires clusters with tens of thousands of GPUs and months of wall-clock time; inference, though cheaper per query, still strains edge devices and even well-provisioned data centers during traffic spikes. Energy consumption grows superlinearly with model size, and the carbon footprint of a single training run can exceed that of multiple passenger flights. Access is therefore restricted to well-funded labs and industry, limiting both scientific reproducibility and downstream adoption.
