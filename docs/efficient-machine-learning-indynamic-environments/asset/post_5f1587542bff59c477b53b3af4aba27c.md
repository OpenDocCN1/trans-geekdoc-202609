
## 4.6 Conclusion

This chapter introduced *continual optimization* (CO)—a principled framework for exploiting the *gradual evolution* of large-scale resource allocation problems. Leveraging the insight that successive rounds in many systems differ marginally, CO amortizes solver effort across time rather than treating each round as an isolated instance.

We formalized this intuition through the notion of path lengths and demonstrated how schedulers follow a Follow-The-Leader (FTL) process, which enjoys path dependent dynamic regret. Building on this analysis, we explained how a warm-started **Proximal Point Algorithm** naturally suits continual optimization in terms of run-time: its proximal updates are contractive, benefit directly from good initializations, and—when paired with a coordinate-descent inner solver—remain factorization-free. We instantiated the framework as COPTER, which combines

- a *differential update interface* that modifies the LP/MILP incrementally,
- a *factorization-free* first-order solver that reuses work across rounds, and
- lightweight *shim* heuristics that convert near-integral LP relaxations into high-quality integer solutions without an exhaustive branch-and-cut search.

Experiments on realistic GPU-cluster traces confirmed the practical impact: COPTER delivers up to **100×** faster end-to-end scheduling than environment-agnostic baselines, while matching—or surpassing—baseline job-completion times. Together, the theoretical basis and empirical results illustrate that recognizing, and systematically exploiting, gradually evolving dynamics can unlock substantial efficiency gains in modern machine-learning infrastructure.

## 4.7 Related Work and Discussion

### Cluster Scheduling as Optimization Problems

Plan-ahead schedulers like Tetri-Sched [59] and Shockwave [183] solve one MILP to determine allocations for multiple rounds, and as a result, are reasonably robust to missing scheduler deadlines in large clusters at the cost of increased queue times and reduced resource utilization. Meta’s RAS [138] solves MILPs hourly for large-scale capacity reservation uses a two-stage decomposition (MILP for server-reservation assignment, then parallel container allocation), but requires problem-specific optimizations and heuristics to scale the MILP stage to large-scale problems. Tetris [185] solves MILPs for load balancing ML workloads and data placement optimization across regions, and employs multi-level scheduling for scalability at the cost of global optimality. Gavel [113] supports time-slicing DNN jobs across heterogeneous GPUs using LP formulations for many scheduling objectives, but runs into solver bottlenecks if jobs request space-sharing leading to missed scheduler deadlines in large clusters. Rebalancer [193] is a framework to express and compile scheduling policies into MILPs. The MILPs are then solved using off-the-shelf solvers at small scales, and use a faster, suboptimal local search algorithm on a graph representation at larger scales. COPTER complements Rebalancer in two ways – (a) potentially extending the size of problems directly solvable as MILPs, and (b) using COPTER’s outputs as initial guesses to initialize the local-search algorithm at scale for better quality solutions.

### Solving Optimization Problems

**Offline optimization.** Offline optimization deals with problems that are fully specified before optimization starts. State-of-the-art LP and MILP solvers (e.g., CPLEX [154], Gurobi [202], GLPK [23]) often use simplex or interior-point (barrier) methods that rely on solving linear systems iteratively, and employ optimizations like matrix factorizations (LU, QR, LDL) [23, 154]. These methods scale poorly to large problems due to high per-iteration costs. Additionally, factorization often destroys sparsity patterns in the constraint matrices and must be recomputed if the problem changes, rendering them inefficient for continual optimization where problems change frequently. First-order methods, such as OSQP [116], ADMM [28], ALCD [54, 68], PGD [2] emerged to address these limitations, trading lower per-iteration costs for slower convergence. For instance, to scale solvers to large-scale problems in seismic reflection tomography applications, Delbos and Gilbert [19] eschew simplex and interior point based methods, and instead develop a first order method based on the Augmented Lagrangian and conjugate gradients. This approach was further adapted to large-scale sparse linear programs in [54] with further improved analysis and runtime improvements coming from [68]. Stochastic versions of these algorithms, such as Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) are also popular, further trading convergence (and feasibility) for simpler per-iteration complexity.

**Online optimization.** The approaches discussed thus far are fundamentally offline approaches — they construct a linear program offline and employ a solver to solve it. In contrast, online optimization [57] methods instead optimize problems that are sequentially revealed and introduce a notion of time steps. Algorithms for online optimization often have some notion of uncertainty, and typically attempt to minimize regret against a fixed optimum [35, 57, 125]. The goal of an online optimization algorithm is to guarantee that the regret is sublinear in the number of iterations of the algorithm; so called no-regret algorithms. Although round-based resource allocations can be cast as an online optimization problem, the setting is not directly applicable, as comparing against any fixed static allocation is meaningless. To address this problem, recent approaches to online optimization have extended this notion of regret to *dynamic regret*; where we compare our allocations to a sequence of minimizers instead of a fixed minimizer—in our case, the sequence of optimal allocations. With the notion of dynamic regret the algorithmic goal in online learning now becomes providing dynamic regret guarantees that scale favourably with the variation in optimum allocations across time; that is, if the change in solutions across time is small then our algorithm should also provide solutions with small dynamic regret [146, 150, 163]. However, even this modification is unsuitable for round-based resource allocation, especially in the context of slowly evolving systems that is the subject of this chapter, as the run-time of the algorithms is not typically a concern in this setting. In contrast, in continual optimization, we aim to provide algorithms that have both low dynamic regret as well as run-times that depend on the variation in optimum across rounds.
