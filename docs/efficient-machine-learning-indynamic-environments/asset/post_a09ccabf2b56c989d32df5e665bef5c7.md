
## 2.6 Related Work

### Efficient Inference

Machine learning inference is often resource-constrained when deployed in practical applications due to memory, energy, cost, or latency constraints. This has spurred the development of numerous techniques for efficient inference. Pruning and approximations of pretrained parameter tensors through low-rank, sparse and quantized representations [49, 90, 101, 131] have been generally effective in reducing resource requirements. There are also architecture and task-specific techniques for efficient inference [87, 145, 168]. In contrast to compressing an already trained model, algorithms have also been developed to train compressed, or tiny models by incorporating resource constraints as part of their training routines [60, 69]. More recently, algorithms have been developed to search for smaller submodels from a single pretrained model [82, 151].

### Knowledge distillation

Knowledge distillation aims to transfer the knowledge of a larger model (or model ensemble) to a smaller one [21, 50]. Despite its popularity, performing compression via distillation has several known pitfalls. Most notably, it is well-documented that distillation performs poorly when there is a *capacity gap*, i.e., the teacher is significantly larger than the student [86, 112, 129, 149]. When performing distillation onto a weighted combination of ensembles, it has been observed that adding additional models into the ensemble does not markedly improve performance over that of a single distilled model [149]. There is also a lack of theoretical work characterizing when and why distillation is effective for compression [132]. We refer readers to [132] for a recent survey on varied applications of and approaches for distillation at large.

### Early exits and anytime inference

Many applications stand to benefit from the output of progressive ensemble distillation, which allows for flexibly tuning accuracy vs. inference cost and executing inference in parallel. Enabling trade-offs between accuracy and inference cost is particularly useful for applications that use early exit or anytime inference schemes. In on-device continuous (online) inference settings, *early exit* models aim to evaluate common cases quickly in order to improve energy efficiency and prolong battery life [61, 70]. For instance, a battery-powered device continuously listening for voice commands can use early exit methods to improve battery efficiency by quickly classifying non-command speech. ## Two-player games, online optimization and boosting

In this chapter we formulate progressive ensemble distillation as a two-player zero-sum game. The importance of equilibrium of two-player zero-sum games has been recognized since the foundational work of von Neumann and Morgenstern [1]. Later applications by Schapire [4] and Freund [3] and Freund and Schapire [13] identified close connections between boosting and two-player games. On the basis of this result, a number of practical boosting-based learning approaches such as AdaBoost [6], gradient boosting [9], and XGboost [55] have been developed. Boosting has only recently seen success in modern deep learning applications. In particular, Suggala, Liu, and Ravikumar [118] propose a generalized boosting framework to train boosting-based ensembles of deep networks. Their key insight is that allowing function compositions in feature space can help boost deep neural networks. Although they focus on training and do not produce decompositions of pretrained models, we use a similar approach in our work to select intermediate layer connections between ensemble components (Section 2.2.3). A more general application of boosting that is similar to our setup is by Trevisan, Tulsiani, and Vadhan [27]. They prove that given a target bounded function g (e.g., the teacher model) and class of candidate approximating functions f ∈ F, one can iteratively approximate g arbitrarily well with respect to F using ideas from online learning and boosting. However, these results depend on the ability to find a function ft in iteration t that leads to at least a small constant improvement in a round-dependent approximation loss. A key contribution of the work discussed in this chapter is showing that such functions can be found for the practical application of progressive ensemble distillation by carefully selecting candidate models.

## Chapter 3

### Dynamically Determining Elasticity

This chapter builds on the elastic models introduced in Chapter 2. In Chapter 2, we addressed variability in resource budget—situations where the available compute budget changes from query to query. Here, however, we confront variability in query difficulty: cases where some queries are trivially answered by a lightweight model, while others require a far larger model. We present ABC (Agreement-Based Cascading), a lightweight, training-free procedure that chooses an inference elasticity based on each query's difficulty, thereby reducing the average inference cost in proportion to the fraction of easy queries. The material in this chapter is built on the work of Kolawold, Dennis, Talwalkar, and Smith [192].

### 3.1 Query Difficulty

In many real-world ML deployments, a considerable portion of the data seen during inference can be effectively evaluated using lightweight models rather than state-of-the-art giants [102, 171]. That is, it is possible to associate a notion of difficulty with each inference query. Intuitively, given a Pareto-optimal set of models for a given inference task, we can order the queries based on the least expensive model that can produce a high-quality inference output for the query. For instance, in language modeling tasks such as question answering, one can get away with using a relatively low parameter-count model to answer simple factual questions, while a high parameter-count reasoning based model might be required for more sophisticated questions requiring research and synthesis.

From an efficiency point of view, this means that if we can identify the subset of inference queries that can be accurately evaluated by less expensive models, average inference costs can be reduced. Recently, cascading has seen renewed interest in the ML community with the advent of large language models (LLMs) and vision foundation models [e.g., 165, 190]. Recent approaches consider a variety of techniques to construct model cascades, including designing novel model architectures that have built-in cascading capabilities [85, 167, 172], or learning routing schemes/deferral rules that require data-dependent training for every task considered [165, 186]. While these approaches can improve accuracy/efficiency trade-offs, they may also introduce significant computational overhead in setup and training/fine-tuning costs.

We instead focus on a simple, *training-free* cascade scheme that uses the agreement amongst an ensemble of existing models at each cascade level as its deferral rule. We refer to this approach as *Agreement-Based Cascading* (ABC). Intuitively, when the outputs of the ensemble at a particular cascade level do not align, then ABC triggers cascading, and inference is attempted at the next tier of (larger) models (see Figure 3.1).
