## 3.5 Conclusions, Limitations & Further Directions

This chapter introduced ABC (Agreement-Based Cascading), a training-free method for adaptive inference that reuses existing models to form an elastic cascade and sets elasticity implicitly through deferral decisions based on ensemble agreement. By defining and constructing *safe deferral rules*, we showed that ABC can serve as a drop-in replacement for a single model while often *improving* accuracy. While not as close to the Pareto-optimal cost-accuracy trade-off as B-DISTIL from Chapter 2, ABC offers a training-free way of constructing elastic models that are competitive with the Pareto-optimal set in certain situations; when the relative cost $\gamma$ is small.

Although computing an ensemble as part of ABC incurs extra overheads compared to just using a single model, the large size gap between today's lightweight and state-of-the-art models means that, in many settings, ABC still lowers end-to-end cost. Case studies confirmed this benefit: ABC cut communication cost in edge-to-cloud inference, reduced GPU rental fees in cloud deployments, and slashed API bills in black-box service scenarios. These results highlight ABC's ability to boost efficiency without the routing networks and custom architectures that traditional cascade frameworks require, making it an attractive choice for practitioners targeting lower inference latency. A natural next step is to extend the approach from classification to *generation* tasks, where the cost-quality trade-off is even more pronounced.

## 3.6 Related Work

Adaptive inference schemes have been a topic of interest in machine learning for many years. This section discusses three main existing approaches to cascading and adaptive inference: score-based methods, trained routing procedures, and dynamic networks. We highlight how ABC's approach relates to and differs from these existing methods.

### Cascading using Score-Based Deferrals

Traditional cascading methods often rely on simple score-based metrics for deferral decisions. We compare to the recent Wisdom-of-Committees method of Wang et al. [143] as a general, representative method in this category but note that specialized instantiations of this approach have been applied across various domains in prior work, for instance, in object detection [8, 18, 30, 44, 45, 78], image classification [79, 143], and text classification [135, 156, 161, 174]. In these settings, deferrals within cascading systems use metrics such as confidence scores, entropy, and probabilities [91, 128, 158]. While using confidence scores of existing models is inexpensive, these scores are required to be well-calibrated, which is less common for off-the-shelf models [63, 127]. Recent methods have thus considered a variety of techniques to enhance score-based deferrals, such as explicitly adding deferrals as a prediction [79], difficulty-aware regularization [135], temperature scaling [181], and calibration mechanisms [195].

Recently, Jitkrittum et al. [171] explored the specific practical settings where confidence-based deferral in cascades could suffer; these settings include: scenarios where downstream models are specialists (the error probability of the downstream model is highly nonuniform across samples), when samples are subject to label noise, and in the presence of a distribution shift between the train and test set. Notably, in all these failure modes, where confidence-based cascades can be inadequate, our approach is likely to offer improvements, as ensembles help induce diversity, enable noise-robustness, and mitigate issues of distribution shift [5, 11, 17, 152].

### Cascades with Routing Procedures

Other methods avoid confidence scores entirely by training procedures independent of the model-set to route instances as needed. This trend has gained prominence with the rise of black-box model API services for LLMs, where prediction scores are often unavailable. Guan et al. [71] developed a selection module trained to determine the best-fit classifiers for data instances. Yue et al. [201] (MoT LLM Cascade) used sampling and consistency checking to determine when to defer to a high-cost model. AutoMix [177] proposed a few-shot self-verification mechanism, similar to Yue et al. [201], but also introduced a Markov-based meta-verifier for cascading in context-grounded tasks. FrugalGPT [165] leveraged a cascade strategy that triages incoming queries using a DistilBERT router and scoring function. HybridLLM [186] used a fine-tuned DeBERTa [106] to route queries to models based on the predicted query difficulty and the desired quality level. On the same note, OrchestraLLM [175]—using hand-labeled data to create expert model pools—selects model to query based on embedding distances between the pools’ instances and the test instances. Other methods also used some form of trained router, including RouteLLM [196], ‘Fly-swat or cannon’ [198], and Shnitzer et al. [180].

### Dynamic and Adaptive Networks

As discussed in Section 3.4.2 (where we compare to FrugalGPT, AutoMix, and MoT LLM Cascade), these more sophisticated methods involve complex setups, data-dependent training, and increased computational overhead. They often require retraining routers for each new task, dataset, or model, limiting adaptability to unseen data distributions or new models. In contrast, ABC provides a simpler, and widely applicable alternative at no additional training or setup cost.

Routing procedures typically train an independent routing mechanism, keeping the Pareto-set of models untouched. However, for many medium-scale applications, methods have been explored that learn a Pareto-set of models and stopping criterion together. For example, early exit methods of Huang et al. [74], Wang et al. [80], Liu et al. [111], Xin et al. [119], Zhou et al. [120], Geng et al. [130], and Schuster et al. [159], subnetworks extraction methods [83, 100, 109, 123, 153, 167], composition-based methods [117, 166, 187] are some of the more recent examples.

Our approach diverges from these methods, as it does not involve altering model architectures or retraining from scratch. Instead, ABC leverages existing models as they are, utilizing ensemble agreement as a deferral condition, which can be applied directly to these models without any need for fine-tuning or specialized training.

### Continually Adapting to Gradually-Evolving Environments

The first part of this dissertation studied efficient inference in *arbitrary* environments: Chapter 2 handled resource budgets that fluctuate unpredictably across inference requests, and Chapter 3 addressed queries whose difficulty varies from one request to the next. Part II turns to the complementary case of *gradually-evolving environments*—settings in which the relevant property of the environment evolves slowly and in a regular, predictable manner. Such structure lets us reuse past effort and anticipate future changes and to design algorithms that adapt more efficiently.

This chapter focuses on *resource allocation* in large-scale deep-learning clusters. We formalize a notion of slow evolution for this setting and introduce COPTER (**C**ontinual **O**ptimizer), an algorithm that exploits gradual changes in such resource allocation problems to produce high-quality allocations effectively. The material builds on work by Jayaram Subramanya, Dennis, Smith, and Ganger [203].
