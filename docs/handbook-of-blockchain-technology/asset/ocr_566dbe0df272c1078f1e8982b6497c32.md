The purpose of the rage quitting DAO decision-making model is to protect minority voters and check the power of the majority. The model borrows its name from the gaming term “rage quit”. In gaming rage quit is the situation where a player suddenly quits a game due to frustration caused by either the game outcome or the actions of other players. In a similar fashion, DAO members can withdraw their funds from the organization if they disagree with the result of a vote.

The hypothetical “GreenFund” DAO is a platform for the funding of green energy projects. Like other decision-making mechanisms used in DAOs, community members vote on different projects, and the projects with the most votes receive funding. In case a member strongly disagrees with the outcome of the voting process, they can protest to this decision by withdrawing their funds and quitting (leaving). This action demonstrates a strong disagreement with the decision. In turn, this may lead to further discussions or reconsideration of the decision.

Rage quitting can be a powerful tool for ensuring that the voices of all members are heard and that the majority does not have unchecked power. However, if used excessively, it could lead to instability and a lack of funds in the DAO. It also requires careful design and implementation to ensure that it does not lead to adverse outcomes, such as the premature dissolution of the DAO. Most importantly, because of how drawn-out the voting procedure is, it might not be an option for all DAOs.

For a real-world example of rage quitting, you can look at the Moloch DAO. This DAO, which focuses on funding Ethereum projects, implemented a rage quitting mechanism to allow members to withdraw their funds if they disagreed with funding decisions. However, it is worth noting that the use of rage quitting in DAOs is a relatively new area. Its impacts and effectiveness need to be better investigated and understood.



Conviction Voting is one of the most recent DAO voting systems that is still being tested. It utilizes time as a utility and depends on the community’s overall choice. Community members cast votes on a variety of ongoing issues, and the longer they hold that position, the more weight that vote has. As the voting utility gets closer to a maximum point, the rate of rise gradually decreases. Voters always have the option to amend their vote at any time, which will consequently reduce the value of their initial vote.

This technique is an excellent means of illustrating the level of voter interest in a proposal, as well as the potential influences on their attitudes from both internal and external sources. In this model, proposals can be approved without a majority vote, as decisions can be made based on the views of the community. In the DAO protocol, it is also a practical means of eliminating the possibility of new members gaining too much power.
