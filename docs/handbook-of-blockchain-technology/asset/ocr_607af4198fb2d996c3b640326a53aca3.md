There is a need for customary practices to be utilized alongside blockchain-specific initiatives; in the same way the blockchain has been criticized for creating new pathways for criminals to launder their money and handle illicit funds, it equally provides new ways to target such activities. Because of the nature of the blockchain, data stored in blocks cannot be destroyed and is visible to all. Further, they provide an additional set of data to analyze and draw conclusions from, allowing for the broadening of police investigations and providing new leads. To make use of the full potential of the blockchain, law enforcement agencies need to leverage on the experience of multidisciplinary teams, including in the areas of AML/CFT, cybersecurity and data protection. It is crucial that law enforcement agents receive proper training in blockchain and cryptoassets and are equipped with the proper technology tools to carry out their role.

Concurrently, law enforcement agencies must be ready to use international channels available to them that allow for speedy and effective cooperation with one another. Financial crimes of this type are by nature borderless. Without proper enforcement and judicial cooperation across the globe, investigations and the collection of evidence largely remain incoherent, placing the prosecution of criminals to justice at risk. A good example of the importance of effective cooperation is highlighted in the introduction of this chapter, which refers to the case of the seizure of the ChipMixer servers and website following the coordinated efforts of German and US authorities, as well Belgium, Poland, Switzerland and Europol. Equally, in the case of Hydra, mentioned in Section 18.3, the servers of the darknet marketplace were situated in Europe (Germany specifically) and were seized as a result of the coordinated efforts of the German and US authorities. At the other end, the case of Garantex highlights the difficulties associated with combating crime on the blockchain in the absence of international cooperation (Grauer et al., 2023, p. 24). Garantex is an exchange that allows the sale and purchase of cryptocurrencies in exchange of fiat currencies. While originally registered in Estonia to provide virtual currency services, its authorization was revoked following findings that the identity of “90% of its customers had not been properly verified” and that the property that had been removed through the virtual exchange “was related to crime or wallets used by criminal offenders for illegal purposes” (Financial Intelligence Unit, 2022). Despite the fact that the US Treasury Department and the Estonian authorities had worked closely together up to the revocation of the Garantex license, with the US Treasury imposing its own sanctions package targeting Garantex, the exchange has been able to relocate to Russia and continue its operations from there (Financial Intelligence Unit, 2022).

At the private level, there are a number of ways to guard against risks associated with crime on the blockchain. As the first line of defense, compliance officers must receive appropriate training in blockchain analytics, in order to be able to spot suspicious transactions involving cryptocurrencies. Equally, they must be given access to purpose-specific software and digital tools to carry out screening of their clients and online wallets, their IP addresses and transaction history, including pre and post transaction monitoring. In the same spirit, regular IT audits will be crucial to spot any vulnerabilities in the systems of a company or financial institution dealing in cryptocurrency. Further, the private sector needs to engage in open dialogues with financial intelligence units, to understand the threats existing in the market at any given time and receive advice on best practices and systems adequacy. Considering the fast evolving nature of the industry, continuous education and trainings are key. What has traditionally been a red flag amongst compliance officers, can equally be a red flag in the cryptocurrency space
