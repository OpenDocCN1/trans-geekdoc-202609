A third version is a mix of both, where token holders can either vote directly or delegate their vote. This last voting mechanism is thereby based on the concept of ‘liquid democracy’ (Axelsen et al., 2022). However, while the original concept of liquid democracy clearly incorporates the idea of ‘one-person-one-vote’, most DAOs still adopt the one-token-one-vote approach. This is mainly because most DAOs allow members to participate anonymously, which allows them to have multiple identities. By multiplying member accounts, a ‘one-account-one-vote’ mechanism would therefore be vulnerable to sybil attacks.

Examples: Tezos, MakerDAO, UniSwap, Ethereum Name Service, BitDAO, Curve DAO



In 2018 Lalley and Weyl (2018) proposed ‘quadratic voting’ as a new voting mechanism with the aim to overcome the centralization of the voting power in a DAO as it occurs with ‘token-based votings’. Quadratic voting introduces a non-linear funding principle which allows voters to not only vote for or against a specific proposal, but also to express how strongly they agree on it (Buterin et al., 2018). Each DAO member receives a specific amount of voting credit. In a consecutive step, the members must allocate their credit to the different proposals. As an example: DAO member Alice receives ten voting tokens. She allocates three votes to proposal A, one to proposal B and zero to proposal C, which results in voting costs of 3²+1²+0² = 10. However, in this example, Alice was not only able to vote, but she could also express her preferences. The voting is called ‘quadratic’ because the costs for n votes are n². This approach discourages people without a vested interest from voting on an issue and gives a minority a more prominent voice on issues which are important to them. Additionally, it increases ‘costs’ exponentially for ‘wealthy’ voters to influence a decision about a proposal. In this way, quadratic voting attempts to reduce the influence of an individual on the outcome of a decision in a DAO. However, it is important to mention that quadratic voting requires an identity process to ensure its fairness. Otherwise, the mechanism is prone to sybil attacks, where a ‘wealthy’ DAO member can split their tokens among multiple identities to increase their voting power again (Dimitri, 2022). As a consequence, DAOs would need to implement more advanced technologies to identify its members, which again is one of the main reasons why quadratic voting is not yet widely implemented in DAOs. A further adoption of the classic, token-based voting system is ‘conviction voting’. According to Emmett (2019), conviction voting offers a novel decision-making process that funds proposals based on the aggregated preference of community members, expressed continuously over time. Voters are thereby expressing their approval for proposals over time. Figuratively speaking, proposals are buckets and all DAO members are able to ‘pour in’ voting power into these buckets. The longer a voter holds his opinions and keeps staked its voting power, the more conviction there will be in this proposal. It is expected that maintaining the voting token staked over time will give an advantage to those DAO members who are convinced of their decision and keep their decision unchanged over a longer time period (Ding et al., 2023b).
