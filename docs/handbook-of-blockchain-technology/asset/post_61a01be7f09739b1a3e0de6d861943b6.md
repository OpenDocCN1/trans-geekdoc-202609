One of the first recorded cases, if not the first, is the Cybersyn project. Cybersyn was developed in Chile during the 1970s and marked a significant point in the development of digital governance concepts. Cybersyn investigated the development of a computer system that would oversee and regulate the entire Chilean economy. The system was intended to gather information from several economic sectors, use algorithms to interpret the information, and then make judgments based on the findings. Despite the project's eventual failure, it was a noteworthy turning point in the development of algorithmic governance (Issar and Aneesh, 2022).

The introduction of the World Wide Web (WWW) and its associated technologies marked another important era in algorithmic governance. During the 1990s to early 2000s, the tremendous amount of data generated, gathered, and processed within the context of the WWW changed many facets of social dynamics and governance. This resulted in even more complex types of algorithms in governance that include personalized and focused recommendations, search engine algorithms, and social media analytics (Cantero and Ebers, 2021).

Since 2009, the emergence of blockchain technology has significantly contributed to the evolution of algorithmic governance. Blockchain can serve as the consensus layer in any kind of transaction or data. This can vary from the delegation of power to even election procedures. The immutability of blockchain can offer greater transparency, security, and fairness across almost any governance procedure. This can potentially transform the way that governance and decision data can be stored and/or executed (Atzori, 2015; Papangelou et al., 2023).

As the complexity of algorithmic governance has increased, the rise of difficulties has also risen. The problem of accountability and openness is one of the primary obstacles. Because algorithms are frequently non-transparent and sophisticated, it can be challenging for people to comprehend and take part in the decision-making process. This lack of openness may cause people to be excluded from the system and produce unfair or discriminatory results. The problem with data security and privacy presents another issue. The risk of data breaches and data misuse has increased as our society depends more on automated processes and data to make decisions. As a result, demands have been made for stronger laws and security measures to protect people's private information and, in extension, their decisions (Issar and Aneesh, 2022).

## 7.3 DAOs
Algorithmic governance has come to the blockchain ecosystem in the form of DAOs. This was a move away from more "conventional" methods of private governance structures (e.g., centralized governance) and towards decentralized, open, networked, and automated systems that take control of the decisions in such environments. With the benefits of blockchain technology that were previously discussed, this form of governance can potentially offer a more credible and efficient way of how an organization is governed and operated. Additionally, by enabling more inclusive and participatory forms of governance, the decentralization inherent in blockchain technology and DAOs can empower people individually and in communities (Sims, 2019).

A substantial part of DAOs is the self-executing code stored on a blockchain, also known as smart contracts. Like traditional contracts that you sign on paper, smart contracts are unbreakable without the consent of all parties involved since they are encoded on a blockchain. By doing away with the necessity for an intermediary, smart contracts automate and simplify decision-making. Smart contracts define the operational parameters of a DAO, including member interactions, fund allocation, and decision-making procedures (Sims, 2019).

A DAO eliminates the need for a single central governing body in an organization. This is done by implementing rules, governance structures, and automated executions that are encoded in smart contracts and kept on the blockchain. A DAO operates by relying on the consensus of its members, who vote on specific proposals that are in the form of contracts and are automatically executed (if successful), rather than having a management or executive board make decisions. Theoretically, almost any member of a DAO has access to propose and author smart contracts. For example, an individual member may desire to propose a modification of the regulations of a specific service that the organization offers, while another individual member may propose and vote on a strategy regarding the allocation of the DAO’s financial resources. Once a smart contract is created, it becomes publicly accessible to all participants on the blockchain. Subsequently, the members engage in a voting procedure on the smart contract, which is conducted in a completely transparent manner. The execution of the smart contract by the DAO is contingent upon the attainment of consensus among its members. A DAO’s mechanisms encompass intricate cryptographic algorithms and computer programming. DAOs play a significant role in both Web 3.0 and the Metaverse (Barbeau et al., 2023).

### 7.3.1 The 'D' in DAOs: Decentralization
Decentralization is a critical part of a DAO. This characteristic is at the core of its consensus, operation, and governance structure. Decentralization is about avoiding single points of failure, which in turn diversifies the risk and thus creates an environment of trust. For instance, the term "decentralized autonomous organizations" is used to describe the entire structure of Bitcoin. This is because Bitcoin does not have a single authority governing it or operating it. Bitcoin’s network uses computers called miners which enforce laws by validating that every transaction is correct on the blockchain. Due to this decentralized design, Bitcoin itself can function in this "trustless" environment, allowing any kind of information that is in the network to be validated without the need for a centralized governing body or reliable middleman (El Faqir et al., 2020).

This contrasts with public organizations, such as large corporations, where shareholders delegate mostly to the board of directors, who are responsible for the team. Indeed, this contrast between decentralized and centralized (or more hierarchical) governance processes permeates many social, economic, political, and private institutions. The use of decentralized decision-making has two primary benefits. First, it can lessen agency issues. Secondly, it facilitates increased interaction with the stakeholders of an organization, including workers and clients, which can boost a sense of identity and affiliation with the company and enhance communication (Feichtinger et al., 2023).

### 7.3.2 The 'A' in DAOs: Autonomy
The autonomous part of a DAO is the ability of the organization to operate, in part or in its entirety, without the need for human intervention. This is supported using smart contracts as, within a DAO, smart contracts automate the execution of tasks. For instance, a smart contract might automatically pay money to a project after it reaches its funding target in a DAO that runs a crowdfunding platform. This reduces the possibility of fraud or poor management by simply not requiring any human middleman to take part in the transaction. Due to the lack of a middleman, these operations may be carried out with more speed and precision than human alternatives.

- Smart Contracts and Automation: The practice has demonstrated that DAOs are not capable of adjusting to shifting conditions and making decisions based on prior knowledge, thus indicating them as autonomous. Smart contracts set up rules, conditions and logical operations in a deterministic way, thus they cannot really adjust or adapt to their environment without human intervention. This implies that DAOs are unable to adjust to changing conditions unless humans change the code. This is a major drawback and the main explanation for why DAOs are more frequently referred to as automated than autonomous (Pradhan and Singh, 2021).

- Human Dependency: DAOs still mostly rely on human input, even with the automation that smart contracts allow. Smart contracts have human-designed and human-implemented rules. Humans are the creators of proposals. Human members of the DAO make the judgments about which proposals to support. Therefore, DAOs are not autonomous in the sense of being self-governing, even though smart contracts can automate some processes (Rikken et al., 2019).
