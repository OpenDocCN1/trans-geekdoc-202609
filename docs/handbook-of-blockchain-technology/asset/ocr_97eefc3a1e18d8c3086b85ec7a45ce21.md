A general point which surfaces from the abovementioned examples, and will become more evident from the below analysis, is that tracking cryptoassets in illicit transactions on the blockchain takes the form of a race between legal authorities and criminals. As the latter develop new tools and practices to involve cryptocurrencies in their illicit activities, much more imperative will be needed for authorities to upgrade their systems, knowledge and expertise in order to combat this unique form of crime. Concurrently, by its nature, crime investigation is reactive rather than proactive, focusing on a series of events linked to carrying out criminal activity. Equally, crime prevention is more effective when it is targeted to prevent the repetition of past experience and conduct, in which case it will address specifically identified behaviors and scenarios. Considering the novelty of this space, it is expected that both crime prevention and crime investigation will continue to significantly develop.

# ### 18.3.3 Cryptocurrencies in Ransomware, Extortion and Theft

Focusing on the most prominent cases of cryptocurrency-related offenses, cryptocurrencies are commonly used in ransomware attacks, cases of extortion and theft. In the case of ransomware, a malicious malware is typically used in order to encrypt the victim’s data or block their systems. Thereafter, the perpetrator will require the payment of a ransom in exchange for the decryption of the victim’s data or the restoration of access to their systems. In other cases, it may be that both a victim’s data is decrypted (albeit outside of the users’ systems) and their systems are blocked, such that even if the victim manages to gain access to their systems, the threat of publication of the stolen data remains (also called double extortion). The extortion might equally target third parties to the extortion (triple extortion), who can be affected by the disclosure of the stolen information (e.g., the disclosure of patents, intellectual property, personal data), or might even involve more than a single extortion method (multiple extortion). It is not uncommon for victims of ransomware attacks to pay the ransoms demanded from them, in an effort to prevent the leakage of sensitive data to the public, to avoid reputational damage to their business and becoming the subject of legal suits or police investigations. Victims may also fear that if they report a ransomware attack, they will become the target of similar future attacks, as they fear that perpetrators might decide to retaliate against such behavior. As a result, ransomware cases are considered to be underreported (FATF, 2023).

At the same time, despite ransomware attacks being one of the most common types of cryptoasset crime, the ransomware ecosystem is actually limited in numbers (Grauer et al., 2023, p. 30). This is linked to the fact that ransomware itself has become a service (RaaS), whereby cybercriminals (the affiliates) pay a fee (typically a percentage of the ransom) to designers of a ransomware strain (the administrators), in order to gain access to or use that strain. RaaS may include infiltration into a victim’s system, the stealing of their data, even paying administrators to engage in negotiations with the victims of ransomware attacks. Therefore, RaaS has offered the possibility of carrying out ransomware attacks to an increasing number of criminals willing to pay to use that service, as opposed to only those who have the technological capabilities and knowledge to carry them out.
