
Participation using digital technologies has been on the research agenda since the 1990s, with some drawing historical lines back to “teledemocracy” scholars in the 1970s, speculating on how emerging communication technologies could impact democracy. As the Internet and the World Wide Web came online in the 1990s, attention exploded, and between 1990 and 1995, hundreds of publications discussed electronic participation in various ways (Gomes, 2018). Fifteen years later, Ann Macintosh (2004) defined e-democracy as “the use of information and communication technologies to engage citizens, support the democratic decision-making process, and strengthen representative democracy.” Macintosh also divided the term into e-voting and e-participation, where e-voting is concerned with the electoral process and e-participation with democratic decision-making. In this chapter, we are primarily discussing e-participation.

Further, Macintosh created a framework of participation where levels of participation, the policy-making lifecycle, actors, technologies, rules, and duration were critical dimensions. Later, Sæbø et al. (2008) and Medaglia (2012) built upon Macintosh’s framework and conducted reviews of the participation literature, where they found a wide range of actors performing different activities such as consultations, petitioning, agenda-setting, and voting. These frameworks can be extended by applying Birkland’s (2001) decision-making framework to see where different technologies are used.

While Macintosh's (2004) definition was clear that e-participation should strengthen representative democracy, there is an ongoing debate on what kind of democracy to aim for. Arnstein, for example, was clear in her opinion that participation should lead to a transfer of power from politicians to citizens (Arnstein, 1969). Several scholars (Held, 2006; Strömbäck, 2005; Van Dijk, 2000) also discuss different models of democracy based on who has the ultimate decision-making power, agenda-setting power, and so on, and these models point out that e-participation should take different shapes based on what model of democracy you are aiming for.

Trust and transparency are essential factors in e-participation. Transparency is a simple concept concerned with openness and access to government information through proactive dissemination, freedom of information acts, or public meetings (Bertot, Jaeger, and Grimes, 2010). Transparency is a prerequisite of trust, and trust is, in turn, seen as the “glue that binds society together” if we believe others are acting trustworthily (Svendsen and Svendsen, 2006). Defining trust precisely can, however, be a more challenging exercise. McKnight and Chervany (2000) show that trust is related to many factors, such as competence, integrity, benevolence, and predictability – a mix of values and actions. Our disposition to trust is based on our experiences of the past. If we have found that people are generally inclined to do what they say they will do, with moral values we believe in, then we will typically have high trust in our fellow citizens or government. High levels of trust can reduce transaction costs and even save lives. A study of the COVID-19 pandemic found that high levels of trust were correlated with fewer deaths from the virus (Reiersen et al., 2022).

For example, Trutnev and Vidiasova (2019) studied citizen involvement in St. Petersburg and found that citizens are less likely to believe in city initiatives or participate when they do not trust city officials and politicians. The blockchain can be defined as a decentralized, immutable, distributed ledger (Pierro, 2017). The blockchain makes it possible to perform transactions without an intermediary, and the immutable nature of blockchains means information cannot be tampered with once it is submitted.

A blockchain consists of blocks chained together, time-stamped, and linked to the previous block using cryptographic techniques. This secures the integrity of the blockchain, as a change to one block will invalidate the entire chain. Before a block can be added, transactions are validated by solving a complex mathematical problem. Blockchains are networked, and each node of the network maintains a copy, and all nodes are connected to each other. After validation, the block is submitted to all nodes in the network and only accepted if the majority of nodes agree that this is a legitimate transaction. This is the “distributed” part of the blockchain (Pierro, 2017).

While blockchains have many benefits, there are also some challenges. Transaction costs can be high, especially if many smaller transactions occur. Efforts are underway to address this issue (Shahab and Allam, 2020). The distributed consensus mechanism that ensures the security of transactions requires a lot of energy and computing power, and from a climate change perspective, blockchain technology is currently not sustainable. However, there are also experiments and emerging mechanisms underway that could change this. Moving from the current proof of work to a proof of stake verification of blocks could reduce the carbon footprint of blockchains by as much as 99 percent, but it has yet to be implemented (Shi et al., 2023).

A blockchain can be both public and private. In a public blockchain, everyone has access and can examine the transactions made, and everyone can add to the blockchain (for a fee). Public blockchains are semi-anonymous. The user is only identified through a binary address. In a private blockchain, the situation is different. You might need permission to add to or examine the blockchain’s content here, and only approved nodes can submit new blocks. Private blockchains typically have a limited number of nodes and belong to a company or organization. Security in private blockchains relies on the authentication of users rather than proof of work (Mohan, 2019).

Our literature review found several white papers addressing how blockchains can benefit government, democracy, and smart cities. The Hyperledger Foundation’s public sector interest group, for example, states that:

> Blockchain will be among the most consequential technology for Public Sector because of its specific role as a facilitator, validator, and custodian of trusted information. This technology promises to enable governments and other public sector entities to interact with societal participants.
>
> — O’Kelly, Muscara and Choudhury, 2019

The European Commission published a report in 2019 and found that “blockchain technology can reduce bureaucracy, increase the efficiency of administrative processes and increase the level of trust in public recordkeeping.” However, most initiatives have been theoretical or proof of concept. Nevertheless, the authors still see the potential and recommend “using the transformative power of blockchain to create new processes, organizations, structures and standards” (Allessie et al., 2019). The China Academy of Information and Communication Technology and Trusted Blockchain Initiatives (CAICT, 2018) analyzes the current state of the art and proposes a technology architecture for public sector blockchain use. They suggest four key measures to succeed with blockchain development in the public sector:

- The public needs guidance and information about blockchain technology and its use.
- There is a need for more research into the core technology and how it can be optimized.
- There are several use cases for how the economy can be strengthened, and deep integration between blockchains and the real economy should be promoted.
- Finally, the white paper suggests improvements at the policy level, as there are (in China) still policies in place that hinder development.

Policy issues are not just relevant for China, though, and the consulting company PWC has released a white paper on strategic blockchain policy (PWC, n.d.). They point to several countries that have succeeded with blockchain implementations, such as the UAE, Malta, and Liechtenstein. They suggest high-level strategies for technology and governance and call for organizations and governments to adopt technology-friendly and flexible policies. Looking further towards governance and regulation, the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) surveyed blockchain technology for intellectual property purposes (WIPO, n.d.). The WIPO report is interesting in a smart city and participation context, as it points out the collaborative possibilities and the potential of both protection of and increased access to public data. The report discusses governance and regulatory issues needed for successful blockchain implementation.

Looking more concretely at use cases, the Belgian ministry Ministerie van Binnenlandse Zaken en Koninkrijksrelaties (BZK) commissioned a report in 2021, which lists 21 use cases for blockchain and distributed ledger technology (Komorowski, Claeys, and Dam, 2021). The use cases span all government areas, from democracy to governance and public services. Examples relevant to this chapter include e-voting and electronic ID, welfare services, government spending data, personal health records, and moving government registries to the blockchain. According to the report, these use cases can lead to stronger democratic values, increased public integrity and collaboration, and government efficiency. A 2018 report by the consultancy company Deloitte (Deloitte, 2018) shows that Belgium is one of many countries wanting to experiment with blockchains. The report found examples from the Western world and middle-income countries such as Brazil, South Africa, and India related to health records, electronic ID, voting, smart contracts, and digital certificates. India receives special attention, as the country is leapfrogging development by moving several public services, such as land registration, to the blockchain. For countries struggling with corruption, poor transparency, and low levels of trust in government, the report states that the blockchain can be valuable in reducing corruption and increasing trust (Deloitte, 2018).
