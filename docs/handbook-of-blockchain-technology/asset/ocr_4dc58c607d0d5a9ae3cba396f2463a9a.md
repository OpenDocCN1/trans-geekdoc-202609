When court documents need to be serviced, in the context of an application to the court or a civil case most notably, this service is normally done personally to the intended recipient of the documents. Nonetheless, in cases where it is not feasible or practicable to deliver by personal service, courts will allow for substitute service.

In the context of cases where cryptoassets have been misappropriated, when faced with the difficulty of identifying potential wrongdoers, courts have permitted the servicing of documents via means of an airdrop of a non-fungible token (NFT), both where the identity of the wrongdoer was unknown and known. For example, in the UK case of Jones v Persons Unknown [2022] EWCH 2543, the claimant transferred £1.5 million-worth of Bitcoin to an investment vehicle operated by fraudsters, who had previously managed to convince the claimant to make the relevant payments. Specifically, the claimant had authorized the fraudsters to execute various transfers, as a result of which the claimant had supposedly made substantial gains. After realizing that he could not withdraw his deposited funds and alleged earnings, the claimant managed, with the help of investigators, to track his misappropriated Bitcoin to a wallet on Huobi, an international crypto exchange based in the Seychelles. As the defendants had not engaged in the proceedings, the claimant had been able to receive a summary judgment, ordering the delivery of the stolen Bitcoin and blocking the transfer thereof via a proprietary and freezing order. It was further determined that Huobi had held the stolen Bitcoin on constructive trust for the claimant, which meant that the Bitcoin could be recovered from the exchange itself. Most importantly for the purposes of this analysis, the Court had also held that as traditional means of service were likely to be ineffective on the facts of the case, the claimant had been given permission to serve the order via NFT airdrop to the unidentified defendants and to Huobi, as service through traditional routes (namely, through the Hague Service Convention) would not have been expedient enough and would have jeopardized the recovery of the stolen Bitcoin. A similar approach was adopted in the case of D’Aloia v Persons Unknown [2022] EWHC 1723. In the US case of Benjamin Arthur Bowen v Xingzhao Li (Case No. 23-cv-20399), the Florida District Court also allowed the service of a claim on a fraudster via an NFT airdrop to the wallets in which the misappropriated $2.2 million-worth of cryptoassets had been located, in circumstances where the identity of the defendant was known to the court. The Court considered that, since the fraud had been conducted on the blockchain, an NFT airdrop was reasonably calculated to give notice to the defendants.
