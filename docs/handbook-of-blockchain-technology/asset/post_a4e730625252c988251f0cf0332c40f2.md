## 4. Blockchain and the arts: foes – or unlikely allies?1

The arts are broadly recognized as an integral part of not only economic growth but also social flourishing, providing a source of inspiration, creativity, imagination, and expression (Baumol and Bowen, 1968; Throsby, 1994; Throsby, 1999). Unfortunately, the performing arts, and the arts more broadly, have been facing profound headwinds for years, which were accelerated following the closure of theaters during the Covid-19 era, despite a slight uptick in charitable donations, at least in the United States (Makridis, 2024a). However, artists’ lives have become more precarious, with deteriorating financial and mental health conditions noted in the United States and worldwide (Makridis, 2023). Nonetheless, arts and culture amenities remain a staple of the economy that consumers value and are willing to pay for (Makridis, 2024b).

Despite these headwinds that have challenged the arts and culture sectors, one of the first use cases for blockchain technology has been in the arts, through the emergence of Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs), which promise artists the opportunity to go directly to consumers without interacting with intermediaries. That promise holds significant weight for artists who often forfeit rights over their work to institutions and intermediaries, which hold the carrot of large audiences and the prospect of increased visibility and sales, but often fail to deliver on the promise. Furthermore, because NFTs live on blockchains, they have the potential to help enforce remuneration of creative content if the NFT is sold in the future or used in the production of new NFTs, providing artists with a stream of future income. Many scholars have argued that NFTs may have a democratizing effect (Makridis and Liao, 2023; Whitaker and Abrams, 2023).

The increasing adoption of, and interest in, NFTs among artists is particularly noteworthy, given that the sector has often been the last to adopt emerging technologies. For example, the digital assets’ cumulative trading volume, including digital artworks, collectibles, music, and other digital items, grew from almost nothing to almost $80 billion in early 2022 on the Ethereum blockchain alone. While volume growth has slowed substantially, it still accounts for several hundred million USD each week (see Figure 4.1), and institutions are increasingly opening up to it, both in the arts and beyond (Makridis, 2022).

Although most art NFTs would not be considered ‘art’ by the average consumer, art NFTs are still an important use case because they provide a simple way for an artist to publicly record his/her contribution. The heterogeneity in quality (and the presence of bad actors) largely reflects the reality that applications of distributed ledger technologies are still relatively new. Communities are wrestling with organic integrations of blockchain and art. Some argue that the expansion of blockchain in the arts may lead to a “financialization” of art that stifles creativity (e.g., Zeilinger, 2018) or that the use of crypto will erode true art (Joselit, 2021). Others remain optimistic, pointing towards the democratizing and privacy-preserving effects for artists and others (Evans, 2019; Makridis and Liao, 2023; McConaghy et al., 2017; Whitaker and Abrams, 2023).

Building on other reviews of the art NFT market detailing the history and evolution of blockchain for NFTs (van Haaften-Schick and Whitaker, 2022; Whitaker, 2019; Whitaker and Abrams, 2023), this chapter studies the use of NFTs as a vehicle for democratization in the arts with an honest evaluation of their efficacy to date and the challenges and barriers that have stifled progress. Unlike past reviews, which focus more on the history of NFTs, we critically examine the good, the bad, and the ugly of the art NFT market, with a focus on how NFTs interact with existing intellectual property (IP) law and the challenges that the market has faced to date. While we are optimistic about the potential for NFTs to help resolve IP disputes, particularly in an era of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) where IP issues have become even more contentious (Makridis and Ammons, 2024; Makridis et al., 2024), we nonetheless provide a healthy dialogue about the challenges that will need to be overcome to realize this future.

The remainder of this chapter is structured as follows. Section 4.2 provides some background on the art NFT market, focusing on the legal implications of NFTs, such as what owning an art NFT means, and their enforcement in the existing judicial system. We find that ambiguity about legal rights has stifled adoption, particularly when buyers have not been satisfied with what they have purchased. Section 4.3 provides empirical evidence on the trends in price and volume, including the expansion of wash trading (i.e., the fraudulent selling and buying of tokens). Such behavior has led to a surge of bad publicity in the media and detracted from value-enhancing use cases of NFTs. Furthermore, understanding the determinants of NFT prices and volume disciplines the diagnosis of the sector and gives an indication of what is to come.
