# Part III: Algorithmic Governance

The third part of the book examines how blockchain enables decentralized decision-making processes through the use of smart contracts, governance tokens, and Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs). This part presents various aspects of algorithmic governance in DAOs and explains its main challenges. It also discusses issues related to decision-making in DAOs and closes with an assessment of consensus algorithms that are used in blockchain systems.

In Chapter 7, Stamatis Papangelou, Klitos Christodoulou, Marinos Themistocleous, and Leonidas Katelaris investigate algorithmic governance and DAOs. This chapter examines the potential of DAOs to reshape governance structures and explores numerous DAO governance mechanisms like Token-based Quorum Voting, Quadratic Voting, Weighted Voting, Holographic consensus and more. The authors observe that each mechanism comes with its own advantages and disadvantages. This fact highlights the need for a framework that can be used to assess DAO governance mechanisms.

Continuing the exploration of DAOs and decision-making processes, Florian Spychiger, Michael Lustenberger, and Lukas Küng present their perspective on this topic in Chapter 8. Their work concentrates on governance considerations and outlines how decision-making is conducted within DAOs, from proposing ideas to voting and executing decisions. Similar to Chapter 7, this chapter reviews various DAO governance mechanisms, including Futarchy Voting and Reputation-based Voting. Both Chapters 7 and 8 focus on the same area, discuss DAO governance mechanisms, and provide interesting findings. The combination of the two chapters enriches the discussion and enhances understanding in this area.

The last chapter of this third part of the book sheds light on another interesting topic related to consensus algorithms. Today, a wide array of Consensus Algorithms (CAs) are used to support the operation of numerous blockchains. Each one of these CAs concentrates on improving a specific parameter of the blockchain trilemma. The work of Marios Touloupos, Marinos Themistocleous, and Klitos Christodoulou, reported in Chapter 9, provides an evaluation framework that can be employed to compare and assess CAs. The proposed framework can be used as a tool to clarify the confusion that exists in this area and extends the body of knowledge.

# Part IV: Blockchain Adoption and Maturity

The fourth part of the book navigates through the complexities of blockchain adoption and maturity. To achieve widespread adoption, we need to focus on education-training, infrastructure, capital, and talent. Education contributes to the development of new knowledge, awareness, and best practices, resulting in the development of innovative ideas. The infrastructure can be divided into physical, digital, and regulatory, and it refers to the necessary infrastructure that needs to be in place to speed up adoption. The existence of infrastructure has a positive impact on blockchain investments. It attracts capital as organizations tend to invest in countries where infrastructure is in place. From a different perspective, capital is required to invest in new ideas and startups. Thus, capital attracts talent who generate ideas and develop applications. Last but by no means least, talent participates in all four pillars, from building the infrastructure to converting ideas into products and revenues. Thus, talent generates income and capital.
