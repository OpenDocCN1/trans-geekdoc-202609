The resource management mechanism is based on the use of a smart contract (SC) that implements three main functions: the addNetworkProvider, the GetBestMatch, and the ResourceReservationTransaction. The first function is used for the registration of a network provider in the system, while the other two implement the resource management process. Essentially, when a network provider requests resources from the solution, the GetBestMatch function is triggered, which finds the network provider that can fulfill the request at the lowest price. Then the ResourceReservationTransaction is triggered to finalize the resource trading process between the two parties. The authors examined the feasibility of their proposal by conducting experiments in real blockchain testbeds using the Ethereum-based Quorum software. Their work extends Xevgenis et al. The selection of the most suitable consensus is based on the experimental results, taking into account the performance requirements of 5G networks, which are used as a reference for the evaluation of the proposed solution.

## 19.4 BLOCKCHAIN FOR NETWORK SLICING

A major challenge associated with NS and resource brokerage is the need to keep a transparent, fair, and open system within the available number of resources and several suspicious players. Blockchain and DLT functionalities can be utilized to address trust and security issues associated with the implementation of NS, either for the coexistence of various applications and services or for both the service and operational use cases of network providers. The trading of a network slice can be blockchain-based, where the smart contract orders the slice orchestration based on the agreed SLA from the 5G network slice broker (NSB). In this section of the chapter, we present a survey of several research works that examine the use of blockchain in NS.

Nour et al. (2019) propose the use of DLT in NS by presenting a blockchain-enabled NSB. The purpose of the NSB is to guarantee the construction of secure end-to-end network slices in order to support applications of 5G vertical industries, using resources from different stakeholders of the 5G network. When a slice provider receives a request to build an end-to-end slice, it publishes a request in the blockchain for resources regarding each sub-slice composing the end-to-end slice. After receiving the different offers for each sub-slice, the slice provider selects the best offer in terms of cost and the capabilities to meet the requested performance. The proposed solutions introduce the use of two blockchains, one permissionless and one permissioned. The negotiation regarding the resources takes place on the permissionless blockchain, where the prices and capabilities of all offers are visible to everyone. Once the selection of the provider has been made, the permissioned blockchain is used for the creation of the end-to-end service chain. This work examines the use of the Hashcash blockchain, which utilizes a Proof of Work (PoW) consensus, and the results present its poor performance in terms of time needed to instantiate a slice. Additionally, the authors do not mention which platform they recommend for the public blockchain and which for the private. Moreover, the use of wallets is not examined, and the experiments take place on machines located in the same area, which automatically excludes network-related parameters in performance evaluation.

Although the experimental results are promising, it should be noted that the experiment was conducted, categorized based on their requirements.

Rebello et al. (2019) propose a blockchain solution for NS, where they introduce the use of different blockchains for different slice requirements. In this work, the network slices are categorized based on their requirements, and the blockchain data structure, the consensus, and the communication protocol are tailored to each specific network slice functionality. The goal of this work is to present a blockchain architecture for the creation of secure network slices for each end-to-end use case in 5G. The implementation of this solution is also based on the HLF software. Rebello et al. (2019) test their solution in data center environments where there is no restriction regarding the resources. To ensure fairness in consensus, each data center of the network providers may host at most one blockchain node per blockchain (remember that each slice type is associated with a different blockchain). Blockchain nodes in a slice type are invisible to anyone outside the slice. This study proposes the use of a management blockchain where all VNF orchestration operations are logged to provide auditability and management regarding the slice creation. The management of various VNFs is accomplished by using SCs to introduce transparency and automation in this decentralized system. The architecture of this system is composed of four components: a user interface, the NFV MANO module, a blockchain creation server module, and a management blockchain server module. However, the evaluation of the prototype is conducted on one physical machine where the HLF nodes are running inside a container. As a result, we cannot be sure how this solution would operate if the nodes were in different networks and locations. Additionally, this work assumes that the blockchain runs in a data center environment. Furthermore, the scalability of the presented solution is not well defined, although in contrast to previous works, here a detailed analysis of blockchain’s operation is illustrated. Finally, the authors are not focusing on the resource negotiation procedure that takes place among providers in this multi-tenant and multi-domain NFV environment.

Xu et al. (2020) present use cases of blockchain in 5G networks in a very abstract manner. Focusing on NS and resource management, the authors propose the use of blockchain and SCs to introduce transparency and fairness to the system. The trading of a network slice is based on blockchain, where the SC orders the slice orchestration based on the agreed SLA described in the 5G NSB. The blockchain is integrated to store the usage of each leased resource and check the performance of a service provider against the SLA. According to the authors, the key benefit introduced through the blockchain is the establishment of a trust layer, which lowers the collaboration/cooperation barrier and enables an effective and efficient ecosystem. Also, blockchain prevents the single point of failure (SPoF) problem and thus improves the system’s security. Moreover, one of the elements that play a significant role, according to the authors, to the performance of this solution is the consensus mechanism. However, it remains at a theoretical level.

Zanzi et al. (2020) propose the use of blockchain technology in the telco industry, more specifically for the NS process, by presenting the NSB Chain framework. This study proposes the use of a blockchain-based NS brokering framework that will serve the needs of the industry vertical tenants. A new entity is introduced in this research work called the Intermediate Broker (IB), which uses blockchain technology to develop the NSB solution, allowing the infrastructure providers to allocate network slices to IBs through SCs. The IBs are responsible for the allocation and redistribution of those resources to their tenants. The main contribution of this work is the presentation of a hierarchical NSB framework and the design of a blockchain-enabled solution that uses SCs to perform dynamic resource allocation among tenants. To demonstrate the feasibility of this solution, the authors evaluate the NSB Chain framework by developing a testbed using the HyperLedger platform and the HyperLedger Caliper tool to check important performance metrics such as transaction throughput and latency.
