## 18. Fraud, crime prevention and financial crime investigation in blockchain

## 18.1 INTRODUCTION

The potential use of cryptoassets in illicit and criminal activities lies at the center of the greater conundrum on the adoption of cryptoassets and use of the blockchain. Cryptocurrencies, specifically, have been associated with different cases of fraudulent and unlawful activity, having been used in reported instances of terrorism financing, money laundering, extortion and ransomware. A 2023 Europol announcement (Europol, 2023) is indicative of the size and reach, that characterizes cryptocurrency-related offenses. In a recent investigation conducted by German and US authorities, both have been able (with the support of Belgium, Poland, Switzerland and Europol) to take down the infrastructure of a cryptocurrency mixer platform (ChipMixer) for its alleged involvement in money laundering activities. In the context of this investigation, about 19,094 Bitcoins (approximately €44.2 million on the basis of the then applicable market prices) were seized – a modest amount considering that the platform was calculated to have potentially facilitated the laundering of 152,000 Bitcoins (approximately €2.73 billion by reference to the same prices). Instances akin to the one described will inevitably enhance the arguments of crypto-skeptics vis-à-vis cryptocurrencies and blockchain, as a key aspect underlying the use of these assets.

On the other hand, cryptocurrency transactions have been found to occupy only a limited share of the criminal economy, when compared to cash and other forms of transactions (Europol, 2021). Even more importantly, there is a growing appreciation amongst law enforcement agencies and international bodies of the role that blockchain technology, cryptoassets and cryptocurrencies can have in the combatting of money laundering (Joint Working Group of Criminal Finances and Cryptocurrencies, 2022) and crime in general. The immutability and transparency of the blockchain are key aspects in this regard, providing access to a readily available source of information for tracing funds which have been the subject of suspicious transactions, as well as information on the identity of associated parties. Private actors and public bodies are tightening their cooperation and efforts in developing these tools and sharing their resources and knowledge to prevent and investigate crime on the blockchain (Joint Working Group of Criminal Finances and Cryptocurrencies, 2022). Recent cases have proven the usefulness of such synergies. Ultimately, as it is often the case, there are two sides to the same coin, which must be analyzed in relation to past experiences, the present and future expectations.

This chapter does not advocate in favor of, or against, cryptoassets; nor does it compare criminal activity on and off the blockchain. Rather, it focuses on the unique nature of cryptoassets and the blockchain to examine how their particular characteristics influence crime, crime prevention and crime investigation. It further examines policymaking, legislation and enforcement around the said topics and studies past cases of criminal activity involving the blockchain and cryptoassets.

## 18.2 CRYPTOASSETS, BLOCKCHAIN AND CRYPTOCURRENCIES

As a first step, it is necessary to appreciate the link between blockchain, cryptoassets and cryptocurrencies. Blockchain constitutes a database, in which the data is stored on individual blocks, which are linked and secured together (i.e. “chained” together) using cryptography. Each block usually contains a cryptographic hash of the previous block, a timestamp and transaction data. Changing the data contained in one block would necessitate the alteration of the data of all subsequent blocks. As a result, corrupting the data contained on the blockchain is extremely difficult. Because of this characteristic, the blockchain is often referred to as being immutable or very hard to tamper with.

With regards to cryptoassets, they can be perceived as a digital representation of a value or of a right, that can be transferred and stored electronically. Cryptoassets often rely on the blockchain to operate, utilizing its robust and anonymous nature. Therefore, cryptoassets and blockchain often go hand in hand, and usually form the subject of common debates. As subsequent analysis will showcase, cryptoassets have been at the center of some major cases involving financial crime on the blockchain. Hence, although the two constitute separate innovations on their own, cryptoassets are central to the analysis of crime investigation in the blockchain.

Finally, cryptocurrencies constitute a subcategory of cryptoassets and are used as tradable digital currency. While they do not rely on the existence of a central monetary authority to oversee their use, they are often accepted as a form of payment, akin to a traditional fiat currency. Nonetheless, other than with regards to central bank digital currencies, it is accepted that cryptocurrencies do not qualify as money, as they are not universally accepted as a medium of exchange, store of value and/or unit of account. Major cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin, are often the target of blockchain-related fraud. Hence, many of the examples cited in the following sections and below analysis will focus on cryptocurrencies in particular.

## 18.3 COMMON FORMS OF CRIME ON THE BLOCKCHAIN

## 18.3.1 The (Not So) Golden Era of Initial Coin Offerings (ICOs)

Beyond the links between cryptoassets, blockchain and cryptocurrencies, it is paramount to also identify the associations between cryptocurrencies, fraud and financial crime. One of the first cases that exposed the challenges of cryptocurrency adoption was initial coin offerings. Used as a means to raise capital, ICOs offer digital tokens to early-stage investors in an enterprise, in exchange for fiat currencies or other cryptoassets (Delivorias, 2021). ICOs have traditionally employed online marketing practices and the use of whitepapers in order to attract investors and funding and have been particularly attractive during the early days of the cryptocurrency boom. The initial popularity of ICOs can be attributed to a variety of reasons, including the rising popularity of Bitcoin, the ignorance of investors and the absence of alternative funding as a result of the EU’s greater dependence on banks for lending and financing, at a time when trust in banking had been particularly low.

Despite the revolutionary potential of ICOs, prominent examples were proven to be Ponzi schemes, short-lived and/or scams (Zetzsche et al., 2017). For example, during the lifetime of the cryptocurrency OneCoin, its founders had been able to raise approximately $4 billion from defrauded investors, who invested their money believing that their funds would be used towards the mining of (non-existent) OneCoin tokens. Against this backdrop, certain jurisdictions (China and South Korea are illustrative examples), went as far as banning ICOs altogether. In the EU, the European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs) issued warnings to investors (ESMA, EBA, EIOPA, 2018), often highlighting the speculative and volatile nature of virtual currencies (the terminology the ESAs used), the absence of a tailored legal regime to regulate them and the marketplaces on which they were exchanged (ESMA, 2019), as well as the absence of protective measures and safeguards that would otherwise exist under the traditional financial services framework (EBA, 2019). As the initial glam surrounding ICOs faded in light of major scams, negative headlines and the appearance of the much more regulated and controlled Security Token Offerings (STOs), other forms of cryptocurrency-linked criminal activity have come to the forefront.

## 18.3.2 Cryptocurrencies as a Currency for Crime

Cryptocurrencies have been criticized for being a common payment method amongst criminals, having been traced across the dark web in illegal transactions. On the one hand, when compared to cash and other forms of transactions, the overall number and value of cryptocurrency transactions related to criminal activities still represents only a very limited share of the criminal economy (Europol, 2021). On the other hand, seminal examples showcase how major darknet markets have relied on cryptocurrencies as the primary form of payment in purchasing illegal goods and services.

The Silk Road investigation goes back to the early days of Bitcoin adoption. Silk Road operated as a dark web marketplace where its users could purchase illicit products, such as drugs, with an estimated 9,519,664 Bitcoins having been exchanged on the marketplace from February 2011 to July 2013 (United States of America v Ross William Ulbricht [2013] 858 F.3d 71). In the same spirit, the cryptocurrency trading platform BTC-e, is estimated to have received more than $4 billion worth of Bitcoin over the course of its operations. BTC-e operated its servers from the United States (US) and catered primarily to the Russian market, before it halted its operations in 2017, after US authorities shut down the platform for facilitating transactions in cryptocurrencies linked to cybercrime, corruption and drug trafficking. A recent, and arguably the most seminal, case of the closure of a darknet market has been that of the OFAC-sanctioned Hydra Market, the longest running darknet market in the world. Hydra Market operated as a marketplace for drugs, stolen data (personal identifications and financial information) and money laundering services. According to Chainalysis, in 2022, Hydra held 93.3 percent of the economic value of darknet markets.
