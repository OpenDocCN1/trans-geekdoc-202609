# 14.4.3  Alternative Selection

An alternative selection is when politicians debate and deliberate on an issue. Typically, they will receive a case file from government officials, who have set up various options for the politicians to vote on, with a report on the potential consequences of each decision (Birkland, 2001). At this stage, several rounds of open discussion will typically occur before government officials are asked to provide background materials and potential solutions. Cities have a wide range of responsibilities, from schools and health to local infrastructure to zoning and urban planning, each with its own requirements for case handling.

We will examine one more closely. Urban planning is an important, sometimes controversial, and heavily debated part of local politicians’ responsibilities. Constructors want things high and big; neighbors wish for something else, and government entities such as environmental agencies, road authorities, and heritage protection agencies can stop development (Johannessen, Sæbø, and Flak, 2016). Digital urban planning makes it easier to involve citizens in planning processes and can be seen as a specific form of consultation (Hofmann, Münster, and Noennig, 2020).

Depending on the model of democracy, several technologies can be applied to these processes. Polling can be useful if you want a simple input on what citizens think about plans A, B, and C for an area. If you want to involve citizens more deeply, consultation using digital urban planning tools is more suitable, and for direct democracy, voting applications can be used. Blockchain technologies can increase transparency and potentially improve trust in political processes, as it is impossible to tamper with submitted opinions, and everyone’s opinions and politicians’ comments can be made visible.

# 14.4.4  Enactment

Enactment is the stage where politicians decide by voting on the different proposals put forth by government officials (Birkland, 2001). In a representative democracy, citizens are not involved at this stage, or if they are involved, it is via an advisory vote. In a direct democracy, citizens can vote and decide which proposal should become policy. Blockchain technology can also ensure that votes are not tampered with and make data available for public scrutiny. Documenting and archiving these documents can also be made easier using blockchain in the enactment process.

# 14.4.5  Implementation

Implementation is the process where the alternative decided upon is being put into practice. This typically involves an open call for private sector contractors to bid on a solution, for example, if the city wants a new bridge or school to be built or needs new software for public service. If there is no need for purchases, it is the point where the government administration goes to work to implement a political decision (Birkland, 2001). At this stage, consultation is arguably the most important participation tool. Crowdsourcing outside experts can help improve the process, and citizens, especially those who are directly affected by the implementation (e.g., as service users), can be involved as co-creators of the service or by providing input and testing the service during the implementation process (Breidbach and Maglio, 2016).
