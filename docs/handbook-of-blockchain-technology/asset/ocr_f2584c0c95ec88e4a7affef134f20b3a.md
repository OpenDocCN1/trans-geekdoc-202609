# 14.3.1 Smart Cities

Smart cities have many different but related definitions. One popular and simple definition is that smart cities are “places where information technology is combined with infrastructure, architecture, everyday objects, and even our bodies to address social, economic, and environmental problems” (Townsend, 2014). The smart city includes many application areas: communication, culture, energy, emergency services, environment/climate, health, leisure, safety and security, tourism, transport, and work (Gil-Garcia et al., 2015; Lazaroui and Roscia, 2012). In other words, the concept encompasses almost everything happening in the city.

Smart cities are a popular research area, with more than 2 million hits in Google Scholar and topics ranging from sustainability and urban planning to citizen inclusion and participation. We include a few examples to illustrate the breadth of smart city research: Carrozzino et al. (2014) show how augmented reality can be applied to increase tourist satisfaction in cities; Roche (2014) demonstrates geographic information system-based solutions for citizen participation; while Stratigea, Papadopoulou, and Panagiotopoulou (2015) demonstrate a framework for involving citizens in the planning of urban sustainability. Others have studied social media use for citizen involvement in smart cities and found that cities that actively use social media or online communication are more successful in involving users (Sáez-Martin, Haro-de-Rosario, and Caba-Perez, 2014).

Regarding technologies being used, the IoT, including smartphones and other sensors, and data analytics, are popular topics in the literature. Bhoraskar et al. (2012) demonstrate how the sensors built into our smartphones, such as GPS and accelerometers, can be used for traffic monitoring, something we see in practice when applying the traffic layer in Google Maps today. Collected data can be analyzed using machine learning or statistical techniques to predict, recognize patterns, make forecasts, or visualize and aid decision-making (Madni, Anwar, and Shah, 2017). Appel et al. (2014) demonstrate how data mining and predictive analytics can predict the number of vacant properties in a city. Massa and Campagna (2014) show how geotagged data from social media can improve urban planning projects. Social media data can also be used, for example, to identify under-developed neighborhoods (Quercia and Saez, 2014).

While smart cities are often linked with technology, the objectives can be social: citizen empowerment, social and digital inclusion, and behavioral change are some examples of smart city objectives (Angelidou, 2015). Urban challenges addressed with smart solutions are seen as “wicked problems” – problems requiring coordination and collaboration between different disciplines, public institutions, and organizations (Goodspeed, 2015).

Most definitions of smart cities include some reference to information and communications technology and the overall objective of improving the quality of life for its citizens. Some examples of improvements are better services, reduced environmental footprint, and more citizen participation (Dameri and Cocchia, 2013). Chourabi et al. (2012), for example, emphasized how “smart cities initiatives allow members of the city to participate in the governance and management of the city,” and Dameri and Dameri (2013) points to the importance of inclusion and participation in their definition. This is why we chose to examine inclusion and participation in this chapter.
