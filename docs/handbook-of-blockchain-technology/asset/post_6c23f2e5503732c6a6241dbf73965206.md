
## 8.4.5 Holographic Consensus Voting

In classical voting mechanisms, such as token-based quorum voting, a threshold of votes needs to be reached to decide on a proposal, which makes it not only difficult for DAOs with many members but also time-consuming.

To solve this scalability issue, the Holographic Consensus voting mechanism has been suggested by Field (2018). By assuming that market predictions can help to make better and more efficient decisions, it can be perceived as a variant of the above described futarchy mechanism. However, the basic idea of Holographic Consensus states that a scalable voting mechanism should allow for decisions in a DAO to be made locally with limited attention and voting power, as long as these decisions are in line with the global opinion of the DAO. According to Field (2018), such a mechanism would allow for faster decision-making by a small fraction of the members within the DAO that still represents the opinion of the DAO – thus like a hologram, where every little piece contains the information of the entire image. The alignment of the local decisions by a few DAO members with the global opinion of all members is achieved with the help of a prediction market, transforming a possible mismatch between the local and the global opinions into an economic opportunity.

To allow for a proposal to transition from an absolute-majority to a relative-majority vote, agents can stake tokens for or against a specific proposal which they think will be approved or declined by the DAO. Whenever agents are aligned with the DAO’s outcome, they will be accordingly compensated with further tokens – and if they are not aligned, they will be penalized by losing the tokens. A proposal is voted on only when it has reached a sufficient stake in its favor, and then it only requires a relative-majority vote. This so-called ‘boosting’ of a proposal is based on a dynamic threshold using an exponential function depending on the number of currently boosted proposals (Field & Weller, 2019). This implies that the Confidence Score on which a proposal is boosted is exponentially increasing with the number of boosted proposals. This dependence ensures that the DAO members do not have to make too many decisions, and instead their attention is focused on the most important proposals. A first analysis by Faqir-Rhazoui et al. (2021b) indicates that the mechanism indeed seems to follow the expected behavior.

## 8.4.6 Reputation-based Voting

In reputation-based voting – sometimes referred to as weighted voting – the weight of a vote is determined by its reputation within the DAO. The voting power in a DAO is therefore distributed as a function of reputation, whereby DAOs can overcome the issue of token-based voting where ‘wealthy’ members can accumulate voting power. Nevertheless, a reputation-based voting contradicts the democracy-based idea of ‘one-person-one-vote’ as some members’ opinions will be weighted higher than others based on some notion of merit. Kiayias and Lazos (2022) categorize a system to be meritocratic if the decision-making rights are distributed among participants that contribute to the platform in a positive manner. Members of a DAO can therefore gain a reputation if they have made positive contributions to the DAO in the past, which again serves as an incentive to actively participate in the DAO. Of course, this notion of ‘positive contribution’ is vague and needs to be defined carefully by each DAO. The use of non-transferable governance tokens is one possibility to account for this metric. This aligns with the governance structures introduced by Germonprez et al. (2014), where control is given to those who deliver. The non-transferable attribute of these tokens is of high importance, because otherwise the system could become vulnerable to attacks related to plutocratic systems, as introduced earlier. Reputation-based voting based on non-transferable tokens can thus counteract sybil attacks. However, the practice has shown that fair and meaningful reputation-based voting mechanisms can be costly and difficult to implement in DAOs (Zhuang et al., 2019).

## 8.4.7 Rage-quitting Voting

The rage-quitting voting mechanism is not a standalone voting mechanism but is rather used in practice as a complementary mechanism in token-based voting. After a proposal is approved by a majority in a token-based vote, rage quitting introduces a grace period before the proposal is implemented. During this grace period, DAO members who voted against a proposal or did not vote can withdraw their deposits from a DAO if they disagree with the outcome of the proposal and see a risk to the DAO and their investments. Rage quitting provides DAO members with risk protection and some control over the funds invested in the DAO (Wright, 2021). Moreover, as members who voted for a proposal cannot rage quit during the grace period, this mechanism prevents members from exercising self-interest in voting as they risk being left alone in the DAO if everyone else rage quits before the proposal is implemented. To some extent, rage quitting in a DAO protects the token minority from potential ‘dictatorship’ by the (wealthy) token majority, as they can leave the DAO at any time if the majority wants to take advantage of the minority (Ding et al., 2023b). Furthermore, DAOs with a rage-quitting mechanism are particularly attractive to those members for whom active participation in the DAO is more important than financial investment. This helps to keep DAO members more focused on the common goals than the financial aspects of a DAO. However, rage-quitting voting can also have a negative impact on the performance of a DAO, as it takes longer for a proposal to be adopted. This can lead to a decrease in the value of the DAO token or a lower participation in future votes.

## 8.4.8 Challenges and Issues Addressed by the Voting Mechanisms

In Section 8.3.3 of this chapter we presented current technological challenges, risks and issues connected to the design of decision-making in DAOs. The previous sections (8.4.1–8.4.7) described the main concepts of seven different voting systems, each focusing on different implementations trying to create an open and fair voting process. Table 8.1 shows the previously introduced voting mechanisms and explains how they are related to the current challenges and issues in decision-making. Hereby, we will focus on the aspects of governance centralization, power concentration and low participation.

| Voting System | Governance Centralization | Power Concentration | Low Participation |
|---------------|---------------------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| Token-based Quorum Voting | Governance is associated with token holdings whereby a members’ preference is simplified into a single vote. | A larger token holder can concentrate voting power and dominate the voting outcome. | A minimum participation required for a vote to pass (quorum) can encourage but also discourage voter participation. |
| Quadratic Voting | Involves a non-linear allocation of resources helping individuals to express the degree of their voting preferences. | Balances power distribution and gives minorities a more prominent say in organizations with large spreads in token holdings. | Discourages people without a vested interest and encourages broad participation by reducing the effect of one single vote. |
| Conviction Voting | Members can vote on various ongoing proposals, and the longer a vote remains unchanged, the more power the vote gets (4.3). | Eliminates the ability to influence votes last minute; reduces attractiveness for sheer token allocation by using decay curves. | Conviction voting gives persistent voters the ability to gather more voting power over time, and helps minor token holders to be heard. |
| Futarchy Voting | Futarchy aims to optimize decision-making by using prediction markets to achieve the best possible outcome for the DAO community (4.4). | Uses wisdom of the crowd and makes it harder for central actors to control both the DAO and society for their own interests. | Reduction of power concentration by opening the decision-making process to the many, instead of having it in the hands of a few. | Encourages to put some skin in the game, but can also lead to risk aversion or merely financially driven speculation. |
