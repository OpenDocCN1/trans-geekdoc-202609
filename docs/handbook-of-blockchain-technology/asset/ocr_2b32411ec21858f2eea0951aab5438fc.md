However, most developers have agreed that human intervention is still crucial, and the term 'decentralized autonomous organization' in the context of blockchains originates from a blog by Ethereum founder Vitalik Buterin in 2014. According to Buterin (2014a) a DAO 'is an entity that lives on the internet and exists autonomously, but also heavily relies on hiring individuals to perform certain tasks that the automaton itself cannot do.' In distinction to other organizational forms a DAO is characterized through three main aspects: first, its 'decentralization' that implies that a DAO is not managed based on hierarchical structures and property rights, but rather people interacting with each other according to a protocol encoded and enforced on a blockchain; second, its 'internal capital', meaning that a DAO owns some type of asset that can be use by the DAO itself to incentivize certain activities; and finally, its 'autonomy', that is, a DAO can decide for itself based on codes, which means that a DAO is not necessarily democratic, in the sense that a majority can decide, but rather predefined code. In this sense, in a DAO, pre-programmed rules are executed through smart contract on a blockchain.

However, a very narrow interpretation of 'autonomous' implies that a DAO can take all decisions on its own, which again would mean that all possible decision-making situations need to be foreseen and pre-programmed on the blockchain – or a DAO would need to have some sort of artificial intelligence to decide autonomously in unforeseen situations. This strong focus on a technical autonomy of a DAO is often referred to as the 'code is law' and represents an idealistic way of achieving governance within a DAO, where decisions would be made on-chain by DAO token holders based solely on the procedures encoded in the blockchain (Wang et al., 2019). Nevertheless, this concept of 'algorithmic governance' has been widely criticized for being dysfunctional in the presence of an unforeseen significant event (DuPont, 2017). The legitimacy of this critique can be seen by the downfall of the first large DAO, The DAO (DuPont, 2017; Mehar, 2019), where the code is law paradigm was ultimately broken by a small group of people who made a decision outside the encoded rules to alter the immutable history.

Therefore, in DAOs, there is a distinction between on-chain and off-chain governance that boils down to whether rules and decision-making processes are strictly encoded in smart contracts on the blockchain (on-chain) or if processes happen in a non-deterministic way without blockchain interaction. Based on practical experience, most DAOs today have both on- and off-chain processes in place which contextualizes DAOs more in the light of 'code is constitution' (Miscione & Schwabe, 2022), by defining how power is divided, and how regulations and their changes must be agreed upon before being enforced. However, as technologies mature over time a shift towards more on-chain focused processes might be possible in the future. Whether at one point a DAO can detach itself completely from off-chain decisions and live up to the vision of 'algorithmic governance' needs to be explored in practice. Currently, there are a plethora of different DAOs up and running and all of them differ in their purpose, structure, and governance models (Lustenberger et al., 2024).

That said, the common view today is that DAOs are dependent on human interaction and active participation, even off-chain, and thus outside the blockchain.
