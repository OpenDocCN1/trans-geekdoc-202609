The expertise of humans cannot be replaced by machines; however, it is widely acknowledged that machines can be more efficient in performing repetitive tasks. By utilizing advancements in machine learning and applying computational power, machines enable the completion of complex tasks with greater efficiency. Presently, the world is experiencing the fourth industrial revolution (IR4.0), a concept popularized in 2015 by Klaus Schwab, the founder of the World Economic Forum. IR4.0 encompasses technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI), cloud computing, the Internet of Things (IoT), and cyber-physical systems (Gazzaneo et al., 2020). These digital technologies are disrupting business models across all sectors, necessitating the acquisition of relevant skills by HR to effectively utilize them. However, there is a rising problem of insufficient skills among humans, attributed to the slow response of educational institutions, such as high schools and universities, which are key players in preparing the workforce. The world is gradually entering the fifth industrial revolution (IR5.0), characterized by smart employees, efficient management systems, and computer-aided, internet-based HRM hiring. The benefits of this approach were demonstrated by Telefonica, a renowned Spanish telecommunication company, where the use of information technology improved the effectiveness of HRM systems considerably (Gascó et al., 2004).

In this digital IR5.0 era, it is crucial to identify the competencies required to tackle current and future challenges, which differ from those of the pre-IR4.0 age. HR needs to possess competencies in areas that cannot be automated, such as creativity, problem-solving, and specialized knowledge in technological domains like IoT, Robotics, and AI. Different industry sectors face a competitive marketplace, and the knowledge and skills required may vary depending on the specific role within the organization. To address this challenge, some organizations adopt micro-credentials, which are developed by relevant training bodies. However, there is currently no agreed-upon standard for micro-credentials, leading to a lack of uniformity among different providers and variations in the training received by individual HR. This situation can also result in a mismatch between the skills provided by trainers and the job requirements of the IR5.0 industry, contributing to a vicious cycle of low economic activity and high unemployment.

Blockchain, being based on computational models, offers systems that are acceptable to all parties involved (Li et al., 2018). As mentioned in the introduction, Blockchain is a distributed database that allows direct access for all involved parties without any single entity controlling it. This eliminates the need for intermediaries and enables direct communication among the parties. With complete audit trails, all informational transactions can be traced, and once a transaction is made, it cannot be altered without the consent of each party, making it nearly impossible to falsify records.
