# 14.1 **INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND**

Smart cities are defined as “a well-defined geographical area, in which high technologies such as Information Communication Technology (ICT), logistics, energy production, and so on, cooperate to create benefits for citizens in terms of wellbeing, *inclusion* and *participation*, environmental quality, intelligent development; it is governed by a well-defined pool of subjects, able to state the rules and policy for the city government and development” (Dameri and Dameri, 2013).

Inclusion and participation are integral to the smart city (Berntzen et al., 2018), hence our focus on this area. Electronic Participation (eParticipation) refers to any participation in “societal, democratic, and consultative processes [that are] mediated by information and communication technologies” (Sanford and Rose, 2007). eParticipation is typically divided into different categories, such as polling, voting, consultation, petitions, or citizens as “human sensors,” providing data through the sensors we already have in our smartwatches and mobile phones (Berntzen et al., 2018).

eParticipation activities and smart cities generate a great deal of data. The European Union (EU) considers data essential to value creation in the coming years. The EU data strategy estimates that the value of the data economy in the EU27 will reach 829 billion euros by 2025. It, therefore, highlights big data, analytics, and artificial intelligence (AI) as key investment areas in the coming years (European Commission, 2017). This data economy is driven by several key technologies, such as sensor technology and the Internet of Things (IoT) for data collection, big data analytics and AI for analysis, and cloud services which provide scalable and flexible solutions for storage and processing, and blockchains for transactions (Günther et al., 2017).

The political decision-making process, illustrated in Figure 14.1, can be seen as a six-step iterative process consisting of issue emergence, agenda setting, alternative selection, enactment, execution, and evaluation. First, an issue becomes visible to politicians or citizens. If the issue reaches critical mass, it will become part of the public agenda, and the government will start working on alternative solutions to be put before politicians. The next step is enactment, such as a vote in the city council, before the administration implements the solution and, after some time, evaluates it (Birkland, 2001).

Several of these steps involve documentation needs and transactions that must be secure and free from outside intervention. Otherwise, we risk corruption and declining trust in the public sector. This is where blockchain technology comes into play.
