## 16. **Blockchain and the internet of vehicles:**
a conceptual model for demand response management

## 16.1  INTRODUCTION

As the popularity of decentralized renewable energy sources and energy storage devices continues to increase, it is estimated that future energy systems will incorporate decentralized components extensively. This trend is driven by three key factors: (a) the rapid adoption of Electric Vehicles (EVs); (b) strict government rules to reduce greenhouse gas emissions; and (c) popular hostility to centralized large-scale energy solutions, such as nuclear power and tall wind turbines (EU Blockchain Observatory and Forum, 2022). Within this shift towards decentralization, a challenge arises relating to effective Demand Response Management (DRM) in such a distributed system, while simultaneously ensuring secure information transmission.

In recent years, the Internet of Vehicles (IoV) has significantly reshaped the landscape of energy systems. IoVs leverage the capabilities of EVs and are combined with the Internet of Things (IoT) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) to form a highly decentralized network (Lone et al., 2021). Even though this network improves the potential and capacity of energy systems, it also considers the complexity that comes with the trend toward decentralization. As such, it seems like a way to solve the aforementioned problem, connecting the global trend toward decentralization and the revolutionary idea behind IoVs.

Blockchain technology has the potential to address this challenge and, over the years, there has been a growing interest in its utilization in smart energy systems and IoV for DRM. This is because blockchain’s features such as security, transparency, and decentralization align with IoVs requirements. In such environments, data sharing among various actors is crucial. Numerous studies have investigated the convergence of blockchain technology with IoV (Dwivedi et al., 2019; Khalid et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2022; Samuel et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020) with their outcomes reporting various solutions in areas such as (a) Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) integration, (b) charging infrastructure management, and (c) Peer-to-Peer (P2P) energy trading. Blockchain characteristics enable it to enhance the efficacy of DRM schemes within the IoV. For instance, it can ensure participant anonymity and incentivization while facilitating energy distribution, consumption, and trading over the IoV, including Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) and V2G energy trading (Samuel and Javaid, 2021). Therefore, the convergence of blockchain and IoV may have great potential for establishing a more efficient and secure DRM framework.

Although the theoretical potential of blockchain application in IoV is clear, empirical studies are required to test these claims and understand the practical implications. Scientific research in the domain of IoV DRM is still in its early stages and there is plenty of space for additional research and development (Aggarwal et al., 2019). Below are a few examples:

- IoV lacks suitable security and privacy protocols to reduce incorrect and malicious information transfers between EVs (Gill et al., 2019; Akhter et al., 2021). Such incidents can lead to accidents that put the lives of drivers, passengers, and pedestrians in danger.
- A lack of incentive mechanisms holds back end users (e.g., EV drivers) from participating in DRM schemes (Kapassa and Themistocleous, 2022; Kapassa et al., 2021). Because of the higher battery consumption and other costs associated with discharging, EV owners are hesitant to engage in large-scale decentralized energy trading systems unless they are adequately rewarded (i.e., incentivization schemes) (Moniruzzaman et al., 2019).
- Scalability and interoperability of blockchain systems, as well as the need for regulatory frameworks that support blockchain-based energy systems, need to be resolved to speed up the adoption of such solutions (Patel et al., 2019).

This study explores the convergence between blockchain technology, DRM, and IoV, and clarifies their potential synergy for improved energy efficiency in IoV environments. Specifically, it seeks to provide a clear outline of these separate concepts, identify research challenges and gaps, and then integrate them in an innovative manner into a novel conceptual model. The aim of this research is to introduce a new model for DRM in the context of IoV and blockchain technology. In addition, it seeks to highlight future opportunities and potential research directions to encourage continuing research and innovation in this area. This chapter also attempts to provide a substantial theoretical basis that can guide practical implementations for a more sustainable, efficient, and resilient IoV.

The remainder of this paper provides a thorough exploration of the topics under study, and is structured as follows. Section 16.2 provides a comprehensive understanding of key concepts like IoV, blockchain, and DRM. Then, the chapter sheds light on the intersection of these concepts (Section 16.3) and identifies key research challenges and gaps. In Section 16.4 the novel conceptual model is proposed, encapsulating the interrelationships between identified variables and addressing the research gap. Future perspectives are outlined and opportunities for both blockchain-enabled DRM in IoV and future research directions are discussed. Finally, in Section 16.5, the paper concludes by summarizing the major insights, reinforcing the significance of the conceptual model, and underlining its contribution to both theoretical and practical advancements in the field.

## 16.2  UNDERSTANDING THE KEY CONCEPTS

## 16.2.1  Internet of Vehicles (IoV): Definition, V2X and Challenges

As a result of technical advancements in networking, sensors, and electrical systems, traditional embedded systems are being replaced by Cyber Physical Systems (CPS). CPS is typically linked to internet technology to establish a connection between the cyber and physical worlds (Rajkumar et al., 2010). CPS has recently acquired popularity and is already prevalent in many aspects of our daily life. The Intelligent Transportation System (ITS) is an example of a CPS. The primary purpose of ITS is to build a safe, dynamic, and efficient transportation system of a CPS (Xiong et al., 2015).

IoV is proposed as a concept inside ITS; it is powered by smart vehicles, IoT, and AI techniques. Through the Internet and dedicated short-range communication technology, intelligent vehicles may communicate with other vehicles, drivers, passengers, and roadside units (Lone et al., 2021). Sometimes, the vehicular communication system is referred to as Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X). V2X enables interaction between vehicles and any other entity (e.g., vehicle, infrastructure, grid, pedestrian, etc.) (Mahmood, 2020). Therefore, IoV represents an integrated network of interconnected vehicles, smart infrastructure, and devices that enhances vehicle communication, safety, and efficiency. It is a significant branch of the broader IoT ecosystem, where vehicles act as moving nodes, exchanging information and interacting with their surrounding environment.

V2V: The V2V paradigm came into our lives with the introduction of smart city applications. Accordingly, V2V suggests that everything and everyone has the potential to be connected and communicate with each other. In this sense, the V2V paradigm allows two or more geographically close vehicles to interact and communicate (NHTSA, 2014). V2V communication takes place on top of a wireless network where cars can send messages to each other, with information like speed, position, travel directions, fuel consumption, brake status, and so on. For instance, vehicles that support an automated braking system and benefit from V2V communication will be able to proactively detect emergencies based on data from their own and surrounding vehicles, and avoid unexpected collisions. Road safety, traffic management, and energy efficiency are the key motivations behind V2V. Thus, the V2V paradigm has the potential to save human lives and increase driving performance, while at the same time reducing pollution and carbon emissions, especially in urban areas.

V2G enables energy to be pushed back to the power grid from the battery of EVs, and its concept is similar to smart charging. Smart charging allows the EV driver to remotely track, control, and limit the usage of the battery to maximize energy consumption. The V2G paradigm takes this a step forward, allowing energy to be transmitted from the EV’s batteries back to the grid and vice versa, offsetting differences in energy production and consumption. This way, EV drivers are not only consumers of energy but they are actively participating in the trading process. Thus, they can be characterized as prosumers (i.e., producers and consumers of energy at the same time) (Themistocleous et al., 2019). V2G charging technology can help prosumers save money or make a profit by allowing their EV batteries to store energy and return it to the grid when it is most required.

IoV Challenges: Data management and security are significant barriers in IoV. Through V2V and V2G interactions, IoV systems generate high amounts of data. If this data is not properly managed, it can lead to system inefficiency and the exposure of sensitive information. Considering the nature of the information at stake, data security in the IoV environment is highly important. Location-tracking data, driver-specific information, and specific vehicle status or diagnostic data must be carefully protected. A security breach could not only result in a violation of privacy but also enable malicious entities to manipulate vehicle systems, posing grave safety concerns. Real-time, dependable communication is another significant obstacle. IoV systems are based on instantaneous information exchange and thus, the smallest delay in information transmission can cause inefficiencies in the operation of autonomous vehicles or safety systems, which may lead to accidents.
