# 8.4.5 Holographic Consensus Voting

In classical voting mechanisms, such as token-based quorum voting, a threshold of votes needs to be reached to decide on a proposal, which makes it not only difficult for DAOs with many members but also time-consuming. To solve this scalability issue, the Holographic Consensus voting mechanism has been suggested by Field (2018). By assuming that market predictions can help to make better and more efficient decisions, it can be perceived as a variant of the above described futarchy mechanism. However, the basic idea of Holographic Consensus states that a scalable voting mechanism should allow for decisions in a DAO to be made locally with limited attention and voting power, as long as these decisions are in line with the global opinion of the DAO. According to Field (2018), such a mechanism would allow for faster decision-making by a small fraction of the members within the DAO that still represents the opinion of the DAO – thus like a hologram, where every little pieces contains the information of the entire image. The alignment of the local decisions by a few DAO members with the global opinion of all members is achieved with the help of a prediction market, transforming a possible mismatch between the local and the global opinions into an economic opportunity. To allow for a proposal to transition from an absolute-majority to a relative-majority vote, agents can stake tokens for or against a specific proposal which they think will be approved or declined by the DAO. Whenever agents are aligned with the DAO’s outcome, they will be accordingly compensated with further tokens – and if they are not aligned, they will be penalized by losing the tokens. A proposal is voted on only when it has reached a sufficient stake on its favor, and then it merely requires a relative-majority vote. This so-called ‘boosting’ of a proposal is based on a dynamic threshold using an exponential function depending on the number of currently boosted proposals (Field & Weller, 2019). This implies that the Confidence Score on which a proposal is boosted is exponentially growing with the number of boosted proposals. This dependence ensures that the DAO members do not have to make too many decisions, and instead their attention is focused on the most important proposals. A first analysis by Faqir-Rhazoui et al. (2021b) indicates that the mechanism indeed seems to follow the expected behavior.

*Examples:* DXdao, NecDAO, Prime DAO, DAOstack

# 8.4.6 Reputation-based Voting

In reputation-based voting – sometimes referred to as weighted voting – the weight of a vote is determined by its reputation within the DAO. The voting power in a DAO is therefore distributed as a function of reputation, whereby DAOs can overcome the issue of token-based voting where ‘wealthy’ members can accumulate voting power. Nevertheless, a reputation-based voting contradicts the democracy-based idea of ‘one-person-one-vote’ as some members’ opinions will be weighted higher than others based on some notion of merit. Kiayias and Lazos (2022) categorize a system to be meritocratic if the decision-making rights are distributed among participants that contribute to the platform in a positive manner. Members of a DAO can therefore gain a reputation if they have made positive contributions to the DAO in the past, which again serves as an incentive to actively participate in the DAO. Of course, this notion of ‘positive contribution’ is vague and needs to be defined carefully by each DAO. The use of non-transferable governance tokens is one possibility to account for this metric.
