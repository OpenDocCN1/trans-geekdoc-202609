
This means that it has not only become more difficult to carry out a ransomware attack, but also, even where a ransomware attack has been carried out against a company, the disruption and the economic repercussions that the attack might cause to the target's business is much less disruptive. Nonetheless, these standards only provide partial comfort, as setting up and improving on systems and back-ups can take several months, leaving the targeted company vulnerable to new attacks. Other initiatives that companies can take to avoid being the victim of a ransomware attack include the preparation of an internal incident response plan, employing third-party, specialist providers to advise on the payment or non-payment of the requested ransom, avoiding making payments in privacy coins, which may hinder the traceability of funds, and follow best practices recommended by appropriate authorities (Ciphertrace, 2023b, p. 17).

### 18.3.4 Cryptocurrency in the Laundering of Money and Financing of Terrorism

In addition, cryptocurrencies often come under the spotlight for their potential involvement in money laundering and terrorism financing. Such concerns have been linked to the nature of cryptocurrencies, the speed with which they can be traded, their international reach and pseudo-anonymous nature, the significant absence (until recently) of Anti-Money Laundering (AML)/Counter-Terrorist Financing (CFT) requirements surrounding their use, and the employment of new methods (mixers for example) undermining the transparency which otherwise characterizes cryptocurrency transfers. On the one hand, cryptocurrencies are being used in increasingly complex money laundering schemes in order to obfuscate money flows. On the other hand, cryptocurrencies have only been used in a limited number of cases for the financing of terrorism according to Europol (Europol, 2021). The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) has previously identified two challenges that are at the center of supervising cryptoassets for AML purposes (Coelho et al., 2021). Namely, the absence of a common taxonomy to classify cryptoassets and the absence of a common definition of providers of cryptoassets services, to define and identify them by reference to their activities.

The EU has sought to address these challenges via the introduction of a comprehensive framework on cryptoassets. The first important step in this respect has been the introduction of the fifth Anti-Money Laundering Directive (AMLD), Directive (EU) 2018/843, which has brought certain categories of providers of cryptoasset services under the EU AML/CFT framework. To this end, the Directive expands the definition of “obliged entities” to include custodian wallet providers and entities that provide exchange services between virtual currencies and fiat currencies. As such, the fifth AMLD has mitigated the problem of anonymity that is associated with the use of cryptocurrencies. By designating crypto exchanges and wallet providers as obliged entities under the EU AML/CFT framework, these entities are required to act as the gatekeepers against illicit transactions. At the same time, the Directive has allowed national competent authorities and ESMA to monitor the evolution of the sector and has operated as a stepping stone to the introduction of Regulation (EU) 2023/1114; the Market in Cryptoassets Regulation (MiCA).

As an EU regulation, MiCA is directly applicable in all Member States, without any need to be transposed in the national legal order. This ensures uniformity amongst Member States, avoids regulatory arbitrage and provides common standards to regulate what is an international phenomenon by its nature. MiCA has introduced key requirements on issuers of cryptoassets, persons offering cryptoassets or seeking admission of cryptoassets to trading and CASPs. Such requirements extend to key areas, including prudential and governance arrangements, conduct of business rules, supervision and transparency of operations.

Further, MiCA builds on the framework introduced by the fifth AMLD and extends a number of obligations commonly found in the traditional financial services framework to MiCA-licensed CASPs. Such obligations include an obligation of the management body to be of sufficiently good repute, possess appropriate knowledge, skills and experience to perform their duties.[2] MiCA-licensed CASPs must employ appropriate and proportionate resources and procedures, including resilient and secure ICT systems.[3] Further, CASPs are required to keep records of all cryptoasset services, activities, orders, and transactions undertaken by them, with these records being sufficient to enable competent authorities to fulfill their supervisory tasks and to take enforcement measures.[4]

At the same time, the definition of CASPs under MiCA extends beyond the more limited scope of the fifth AMLD to cover entities such as CASPs providing advice on cryptoassets and portfolio management on cryptoassets. As a result, MiCA also extends the possibility of enforcing sanctioning powers and remedial actions of supervisory and regulatory authorities to such actors, in the same way they are applied in the case of other regulated financial institutions, including the imposition of warning letters, public naming, fines, suspension or revocation of authorizations, the carrying out of fit and proper checks on their management. Overall, MiCA brings within its scope a number of actors of the cryptoassets space who have, until recently, been operating under a lax regime which is not tailored to their operations and now enhances their requirements and obligations, which is bound to have a positive effect in the fight against money laundering and terrorist financing.

Beyond the importance and specific requirements introduced by MiCA, what the above analysis also highlights is a broader picture of the role of regulation on cryptoassets. Regulation in this area is characterized by efforts to establish comprehensive legal frameworks which tailor for the specifics of cryptoassets and a body of existing laws which often do not apply to cryptoassets, as those laws were introduced prior to the uptake of cryptoassets. In this regard, initiatives which are adopted at the international fora, including persuasive consultation papers with recommendations from international standard setting bodies and cooperation initiatives, constitute very welcoming steps. Such initiatives help add uniformity to what has so far been a fragmented approach by the international community to the regulation of cryptoassets, an intrinsically cross-border innovation and addresses risks which are common to the sector. The work of the Financial Stability Board (FSB) (FSB, 2022), the Bank for International Settlements (Coelho et al., 2021, p. 17), the International Monetary Fund (IMF) (IMF, 2023) and the FATF (FATF, 2020; FATF, 2023)[5] in particular are key in this area. Concurrently, where international standards have been introduced, their effectiveness will equally be contingent on how they are being implemented at the national level.

As such, a proactive and comprehensive stance adopted at the national level is particularly important to navigate through the legal and compliance complexities that characterize this area, both for supervisory authorities and private actors. A good example of this approach is the case of the UK, which, following the introduction of the fifth AMLD, provided separate guidance to market actors on how cryptoassets could be subject to Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) regulation and the significance of this for market participants (FCA, 2019). Furthermore, the FCA set up a “cryptoasset perimeter” group, comprised of different teams of experts within the FCA (focusing on supervision, legal and policy), which handled the applications of firms with complex business models, identifying, inter alia, the risks of money laundering and terrorist financing posed by their unique models.
