The initial popularity of ICOs can be attributed to a variety of reasons, including the rising popularity of Bitcoin, the ignorance of investors and the absence of alternative funding as a result of the EU’s greater dependence on banks for lending and financing, at a time when trust in banking had been particularly low.

Despite the revolutionary potential of ICOs, prominent examples were proven to be Ponzi schemes, short-lived and/or scams (Zetzsche et al., 2017). For example, during the lifetime of the cryptocurrency OneCoin, its founders had been able to raise approximately $4 billion from defrauded investors, who invested their money believing that their funds would be used towards the mining of (non-existent) OneCoin tokens. Against this backdrop, certain jurisdictions (China and South Korea are illustrative examples), went as far as banning ICOs altogether. In the EU, the European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs) issued warnings to investors (ESMA, EBA, EIOPA, 2018), often highlighting the speculative and volatile nature of virtual currencies (the terminology the ESAs used), the absence of a tailored legal regime to regulate them and the marketplaces on which they were exchanged (ESMA, 2019), as well as the absence of protective measures and safeguards that would otherwise exist under the traditional financial services framework (EBA, 2019). As the initial glam surrounding ICOs faded in light of major scams, negative headlines and the appearance of the much more regulated and controlled Security Token Offerings (STOs), other forms of cryptocurrency-linked criminal activity have come to the forefront.

# 18.3.2  Cryptocurrencies as a Currency for Crime

Cryptocurrencies have been criticized for being a common payment method amongst criminals, having been traced across the dark web in illegal transactions. On the one hand, when compared to cash and other forms of transactions, the overall number and value of cryptocurrency transactions related to criminal activities still represents only a very limited share of the criminal economy (Europol, 2021). On the other hand, seminal examples showcase how major darknet markets have relied on cryptocurrencies as the primary form of payment in purchasing illegal goods and services.

The Silk Road investigation goes back to the early days of Bitcoin adoption. Silk Road operated as a dark web marketplace where its users could purchase illicit products, such as drugs, with an estimated 9,519,664 Bitcoins having been exchanged on the marketplace from February 2011 to July 2013 (United States of America v Ross William Ulbricht [2013] 858 F.3d 71). In the same spirit, the cryptocurrency trading platform BTC-e, is estimated to have received more than $4 billion worth of Bitcoin over the course of its operations. BTC-e operated its servers from the United States (US) and catered primarily to the Russian market, before it halted its operations in 2017, after US authorities shut down the platform for facilitating transactions in cryptocurrencies linked to cybercrime, corruption and drug trafficking. A recent, and arguably the most seminal, case of the closure of a darknet market has been that of the OFAC-sanctioned Hydra Market, the longest running darknet market in the world. Hydra Market operated as a marketplace for drugs, stolen data (personal identifications and financial information) and money laundering services. According to Chainalysis, in 2022, Hydra held 93.3 percent of the economic value of darknet markets.
