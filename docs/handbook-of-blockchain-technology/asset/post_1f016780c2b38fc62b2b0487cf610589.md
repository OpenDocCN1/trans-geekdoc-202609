
## Proof of Work (PoW)

Proof of Work (PoW) is a popular consensus algorithm used in various blockchains such as Bitcoin. Transactions are recorded in blocks, each containing the hash of the previous block, which links the blocks together in a chain. The decentralized nature of the network supports the administration of transactions in a distributed and totally decentralized way. In PoW, all new transactions are sent to a transaction pool. Specialized miners gather transactions from this pool and create a candidate block. Usually, the oldest transactions in the pool are included in the candidate block. Exceptions are transactions with higher priority. Before creating the new block, specialized miners verify these transactions to ensure they follow the rules (e.g., not spending more cryptocurrencies than they have). Besides the transactions, the candidate block includes metadata, the hash of the previous block, Merkle root, timestamp, target, nonce, and the number of transactions included in the block. Once the candidate block is ready, it is sent to the miners. To append a new block to the blockchain, participating miners must solve a mathematical challenge and produce a verifiable solution. For example, miners must find the correct nonce value based on the candidate block’s data. Miners compete to achieve this task, and the first person who finds the solution broadcasts the answer to the rest of the participants for validation. To validate a new block, more than 50% + one miner should verify the answer. When this occurs, the block is anchored to the blockchain and the miner who solves the problem receives the reward.

The quantity of participants, their computational power, and the volume of network traffic at any given time all affect how complex this puzzle is. Each block’s hash includes the hash of the block before it, a feature that strengthens security and provides a strong defense against any possible breach of individual blocks (Lucas and Paez, 2019).

## Proof of Stake (PoS)

Proof of Stake (PoS) is best illustrated by Ethereum’s switch from PoW to PoS and is considered a substitute for PoW. A key difference between PoW and PoS is that the participants of PoW invest heavily in computation power to solve difficult mathematical puzzles, whereas in PoS validators stake the network’s native token. They “stake” or “lock up” a portion of their token holdings while new blocks are validated by these staked validators. Incentives are given out based on how many blocks are verified and uploaded to the blockchain, and the decision of which validator to choose while creating a new block is impacted by their financial interest in the network. PoS uses a motivated mechanism to encourage consensus among validators. It encourages participation, efficiency, and network‑wide economic equilibrium. By staking their tokens, validators show that they have a stake in preserving the functioning and integrity of the network, hence reducing any malicious activity inside the blockchain network (Vasin and Co, 2017). The main stages of PoS are:

- Participation: Stakeholders express their interest to participate in PoS by staking native tokens. In doing so, they become validators, and their goal is to produce new blocks.
- Block creation: Validators are selected by the network to create and propose a new block. The selection is based on an algorithm weighted by the number of native tokens each validator stakes.
- Transactions validation: The validator responsible for creating a new block validates the transactions according to the rules (e.g., proper formatting, valid digital signatures) and then includes them in a new block.
- Consensus, incentives and security: Validators who participate in PoS agree to act honestly and follow the consensus rules. In cases of malicious behavior, penalties apply (e.g., risk of losing their stakes).
- Finality: After a new block is developed, other validators confirm that it complies with the rules and can be added to the blockchain. Once appended, the validator receives the agreed reward.

## Tendermint

Tendermint uses the BFT algorithm to address issues like transaction speed, scalability, and environmental effects. The Tendermint consensus algorithm stands out for its ability to withstand up to one‑third of Byzantine participants in the network, making it strong against potentially harmful activities. Like PoS, Tendermint participants stake their tokens to become validators. Another important element of Tendermint is the concept of delegation, which allows delegators to stake their tokens with a chosen validator (Baliga, 2017).

Tendermint works using three main stages:

- Propose stage: A validator node is chosen from a group of validators. The selected validator proposes a new block by collecting transactions, creating the block, and sharing it with all nodes in the network.
- Pre‑vote stage: Validators vote for or against the proposed block.
- Pre‑commit stage: When most validator votes are positive about the new block, the block is added to the blockchain.

## Proof of Luck (PoL)

The Proof of Luck consensus algorithm is primarily used in the Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) of Intel SGX platforms, but it may be modified for other systems with similar TEE characteristics. This protocol was created to overcome several issues with traditional consensus algorithms, including poor transaction rates, high energy usage, and time‑intensive operation. However, one area of contention with PoL is its reliance on specialist technology, which may not be accessible to everybody and may be especially advantageous to those endowed with such equipment.

In the context of PoL, each round commences with the participant invoking the PoLRound function and submitting the most recent block to a designated chain. Upon the conclusion of the round time, the participant activates the PoLMine function to create a new block. This newly minted block is then linked to its predecessor via the new block’s headers. The protocol stipulates an inspection for parent blocks in cases where the preceding block deviates from the round block (Milutinovic et al., 2016).

## Proof of Disease (PoD)

The Proof of Disease consensus algorithm is designed exclusively for use in the healthcare blockchain, aiming to provide speedy, cost‑effective access to high‑quality medical care data. This approach separates miners into two categories: currency miners and medical miners. Medical miners verify the legitimacy and appropriateness of medical transactions and health status inputs on the healthcare blockchain ledger. Currency miners are also responsible for evaluating financial transactions to detect and avoid potential threats such as Sybil and double‑spending attacks. PoD is based on the Ethereum blockchain, and thus, the transaction fee structure and reward mechanisms for currency miners are consistent with Ethereum’s existing infrastructure (Talukder et al., 2018).

## Raft

The Raft consensus algorithm (Huang et al., 2018) serves as a methodology for distributed systems, designed with an emphasis on simplicity and ease of understanding. Its aim is to address the challenge of achieving agreement on a shared state across multiple servers, even when one or more servers experience failures. The shared state is typically maintained with a replicated log, which is, in most cases, a data structure. The system’s full functionality is assured, provided that the majority of the servers remain operational. In the Raft model, consensus is achieved by electing a leader within the cluster. The leader is responsible for processing client requests and managing the replication of the log to other servers within the network. Moreover, Raft breaks down the complexity of consensus into three sub‑problems:

- 1. Leader Election: In the event of the leader’s failure, the protocol necessitates the election of a new leader.
- 2. Log Replication: The leader must ensure the synchronization of all servers’ logs with its own, through replication.
- 3. Safety Ensurance: If a server has committed a log entry for a particular index, it must be guaranteed that no other server can apply a different log entry for the same index.

## Proof of Personhood (PoP)

Proof of Personhood (PoP) is a consensus algorithm designed specifically to address security issues such as Sybil and double‑spending attacks (Borge et al., 2017). PoP ensures pseudonymous accountability by associating real‑world identities with the coin minting process. This algorithm combines the RandHound algorithm, the Pseudonym party concept, and the ByzCoin protocol. Aspiring coin minters must first join PoP as members of the Pseudonym Party. Membership involves physical presence at a specific location where the Pseudonym Party is hosted.
