Such entries are also referred to as “pragmatic constraints”. Let S denote the set of pragmatic constraints.

Based on the above definitions and discussion, it is clear that query complexity is a key characteristic of the proposed model. For the case of a single country c, this simply refers to the number of queries needed for estimating the R_c score. There are various ways to formulate queries. This variety can be characterized by two ends as follows. For each of the aforementioned set (P, N, C and S), all of its lexical entries are combined in a single query fragment using disjunctive operators. Accordingly, this requires three queries per country, while the query complexity, O_l, is constant and denoted as follows:

$$O_l$$

On the other hand, one may formulate a separate query fragment for each entry of the aforementioned sets. This approach significantly increases the query complexity, O_h, as:

$$O_h\left((|P|\,|C|\,|S|)+(|N|\,|C|\,|S|)+(|C|\,|S|)\right)$$

Which can be factorized as:

$$O_h\left((|P|+|N|+1)\,|C|\,|S|\right)$$

This factorization directly reveals a cubic degree of complexity. For the sake of clarity, it should be noted that “| . |” stands for the cardinality of the respective set, which is irrelevant to the “|” search operator used in this chapter. It is obvious that there is a significant difference between the two ends, i.e., constant vs. cubic query complexity. In general, many query formulation strategies can be devised at any point between the two ends. From a modeling perspective, the aforementioned complexities represent the lowest (O_l(.)) and the highest (O_h(.)) bounds, respectively, as far as (1) is concerned, for estimating R_c. The advantage of employing a complex query formulation strategy is the semantic control gained by reducing the use of search operators like the disjunctive “|” operator. The application of the proposed model for a list of countries exhibits linear query complexity, with respect to the size of the list, since countries are independent.

## 12.4 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

In this section, we present the web mining results for estimating the regulatory tendency of 194 countries towards blockchain technology and cryptocurrencies. The purpose of this experiment is to provide each country’s regulatory stance hypothesis and act as an individual indicator towards the 2021 version of the Blockchain Readiness Index (Vlachos, Christodoulou and Iosif, 2019).

For this purpose, the dataset was extracted for all assessed countries, and takes into account all historic web mining data up to February 1, 2022. The Blockchain Readiness Index will be updated and published annually. We have decided to assess all historic data, because if we only assess data for one calendar year, that is, the 365 days of 2021, the results for some countries may seem misleading due to the small amount of data gathered. For example, Montenegro only returned seven positive and negative results for the year 2021, and consequently the algorithm placed Montenegro as the top-scoring country. This is not accurate as the blockchain regulatory stance of Montenegro appears to be questionable compared to other countries (Global Legal Insights, 2021).

The experimental setup followed for this study is as follows:

- Web search.

Google’s Programmable Search Engine was utilized.3 Specifically, this engine was configured in order to take into account the entire World Wide Web (as indexed by Google and offered via the used service), while the supported language was set to English.

- Query formulation.

Regarding C, only a single lexicalization was used – the country names included in Appendix A. For the case of pragmatic constraints (members of set S), the following were used: “cryptocurrencies”, “blockchain regulation”, “mining”, “cryptocurrencies as property”, “crypto assets”, “stablecoins”, “CBDCs”,4 “tokens”, “ICOs”, “cryptocurrencies as security”, “cryptocurrency trading”, “Bitcoin”, “blockchain”, “Bitcoin as legal tender” and “NFTs”5 (Antonopoulos, 2015; Christodoulou et al., 2022; Themistocleous et al., 2023). Also, the hq parameter6 of the search engine was set to “regulation of cryptocurrencies”. In order to achieve constant query complexity, for the case of positive and negative lexical cues (members of P and N), the following cues were used:

- Positive Cues: recognizes, allows, issues, classifies

- Negative Cues: does not allow, has forbidden, restricts, bans

The addition of the “ ” search operator (i.e., “recognizes” and “restricts”), enables the consideration of synonyms.

The results are presented in Appendix A. It is observed that within the top 50 ranks, the algorithm places nations that have occasionally issued favorable blockchain regulatory guidelines and/or frameworks, such as Bermuda (6th), Malta (12th), Gibraltar (25th), Hong Kong (33rd) and Singapore (43rd). Among the low-scoring countries we find countries that are known for banning cryptocurrency-related activities, like Nepal (129th), Morocco (136th), Tunisia (160th) and Algeria (161st). We also observe an interesting finding; the top five scoring countries are Micronesia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Nauru, Kiribati, and Antigua and Barbuda. Some of our top-ranked countries, like Saint Vincent and the Grenadines (2nd), Antigua and Barbuda (5th), St Kitts and Nevis (11th), Dominica (14th) and Grenada (34th) have been pioneers in blockchain regulation as they belong to the economic area of the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank that has issued the “DCash” Central Bank Digital Currency (Auer et al., 2020).

The results indicate that most of the countries that are aware of their regulatory approaches, either hostile or non-hostile, are fairly scored. However, since we do not have a justified expert opinion on all 194 assessed countries, we developed a ground truth mechanism, as follows:
