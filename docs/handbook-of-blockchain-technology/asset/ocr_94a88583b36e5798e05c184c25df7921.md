Gregores Fylaktou and Charis Savvides

# 18.1  INTRODUCTION

The potential use of cryptoassets in illicit and criminal activities lies at the center of the greater conundrum on the adoption of cryptoassets and use of the blockchain. Cryptocurrencies, specifically, have been associated with different cases of fraudulent and unlawful activity, having been used in reported instances of terrorism financing, money laundering, extortion and ransomware. A 2023 Europol announcement (Europol, 2023) is indicative of the size and reach, which characterizes cryptocurrency-related offenses. In a recent investigation conducted by German and US authorities, both have been able (with the support of Belgium, Poland, Switzerland and Europol) to take down the infrastructure of a cryptocurrency mixer platform (ChipMixer) for its alleged involvement in money laundering activities. In the context of this investigation, about 19,094 Bitcoins (approximately €44.2 million on the basis of the then applicable market prices) were seized – a modest amount considering that the platform was calculated to have potentially facilitated the laundering of 152,000 Bitcoins (approximately €2.73 billion by reference to the same prices). Instances akin to the one described will inevitably enhance the arguments of crypto-skeptics vis-à-vis cryptocurrencies and blockchain, as a key aspect underlying the use of these assets.

On the other hand, cryptocurrency transactions have been found to occupy only a limited share of the criminal economy, when compared to cash and other forms of transactions (Europol, 2021). Even more importantly, there is a growing appreciation amongst law enforcement agencies and international bodies of the role that blockchain technology, cryptoassets and cryptocurrencies can have in the combatting of money laundering (Joint Working Group of Criminal Finances and Cryptocurrencies, 2022) and crime in general. The immutability and transparency of the blockchain are key aspects in this regard, providing access to a readily available source of information for tracing funds which have been the subject of suspicious transactions, as well as information on the identity of associated parties. Private actors and public bodies are tightening their cooperation and efforts in developing these tools and sharing their resources and knowledge to prevent and investigate crime on the blockchain (Joint Working Group of Criminal Finances and Cryptocurrencies, 2022). Recent cases have proven the usefulness of such synergies. Ultimately, as it is often the case, there are two sides to the same coin, which must be analyzed in relation to past experiences, the present and future expectations.

This chapter does not advocate in favor of, or against, cryptoassets; nor does it compare criminal activity on and off the blockchain. Rather, it focuses on the unique nature of cryptoassets and the blockchain to examine how their particular characteristics influence crime, crime prevention and crime investigation.
