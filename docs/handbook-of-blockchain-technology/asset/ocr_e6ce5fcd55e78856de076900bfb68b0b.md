On the basis of the analyzed data, it appears to be a contradiction: ML is regarded as the most significant technological advancement of recent times, yet businesses continue to use it infrequently, particularly in Italy. All of this can be explained in a variety of ways, but it is clear that the root causes are a lack of skills and appropriate tools, as well as a lack of confidence in ML.

This idea has to do with the so-called “unexplainability” (not being able to be explained) of ML, which is the inability to understand the processes and reasoning behind the decisions made by a ML system. ML is often thought of as a “black box” that contains data and algorithms and works in ways that are hard to understand because it is not clear how it works. If the system could explain how it works, it could foster a natural connection of trust with the technology in question, especially when it comes to highly sensitive issues like safety. Achieving confidence is one of the primary goals of European law, which seeks to avoid unduly restricting development. A second goal is to protect the ideas and values that make up the common legal system.

However, it is not a comprehensive regulation in all areas that should be addressed, and it focuses on high-risk systems in particular. Systems that must comply with particular technical standards, such as the use of high-quality data sets, proper documentation to offer transparency and traceability, and the use of compliance monitoring and certification systems, including human surveillance techniques. Enhanced security, cybersecurity, and accuracy measures are also essential. There are fines and penalties for noncompliance. Compliance with the minimum transparency standards is adequate for low-risk systems.

Liability, considered to be any damage caused by ML applications, remains an unsettled question. There is a two-year window for putting the rule into place, improving it, and allowing companies to invest more in ML.

To summarize, our findings align with Kitching, Hart, and Wilson’s (2015) theory of the dynamic impact of rules on the development of a corporation. Regulations can help businesses offer new products and services and build trust, reputation, and skills, but they can also take a lot of time and money, which can slow down growth. While rules permit some firms to operate in a legal environment and give consumers, investors, and users trust, confidence, and peace of mind, they come at a cost and are believed to hinder the growth of businesses.
