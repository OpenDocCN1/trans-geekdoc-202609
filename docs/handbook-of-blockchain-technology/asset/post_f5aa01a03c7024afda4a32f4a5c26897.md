The concept of a smart (sea or dry) port is aligned with the digitalization efforts using blockchain, and many ports around the globe are already creating their proofs of concept to improve the efficiency and sustainability of their operations (Yan et al., 2021).

Ships, ports, and many other stakeholders of modern transportation have identified multiple challenges that can be addressed with digitalization and ledger technologies. Classical examples include:
- tracking and tracing of shipments to ensure security;
- eliminating excessive paperwork;
- real-time location for transparency;
- solving delivery problems related to damaged material or delays;
- addressing payment problems that may involve conflicts between different parties (Baygin et al., 2022).

The positive answer to whether blockchain can be a solution to freight transportation by land, sea, or air was already provided by Irannezhad (2020a). However, the author also points to several obstacles, including capacity and scalability limitations, insufficient commercial applications, throughput, interoperability, usability, and even the lack of standards.

## 15.4 CASE STUDIES AND REAL-WORLD EXAMPLES

There are several features that blockchain-based solutions are capable of delivering and are relevant when cargo is being transported. Such solutions allow the tracking and tracing of products, providing more visibility and transparency to that part of the supply chain. Blockchain is gradually being tested in real shipping settings, involving a plethora of business partners around the globe. The types of use cases are also impressive, as revealed by Green et al. (2020). Green et al. found examples in paperless shipping processes, freight tracking, secure data sharing, payments and finance, certification and standards development, insurance, and safety. Relevant ports can be found in the list, for example, the Port of Rotterdam, Port of Antwerp, Abu Dhabi Ports, Singapore, and the Port of Koper. Their roles vary, ranging from leaders in blockchain testing, like the initial examples in the list, to assuming a participant role in the initial stages of eBL use. Nevertheless, ports are not alone in the quest for increased trust and visibility in transportation. High-tech companies like Microsoft, IBM, and Hyundai, certification entities like Lloyd’s Register, and global logistics companies like Maersk and COSCO are combining their efforts in blockchain proof of concepts, not forgetting the essential role of startups.

Several proofs of concept provided positive results. Maersk, a shipping company from Denmark, in partnership with IBM, developed a proof of concept and tracked a container carrying flowers from Mombasa to Rotterdam (Groenfeldt, 2017). Following the encouraging results of the partnership created by ABN AMRO, Port of Rotterdam, and Samsung SDS, the first multi-modal container labeled as paperless, completely traceable, and with quick financing was tested in 2019. This project is part of the investment agenda of this vital port, using blockchain as a foundation for the port of the future and supporting new entrepreneurs in the area (Port of Rotterdam, 2023).

Baygin et al. (2022) achieved real-time shipment tracking and full transparency in shipping management systems through the use of RFID and blockchain technologies. They developed an application for local cargo distribution that prevents sharing of personal information with third parties without permission, provides reliable shipment tracking without violation of rights between the different parties involved, as well as traceability, transparency, auditability, and accountability through the use of smart contracts.

Walmart Canada and its carriers used to have multiple information systems that were unable to communicate with each other. This led to payment disputes and a manual and time-consuming reconciliation process. To overcome this issue, they developed a blockchain-based freight payment system called DL Freight in 2019. The system was a tremendous success, allowing Walmart Canada to reduce the number of invoices with discrepancies (i.e., disputes) from over 70 percent, prior to DL Freight, to less than 1 percent today. Moreover, these disputes are easily flagged and quickly resolved (Vitasek et al., 2022).

Nevertheless, there are also some drawbacks in the process, like the failure of the ShipChain project which aimed to create a token to simplify transactions in shipping, the end of the Hong Kong-based 300Cubits, or the recent shutdown of TradeLens created by Maersk and IBM (Green et al., 2020). These examples reveal that technology is only one side of the coin of blockchain success in traceable shipping data. How the market is faring, collaborative networks, and standardization carry a significant share of responsibility in blockchain success.

There are three prevailing categories of projects presented in Table 15.1, namely, document management platforms, aiming to dematerialize workflows (e.g., eBL, eCMR, among other required documents for port operation and regulatory compliance), improve communication between stakeholders, share information about shipment, cargo, and other relevant information for efficient transport systems, and traceability systems, which may involve other technologies like the Internet of Things (IoT). Only a few projects include payment functionalities. Although these categories overlap significantly in some projects (e.g., document management solutions are also aimed at improving communication), the findings are aligned with the applications selected by one of the most comprehensive studies made by Ahmad et al. (2021), that also points to the importance of automation in port terminal operations or other less common applications in scheduling and container stacking, asset certification, customs clearance, or prioritizing container shipment using IoT-based blockchain (Ahmad et al., 2021).

Most projects found are related to seaport contexts, opening up new research opportunities to adapt the suggested architectures to dry ports. Significant advances have been made in multimodal information flows and blockchain-based traceability that expand sea transport, like the work of Elmay et al. (2022). Nevertheless, future projects should adhere to essential architectural design requirements, namely:
- exploring permissioned public blockchains to lower entry barriers and create trust among several actors;
- enabling interoperability via APIs;
- preparing for thousands of concurrent transactions per hour in some use cases;
- pre-identifying the roles of the nodes;
- enabling off-chain data storage and encryption (Irannezhad, 2020b).

One of the most recent advances in integrated blockchain-based shipment transportation started in 2023 and is under development at the Port of Sines in Portugal,¹ a strategic location for green and digital port transformation. NEXUS will integrate maritime and road digital documentation using blockchain, supporting critical information flows with dry ports and governmental agencies (e.g., finance).
