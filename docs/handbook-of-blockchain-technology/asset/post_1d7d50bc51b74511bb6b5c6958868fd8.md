## 15.2 BLOCKCHAIN TECHNOLOGY

Blockchains are a type of database supported by a decentralized, distributed ledger that enables secure, transparent, and immutable data storage.

The ledger is organized as a chain of blocks, each one containing data, a timestamp, and a unique cryptographic hash calculated using the data and the previous block’s hash, creating a structure linking each block to the previous one in a tamper-evident manner (Yaga et al., 2018). Any attempts to modify the data in an existing block will cause the hash to change and, consequently, invalidate the hashes of all the subsequent blocks in the chain. This is why blockchains are said to be “immutable.” Following the same logic, they are also append-only, with the chain of blocks growing indefinitely and securely preserving the history of all past transactions.

Furthermore, the ledger is not maintained by a single authority but by a network of peers. Each holds a copy and only updates it after a consensus about the legitimacy of a data-adding transaction is reached using specialized algorithms. Consequently, no entity can singlehandedly modify the ledger.

More modern blockchains also support smart contracts. These are Turing-complete computer programs stored on the ledger and automatically executed by the network of peers when predetermined conditions are met (Swan, 2015). Like any other data stored on a blockchain, smart contracts cannot be tampered with, providing confidence to the parties that they will be executed as written, eliminating potential non-compliance with previously agreed terms.

Lastly, blockchains can generally be classified as ‘permissionless’ and ‘permissioned.’ In the former, anyone can join the network, whereas the latter limits participation based on some type of authorization (Yaga et al., 2018). Some authors make more fine-grained distinctions, such as public, private, consortium, and hybrid blockchains (Kaur et al., 2020). The first type is open for anyone to access, and all participating peers have the same authority to verify transactions and validate blocks. The second type is owned by an organization and restricted to authorized users. The owner also determines the controlling peers. The main difference in the third type is that a community rather than a single organization manages the blockchain.

Finally, the fourth type is a hybrid of the public and private blockchain, enabling users to decide who can participate and which transactions can be made public (Javed et al., 2021).

The above design decisions afford blockchains several important characteristics. They are:

- **Trustworthy**, since (a) recorded data cannot be retroactively changed, and (b) agreed terms encoded in smart contracts will be respected, as they automatically execute in the face of the previously specified conditions.
- **Transparent and auditable** single sources of truth exist since recorded data is tamper-proof, timestamped, and widely available from multiple peers, eliminating the possibility of a single entity blocking access.
- **Ideal for preserving historical data**, as nothing is deleted or amended, just added.
- **Resilient to power or communication outages**, and even some forms of cyberattack, since the data is available from many locations.
- **Decentralized**, since they do not require a trusted third party (e.g., a public notary or a bank) to mediate transactions.

Bitcoin is the most famous use of blockchain technology (Nakamoto, 2008). In this case, it enabled the implementation of a peer-to-peer cash system without resorting to an intermediary trusted by all participants. Since then, there have been multiple other experiments, such as enabling traceability in supply chains (IBM, 2023; Volpicelli, 2017), improving logistics (White, 2018), reinventing notarization (bitcoin.com, n.d.), solving the fragmentation of health records (Green, 2018), or addressing intellectual property rights (Barata et al., 2020). Further, several uses for socioeconomic development are reported by Cunha et al. (2020) and Cunha et al. (2021), namely for secure and lean digital identification (ID), enabling more efficient and transparent funding of humanitarian aid, improving government management of public benefits, ensuring the integrity of public records, fighting tax fraud and corruption, and enabling innovative digital financial instruments for the traditionally unbanked, among others. Blockchain has also been used to support migrant and refugee health (Corte-Real et al., 2022).

There are, however, several risks and limitations to be aware of (Yaga et al., 2018). Notably, it is critical that any information entering the blockchain is correct and timely. So-called oracles are services that provide these verifications, but there are challenges in ensuring that they do not provide corrupt, malicious, or inaccurate data (Al-Breiki et al., 2020). The blockchain network should also be carefully established and governed since key blockchain assurances, such as its immutability, can be defeated by the collusion of more than 50 percent of its peers.

## 15.3 BACKGROUND ON SHIPMENT TRANSPORTATION

Point-to-point shipment transportation received an enormous boost with the growth of electronic commerce supporting global networks (Baygin et al., 2022). Trucks, airplanes, trains, and even drone delivery are examples of vehicles used to move cargo around the globe. Maritime transportation is today “the backbone of international trade and globalization [and] research can be roughly divided into two categories, namely the shipping side and the port side” (Yan et al., 2021). More recently, dry ports have also entered the agenda of intermodal transports, directly connected to seaports but reducing environmental impacts. These structures allow customers to move, deliver, or pick up their cargo as they interact with seaports (Khaslavskaya & Roso, 2020).

The shipment transportation process starts when a customer (e.g., an exporter) contacts a freight forwarder, and several documents begin to be prepared. The goods are then sent to a depot or port, possibly moving to different locations by land, sea, air, or a combination of transports, before arriving at the buyer. Ports are particularly relevant for international trade in emerging economies, requiring continuous quality improvement of port infrastructure (Munim & Schramm, 2018).

Vast amounts of data and specific documents are necessary for shipment transportation. One of the most well-known is the Bill of Lading (BL), which is issued by a carrier or agent to a shipper and describes the cargo and delivery specifications, similar to a receipt and a contract that must be signed at the end of the maritime delivery. The Convention on the Contract for the International Carriage of Goods by Road (CMR), also known as CMR Waybill or International Consignment Note (ICN), is the transport document used for road delivery, while the Air Waybill (AWB) is used to confirm the acceptance of goods transported between airports. The list continues with the International Federation of Freight Forwarders Associations (FIATA) Multimodal Transport Bill of Lading (FBL) adopted by authorized forwarders, but the insurance certificates, origin certificates, packing lists, or invoices should also be mentioned. Therefore, both the physical cargo and related documentation require maximum efficiency and may compromise performance indicators when their flow is affected.

Other issues related to the current documentation requirements can be found in the literature, besides the inherent paper inefficiencies already presented in the introduction section. For instance, the “neutralization of the consignment note,” a procedure related to the CMR consignment note, is known to the transport sector and has been used in road freight transport for several years. In most cases, the neutralization of transport documents is associated with an illegal activity in logistics called “parallel trade.” Parallel trade is when the trade of products takes place outside the official distribution channels. The purpose of neutralizing the consignment note is to ensure trade secrets. It is an illegal activity that violates the CMR Convention, which governs the international carriage of goods by road. The neutralization of the consignment note can also have other negative consequences, such as delays in the delivery of goods, damage to the reputation of the parties involved, and even criminal charges (Tomicová et al., 2021).

BLs are also prone to problems. For instance, the issuance of duplicated BLs either by administrative mistakes or by masqueraded documents. The latter issue is a common problem when shipping lines are in competition with freight forwarders by offering door-to-door services to importers and exporters. In those cases, they obscure the name of the importer or exporter, or transport carrier on BLs. Two examples of masqueraded or duplicated BLs are presented in the work of Irannejzad and Faroqi (2023), which led to unnecessary court expenses, delays in recovering damage loss, or harm to financial institutions. These issues are also reported in Kshetri (2018).

The uncertainties in economic growth and changes in the globalization paradigm are cooling down expectations in container transport, possibly falling between 2 percent and 4 percent in 2023, with a similar trend in freight rates. These predictions are made by Maersk, one of the leading shipment transportation groups, pointing to the increasing importance of digitalization, which opens up more opportunities to digital freight forwarders (Maersk, 2022).
