Concerns relating to cryptocurrencies being used for money laundering, financing of terrorism, scams, ransomware, extortion and thefts, will inevitably have their merits in some cases, but should not be generalized. Rather, they must be approached in a manner proportionate to their complexity and risks, which takes into account that they equally pose the subject of legitimate transactions and commercial operations, freely concluded between informed parties (Dabrowski and Janikowski, 2018).

Following the global financial and economic crisis of 2008–2012, several measures had been introduced to give account to the fact that major financial conglomerates need to be regulated and supervised on group and entity levels equally.8 However, the recent collapse of FTX highlights how international crypto groups have been able to operate without being subjected to a comparably uniform approach, pending the adoption of a framework corresponding to their intricacies. FTX comprised of over 100 subsidiaries and associated entities. Its corporate structure was muddled and purposefully complicated, while the dealings between the different entities which made up the group lacked transparency. Equally, FTX failed to apply any fit and proper tests, the assets of the different entities of the group were not adequately segregated from one another, and clients’ assets had been misappropriated. Importantly, the FTX case poses a case of fraud which is not primarily associated with the nature of cryptocurrency or the blockchain. Rather, it highlights on the one hand, problems which are common to the traditional financial services sphere, including supervision, prudential and governance issues. At the center of the collapse was a liquidity crisis9, which led to an insolvency crisis, an event which has prompted commentators to characterize the FTX failure as the “Lehman Brothers” moment10 for cryptos (Buckley, Douglas, Kirkwood, and Zetzsche, 2023). Similarly to banks, cryptocurrency exchanges do not maintain sufficient liquidity to satisfy all client withdrawal requests in case those clients simultaneously decide to withdraw the money held in their wallets. In particular, FTX would use the pool of its clients’ money to give out loans to its associated investment branch, namely, Alameda Research, which followed a risky investment strategy. Following the drop in the value of its associated investments and the resulting lack of available liquidity, FTX was unable to satisfy clients’ orders who were trying to withdraw their money held with FTX. On the other hand, what the FTX case also highlights is the importance of understanding possible new elements in the profile of crypto criminals. The examples of young, entrepreneurial CEOs of collapsed, massive crypto exchanges and other crypto businesses, who have faced, or are facing, criminal charges, point to an arguably new class of alleged criminals. Identifying how cryptocurrencies are being used in new methods of crime and illicit activity only constitutes a starting point. Properly profiling these cases from a criminology perspective can shed more light on the criminal behavior and patterns. If regulatory and law enforcement intervention is to be as efficient and targeted as possible, the relevant authorities will inevitably need to draw on experience and parallels between past and ongoing criminal behavior, mapping out criminals’ profiles, identifying new risks and laying down the groundwork to be in a position to combat the actions of suspected criminals, before and as they develop.
