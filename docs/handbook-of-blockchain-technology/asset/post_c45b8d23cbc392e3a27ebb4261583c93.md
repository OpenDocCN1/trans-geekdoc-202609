
## 8. Decision-making in Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs)
Florian Spychiger, Michael Lustenberger and Lukas Küng

Blockchain technology has enabled a new phenomenon, Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs). DAOs can be defined as self-governing organizations whose decision-making processes are implemented via smart contracts on a blockchain (Hassan & De Filippi, 2021). These DAOs usually manage and allocate assets, mostly in the form of cryptocurrencies or NFTs. Managed entirely by equal members, DAOs have been described as organizations without hierarchical structures (Wang et al., 2019). Hereby, a DAO requires its members to make decisions either for the daily business (e.g., investments) or for developing organizational structures (e.g., protocol changes). For online communities to reach agreement in a decentralized infrastructure, DAOs have implemented a variety of different decision-making processes where members are able to vote on proposals submitted by the community (Faqir-Rhazoui et al., 2021a).

To understand these new organizational phenomena from an economic perspective, it is important to introduce the concept of blockchain as a technology and hereby examine the invention and use of the technology using economic theory. From a theoretical perspective, Glaser et al. (2019) argue that due to its transparent, autonomous, and decentralized nature, blockchain can reduce the negative impact of information asymmetries, democratize decision-making processes, simplify contracting and enforcement, and limit individual opportunistic behavior. Hence, blockchains can be viewed as a technological layer to control opportunism by compensating for the need for trust within organizations. In this sense, Davidson et al. (2018) argue that the openness of blockchains with full rationality, complete information, and costless transactions can significantly reduce costs in achieving trust within organizations. Further developing this idea Davidson et al. (2018) conclude that blockchains are not only trustless but also self-monitoring and could thus enable completely new, decentralized, self-organized and self-controlled economic institutions. Blockchains thus enable a new technological possibility for the joint administration and control of digital platforms introducing a new form of economic coordination and a novel way of establishing governance. As a result, Iansiti and Lakhani (2017) contend that blockchain is a foundational technology with the potential to change the very nature of our current economic, social, and political systems. Whereas Catalini and Gans (2020) further describe blockchains as a network technology, claiming that in the absence of powerful intermediaries blockchains enable novel types of innovation ecosystems that allow open-source projects and startups to directly compete with entrenched incumbents due to the lower costs of verification and networking.

As stated, blockchain technology is changing human interactions and coordination of organizational behavior and could potentially promise new forms of organizations (DuPont, 2017). Bureaucratic and hierarchical organization structures have worked well in the past, especially during the era of mass production, but are not well suited for today’s dynamically changing business realities driven by technology (Fenwick et al., 2019).

Especially, the younger workforce doesn’t feel satisfied within enterprises being too centralized in their decision-making processes and too hierarchical in their corporation structures (Vermeulen et al., 2018). Consequently, it can be witnessed that the norm of today’s organizations emphasizes a narrow agenda defined by the few, putting aside broader considerations of the many. Hence, Vermeulen et al. (2018) state that subordinate communities and workers, and other key stakeholders are not included in the success of these corporations. With the help of the rapid developments in cryptocurrency and blockchain technology DAOs have emerged as a phenomenon offering a new way to create more inclusive organizations moving towards ‘community-owned’ platforms, which raise questions about today’s organizational environment. According to the DAO analyst DeepDAO, as of July 2023, a total of 19,553 DAOs have been created and a total of $22.8 billion has been invested into DAO treasuries, while the number of participating members is 7.1 million governance token holders. This number is put into perspective by Wang et al. (2023) discovering a member increase by the multiplication factor of 531 over the past six years in DAOs. Even though the technology is still maturing, and many issues remain unsolved, the new organizational structures of DAOs have become a trending topic (Wang et al., 2019) and the new possibilities are finding wide application within the blockchain-ecosystem. Having said that, DAOs are still considered an experimental and nascent phenomenon. Thus, DAOs challenge the traditional assumptions and norms of how organizations need to be designed, still many issues like centralization of governance processes, concentration of voting power and low participation rates need to be addressed.

The following section provides an understanding of DAOs and explains the main concepts behind this new organizational form. Additionally, this section describes important elements of DAOs and will explain the four key aspects of DAOs.

- first, its 'decentralization' that implies that a DAO is not managed based on hierarchical structures and property rights, but rather people interacting with each other according to a protocol encoded and enforced on a blockchain;
- second, its 'internal capital', meaning that a DAO owns some type of asset that can be used by the DAO itself to incentivize certain activities;
- finally, its 'autonomy', that is, a DAO can decide for itself based on codes, which means that a DAO is not necessarily democratic, in the sense that a majority can decide, but rather predefined code.

In this sense, in a DAO, pre-programmed rules are executed through smart contracts on a blockchain.

However, a very narrow interpretation of 'autonomous' implies that a DAO can take all decisions on its own, which again would mean that all possible decision-making situations need to be foreseen and pre-programmed on the blockchain – or a DAO would need to have some sort of artificial intelligence to decide autonomously in unforeseen situations. This strong focus on a technical autonomy of a DAO is often referred to as the 'code is law' and represents an idealistic way of achieving governance within a DAO, where decisions would be made on-chain by DAO token holders based solely on the procedures encoded in the blockchain (Wang et al., 2019).

Nevertheless, this concept of 'algorithmic governance' has been widely criticized for being dysfunctional in the presence of an unforeseen significant event (DuPont, 2017). The legitimacy of this critique can be seen by the downfall of the first large DAO, The DAO (DuPont, 2017; Mehar, 2019), where the code is law paradigm was ultimately broken by a small group of people who made a decision outside the encoded rules to alter the immutable history.

Therefore, in DAOs, there is a distinction between on-chain and off-chain governance that boils down to whether rules and decision-making processes are strictly encoded in smart contracts on the blockchain (on-chain) or if processes happen in a non-deterministic way without blockchain interaction. Based on practical experience, most DAOs today have both on- and off-chain processes in place which contextualizes DAOs more in the light of 'code is constitution' (Miscione & Schwabe, 2022), by defining how power is divided, and how regulations and their changes must be agreed upon before being enforced.

However, as technologies mature over time a shift towards more on-chain focused processes might be possible in the future. Whether at one point a DAO can detach itself completely from off-chain decisions and live up to the vision of 'algorithmic governance' needs to be explored in practice. Currently, there are a plethora of different DAOs up and running and all of them differ in their purpose, structure, and governance models (Lustenberger et al., 2024).

That said, the common view today is that DAOs are dependent on human interaction and active participation, even off-chain, and thus outside the blockchain. A community of DAO members, which can also be referred to as internal stakeholders (Hsieh et al., 2019) of the DAO, is therefore fundamental to the operation, governance, and development of the DAO.
