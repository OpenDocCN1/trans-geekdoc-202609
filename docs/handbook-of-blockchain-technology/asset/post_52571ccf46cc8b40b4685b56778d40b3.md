### 14.4.6 Evaluation

Evaluation (and benefits realization) occurs after implementation. Using various tools and frameworks, a policy is evaluated based on certain criteria depending on the decision we are discussing (Birkland, 2001). At this stage, we would mainly recommend two digital technologies: polling and sensing. If the implementation in question is a public service, polling can show how satisfied or dissatisfied citizens are with the new service, and if results show dissatisfaction, there might be a need to take a step backward and conduct new rounds of enactment. Polling can also be relevant for technical, planning, and infrastructure services, for example, by asking if people are satisfied with the new cycling lane or if crossing the street became easier with new traffic lights. But in these cases, sensing is even more important. For example, citizens volunteering to collect data can register where they cycle or walk, report issues, or allow the city access to their phone GPS for a complete (aggregated) picture of pedestrian and cycling movements. Reporting via FixMyStreet-type applications can show if the implementation has improved conditions compared to what they used to be; the city can track citizens to evaluate the use of the new park, or if a zoning change had the intended effects – to name a few examples. Using blockchain technologies to register and store this data can make data more readily available for third-party use, can increase transparency, as everyone can access and examine the data, and the local council can be held accountable if a project did not meet the intended goals and objectives.

## 14.5 Conclusion and Implications

In this chapter, we have examined the use of blockchain technologies for citizen participation in smart cities. City municipalities have many responsibilities, from schools and health to planning and local infrastructure. Involving citizens in the various processes can benefit quality and increase transparency and trust in government. Using blockchain technology can further help with transparency and trust due to its immutability and decentralized nature.

We have demonstrated how various participation technologies can be applied at different stages of the political decision-making process and discussed how blockchain technologies can be useful.

### 14.5.1 Challenges and Limitations of Using Blockchain Technologies for Participation

While there are many potential benefits, there are also some challenges and limitations of blockchain technologies for participation. Transaction costs can be an obstacle, especially if there are many smaller blocks being added to the blockchain, such as polling or voting results. For low number/high impact participation, such as consultations, transaction costs become less of an issue, as there are simply fewer responses to record. Generally, private blockchains are less expensive to use but are also less secure since the infrastructure is controlled by an owner. The public blockchain is better suited for democratic purposes since the public domain may increase citizens’ trust in the data being accurate. Another mitigating factor for transaction costs could be to store longer entries, such as documents, in a database, with some kind of notary function being done by the blockchain.

In our age of global warming, we need to be aware of, and minimize, the carbon footprint of the technologies we use. Current blockchain verification mechanisms require a lot of energy and computing power, and from a sustainability point of view, this makes the technology less attractive. However, if new proof-of-stake mechanisms can be successfully implemented, the carbon footprint of blockchains would be greatly reduced.

Finally, there is the question of how and where the technology would be most useful and effective. As shown by the Deloitte white paper, some developing countries are jumping through hoops and skipping traditional databases and repositories in favor of blockchains. For countries that lack an existing technological infrastructure and who also have problems with corruption, tampering with records, low transparency, and poor trust in government and institutions, it might make more sense to move to a blockchain infrastructure. For societies that have different issues and also have an existing functioning infrastructure, the cost/benefit analysis of moving existing services to the blockchain might look different. However, as the European Commission and Belgian Ministry pointed out, blockchain technologies can facilitate new ways of working and technological innovations, which might lead to new and improved services. So for this group, blockchain implementation should not be a purely technical matter but instead go hand-in-hand with innovation, co-creation, and development of novel ways of working and including citizens.

Based on our experience with developing Ethereum smart energy applications, we plan to implement a set of demonstrations to show how blockchain can be used with polling, voting, consulting, and petition/citizen initiative applications. As we have demonstrated in this chapter, there are many possibilities for new participatory practices which could help improve trust. This would greatly benefit society in our current age of polarization and conflict.
