In *Feist*, the Supreme Court ruled against copyright protection for a telephone directory, stating that a mere collection of facts ordered in a systematic way lacked the necessary creativity, pointing out that copyright law was not intended to protect labor, otherwise known as the “sweat of the brow” doctrine, but rather to incentivize creative expression. That is also why AI-generated works are not eligible for copyright.

Since NFTs have become a technological mechanism for creative expression, the Feist Test helps determine how copyright law could be used to protect the ownership rights of NFT creators. However, an NFT on its own does not pass the Feist Test; there must be a demonstration of original, creative expression. That does not exclude generative art, which requires some degree of structure and creative thought by the project owner, even if some degree of randomization is embedded into the full NFT collection.

Lee (2012) argued for expanding the interpretation of the Feist Test by introducing the concept of “digital originality.” In particular, the process of creation, not just the resulting work, should be considered when determining originality. Lee suggests that an innovative use of digital technology in the creative process should be recognized as original under copyright law. Taken to the extreme, the process of minting an NFT could be argued as digital originality, but there is not yet precedent or state law on this matter. By applying Lee’s concept of digital originality to NFTs, the creative process of minting an NFT and associating it with a digital asset could potentially qualify the NFT for copyright protection. This would be a significant departure from traditional copyright law, which focuses on the work itself rather than the process of creation.

However, Lee’s theory is not yet widely accepted, and the idea of extending copyright protection to NFTs in this way has not been piloted before. Courts and legislatures have yet to weigh in on this issue, and the landscape of copyright law related to NFTs is still in flux. In this sense, while existing copyright law provides some guidance for dealing with NFTs, there are significant gray areas, particularly regarding originality. The Feist Test and Edward Lee’s concept of digital originality provide some useful frameworks for thinking about these issues, but future legal developments will ultimately shape the final interpretations.

# 4.2.4  Fair Use Doctrine

Fair use is a doctrine in copyright law that allows limited use of copyrighted material without requiring permission from the rights holders. It allows the legal, non-licensed citation or incorporation of copyrighted material in another author’s work under a four-factor balancing test.

These factors include:

+   - The purpose and character of the use, including whether such use is of a commercial nature or is for nonprofit educational purposes.
+   - The nature of the copyrighted work.
+   - The amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole.
+   - The effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.
