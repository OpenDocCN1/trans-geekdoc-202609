**Dynamic Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (DPBFT):** Hao et al. (2018a) introduced the Dynamic PBFT, a CA that offers further advantages above its PBFT foundation. The security and liveliness of DPBFT are equivalent to those of PBFT. Similar to PBFT, it is reliant on weak synchronization assumptions, which is a crucial characteristic that enables its use across the internet. Furthermore, as implied by its name, DPBFT allows nodes and replicas to join and leave the consensus network with little to no disturbance. To increase the system’s resilience, it includes a way to remove nodes that behave maliciously or have extended outages. “Participation Degree,” a novel idea, is presented in DPBFT. This metric assesses how active a node is in relation to the network. The latter increases the cost of avoiding consensus, which encourages nodes to participate more frequently and improves system security.

**Stellar Consensus Protocol (SCP):** The notions of quorums and quorum slices are used in the Stellar CA (Lokhava et al., 2019). A quorum slice is a subset of a quorum that can persuade a specific node to agree with it, whereas a quorum is a sufficiently big group of nodes needed to attain consensus. A single node may show up in more than one quorum. In order to enable open participation in the network, quorum slices were added to Stellar, allowing each node to choose a subset of nodes within its slice. Based on actual business interactions between different companies, these quorums and quorum slices are formed, making use of the trust that already exists in business relationships. For there to be a worldwide consensus throughout the system, quorums need to intersect. The global consensus is the result of an individual node’s aggregate choice. Stellar consensus protocol functions as follows: Federated voting is the technique by which each node votes once on transactions or statements. At this point, every node selects its own statement and rejects any that contradicts it. If its quorum slice has already approved the statement, it may move on to another one. Acceptance is the following phase. If a node does not receive a conflicting statement and all of the nodes in its v-blocking set have accepted it, then the node accepts the statement. A v-blocking set is a group of nodes that can veto the decision of the current node in each quorum slice. Quorum slices interconnect, leading to quorums agreeing on a set of propositions. When all members of a quorum concur with the statement, it’s referred to as confirmation. The last step in the voting process is ratification, which indicates agreement at the system level.

**Proof of Stake Velocity (PoSV):** In 2014, the PoSV CA was created for the digital social currency Reddcoin (Ren, 2014). Both stake ownership and action (velocity) are permitted under this CA. It was created to address some of the social and economic problems with PoW and PoS networks, providing an alternative to the algorithms used in well-known digital currencies like Bitcoin and Ethereum. Because PoS systems use a linear coinage function to motivate miners to hang onto their stake for longer periods of time, this poses a significant difficulty. PoSV solves this problem by substituting an exponential decay function for the linear coinage function. According to the exponential decay function, an owner’s odds of creating the next block decreases with the length of time they hold a stake without using it for transaction validation. This change incentivizes stakeholders to shift their stakes often and engage in active network participation. Participants are also encouraged to stay online and validate transactions because doing so raises their chances of winning incentives.

**Proof of Activity (PoA):** PoA CA seeks to combine the best features of both PoW and PoS strategies to provide a system that is more secure and effective (Bentov et al., 2014). At first, PoA works similarly to PoW mining in that different miners compete to solve computational problems and provide more processing power in order to find a new block. The system switches to PoS when a new block is found. The miner’s reward address and a header are the sole elements in the recently discovered block. Using the header information of the most recent block, a random group of validators is selected from the blockchain network during the PoW stage. The new block must be signed or validated by these validators. Each validator’s total coin holdings are taken into account throughout the selection process, giving preference to those with a higher stake. Validators’ involvement guarantees the fresh block’s legitimacy and integrity. After all validators have approved the block, it can be finalized and appended to the chain. The block is considered complete when the necessary number of validators have signed it, at which point transactions can be recorded on it. The miner and the validators that helped ensure the block’s validity then split the block’s mining fees or rewards.

**Proof of Weight (PoWeight):** PoWeight allocates users a ‘weight’ based on the number of tokens they hold (Peter Compare, 2018). PoWeight is a model developed at MIT’s Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory that has been incorporated in the Algorand (Gilad et al., 2016) coin. For each transaction, the network creates a committee of randomly selected network members. The committee members are chosen based on their token holdings, with those with the most tokens given a higher weight. This weight represents their impact and importance in the consensus process. The goal of assigning weights is to keep the network safe against potential double spending assaults. As long as the majority of weighted users are trustworthy, the consensus process can maintain transaction integrity and prevent fraudulent behavior. When a transaction is made on the blockchain, a committee of randomly selected network members uses their weights to collectively validate and ratify it. By centralizing the consensus process inside this committee, PoWeight hopes to accomplish efficient and secure transaction confirmation.

## 9.4 DISCUSSION

The CA presented in Section 9.3 can be broadly classified into two main categories, namely proof-based and voting-based algorithms, as illustrated in Figure 9.3.

- **Proof-based algorithms:** In proof-based algorithms validators contribute to the process of adding a new block to the blockchain by either: (a) offering their computational power and solving puzzles, or (b) staking their tokens. Anyone who meets these criteria can participate in the process. Proof-based algorithms are considered secure due to the number of resources or stakes required for an attack. In terms of scalability, it appears that this is limited due to participants’ limited computational power or stakes. Within the proof-based category, several algorithms have been proposed to address the limitations of PoW and PoS. For instance, Proof of Capacity (PoC) utilizes storage space as a resource, rather than computational power, to allocate mining rights, thus reducing energy consumption (Dziembowski, 2015). Similarly, Proof of Elapsed Time (PoET) relies on a randomized timer to determine mining rights, conserving energy while maintaining fairness (Chen and Liu, 2017).

- **Voting-based algorithms:** In voting-based algorithms a democratic voting process takes place which results in the selection of validators through voting. Compared to proof-based algorithms, voting-based are considered more scalable but this may have an impact on decentralization as a smaller number of elected validators participates in the process. Voting-based algorithms have evolved to address scalability limitations of traditional approaches like pBFT. For example, the Stellar Consensus Protocol (SCP) employs Federated Byzantine Agreement (FBA) to achieve consensus while maintaining open membership and network scalability (Kim et al., 2019). Ripple Protocol CA (RPCA) employs a similar approach, using a selected set of trusted nodes to reach agreement rapidly (Christodoulou et al., 2020). Nonetheless, these algorithms may sacrifice a certain degree of decentralization in favor of improved scalability.
