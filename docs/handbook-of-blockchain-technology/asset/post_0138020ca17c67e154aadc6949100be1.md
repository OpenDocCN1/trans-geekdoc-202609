
### 8.3.4 Attacks

These issues have also led to some attacks in DAOs. In 2022, an attacker was able to steal more than 80 million USD from Beanstalk (Coinmonks, 2023), a stablecoin protocol that has a DAO governing its development. The DAO had an emergency function implemented that allowed the immediate execution of a proposal, instead of waiting seven days. To execute this functionality, two-thirds of a supermajority is needed. The attacker used a flash loan to obtain enough governance tokens to 1) make a malicious proposal that paid out the funds of the DAO to their address, and 2) to hold the supermajority in the system. After the attack, the attacker used part of the stolen funds to repay the flash loan. Such an attack was only possible because of the power a centralized attacker can gain in such a system. Similarly, in the Tornado Cash DAO, an attacker was able to pass a malicious proposal in May 2023 that assigned all the governance power to them (Galdwin, 2023). With this proposal, the governance of the Tornado Cash DAO basically ceased to exist as the whole power was transferred to the attacker. The proposal was voted on by the community, but it seems that nobody checked the content of the smart contract. Higher participation and engagement of the token holders might have led to somebody noticing the malicious code snippet.

## 8.4 Seven Main Voting Mechanisms in DAOs

Section 8.4 elaborates on the seven most widely used voting mechanisms in DAOs. Each of the voting mechanisms is trying to tackle the discussed challenges and issues from different angles while opening up new weak points to issue systems attacks.

### 8.4.1 Token-based Quorum Voting

The most common voting mechanisms in DAOs are token-based. Many existing DAOs have thereby implemented a ‘one-token-one-vote’ voting system, often as a result to counteract sybil attacks. While generating new addresses is done for free, tokens cannot simply be reproduced. In this mechanism, each token corresponds to a vote which is also considered one of the simplest voting approaches to implement and manage (Bellavitis et al., 2023; Tsoukalas & Falk, 2020; Zhao et al., 2022). In a token-based voting mechanism it is necessary to define a ‘quorum’ (a threshold of votes) that a proposal needs to reach so it can be approved or declined. This quorum can be set absolute or relative. In an absolute quorum, a predefined threshold of the total number of tokens (e.g., 50 percent of all tokens) must be reached, whereas in a relative quorum, the majority of all votes cast for a particular proposal is sufficient to decide on that proposal. There are two main concerns in respect to token-based quorum voting: first, in the case of the absolute quorum, the threshold might be set too high, meaning the proposals are delayed or never reach the quorum needed to take a decision; in a worst case scenario, a DAO might end up in a deadlock if voting participation drops dramatically and the once set absolute quorum is never reached again. The second issue with token-based voting is their tendency to centralize voting power in the hands of a few ‘wealthy’ DAO actors with a lot of tokens, which may ultimately lead to the already discussed plutocracy. However, some of the one-token-one-vote DAOs use a direct democracy model where each token holder can participate only directly in the votes, whereas others have implemented a two-tier system that resembles an indirect democracy where people can delegate their voting power (tokens) to specific actors (representatives) in the system. A third version is a mix of both, where token holders can either vote directly or delegate their vote. This last voting mechanism is thereby based on the concept of ‘liquid democracy’ (Axelsen et al., 2022). However, while the original concept of liquid democracy clearly incorporates the idea of ‘one-person-one-vote’, most DAOs still adopt the one-token-one-vote approach. This is mainly because most DAOs allow members to participate anonymously, which allows them to have multiple identities. By multiplying member accounts, a ‘one-account-one-vote’ mechanism would therefore be vulnerable to sybil attacks.

**Examples:** Tezos, MakerDAO, Uniswap, Ethereum Name Service, BitDAO, Curve DAO

### 8.4.2 Quadratic Voting

In 2018 Lalley and Weyl (2018) proposed ‘quadratic voting’ as a new voting mechanism with the aim to overcome the centralization of the voting power in a DAO as it occurs with ‘token-based votings’. Quadratic voting introduces a non-linear funding principle which allows voters to not only vote for or against a specific proposal, but also to express how strongly they agree on it (Buterin et al., 2018). Each DAO member receives a specific amount of voting credit. In a consecutive step, the members must allocate their credit to the different proposals. As an example: DAO member Alice receives ten voting tokens. She allocates three votes to proposal A, one to proposal B and zero to proposal C, which results in voting costs of 3²+1²+0² = 10. However, in this example, Alice was not only able to vote, but she could also express her preferences. The voting is called ‘quadratic’ because the costs for n votes are n². This approach discourages people without a vested interest from voting on an issue and gives a minority a more prominent voice on issues which are important to them. Additionally, it increases ‘costs’ exponentially for ‘wealthy’ voters to influence a decision about a proposal. In this way, quadratic voting attempts to reduce the influence of an individual on the outcome of a decision in a DAO. However, it is important to mention that quadratic voting requires an identity process to ensure its fairness. Otherwise, the mechanism is prone to sybil attacks, where a ‘wealthy’ DAO member can split their tokens among multiple identities to increase their voting power again (Dimitri, 2022). As a consequence, DAOs would need to implement more advanced technologies to identify its members, which again is one of the main reasons why quadratic voting is not yet widely implemented in DAOs.

**Examples:** Gitcoin

### 8.4.3 Conviction Voting

A further adoption of the classic, token-based voting system is ‘conviction voting’. According to Emmett (2019), conviction voting offers a novel decision-making process that funds proposals based on the aggregated preference of community members, expressed continuously over time. Voters are thereby expressing their approval for proposals over time. Figuratively speaking, proposals are buckets and all DAO members are able to ‘pour in’ voting power into these buckets. The longer a voter holds his opinions and keeps staked his voting power, the more conviction there will be in this proposal. It is expected that maintaining the voting token staked over time will give an advantage to those DAO members who are convinced of their decision and keep their decision unchanged over a longer time period (Ding et al., 2023b). Depending on how much funding is asked by the proposal, more or less support (conviction) is needed to pass for such a proposal. Meaning, each member of a DAO, regardless of its wealth has a voice, which will increase over the course of time. This voting mechanism should strengthen the small token holders and their impact within a DAO, which in a consecutive step should lead to a higher participation rate compared to other on-chain voting mechanisms. In addition, conviction voting motivates DAO members to think more carefully about the long-term implications of their votes and avoid short-term thinking and speculation. This, in turn, should encourage token holders to focus more on the long-term success of the DAO and align their interests with the purpose of the DAO.

**Examples:** 1Hive, Commons Stack

### 8.4.4 Futarchy Voting

Futarchy is a governance concept introduced by Hanson (2013) that was not specifically designed for DAOs like the other voting mechanisms, but rather to optimize democratic policymaking by using prediction markets to achieve the best possible outcome for the national welfare. Futarchy arose from the assumption that governments often choose inefficient policies, although experts of the specific areas are aware of better alternatives (Hanson, 2013). This happens due to information asymmetry within a democratic system and biased media coverage. Additionally, in an ordinary democracy there is little motivation to engage in the decision-making process, as the cost to do so – for example to seek information – is much higher compared to the insignificant influence on the outcome of an individual vote. However, by betting on a proposal’s impact on the well-being of the community, incentives exist to be well informed and to get involved in the decision-making process, as prediction market participants can lose or gain their invested capital. Therefore, only those policy proposals which prediction markets have clearly estimated will have the most positive effect for the community will get accepted. Futarchy is therefore a mixture of democracy and speculative markets. It was Buterin (2014b) who first proposed Hanson’s futarchy concept as a suitable governance model for DAOs. All DAO members can express their needs in the form of proposals, but only DAO members who place a bet on a proposal would vote by predicting if a proposal would have a positive or negative effect for the community. At the end only proposals with the predicted greatest positive impact on the community would be selected to be implemented in the DAO. Depending on whether the positive prediction was right or not, the predicting DAO member would then win or lose tokens. It is the hope that futarchy would create stronger interest to make informed decisions, as DAO members bear direct financial risk with their votes. However, there are also some arguments against this kind of voting mechanism (see Buterin, 2014b): first, that in markets individuals tend to follow a trend instead of forming their own opinion in detail; second, that participation in the prediction market could be much lower than expected, as most DAO members will not risk their tokens; and third, that it is difficult to measure the actual impact of a selected proposal on the community correctly after its implementation, as other factors might have influenced the outcome. Implementing pure futarchy in DAOs has therefore been difficult in practice, but hybrid forms that incorporate aspects of futarchy seem to be a possible way to go.

**Examples:** Gnosis DAO
