When court documents need to be served, in the context of an application to the court or a civil case most notably, this service is normally done personally to the intended recipient of the documents. Nonetheless, in cases where it is not feasible or practicable to deliver by personal service, courts will allow for substitute service.

In the context of cases where cryptoassets have been misappropriated, when faced with the difficulty of identifying potential wrongdoers, courts have permitted the service of documents via means of an airdrop of a non-fungible token (NFT), both where the identity of the wrongdoer was unknown and known. For example, in the UK case of Jones v Persons Unknown [2022] EWHC 2543, the claimant transferred £1.5 million-worth of Bitcoin to an investment vehicle operated by fraudsters, who had previously managed to convince the claimant to make the relevant payments. Specifically, the claimant had authorized the fraudsters to execute various transfers, as a result of which the claimant had supposedly made substantial gains. After realizing that he could not withdraw his deposited funds and alleged earnings, the claimant managed, with the help of investigators, to track his misappropriated Bitcoin to a wallet on Huobi, an international crypto exchange based in the Seychelles. As the defendants had not engaged in the proceedings, the claimant had been able to receive a summary judgment, ordering the delivery of the stolen Bitcoin and blocking the transfer thereof via a proprietary and freezing order. It was further determined that Huobi had held the stolen Bitcoin on constructive trust for the claimant, which meant that the Bitcoin could be recovered from the exchange itself. Most importantly for the purposes of this analysis, the Court had also held that as traditional means of service were likely to be ineffective on the facts of the case, the claimant had been given permission to serve the order via NFT airdrop to the unidentified defendants and to Huobi, as service through traditional routes (namely, through the Hague Service Convention) would not have been expedient enough and would have jeopardized the recovery of the stolen Bitcoin. A similar approach was adopted in the case of D'Aloia v Persons Unknown [2022] EWHC 1723. In the US case of Benjamin Arthur Bowen v Xingzhao Li (Case No. 23-cv-20399), the Florida District Court also allowed the service of a claim on a fraudster via an NFT airdrop to the wallets in which the misappropriated $2.2 million-worth of cryptoassets had been located, in circumstances where the identity of the defendant was known to the court. The Court considered that, since the fraud had been conducted on the blockchain, an NFT airdrop was reasonably calculated to give notice to the defendants.

### 18.5.4 Evolving Approach of the Courts – Remedies

Safeguarding the most appropriate administration of justice in crypto cases is also inherently linked to the ability of claimants and applicants to receive adequate and appropriate remedies. In Ion Science v Persons Unknown and Others [2020] unreported (Comm), the applicants had been induced to invest a total of £577,002 in initial coin offerings, of what they believed to be legitimate cryptocurrency products. The applicants had been convinced to grant remote access to the alleged perpetrators of the fraud to their computer, who were then able to carry out the relevant transactions. However, as the alleged perpetrators delayed the payment of the supposed earnings to the applicants and faced with the possibility that their funds had been misappropriated, the applicants sought, inter alia, an order for a Bankers Trust, compelling entities associated with the Kraken and Binance exchanges to disclose information on the identity of the perpetrators behind the fraud. The court in that case had identified that in the absence of a Bankers Trust order, it might not have been possible to identify the alleged perpetrators and/or grant an effective remedy to the applicants. Equally, a Bankers Trust order and a Norwich Pharmacal Order (an equitable remedy whereby the Court orders the respondent to disclose documents and/or information to the applicant) had been granted in Fetch.ai Ltd v Persons Unknown [2021] EWHC 2254 (Comm), where the defendants had allegedly hacked the respondents’ accounts on Binance and misappropriated the cryptocurrencies stored on those accounts, causing the applicants a loss of over $2.6 million.

Cases similar to Ion Science v Persons Unknown and Others are particularly important in the context of cryptoasset recoveries, as they provide precedent to compel cryptocurrency exchanges to reveal information on their users. Following the introduction of licensing regimes over such exchanges, including the imposition of money laundering, KYC, organizational and governance requirements thereon, such parties are now under an obligation to maintain information which is necessary to identify the perpetrators of offenses. Thereafter, cryptoassets can be traced in fiat currencies and the objects on which they have been spent; property, houses, expensive jewelry and so on (Craggs et al., 2022). Nonetheless, such orders of disclosure will only be awarded on strict, specific grounds, including in circumstances where the victims of fraud will otherwise be unable to verify the identity of potential perpetrators and seek relief against them. In the same spirit, the readiness of the courts to find that there is a constructive trust (as was the case in Jones v Persons Unknown) where stolen cryptoassets are held in accounts with licensed cryptocurrency exchanges, provides an important gateway for claimants to recover their funds. Overall, the use of the blockchain inevitably raises new legal challenges, as the existing body of law needs to be applied and interpreted in light of new and evolving criminal activity and disputes with unprecedented characteristics. The role of the judiciary in this regard is crucial.

## 18.6 CONCLUSION

The blockchain and cryptoassets are important innovations with revolutionary potential. Since 2008 and the publication of the seminal paper of Satoshi Nakamoto on the establishment of a peer-to-peer electronic cash system (Nakamoto, 2008), until today, these elements have increasingly come to the forefront for their unique characteristics, cross-sectoral application and diverse use cases. Nonetheless, criminals have also taken advantage of the distinguishing features of blockchain and cryptoassets, to defraud investors in the context of ICOs, to engage in the illicit transfer of funds, ransomware, extortion and thefts, money laundering, financing of terrorism and international sanctions evasion. Faced with new and evolving forms of crime involving blockchain and cryptoassets, it is imperative to consider how such crime can be prevented, investigated and mitigated.

Concerns linking criminality with blockchain and cryptoassets must not be exacerbated. This chapter has highlighted ample examples whereby law enforcement agencies and the courts have been successful in combatting crime on the blockchain, whilst also highlighting the importance of proper understanding and education surrounding these technological developments. A legislative framework which is fit-for-purpose is also paramount, with the EU, national supervisory authorities and international standard-setting bodies playing their own key roles to ensure that cryptoassets do not operate in a lawless “wild west”. At any given time, private initiatives will be crucial; a private business will be in the best position to determine the vulnerabilities of its business and adopt specific measures, in real time, to protect itself against criminals. Private and public responses must go hand in hand and complement one another.

This chapter has tried to analyze the most relevant laws and regulatory initiatives (within its scope) which have been adopted to combat crime on the blockchain, offering practical advice and recommendations which incorporate both the public and private sector, while drawing advice from experience and reported cases. Ultimately, contemplating how fast this sector has evolved, one has to consider the possibility that the use of blockchain and cryptoassets may still be at their infancy. The topic of the chapter, namely fraud, crime prevention and financial crime investigation in blockchain, is expected to continue to unravel substantially and quickly.
