Furthermore, it can serve as a foundation for enhancing other aspects of HRM, such as award operations, data transparency, and payroll. Blockchain can enable employees to securely share sensitive information with their employers, including skill references, accomplishments, and qualifications, which can be digitally verified for validity. Consequently, major organizations like Google, Facebook, Amazon, IBM, and Walmart are investing heavily in Blockchain technology and testing its applications across various functions (CB Insights, 2019). Additionally, the United Nations (UN) aims to provide targeted aid in countries utilizing Blockchain (Debter et al., 2020).

Thus, Blockchain adds value to systems through a verification mechanism based on an incentive structure, enabling decision-making processes that involve a network where users become decision-makers (Sharif and Ghodoosi, 2022). This creates trust among users who are now not having to rely on a central authority or government, but rather on verification by other users (Tapscott and Tapscott, 2017). This unique decision-making process is facilitated by engaging with a network through Blockchain platforms, where users can use pseudonymous or be anonymous (Aitzhan and Svetinovic, 2016).

The breakthrough of Blockchain began in 2017 when IBM attempted to integrate the technology into the global supply chain, combining it with IoT (Ho et al., 2021). This initiative led to the development of a managerial platform based on the Blockchain concept, which enabled the recording and tracing of spare parts, resulting in effective decision-making, reduced maintenance, and improved inventory control. Blockchain has also been employed in various other domains, such as storing personal health records (Kim et al., 2022), addressing critical broadcasting issues (Shrestha et al., 2020), resolving privacy concerns (Tian et al., 2019), and supporting fault tolerance and decentralization (Feng and Yan, 2019). However, concerns are growing regarding the digital transformation in organizations and its ability to assist HRM in strategic decision-making (Marler and Parry, 2016). While software tools can assist in reducing administrative work, enabling leaders to focus on strategic tasks and be seen as trusted advisors by HR (Florkowski, 2019; Marler and Parry, 2016), the increasing reliance on technology in HRM increases the possibility of gradually diminishing HR’s functional importance (Parry and Strohmeier, 2014). As we enter the digital age, adopting cloud platforms becomes necessary. Without control over organizational data, the perception of the organization’s importance may decline (Shrivastava and Shaw, 2003). Similarly, HR’s functional significance may gradually diminish as technology is increasingly employed for HR self-service applications. Some specialized software, such as Customer Relationship Management (CRM) and Enterprise Resource Planning, deals with HRM issues; however, their application is limited because it is expensive to apply massive customization or even adjust to add new functions.

The support of employees in developing capabilities and commitment in a societal context, and not only in an organizational context, is a constituent part of the HRM strategy (Bratton and Gold, 2017). Within this context, Bratton and Gold (2017) describe three subdomains of HRM: the first is Micro HRM, which reflects the small working groups of employees; the second is the International HRM dimension, which reflects the situation with employees working in global organizations; and finally the Strategic HRM, which reflects the above-mentioned concerns of the organization’s business strategy on the HR strategy.
