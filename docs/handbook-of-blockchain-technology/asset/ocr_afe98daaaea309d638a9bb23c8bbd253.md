# 8. Decision-making in Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs)
Florian Spychiger, Michael Lustenberger and Lukas Küng



Blockchain technology has enabled a new phenomenon, Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs). DAOs can be defined as self-governing organizations whose decision-making processes are implemented via smart contracts on a blockchain (Hassan & De Filippi, 2021). These DAOs usually manage and allocate assets, mostly in the form of cryptocurrencies or NFTs. Managed entirely by equal members, DAOs have been described as organizations without hierarchical structures (Wang et al., 2019). Hereby, a DAO requires its members to make decisions either for the daily business (e.g., investments) or for developing organizational structures (e.g., protocol changes). For online communities to reach agreement in a decentralized infrastructure, DAOs have implemented a variety of different decision-making processes where members are able to vote on proposals submitted by the community (Faqir-Rhazoui et al., 2021a).

To understand these new organizational phenomena from an economic perspective, it is important to introduce the concept of blockchain as a technology and hereby examine the invention and use of the technology using economic theory. From a theoretical perspective, Glaser et al. (2019) argue that due to its transparent, autonomous, and decentralized nature, blockchain can reduce the negative impact of information asymmetries, democratize decision-making processes, simplify contracting and enforcement, and limit individual opportunistic behavior. Hence, blockchains can be viewed as a technological layer to control opportunism by compensating for the need for trust within organizations. In this sense, Davidson et al. (2018) argue that the openness of blockchains with full rationality, complete information, and costless transactions can significantly reduce costs in achieving trust within organizations. Further developing this idea Davidson et al. (2018) conclude that blockchains are not only trustless but also self-monitoring and could thus enable completely new, decentralized, self-organized and self-controlled economic institutions. Blockchains thus enable a new technological possibility for the joint administration and control of digital platforms introducing a new form of economic coordination and a novel way of establishing governance. As a result, Iansiti and Lakhani (2017) contend that blockchain is a foundational technology with the potential to change the very nature of our current economic, social, and political systems. Whereas Catalini and Gans (2020) further describe blockchains as a network technology, claiming that in the absence of powerful intermediaries blockchains enable novel types of innovation ecosystems that allow open-source projects and startups to directly compete with entrenched incumbents due to the lower costs of verification and networking.
