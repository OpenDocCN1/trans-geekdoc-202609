In 2022, an attacker was able to steal more than 80 million USD from Beanstalk (Coinmonks, 2023), a stablecoin protocol that has a DAO governing its development. The DAO had an emergency function implemented that allowed the immediate execution of a proposal, instead of waiting seven days. To execute this functionality, two-thirds of a supermajority is needed. The attacker used a flash loan to obtain enough governance tokens to 1) make a malicious proposal that paid out the funds of the DAO to their address, and 2) to hold the supermajority in the system. After the attack, the attacker used part of the stolen funds to repay the flash loan. Such an attack was only possible because of the power a centralized attacker can gain in such a system. Similarly, in the Tornado Cash DAO, an attacker was able to pass a malicious proposal in May 2023 that assigned all the governance power to them (Galdwin, 2023). With this proposal, the governance of the Tornado Cash DAO basically ceased to exist as the whole power was transferred to the attacker. The proposal was voted on by the community, but it seems that nobody checked the content of the smart contract. Higher participation and engagement of the token holders might have led to somebody noticing the malicious code snippet.

# 8.4 **SEVEN MAIN VOTING MECHANISMS IN DAOs**

Section 8.4 elaborates on the seven most widely used voting mechanisms in DAOs. Each of the voting mechanisms is trying to tackle the discussed challenges and issues from different angles while opening up new weak points to issue systems attacks.

# 8.4.1 **Token-based Quorum Voting**

The most common voting mechanisms in DAOs are token-based. Many existing DAOs have thereby implemented a ‘one-token-one-vote’ voting system, often as a result to counteract sybil attacks. While generating new addresses is done for free, tokens cannot simply be reproduced. In this mechanism, each token corresponds to a vote which is also considered one of the simplest voting approaches to implement and manage (Bellavitis et al., 2023; Tsoukalas & Falk, 2020; Zhao et al., 2022). In a token-based voting mechanism it is necessary to define a ‘quorum’ (a threshold of votes) that a proposal needs to reach so it can be approved or declined. This quorum can be set absolute or relative. In an absolute quorum, a predefined threshold of the total number of tokens (e.g., 50 percent of all tokens) must be reached, whereas in a relative quorum, the majority of all votes cast for a particular proposal is sufficient to decide on that proposal. There are two main concerns in respect to token-based quorum voting: first, in the case of the absolute quorum, the threshold might be set too high, meaning the proposals are delayed or never reach the quorum needed to take a decision; in a worst case scenario, a DAO might end up in a deadlock if voting participation drops dramatically and the once set absolute quorum is never reached again. The second issue with token-based voting is their tendency to centralize voting power in the hands of a few ‘wealthy’ DAO actors with a lot of tokens, which may ultimately lead to the already discussed plutocracy.
