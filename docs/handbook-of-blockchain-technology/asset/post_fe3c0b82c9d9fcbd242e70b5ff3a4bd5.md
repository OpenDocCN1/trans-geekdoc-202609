In *Feist*, the Supreme Court ruled against copyright protection for a telephone directory, stating that a mere collection of facts ordered in a systematic way lacked the necessary creativity, pointing out that copyright law was not intended to protect labor, otherwise known as the “sweat of the brow” doctrine, but rather to incentivize creative expression. Since NFTs have become a technological mechanism for creative expression, the Feist Test helps determine how copyright law could be used to protect the ownership rights of NFT creators. However, an NFT on its own does not pass the Feist Test; there must be a demonstration of original, creative expression. That does not exclude generative art, which requires some degree of structure and creative thought by the project owner, even if some degree of randomization is embedded into the full NFT collection.

Lee (2012) argued for expanding the interpretation of the Feist Test by introducing the concept of “digital originality.” In particular, the process of creation, not just the resulting work, should be considered when determining originality. Lee suggests that an innovative use of digital technology in the creative process should be recognized as original under copyright law. Taken to the extreme, the process of minting an NFT could be argued as digital originality, but there is not yet precedent or state law on this matter. By applying Lee’s concept of digital originality to NFTs, the creative process of minting an NFT and associating it with a digital asset could potentially qualify the NFT for copyright protection. This would be a significant departure from traditional copyright law, which focuses on the work itself rather than the process of creation.

However, Lee’s theory is not yet widely accepted, and the idea of extending copyright protection to NFTs in this way has not been piloted before. Courts and legislatures have yet to weigh in on this issue, and the landscape of copyright law related to NFTs is still in flux. In this sense, while existing copyright law provides some guidance for dealing with NFTs, there are significant gray areas, particularly regarding originality. The Feist Test and Edward Lee’s concept of digital originality provide some useful frameworks for thinking about these issues, but future legal developments will ultimately shape the final interpretations.

## 4.2.4 Fair Use Doctrine

Fair use is a doctrine in copyright law that allows limited use of copyrighted material without requiring permission from the rights holders. It allows the legal, non-licensed citation or incorporation of copyrighted material in another author’s work under a four-factor balancing test.

These factors include:

- The purpose and character of the use, including whether such use is of a commercial nature or is for nonprofit educational purposes.
- The nature of the copyrighted work.
- The amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole.
- The effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.

Although the fair use doctrine has not been employed significantly as a legal defense in alleged copyright infringement cases, it could apply in situations where a portion of a sound recording is used or when remixing.⁶ Consider the following two prominent examples of its application:

- Art Sector – *Cariou v. Prince* (2013): This is one of the most significant fair use cases in the art world. Richard Prince, a well-known appropriation artist, used Patrick Cariou’s photographs of Rastafarians to create new works of art. When Cariou sued for copyright infringement, Prince argued that his use of the photographs was transformative and thus constituted fair use. The Second Circuit Court of Appeals agreed with Prince, stating that his work “manifest[ed] an entirely different aesthetic” from Cariou’s photographs.
- Music Sector – *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* (1994): This case involved a parody of the song “Oh, Pretty Woman” by the rap group 2 Live Crew. The Supreme Court held that a commercial parody could qualify as fair use, noting that “the more transformative the new work, the less significant other factors, like commercialism, may become.” This case is particularly important because it clarified that a work’s commercial nature does not preclude a fair use defense. It affirmed the importance of transformative use in the fair use analysis.

The fair use doctrine is not a blanket permission to use copyrighted works without the owner’s consent, but rather a defense that can be invoked in a lawsuit for copyright infringement. Fair use is determined on a case-by-case basis, considering many factors, including the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the copyrighted work, the amount and substantiality of the portion taken, and the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the work.

Lee and Moshirnia (2022) present results from an experiment of 500 individuals surveyed on Mechanical Turk. When the subjects were informed about the fair use doctrine, they were more likely to rule in favor of no liability for the defendant – a result concentrated in cases where there was high similarity. These results suggest that the fair use doctrine can help remediate copyright and NFT clutter in cases where many NFTs of similar content exist. However, more work is required to explore how this would apply to NFTs.

## 4.3 NFTs AS DECENTRALIZED PROPERTY

The subtitle, “NFTs as decentralized property,” comes from a recent contribution by Lee (2023), pointing out that artists have not fared well because of the absence of remuneration for their created content and, in large part, because of a gap between theory and practice in the 1976 Copyright Act.⁷ Instead, Lee (2023) argues that the emergence of NFTs is a game changer for artists because it allows them to take ownership of their IP when they mint their NFT and write the rules into the smart contract.

In essence, NFTs and IP rights conferred by copyright and traditional patent and trademark law are different, but they can potentially complement one another in the market for art and beyond. In particular, NFTs can serve as a tool for managing and proving ownership of digital assets in a decentralized way (Colavizza, 2023; Evans, 2019; Makridis and Liao, 2023):

- **Proof of Ownership:** Since NFTs live on the blockchain, which provides a transparent, immutable record of transactions, every time an NFT is minted, there is a clear record of its origin and any subsequent ownership transfers. This transparency can help prove ownership of a digital asset in a way that is more difficult to forge or dispute compared to traditional methods.
- **Provenance Tracking:** The blockchain record can also help track the provenance (history) of a digital asset. Cheap, accessible, and immutable provenance tracking can be particularly valuable in the art world, where the value of a piece often depends not only on its inherent qualities but also on its history and previous ownership.
- **Smart Contracts:** NFTs can include smart contracts, which are self-executing contracts with the terms of the agreement directly written into code. These could potentially be used to manage IP rights in innovative ways. For example, a smart contract could automatically transfer a certain amount of royalties to the original creator every time the NFT is sold. Although code can contain vulnerabilities and gaps, generally speaking, these smart contracts will help manage legal risk since the mundane and often error-intensive portions will be automated away.
- **Access and Control:** NFTs can provide a way for creators to control access to their work. For example, an artist could mint an NFT that gives the holder access to a high-resolution version of a digital artwork while lower-resolution versions are freely available to the public. Here, the artist has full discretion over what benefits to confer.

While NFTs can provide these benefits, they do not inherently provide the same protections as copyright law. For example, copyright is a legal right that gives the creator of an original work exclusive rights to it, usually for a limited time, whereas NFTs — unless explicitly specified in the contract — do not grant these exclusive rights. Furthermore, since NFTs live on the blockchain, and there is not yet judicial precedent for the enforcement of smart contracts, there may be some delay and/or ambiguity over the enforcement of IP rights on the blockchain. The possibilities are vast, but more work is needed at the intersection of technology and law.
