
## 7.4 GOVERNANCE IN DAOs

A DAO is a computer code-based organization that relies on blockchain technology and smart contracts. The latter contain the DAO’s regulations, including its governance framework (Barbereau et al., 2023) and automate decision-making and organizational functions. In terms of the decision-making process, DAOs mostly depend on a token-based voting mechanism, which is considered the foundation of a DAO’s governance.

The members of a DAO often hold tokens that have voting rights. The DAO governance tokens are acquired through various techniques like rewarding, staking, airdropping, incentivizing liquidity provision, exchanging, and more. DAO governance token holders participate in various referendums and cast their votes regarding the operation, evolution, technical development, and other ideas related to the operation of the organization. This could involve choosing which initiatives to fund, altering the organization’s policies, or deciding on other day-to-day operations. Each member’s voting power is typically correlated with the quantity of tokens they possess (El Faqir et al., 2020).

Typically, there are three entities in a DAO, including the board, the technical committee, and the community, with each one of them having the right to submit proposals for voting and vote. Each of these entities has a different role, as summarized in Table 7.1.

| Board role | Technical Committee role | Community role |
|---|---|---|
| Propose sensible referenda | Submit proposals to the board | Nominate board members |
| Veto/cancel uncontroversial dangerous or malicious referenda | Emergency referenda, which are fast-tracked for voting and implementation | Vote for board members |
| Elect the technical committee | Veto referenda if a proposal in a referendum poses a security risk to the project, the TC can veto the referendum by making a recommendation to the board | Create proposals (public referenda) |
| Fast-track referenda, in case of a technical emergency, it can be prioritized for public vote | - | Prioritize referenda (in case of more than one) |

The decision-making process in DAOs varies depending on the voting model and governance mechanisms used. Most of these voting models have the same principles and are designed to ensure fair and efficient governance. However, they do have differences, as each one takes into account the DAO’s unique characteristics and requirements. Here, we will explore eight prevalent voting models in the world of DAOs.

- Token-based Quorum Voting
- Quadratic Voting
- Weighted Voting
- Knowledge-Extractable Voting
- Multisig Voting
- Holographic Consensus
- Rage Quitting Voting
- Conviction Voting

Each of these voting models has its own advantages and disadvantages, and the choice of model can significantly impact the dynamics and outcomes of DAO governance. In practice, many DAOs use hybrid models that combine elements of different voting models to cater to their specific needs and scenarios.

For a proposal to pass under Quorum Voting, a predetermined number of voters must participate. When this threshold is reached, the vote-counting decision with the most votes wins. In many cases, proposals fall short of the necessary number of votes to pass. Traditionally, the threshold has been determined by counting all of the votes cast; however, certain protocols (such as compound governance) have a quorum threshold that only counts the votes needed for a proposal to pass (20 percent quorum, in this case, would mean 20 percent of the voting power must vote in favor of a proposal in order for it to be considered for passage).

For example, let's consider a hypothetical DAO called “Decentralized.” In Decentralized, each member holds $EARTH tokens, which they can use to vote on various proposals, such as changes to community rules or approval of new development projects. Assume that a plan to construct a new virtual reality park in Decentralized is presented. There may be a 20 percent quorum needed for this proposal to be approved. This implies that in order to vote on this proposal, at least 20 percent of all $EARTH tokens must be spent. Regardless of the result, a vote is deemed void if fewer than 20 percent of tokens are spent during the voting procedure. This mechanism guarantees that a tiny, possibly unrepresentative group of token holders does not make choices. Participation from a wide range of community members is required instead, and this can result in more democratic and representative outcomes.

It’s crucial to remember that this model could have certain disadvantages as well. For example, getting a quorum can be difficult, particularly in larger areas where there may be low voter turnout. Furthermore, voting systems based on tokens have the potential to result in a plutocratic government, in which the wealthy and/or those with more tokens have a disproportionate amount of power over decisions.

Overall, Token-based Quorum Voting is the most popular tool for decentralized governance (El Faqir et al., 2020), but like all systems, it should be carefully designed and implemented to ensure fair and representative outcomes.

### 7.4.3 Quadratic Voting

By allowing voters to indicate the extent of their preferences, Quadratic Voting significantly improves on the traditional voting method and eventually helps do away with majority rule. Each voter is given a set of credits in Quadratic Voting, and they are asked to indicate which alternatives they favor based on their personal preferences. A voter might, for instance, give all their points to a single, highly favored candidate. Alternatively, they might give other candidates differing numbers of credits, reflecting differing levels of support for front-runners. The square root of the credits allotted to each option is the number of votes in a Quadratic Voting system. Quadratic Voting’s primary benefit is a more accurate depiction of the public’s preferences.

Let’s use a hypothetical DAO named “EcoChain” as an example. Members of EcoChain can vote on which environmental projects should get funding. A budget of 100 votes is allocated to each member, which they can use for various projects. Assume that there are three projects: C (renewable energy), B (cleaning oceans), and A (planting trees). A member with a strong commitment to ocean conservation might decide to give Project B more votes. On the other hand, the price of every additional vote for the same proposal rises quadratically. Therefore, they would need to spend their whole budget – 100 votes (10x10) – to provide ten votes to Project B. Voters are encouraged to consider their choices and the trade-offs they are willing to make carefully as a result.

Additionally, Quadratic Voting lessens the possibility of the “tyranny of the majority,” in which choices are selected purely on the basis of the quantity of voters rather than the quality of those votes. A somewhat preferred alternative by the majority can take precedence over a minority’s strongly preferred option in a simple majority vote. This is lessened by Quadratic Voting, which gives the minority the opportunity to show how strongly they feel about a choice by allotting it more votes.

Quadratic Voting is not without its difficulties, though. Strategic voting and possible manipulation are worrisome, and it might be difficult to comprehend and use. It is a viable method of decision-making in DAOs and other collective groups despite these difficulties (Lalley and Weyl, 2018).

### 7.4.4 Token-weighted Voting

A voting system known as Token-weighted Voting assigns voting power to each participant based on the quantity of tokens they possess. A normal approach is a one-to-one vote-to-token ratio, which means every governance token represents one vote in the DAO. In many of the DAOs, the vote that comes with the token can either be kept in the owner address or be delegated to another address. This approach is commonly mistaken for mix up Weighted Voting and Quorum Voting.
