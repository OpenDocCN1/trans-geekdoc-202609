IoV is proposed as a concept inside ITS; it is powered by smart vehicles, IoT, and AI techniques. Through the Internet and dedicated short-range communication technology, intelligent vehicles may communicate with other vehicles, drivers, passengers, and roadside units (Lone et al., 2021). Sometimes, the vehicular communication system is referred to as Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X). V2X enables interaction between vehicles and any other entity (e.g., vehicle, infrastructure, grid, pedestrian, etc.) (Mahmood, 2020). Therefore, IoV represents an integrated network of interconnected vehicles, smart infrastructure, and devices that enhances vehicle communication, safety, and efficiency. It is a significant branch of the broader IoT ecosystem, where vehicles act as moving nodes, exchanging information and interacting with their surrounding environment.

V2V: The V2V paradigm came into our lives with the introduction of smart city applications. Accordingly, V2V suggests that everything and everyone has the potential to be connected and communicate with each other. In this sense, the V2V paradigm allows two or more geographically close vehicles to interact and communicate (NHTSA, 2014). V2V communication takes place on top of a wireless network where cars can send messages to each other, with information like speed, position, travel directions, fuel consumption, brake status, and so on. For instance, vehicles that support an automated braking system and benefit from V2V communication will be able to proactively detect emergencies based on data from their own and surrounding vehicles, and avoid unexpected collisions. Road safety, traffic management, and energy efficiency are the key motivations behind V2V. Thus, the V2V paradigm has the potential to save human lives and increase driving performance, while at the same time reducing pollution and carbon emissions, especially in urban areas.

V2G enables energy to be pushed back to the power grid from the battery of EVs, and its concept is similar to smart charging. Smart charging allows the EV driver to remotely track, control, and limit the usage of the battery to maximize energy consumption. The V2G paradigm takes this a step forward, allowing energy to be transmitted from the EV’s batteries back to the grid and vice versa, offsetting differences in energy production and consumption. This way, EV drivers are not only consumers of energy but they are actively participating in the trading process. Thus, they can be characterized as prosumers (i.e., producers and consumers of energy at the same time) (Themistocleous et al., 2019). V2G charging technology can help prosumers save money or make a profit by allowing their EV batteries to store energy and return it to the grid when it is most required.

IoV Challenges: Data management and security are significant barriers in IoV. Through V2V and V2G interactions, IoV systems generate high amounts of data. If this data is not properly managed, it can lead to system inefficiency and the exposure of sensitive information. Considering the nature of the information at stake, data security in the IoV environment is highly important. Location-tracking data, driver-specific information, and specific vehicle status or diagnostic data must be carefully protected. A security breach could not only result in a violation of privacy but also enable malicious entities to manipulate vehicle systems, posing grave safety concerns. Real-time, dependable communication is another significant obstacle. IoV systems are based on instantaneous information exchange and thus, the smallest delay in information transmission can cause inefficiencies in the operation of autonomous vehicles or safety systems, which may lead to accidents.
