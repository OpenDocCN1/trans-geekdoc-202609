# 8.4.4 Futarchy Voting

Futarchy is a governance concept introduced by Hanson (2013) that was not specifically designed for DAOs like the other voting mechanisms, but rather to optimize democratic policymaking by using prediction markets to achieve the best possible outcome for the national welfare. Futarchy arised from the assumption that governments often choose inefficient policies, although experts of the specific areas are aware of better alternatives (Hanson, 2013). This happens due to information asymmetry within a democratic system and biased media coverage. Additionally, in an ordinary democracy there is little motivation to engage in the decision-making process, as the cost to do so – for example to seek information – is much higher compared to the insignificant influence on the outcome of an individual vote. However, by betting on a proposal’s impact on the well-being of the community, incentives exist to be well informed and to get involved in the decision-making process, as prediction market participants can lose or gain their invested capital. Therefore, only those policy proposals which prediction markets have clearly estimated will have the most positive effect for the community will get accepted. Futarchy is therefore a mixture of democracy and speculative markets. It was Buterin (2014b) who first proposed Hanson’s futarchy concept as a suitable governance model for DAOs. All DAO members can express their needs in the form of proposals, but only DAO members who place a bet on a proposal would vote by predicting if a proposal would have a positive or negative effect for the community. At the end only proposals with the predicted greatest positive impact on the community would be selected to be implemented in the DAO. Depending on whether the positive prediction was right or not, the predicting DAO member would then win or lose tokens. It is the hope that futarchy would create stronger interest to make informed decisions, as DAO members bear direct financial risk with their votes. However, there are also some arguments against this kind of voting mechanism (see Buterin, 2014b): first, that in markets individuals tend to follow a trend instead of forming their own opinion in detail; second, that participation in the prediction market could be much lower than expected, as most DAO members will not risk their tokens; and third, that it is difficult to measure the actual impact of a selected proposal on the community correctly after its implementation, as other factors might have influenced the outcome. Implementing pure futarchy in DAOs has therefore been difficult in practice, but hybrid forms that incorporate aspects of futarchy seem to be a possible way to go.
