A general point which surfaces from the abovementioned examples, and will become more evident from the below analysis, is that tracking cryptoassets in illicit transactions on the blockchain takes the form of a race between legal authorities and criminals. As the latter develop new tools and practices to involve cryptocurrencies in their illicit activities, much more imperative will be the need for authorities to upgrade their systems, knowledge and expertise in order to combat this unique form of crime. Concurrently, by its nature, crime investigation is reactive rather than proactive, focusing on a series of events linked to carrying out criminal activity. Equally, crime prevention is more effective when it is targeted to prevent the repetition of past experience and conduct, in which case it will address specifically identified behaviors and scenarios. Considering the novelty of this space, it is expected that both crime prevention and crime investigation will continue to significantly develop.

### 18.3.3 Cryptocurrencies in Ransomware, Extortion and Theft

Focusing on the most prominent cases of cryptocurrency-related offenses, cryptocurrencies are commonly used in ransomware attacks, cases of extortion and theft. In the case of ransomware, a malicious malware is typically used in order to encrypt the victim’s data or block their systems. Thereafter, the perpetrator will require the payment of a ransom in exchange for the decryption of the victim’s data or the restoration of access to their systems. In other cases, it may be that both a victim’s data is decrypted (albeit outside of the users’ systems) and their systems are blocked, such that even if the victim manages to gain access to their systems, the threat of publication of the stolen data remains (also called double extortion). The extortion might equally target third parties to the extortion (triple extortion), who can be affected by the disclosure of the stolen information (e.g., the disclosure of patents, intellectual property, personal data), or might even involve more than a single extortion method (multiple extortion). It is not uncommon for victims of ransomware attacks to pay the ransoms demanded from them, in an effort to prevent the leakage of sensitive data to the public, to avoid reputational damage to their business and becoming the subject of legal suits or police investigations. Victims may also fear that if they report a ransomware attack, they will become the target of similar future attacks, as they fear that perpetrators might decide to retaliate against such behavior. As a result, ransomware cases are considered to be underreported (FATF, 2023).

At the same time, despite ransomware attacks being one of the most common types of cryptoasset crime, the ransomware ecosystem is actually limited in numbers (Grauer et al., 2023, p. 30). This is linked to the fact that ransomware itself has become a service (RaaS), whereby cybercriminals (the affiliates) pay a fee (typically a percentage of the ransom) to designers of a ransomware strain (the administrators), in order to gain access to or use that strain. RaaS may include infiltration into a victim’s system, the stealing of their data, even paying administrators to engage in negotiations with the victims of ransomware attacks. Therefore, RaaS has offered the possibility of carrying out ransomware attacks to an increasing number of criminals willing to pay to use that service, as opposed to only those who have the technological capabilities and knowledge to carry them out.

After the payment of the demanded ransom (payment of ransomware proceeds is almost exclusively done in cryptocurrencies), depending on how methodical perpetrators are, the cryptocurrencies might "sit" in their wallets for multiple months before being transferred or withdrawn (FATF, 2023). Administrators are typically more strategic and careful, whereas affiliates are less experienced and careless, which makes it easier to track and seize the stolen cryptoassets under their possession. The targets of ransomware attacks typically include high-value companies (the "big game hunting"), entities linked to public infrastructures and, more rarely, small businesses or individuals (Grauer et al., 2023, p. 35). Following successful ransomware attacks, the perpetrators will try to "wash" the stolen cryptocurrencies, for example, via the use of mixers (analyzed further in Section 18.4) and peel chains.¹

Encouragingly, latest reports indicate that law enforcement has been successful in restraining ransomware. Firstly, from an enforcement and investigation perspective, a key factor to the curtailment has been the increasing number of reports of ransomware attacks, which in turn has facilitated the recovery of stolen cryptoassets and has led to arrests. This shift is actually linked to obligations imposed and measures addressed to regulate financial institutions, which ultimately aim to target the perpetrators of ransomware attacks. Indicatively, the US Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC), in its latest Advisory on Potential Sanctions Risks for Facilitating Ransomware Payments, focuses on discouraging victims of ransomware attacks from making payments to perpetrators and indicates steps which victims can take to combat ransomware risks (US Department of Treasury, 2021). In cases where a victim does make a ransomware payment, the OFAC will consider a number of designated "mitigating factors" in any related enforcement action, including the adoption of cybersecurity practices, the provision of a self-initiated and complete report of a ransomware attack to law enforcement or other relevant US government agencies, as well as ongoing and full cooperation with law enforcement, both during and after a ransomware attack. An approach which follows the same logic has also been adopted by the United Kingdom (UK) Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation (OFSI) (HM Treasury Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation, 2023).

Secondly, from a legislative perspective, the EU has extended the application of the FATF travel rule to all transfers of cryptoassets, regardless of the value of a certain transfer. Under the recast of the Transfer of Funds Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2015/847), Crypto Asset Service Providers (CASPs) are required to collect and verify information on the sender and beneficiary of a cryptoasset transaction. Such information will then need to be communicated to the receiving CASP. In this way, information will "travel" along with the cryptoassets, with both the receiving party and the transferee having access to them. This extension is meant to help track cryptoasset transfers, identify and block illicit transactions, and prevent the transfer of any other funds. Hence, the travel rule combats peel chains and overall increases the transparency of cryptocurrency transfers. At the same time, a number of exceptions will apply. Most notably, Person-to-Person (P2P) transactions (whereby no CASP will be involved in the transaction) are excluded from the scope of this Regulation. As such, the recast does not extend to cases where a single ransomware payment will be made between private wallets. Indeed, P2P transactions remain largely unregulated, with the FATF advising jurisdictions to monitor the risks associated with them and sharing their experiences with one another in order to determine whether they should come under future FATF standards (FATF, 2023).

Thirdly, as a market and industry-driven factor to the combatting of ransomware, cyber insurance firms (typically required to "pick up the tab" in cases of ransomware payments) have become much stricter in terms of who they will agree to insure and whether the insurance will cover ransom payments (Grauer et al., 2023, p. 34). For example, cyber insurance firms now require insured entities to have firm cybersecurity and backup measures in place.
