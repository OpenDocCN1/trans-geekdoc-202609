## 4.2 BACKGROUND AND DEFINITIONS

Legal precedent and guidance set the economic and social activity framework. Although the Web3 community and the emerging NFT marketplaces have sometimes tried to separate themselves from centralized institutions, the reality is that the people who participate in these communities and marketplaces live and interact with centralized institutions, whether it is a bank or the law, and, as a result, these fundamental issues cannot be overlooked.

### 4.2.1 Defining Art NFTs

The emergence of distributed ledger technologies has the potential to serve as a catalyst for revitalizing the arts by empowering artists with new tools for ensuring the protection of their IP and receiving remuneration for their creative content. Unfortunately, real wages have stagnated—and even declined substantially, relative to non-artists—among artists in the United States (Makridis, 2023) and Europe (European Commission, 2020). In short, artists and workers in cultural sectors more broadly, have faced increased financial fragility.

Blockchain technology has been increasingly adopted in the art industry in recent years, providing artists, collectors, and organizations with a new way to create, share, and monetize their work. Traditional intermediaries, such as record labels and museums, have often required artists to forfeit their IP over the created content in exchange for the promise of heightened visibility and access. While sometimes the exchange is profitable, many artists have felt that they have been taken advantage of by traditional contracts and institutions.

NFTs, in contrast, provide artists with the opportunity to go directly to consumers by placing their content on the blockchain for anyone to see and potentially purchase. Moreover, since the record of the NFT is immutable, future trades can generate revenues for the creator in perpetuity. NFTs are generally a mix of two characteristics (Thorn et al., 2022):

- A digital token, often governed by Ethereum’s ERC-721 standard, holds a unique cryptographic address and contains metadata stored on a blockchain. The metadata is not the image or audio clip, but rather a link to a file that often exists off-chain.²
- A license is issued to the owner of the NFT by the creator of the NFT, granting the owner the right to display and/or possibly commercialize the file to which the NFT points.

More specifically, the application of blockchain to the arts provides several possible benefits:

- **Art provenance and authenticity:** Blockchain allows for the creation of digital certificates of authenticity, which mitigate the risk of forgery and provide a transparent and immutable record of an artwork’s provenance to ensure proper remuneration.
- **Digital art and music:** NFTs are unique digital assets that can be bought, sold, or traded on various blockchain platforms. Artists have started to create and sell digital art as NFTs, providing them with new revenue streams while also giving collectors a new way to own and display digital art or music.
- **Decentralized art galleries:** Blockchain-based platforms such as Decentraland and Sandbox offer artists the opportunity to display their work in virtual galleries. These virtual spaces allow artists to reach global audiences and create unique, although not yet very immersive, experiences.
- **Royalties and resale rights:** Blockchain-based marketplaces can automatically distribute royalties to artists when their work is resold.
- **Crowdfunding and patronage:** Blockchain can also be utilized for crowdfunding and patronage of artists. Some standard platforms, like Patreon, have already begun integrating blockchain technology, allowing fans to support artists with fungible tokens.
- **Art marketplaces:** Blockchain-based art marketplaces such as OpenSea and SuperRare provide a platform for buying, selling, and trading artworks using cryptocurrency. These marketplaces could offer increased transparency and security for transactions and lower fees than traditional art marketplaces, but there are also concerns about their changing of contractual terms with content creators.
- **Collaborative art projects:** Blockchain technology can facilitate collaborative art projects, where multiple artists contribute to a single piece, allowing for the transparent documentation of each artist’s role in the project and ensuring proper attribution of credit.
- **Accessibility:** Blockchain technology enables the fractionalization of art so that more collectors can share ownership of an artwork. Fractional art is more accessible to the broader public and potentially creates a new demand for artworks from audiences that have thus far been excluded from the art market (e.g., Whitaker and Kräussl, 2020).

These are just a few of the potential benefits of using blockchain technology in the arts, but, admittedly, seeing the possibility become a reality will take more work.

### 4.2.2 Legal Doctrine on Patents, Trademarks, and Copyrights

Despite the surge in popularity around art NFTs, their intersection with IP law, specifically copyright, patent, and trademark law, is complex and still underdeveloped. Understanding these aspects of IP law is crucial to conceptualizing the implications of NFTs.

Most developed countries distinguish between three distinct IP protections, each covering different areas:

- Patents protect inventions and improvements to existing inventions.
- Trademarks protect business brand names, slogans, logos, and other brand identifiers.
- Copyrights protect original artistic and literary works.

First, consider copyright law, which protects original works of authorship, including literary, dramatic, musical, artistic, and certain other intellectual works, both published and unpublished. The 1976 Copyright Act gives the owner of the copyright the exclusive right to reproduce the copyrighted work, to prepare derivative works, to distribute copies, and to perform and display the work publicly.³ However, the purchase of an NFT does not necessarily come with a transfer of the copyright behind the digital art. In fact, unless explicitly stated, the artist retains the copyright, meaning that they still have the exclusive right to reproduce and sell copies of the artwork.

This has been recently highlighted by Thorn et al. (2022), who pointed out that the “vast majority of NFTs convey zero intellectual property ownership of their underlying content (artwork, media, etc.)” and that many issuers “appear to have misled NFT purchasers as to the intellectual property rights for the content they sell.” In addition, the often-used Creative Commons license puts IP into the public domain, meaning that NFT holders cannot legally defend their ownership of the NFT in court. Jennings and Dixon (2022) have also proposed the “can’t be evil” license, “transparently codifying the rights of NFT creators, buyers, and sellers so that every party has a common understanding of the rights associated with NFT ownership.”

Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co. (1991) provides the current standard for copyright protection in the US, stipulating that a work must be original and possess a minimal degree of creativity.4 However, for Artificial Intelligence (AI)-generated works, it’s unclear who, if anyone, owns the copyright, as – at least legally – AI does not possess independent creativity. This has been supported by recent rulings. For example, in August 2023, a federal judge upheld a finding from the US Copyright Office, in the case Thaler v. Perlmutter, that art created by AI is not open for protection, emphasizing that “human authorship is a bedrock requirement.”

Second, consider patent law, which protects inventions and discoveries that are new, useful, and non-obvious. Patents grant their owners the right to exclude others from making, using, or selling the invention for a period of time. In the context of NFTs, patent law could come into play if an artist or platform develops a novel and non-obvious method of creating or trading NFTs. However, patent law may not have as many implications for art NFTs since it would not help protect artistic expression, although patent law may have substantial interactions with NFTs more broadly as they expand outside of art and music.

Third, consider trademark law, which protects words, phrases, symbols, or designs identifying and distinguishing the source of goods or services. While an NFT could incorporate a trademark – for instance, an NFT of a digital artwork featuring a brand’s logo – buying an NFT does not transfer any trademark rights unless otherwise noted in the smart contract. Some applications to date allow NFT holders to use the design as a “license” over the trademark, as in the case of Bored Ape Yacht Club, but the ownership resides with its founders, Yuga Labs.

A notable case regarding the intersection of NFTs and trademarks is Nike, Inc. v. MSCHF Product Studio, Inc. (2021).5 In this case, MSCHF sold NFTs of Nike’s “Satan Shoes” without Nike’s permission. The court ruled in favor of Nike, illustrating that the unauthorized use of a trademark in an NFT can result in a trademark infringement.

The legal landscape around NFTs is still in flux, and it remains to be seen how they will be evaluated by the judicial system, as there is little precedent. While existing copyright, patent, and trademark law provides some guidance, there are gray areas, particularly around issues of digital originality, AI-generated art, and the implications of blockchain technology.

The Feist Test has become a tenet of copyright law in the United States.6 The ruling sets out the requirement that a work must be original and possess at least a minimal degree of creativity.
