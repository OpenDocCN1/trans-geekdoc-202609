### 16.2.2 Blockchain Technology and Its Potential

Blockchain technology is a distributed ledger consisting of a collection of blocks that register various data or transaction records (Christodoulou et al., 2019; Nakamoto, 2008). These may include various types of information such as property ownership, national records, healthcare records, shares, stocks, and several others (Duong-Trung et al., 2020; Farooq, Khan, and Abid, 2020). According to Antonopoulos (2015) blockchain is a decentralized ledger that operates on a P2P network and implies that there is no central authority in control. Each network participant, or node, holds a copy of the entire blockchain (Cunha et al., 2020). Transactions are placed in secure blocks, which are cryptographically linked to one another in a chain. Before a transaction can be added to the blockchain, it must be validated by multiple nodes through a process known as consensus, ensuring the integrity and authenticity of the information. Blockchain has unique characteristics, such as immutability, security, trust, and transparency (Viriyasitavat and Hoonsopon, 2019; Xinyi, Yi, and He, 2018). Immutability is achieved as each block contains a hash, which is a unique identifier that is based on the contents of the block. In case a malfunctioning entity (user) attempts to modify the contents of a block, it will automatically change the hash of that block as well as the hashes of all subsequent blocks. Thus, it is currently impossible to alter a single block without the consent of the network participants. Another characteristic of blockchain technology is its transparency, which makes blockchain ideal for use in situations where transparency and accountability are essential (e.g., energy DRM or energy transactions).

Blockchain technology enables the secure and transparent recording and verification of energy trading transactions (Anoh et al., 2020; Kirpes and Becker, 2018). It also has the potential to remove intermediaries such as banks or energy brokers and enables consumers to buy electricity directly from energy producers (Themistocleous et al., 2018). Blockchain-based energy transactions are recorded on a decentralized ledger that is publicly available for verification. Smart contracts are also used to facilitate the trading of energy (Liu et al., 2019).

**Blockchain Advantages:** The implementation of blockchain technology has extensive and varied benefits, which are summarized below:

- Eliminates the need for a central authority or trusted intermediary.
- Mitigates the risks associated with centralized system failure, data manipulation, and corruption.
- All blockchain participants maintain equal control.
- Enables transparency as all transactions are recorded and made accessible to all network participants (Mylrea, 2019; Steiner et al., 2019).
- Ensures immutability and makes blockchain ideal for scenarios requiring an indisputable record of events.
- Secures transactions and blocks recording through cryptographic algorithms.
- Ensures the confidentiality and integrity of transactions.
- Secures transactions against unauthorized access, fraud, and other cyberattacks (Khalil et al., 2022).
- Promotes an inclusive economic system by allowing anyone to participate in blockchain-based transactions. This feature has the potential to expand access to financial services to large populations that are currently unbanked or underbanked (Gillpatrick, Boğa, and Aldanmaz, 2022).

An important challenge within the IoV framework is the balance between energy demand and supply, particularly during periods of peak demand. The concept of “energy demand” refers to the amount of energy consumed by human activities. It drives the whole energy system, influencing the overall quantity of energy used, the location and kinds of fuel utilized in the energy supply system, and the parameters of end-use technologies that consume energy. Due to congested electricity grids that are difficult to manage in the traditional manner, the potential demand for the smart grid network has increased (Sun et al., 2010). DR enables users to play a significant role in grid operations by reducing or altering their energy consumption during peak hours in response to time-based tariffs or other financial incentives. To that end, a number of electric system operators balance supply and demand using DR systems (Verbič, Mhanna, and Chapman, 2019). Such schemes can reduce the cost of electricity in energy markets, resulting in reduced market prices. Time-based pricing, such as time-of-use, critical peak, variable peak, and real time pricing schemes are some examples of the ways to engage market participants in DR activities (Venkatachary, Prasad, and Samikannu, 2017).

DR is a critical technological solution capable of tackling this issue by redistributing energy consumption from high-demand periods to times of lower usage. Fundamentally, DR is an advanced economic management strategy for electric power distribution, enabled and streamlined by advanced technologies. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), it’s estimated that by 2050, DR strategies will have the potential to modify up to 15 percent of the average annual electricity consumption (Qiu and Antonik, 2017). Utility providers can leverage smart metering and smart grid technologies to continuously monitor energy usage and accurately identify periods of peak demand, thus facilitating a more efficient implementation of DR (Stanelyte, Radziukyniene, and Radziukynas, 2022).

DR can be implemented via numerous strategies, such as dynamic pricing, load shedding, and load shifting. When combined with V2G technology, DR can be especially effective within the IoV. Through V2G technology, EVs can act as mobile energy storage units, helping to balance energy demand and supply within the framework of IoV. During times of high electricity demand, the network operator can signal EVs to discharge their stored energy into the grid (Leippi, Fleschutz, and Murphy, 2022). In contrast, when electricity demand is low, the operator can instruct EVs to charge their batteries by drawing power from the grid.

This dynamic interaction not only allows IoV operators to reduce their reliance on costly peaking power plants, but it also provides EV owners with a financial incentive. By participating in this energy exchange, EV owners can generate income by selling their excess electricity back to the grid during times of high demand. Consequently, DR not only contributes to a more efficient and dependable IoV, but it also generates economic benefits for EV owners.

The combination of blockchain technology, IoVs, and DRM reshapes the field of energy management. More specifically, it enables P2P energy transactions between EVs and the grid and offers a secure, transparent, and efficient method for recording and validating V2G transactions. Blockchain technology emerges as an indispensable tool for managing them. The unique characteristics of blockchain make it an ideal fit for this context. This results in an increased level of trust between parties, which is essential for the effective operation of a decentralized P2P energy market. In addition, the combination of blockchain technology with IoV and DRM provides numerous benefits such as decentralization, smart contract support, and tokenization.

## 16.3 IDENTIFICATION OF THE RESEARCH CHALLENGES AND RESEARCH GAP

### 16.3.1 Research Challenges

As elaborated in earlier sections, IoVs experience significant strain due to substantial shifts in production and the spread of DERs (e.g., EVs). Increasing irregularities in power generation necessitate the emergence of innovative paradigms. DR is a well-researched strategy where EV consumers consciously modify their power consumption based on grid demands.

Consequently, intelligent energy management, which promotes the best exploitation of restricted energy resources, becomes a precondition for the development of a smart, environmentally friendly, and sustainable power grid. Nonetheless, the deployment of unpredictably scattered RES and uncoordinated EVs presents considerable obstacles to intelligent energy management. A substantial deployment of centralized generators and energy storage devices would be necessary to balance supply and demand. However, this solution brings substantial Capital Expenditure (CAPEX) and Operational Expense (OPEX). Alternatively, capitalizing on the swift expansion of DR programs, particularly in IoV, allows energy consumers to proactively adapt their energy consumption and production patterns based on fluctuating costs or incentives. Considering that IoV serves as a participatory platform for data exchange and storage, it is essential that its underlying information exchange system ensures security, transparency, and immutability to achieve the desired objectives. In this context, the employment of blockchain technology as a foundational platform to address the needs of the IoV has been explored.
