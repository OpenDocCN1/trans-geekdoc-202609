## 19.1.1 Overview of Computer Networks’ Advancements and Blockchain’s Main Characteristics

The rapid growth of emerging technologies such as virtualization and cloud computing resulted in the development of new networking technologies. Considering the fundamentals of networking, it is known that a network device (i.e., router, switch) consists of a data plane and a control plane. The data plane is usually a switch connecting various network ports on a device, and the control plane contains the logic and is essentially the brain of the device. The idea of a logically distributed control plane was born and has led to the development of SDNs. SDNs took advantage of virtualization technology characteristics and proceeded to decouple the control plane and data plane. At the same time, the idea of developing and implementing network functionalities using programming languages has been gaining ground. Functions such as routing and firewalling, which were previously implemented by hardware networking devices, could then be implemented using code. Hence, the ability to host network functions (NFs) in the form of code in virtual environments such as Virtual Machines (VMs) was proposed. This resulted in the birth of NFV and the creation of Virtual Network Functions (VNFs). NFV allows network operators and service providers to develop and implement NFs in the form of software, leveraging standard servers and virtualization technologies, instead of running on purpose-built hardware. The development of NFV and SDN was extremely beneficial for the networking industry as the manageability of NGNs was increased, their overall capital expenditure (CAPEX) and operational expenditure (OPEX) were decreased, while networks’ flexibility, resource utilization, and performance metrics were improved.

The ability to operate NFs in the form of VNFs in virtual instances where the reserved resources (compute, memory) are controlled, has urged researchers to exploit virtualization technology (Karkazis et al., 2022; Prekas et al., 2020) and develop virtual network slices to support various use cases. These slices have been defined by 3GPP as “a logical network that provides specific network capabilities and network characteristics” (3GPP, 2017). A network slice is implemented by a slice instance, which consists of NFs and their corresponding computing, storage, and networking resources. The description of the structure and the configuration details of a slice are captured in the so-called network slice template. Essentially, with NS, a Network Provider (NP) can deploy multiple logical networks over the same physical infrastructure, which automatically increases the utilization level of the infrastructure. Network slices use VNFs to provide the correct network environment for each use case. A network slice may use one or many VNFs that are chained together to meet predefined requirements for specific applications. The chaining of network services is called Service Function Chaining (SFC), and is an extremely important process that affects the network performance of the slice and the overall operation of the application. Therefore, SFC is considered one of the most interesting research topics in the computer networks field.

The dynamicity of NGNs, the high-quality standards that must be guaranteed, and the high expectations of the end user, make the network management of NGNs a challenging task. New network management tools are being developed, and various network management techniques are being examined to facilitate the flexible management of network services. Such tools and respective frameworks are called Management and Orchestration (MANO), and as their name denotes, they are responsible for the management and orchestration of network slices and VNFs. Trakadas et al. (2020) mention that MANO frameworks are considered a management and orchestration suite for both physical and virtual resources related to the life cycle of the deployed network service. MANOs interact using the Virtual Infrastructure Manager (VIM) component with cloud infrastructures owned by NPs and manage their resources to deploy and manage the requested NSs. However, the increased network complexity, the introduction of new business-oriented services, the need for performance improvement, and the constant research toward the development of future networks beyond 5G forced the research community to examine more sophisticated MANO approaches. The European Telecommunications Standard (ETSI) moved in this direction by creating the ETSI ZSM Group in December 2017 (ETSI Standardization Group). The ultimate goal of this standardization team is the design and development of the ZSM framework. ZSM is designed to enable zero-touch automated network service and management in a multivendor environment to support demanding applications by ensuring the high-performance metrics of NSs.

However, the network management process of NGNs should be characterized not only by dynamicity and automation. In parallel, their security and robustness should be enhanced to defend against malicious actors (attackers). In the last few years, there has been increased research interest in the use of blockchain technology in NGNs, which resulted in the generation of new published works (Maksymyuk et al., 2020; Rebello et al., 2019; Togou et al., 2020) The goal is to harness the benefits of blockchain’s inherent characteristics to improve the security characteristics of the networks. At the same time, researchers examine how they can minimize the impact of blockchain’s drawbacks to fully embrace the advantages of this technology. Blockchain forms a decentralized network of trust in an untrusted environment where secure transactions are initiated between parties that follow the applied consensus mechanism. The content of the transactions is indisputable and immutable as it is registered in the ledger of the blockchain. Moreover, blockchain supports the implementation of logic in the form of immutable computational programs called Smart Contracts (SCs), which allows us to develop many different Distributed Applications (Dapps). As a result, the application area of this technology widens and allows us to examine the use of blockchain in many sectors such as NGNs. To this end, numerous research works have been conducted and published in the community.

The structure of this chapter is organized as follows: in Section 19.2, we highlight the main challenges and limitations of NGNs and present the problem statement that can be addressed using blockchain technology. Then, in Sections 19.3, 19.4, and 19.5, we conduct a survey of research works that leverage the use of blockchain in NGNs in various aspects, such as the resource management process, NS and ZSM-enabled network management scenarios. After examining these novel works, in Section 19.6 we gather our main research findings and proceed to a discussion aimed at shedding light on how blockchain can be used in an efficient and beneficial manner to address the security issues of NGNs. The current chapter ends with the conclusions of our study in Section 19.7.

## 19.2 NGNs’ CHALLENGES AND LIMITATIONS – PROBLEM STATEMENT

5G promises to provide high-quality network services to every corner of our planet to support demanding applications for numerous use cases. To achieve that, the 5G infrastructure must be implemented globally, which is one of the biggest challenges, while NPs must cooperate to fulfill clients’ requests and, at the same time, maintain costs at reasonable levels. The deployment of 5G infrastructure cannot be accomplished by one NP, nor the management of this vast and continuously growing ecosystem. The network must be divided into different segments called management domains, which are the responsibility/administrative areas of the NP. An NP can be responsible for more than one domain and should be able to easily interact with other domains, which may belong to different NPs. Similar to legacy networks where the user of the network could use services implemented by one or many NPs in case of roaming, in the 5G scenario, the user should be able to access the 5G network in a provider-agnostic manner. Therefore, NPs should cooperate to deploy and manage the 5G network globally and offer the desired Quality of Service (QoS) levels to the end-user.

NGN services can be offered over virtualized environments, such as cloud infrastructures; hence, every eligible entity capable of hosting these services is considered an NP. This leads to an increased number of NPs and the formation of a new competitive marketplace where the lack of trust among participants is present and is considered a major challenge. NGNs require the cooperation of NPs, which should be able to easily trade or lease resources to support network services. In other words, it would be highly beneficial for all networking resource providers to be able to dynamically lend/borrow resources, instead of the currently employed semi-static, coarse Service Level Agreements (SLAs). However, this is difficult to achieve in an untrusted environment in the absence of a trusted third party.

To clarify the situation, we present an example of such a collaboration. Supposing NP1 and NP2 are the NPs of our scenario and each one is responsible for and owns a management domain with specific resources (virtual resources, VNFs, Network Slices, etc.). The end-user of NP1 took a boat trip and arrived at the port of an island that is out of reach of NP1 and inside the management area of NP2. The end-user wants to access a streaming service with certain QoS parameters that are guaranteed by the powerful 5G network. Since NP1 will not be able to support the end-user’s request, a collaboration with NP2 takes place. NP1 asks NP2 to fulfill the request of the end-user with the predefined QoS level, and NP2 sets the price for this action. Figure 19.1 illustrates this scenario.

This process is essentially the negotiation process initiated between the NP that requests resources and the one that lends them. When these two entities agree, they sign an SLA where the terms of their transaction are described. Then NP2 fulfills the request of the end-user on behalf of NP1. But what happens if NP2 does not honor the agreement deliberately and fails to fulfill the request of the end user? Obviously, this is an SLA violation, and the proper penalty should be applied to NP2. However, the result from the end user’s perspective is service failure. In a marketplace that consists of competitive NPs, the likelihood of a deliberate SLA violation is high considering that there is no trust among participants. Therefore, trust among participants is one of the main challenges of NGNs.
