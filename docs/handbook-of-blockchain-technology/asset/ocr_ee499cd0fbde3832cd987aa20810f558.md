The court in that case had identified that in the absence of a Bankers Trust order, it might not have been possible to identify the alleged perpetrators and/or grant an effective remedy to the applicants. Equally, a Bankers Trust order and a Norwich Pharmacal Order (an equitable remedy whereby the Court orders the respondent to disclosure documents and/or information to the applicant) had been granted in Fetch.ai Ltd v Persons Unknown [2021] EWHC 2254 (Comm), where the defendants had allegedly hacked the respondents’ accounts on Binance and misappropriated the cryptocurrencies stored on those accounts, causing the applicants a loss of over $2.6 million.

Cases similar to Ion Sciences vs Persons Unknown and Others are particularly important in the context of cryptoasset recoveries, as they provide precedence to compel cryptocurrency exchanges to reveal information on their users. Following the introduction of licensing regimes over such exchanges, including the imposition of money laundering, KYC, organizational and governance requirements thereon, such parties are now under an obligation to maintain information which is necessary to identify the perpetrators of offenses. Thereafter, cryptoassets can be traced in fiat currencies and the objects on which they have been spent; property, houses, expensive jewelry and so on (Craggs et al., 2022). Nonetheless, such orders of disclosure will only be awarded on strict, specific grounds, including in circumstances where the victims of fraud will otherwise be unable to verify the identity of potential perpetrators and seek relief against them. In the same spirit, the readiness of the courts to find that there is a constructive trust (as was the case in Jones v Persons Unknown) where stolen cryptoassets are held in accounts with licensed cryptocurrency exchanges, provides an important gateway for claimants to recover their funds. Overall, the use of the blockchain inevitably raises new legal challenges, as the existing body of law needs to be applied and interpreted in light of new and evolving criminal activity and disputes with unprecedented characteristics. The role of the judiciary in this regard is crucial.

# 18.6  CONCLUSION

The blockchain and cryptoassets are important innovations with revolutionary potential. Since 2008 and the publication of the seminal paper of Satoshi Nakamoto on the establishment of a peer-to-peer electronic cash system (Nakamoto, 2008), until today, these elements have increasingly come to the forefront for their unique characteristics, cross-sectoral application and diverse use cases. Nonetheless, criminals have also taken advantage of the distinguishing features of blockchain and cryptoassets, to defraud investors in the context of ICOs, to engage in the illicit transfer of funds, ransomware, extortion and thefts, money laundering, financing of terrorism and international sanctions evasion. Faced with new and evolving forms of crime involving blockchain and cryptoassets, it is imperative to consider how such crime can be prevented, investigated and mitigated.

Concerns linking criminality with blockchain and cryptoassets must not be exacerbated. This chapter has highlighted ample examples whereby law enforcement agencies and the courts have been successful in combatting crime on the blockchain, whilst also highlighting the importance of proper understanding and education surrounding these technological developments. A legislative framework which is fit-for-purpose is also paramount, with the EU, national supervisory authorities and international standard-setting bodies playing their own key roles to ensure that cryptoassets do not operate in a lawless “wild west”.
