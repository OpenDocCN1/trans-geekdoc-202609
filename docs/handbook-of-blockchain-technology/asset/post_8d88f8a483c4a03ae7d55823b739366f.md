
The vendor market is dynamic and the technology is still evolving in terms of, for example, scalability and interoperability (Cunha et al., 2021). However, defending against rising costs of transactions on a specific blockchain is also an argument to support multiple providers simultaneously. An approach followed by some is to code for the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM). This Turing-complete environment is the backbone of the Ethereum network, responsible for executing all operations and transactions. Several other blockchains are EVM-compatible, meaning that developers can write and deploy the same smart contracts across them without making significant changes. Further, the smart contracts can interact with other EVM-compatible blockchains through the EVM, enabling the creation of multi-chain dApps (Leal, 2023).

The Digital Container Shipping Association (DCSA), which creates “vendor-neutral and technology-agnostic standards for the industry” (DCSA, 2023a), addresses other interoperability concerns. On July 12, 2022, DCSA began the final phase of an eBL platform interoperability proof of concept (DCSA, 2022).

As with many application areas of blockchain technology, there are regulatory and legal implications to consider in shipment transportation. According to Vlačić and Čerklć (2021), international legislation and maritime regulations recognize B/L as private documents for sea transport. Consequently, parties can exchange them in any agreed format or style. Additional discussion regarding the use of electronic BLs is available in the International Chamber of Commerce’s “The Legal Status of Electronic Bills of Lading” (Stephen, 2018). A key takeaway is the importance of countries implementing the United Nations Commission on International Trade Law’s (UNCITRAL) Model Law on Electronic Transferable Records (MLETR) (UNCITRAL, 2017). Additionally, it is important to comply with the International Group of Protection & Indemnity (P&I) Clubs’ requirements to ensure liability coverage (IGP&I, 2023).

For road hauling, the Institut du Droit International des Transports (IDIT) is a reference regarding the CMR convention and transportation law (IDIT, 2023). Further reading and FAQs are available from the International Road Transport Union (IRU) (IRU, n.d.).

## 15.6 FUTURE TRENDS AND OUTLOOK

Future-oriented studies can provide valuable insights into the role of blockchain from the companies’ perspective. A publication presented by Bathke et al. (2022) reveals that transportation companies expect that blockchain will be able to provide “a complete, transparent, and reliable data chain amongst all parties in maritime container transportation by 2035” and be one of the most impactful in their sector (among other projections including drones, autonomous vessels, or alternative propulsions). Interestingly, their work showed that blockchain desirability is higher among practitioners compared to academics and politicians, which is aligned with the significant blockchain investments from multiple participating companies. It is stated that “experts with more knowledge in the shipping business assessed higher desirability for a transparent marine supply chain by blockchain” (Bathke et al., 2022), putting technology in the “low-hanging fruit” category in their future. Nevertheless, the findings also highlight that blockchain success will depend on multiple stakeholders’ participation, and the added value is still unclear in the academic literature. In an era of massive energy transformations, blockchain and smart contracts can assist in the creation of more efficient business models for green energy transportation (Philipp, 2020).

Surprisingly, most blockchain projects focus on document management, diverging from the most popular field of blockchain adoption. Many practical projects can be compiled into three main conceptual areas: document workflow management, financial processes, and device connectivity. However, having clear interlinkages, none of the reviewed projects consider all three areas at once (Tsiulin et al., 2020).

IoT devices, such as humidity, temperature, or location-based sensors, can easily be integrated with blockchains, increasing the range of applications and possibilities. For example, these sensors can equip containers with certain types of products to monitor their transportation conditions or improve traceability. Walmart, together with IBM, tested a service to monitor products while they were being transported between origin and destination, with significant improvements in tracing food origins from several days to a few minutes (Kshetri, 2018). This is particularly relevant in food chains to detect the origin of contaminated products. Nevertheless, sensor calibration and ensuring the reliability of data entering the blockchain are critical. We will certainly need more advanced data governance methodologies to support blockchain adoption, especially as it is tough to increase technological trust, but extremely easy to destroy it with poor implementations.

Market opportunities for financial institutions are emerging. The automatic calculations made by the DL Freight system, as reported by Vitasek et al. (2022), allowed Walmart Canada to realize that other related financial services could also be automated, including invoice payments and carrier financing. This allows for a much more efficient management of working capital and enables financial institutions to provide financing at any stage of the supply chain. Additionally, the DL Freight system, originally designed to diminish invoice disputes, also enabled all partners to be more efficient. The data collected and stored could be used to optimize fuel consumption, route planning, and time of travel, with clear environmental advantages and efficient fleet management.

The literature seems divided about the impact of blockchain on the environment. On the one hand, energy consumption is seen as one of the most obvious barriers to blockchain’s future, although this has been mitigated with the development and adoption of lightweight consensus mechanisms such as PoS. On the other hand, blockchain enables environmentally-friendly solutions that would be hard to implement otherwise. Since sustainability is no longer an option for the shipment transportation industry, more quantitative studies are necessary to assess the trade-off between blockchain and responsible use of technology. This need is not exclusive to blockchain and extends to other disruptive trends like artificial intelligence and autonomous driving.

The emergence of central bank digital currencies (Cunha et al., 2021) may contribute to integrating traceable financial flows in transportation and creating new regulations. Additionally, blockchain can be adapted to in-port processes (Tsiulin et al., 2020), requiring a more integrated approach for the future of transportation. Shifting from a transparent shipping transportation vision to a responsible one will require end-to-end data integration of physical, digital, and financial flows. The major challenges in this future line of research include the cross-border interoperability of digital currencies.

Blockchain in the logistics market is projected to grow from $3.3 billion in 2020 to $1,620 billion by 2028 (Verified Market Research, 2022). According to Gyan Consulting, this growth will require standardization and interoperability, as the increasing number of blockchain-based solutions in the logistics sector will need to communicate seamlessly with one another. Such interoperable solutions will require common standards and protocols that are still to be developed (Gyan Consulting, 2023). For example, WAVE, a provider of eBL solutions, is aware of the importance of including interoperability in their offer, suggesting that “closed” platforms will have greater difficulties imposing their solutions in this market (DCSA, 2023b). As more blockchain-based shipment transportation platforms become available, we can expect to gradually increase cross-chain and multichain network research.

## 15.7 CONCLUSION

In this chapter, we provided an overview of the use of blockchain in shipment transportation. Besides providing some background concepts on how both the technology and shipment transportation work, the chapter also describes existing use cases and real-world examples, including projects with successful and unsuccessful results. The chapter also discusses some of the main implementation challenges and considerations to keep in mind when adopting blockchain solutions and looks at the future trends that can already be anticipated.

Regarding the real-world examples and case studies, they involved relevant ports, such as the Port of Sines, Port of Rotterdam, Port of Antwerp, Abu Dhabi Ports, Singapore, and the Port of Koper, as well as high-tech companies like Microsoft, IBM, and Hyundai, certification entities like Lloyd’s Register, and global logistics companies like Maersk and COSCO. They were mostly related to paperless shipping processes, freight tracking, secure data sharing, payments and finance, certification and standards development, insurance, and safety.

While most of these projects had positive results, some failures were observed, revealing that the challenges are not merely technological ones but are also related to other aspects, such as market readiness, collaborative networks, and standardization.

Most of the challenges identified are also the future trends in development and research directions in blockchain solutions for shipment transportation: scalability and performance, security and privacy, integration and interoperability, and standardization and protocols. All of these are very important, but standardization and protocols are key, given the predicted growth of blockchain-based solutions within the current decade. The absence of such features could prevent the foreseen market outlook.

In the near future, we anticipate significant contributions from blockchain and smart contracts in developing more efficient business models for green energy transportation, and increased seamless integration with IoT devices to enhance traceability and monitoring. Moreover, we expect substantial progress in cross-chain and multichain network research. More advanced data governance methodologies are likely to be developed to support blockchain adoption and capitalize on its technological trust as it is being achieved. This progress will also pave the way for the automation of payments, creating new business opportunities for financial institutions.

It will be exciting to watch how digital ledger implementations evolve in shipment transportation, a sector of the economy with particularities due to the impact on societal goals and the delicate balance of power between the various stakeholders dispersed in different areas of the globe.
