# 4.2.1　Defining Art NFTs

The emergence of distributed ledger technologies has the potential to serve as a catalyst for revitalizing the arts by empowering artists with new tools for ensuring the protection of their IP and receiving remuneration for their creative content. Unfortunately, real wages have stagnated—and even declined substantially, relative to non-artists—among artists in the United States (Makridis, 2023) and Europe (European Commission, 2020). In short, artists and workers in cultural sectors more broadly, have faced increased financial fragility.

Blockchain technology has been increasingly adopted in the art industry in recent years, providing artists, collectors, and organizations with a new way to create, share, and monetize their work. Traditional intermediaries, such as record labels and museums, have often required artists to forfeit their IP over the created content in exchange for the promise of heightened visibility and access. While sometimes the exchange is profitable, many artists have felt that they have been taken advantage of by traditional contracts and institutions.

NFTs, in contrast, provide artists with the opportunity to go directly to consumers by placing their content on the blockchain for anyone to see and potentially purchase. Moreover, since the record of the NFT is immutable, future trades can generate revenues for the creator in perpetuity. NFTs are generally a mix of two characteristics (Thorn et al., 2022):

+   - A digital token, often governed by Ethereum’s ERC-721 standard, holds a unique cryptographic address and contains metadata stored on a blockchain. The metadata is not the image or audio clip, but rather a link to a file that often exists off-chain.²
+   - A license is issued to the owner of the NFT by the creator of the NFT, granting the owner the right to display and/or possibly commercialize the file to which the NFT points.
