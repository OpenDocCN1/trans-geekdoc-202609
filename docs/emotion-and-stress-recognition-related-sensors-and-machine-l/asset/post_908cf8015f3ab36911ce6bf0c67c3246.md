## 3.4. Feature Extraction

Most systems use feature extraction methods in the time, frequency, time-frequency, or space domains. A small percentage of works evaluate the functional connectivity (or differences) in the observed activity between brain regions when emotions are evoked. Features with non-redundant information combined from different domains yield better classification results. However, it is still unclear whether features work better alone or in combination with each other, or what type of features are more relevant for emotion recognition.

In our review, we found that [some studies] addressed these issues through the development of feature extraction algorithms that outperform the frequency bands and extract as much information as possible from brain signals. We believe that further developments should be connected to a comprehensive understanding of the brain's neurophysiology.

The performance of all systems was evaluated using accuracy, except for two systems in which one used [area] under the curve (AUC), and the other presented an F1 measure. Unfortunately, EEG datasets are usually unbalanced, with one or two labeled emotions more than the others, which is somewhat problematic for this approach. This situation could lead to biased classifications. Moreover, EEG datasets are typically unbalanced, and performance measures should be calculated to contextualize their outcomes. Arousal and valence have the highest usage percentages (25.8%). On the other hand, 16.1% categorized with three classes: positive, negative, and neutral. Then, 9.7% classified three discrete emotions like sadness, love, and anger. Moreover, 6.5% ranked valence as two classes (positive and negative), four emotions (happy, sad, fear, and relaxed), one discrete emotion (disgust), or emotions located in one of four quadrants of the VA space (high-valence arousal, high-valence arousal, low-valence arousal, and low-valence low-arousal).

Classifier performance should be evaluated, taking into account that accuracy would be inversely proportional to the number of detected emotions. In other words, classification accuracy should be higher than a random classification process (equal for each class). Thus, as classification class increases, a random classification process would yield a lower accuracy. For instance, a two-class random classification process would be 50% accurate. Likewise, three classes would imply a 33% classification accuracy for a random classification process, and so on. Therefore, such accuracy metrics should provide the classification performance benchmark for our evaluations.

Although the results of the performance of the systems depend on many factors, it is possible to find some relationship between the number of classes, the type of emotions classified, and the accuracy obtained (Figure 12). The best results obtained with two classes, as discrete emotions or positive negative values in a dimensional space. The second-best value is found for the recognition of one discrete emotion like dislike or disgust. The result that the classification of one emotion does not obtain the best performance value could be explained by the fact that in [this] review, we observed that negative emotions are more challenging to classify and tend to yield smaller performance values.

Comparing approaches and results obtained through BCI-based systems is complex. This is because each system uses diverse experimental methods for emotion elic[itation], protocols to detect EEG signals, datasets, extraction and selection of features, classification algorithms, and generally speaking, each implementation has different settings. Ideally, systems should be tested under similar conditions, but [the] scenario is not yet available. However, we can perform a comprehensive analysis to extract trends, bearing in mind such limitations.
