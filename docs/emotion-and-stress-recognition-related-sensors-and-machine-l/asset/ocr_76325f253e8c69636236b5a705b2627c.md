The average accuracy rates for valence and arousal were 66.05% and 82.46%, respectively. We note here that several studies [7,21–23] have targeted the PSD of EEG data as the input features and performed emotion classification by SVM. Other machine learning techniques, such as Bayes, K-NN, LDA, and ANN, have been applied in other studies [9,24–26].

Other areas for scalp-based EEG classification include those in [15,27], which look to develop wearable headband solutions. For monitoring purposes, these designs suffer in conditions such as a warm climate; it might be uncomfortable to wear headband for a long duration due to sweating. Moreover, the sweat could affect the electrode impedance, resulting in noisy and inaccurate measurements.

# 2.2. In-Ear EEG Development

Originally, an in-ear EEG, which is an EEG recording introduced by Cooley et al. in 2012, was demonstrated to have wearable characteristics that could potentially fulfill monitoring requirements [28]. It is small and could work around the ears, and is similar to earbuds or hand-free devices. Since then, research has focused on areas such as materials; system design, especially in terms of practicality; and the verification of signal quality [17,27–29]. For example, Goverdosky et al. [30] suggested a new prototype called EEG that consists of a viscoelastic substrate foam inlay and conductive cloth electrodes to ensure compliance with the ear canal for motion artifacts. Kulkarni et al. [27] designed a soft and foldable device that can capture the EEG from different outer surfaces of the ear and the epidermal features with frital layers. Recent work by Kappel et al. [31] developed an in-ear EEG with a earpiece, which required customized molding to fit individual ears. The prototype showed good signal quality and the potential for long-term EEG monitoring.

# 2.3. In-Ear EEG for Control

In the field of brain-computer interfaces, EEG signals created through muscle activity, such as eye blinks or other facial expressions, have been studied as means for controlling emotion. For in-interfaces, major works in the area include; Matthias et al. [32] reported an in-ear headset based on a haurosky EEG sensor. The prototype utilizes eye winking and ear wigging for explicit control of the function of a smartphone. Additionally, in 2017, Matthias et al. [33] placed multiple electrodes onto a earplug to detect 25 facial expressions and head gestures. Five gestures could be detected with accuracy above 90%, and 14 with accuracy above 50%. # 2.4. In-Ear EEG for Medical and Healthcare Applications

Medical and healthcare applications have been a major theme for in-ear EEG research, especially for monitoring purposes [34]. Sleep has been particularly of interest [35,36]. For example, Nguyen et al. [35] proposed a dual channel EEG in the form of an earplug that showed a stable stage classification with accuracy 99+. In terms of emotion monitoring, which is closely related to this work, previous work [17,37] showed that an in-ear EEG measured was similar to T7 and T8 on the 10-20 system [18]. Moreover, one of the previous works also showed that T7 and T8 provided some informative data for emotion classification [7]. Results suggest that an in-ear EEG has the potential to classify emotions, which our work was to investigate.
