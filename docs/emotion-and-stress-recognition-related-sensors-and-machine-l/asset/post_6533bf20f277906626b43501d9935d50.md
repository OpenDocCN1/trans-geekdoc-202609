The module which initiates the recording start also listens to its own send “start” message, and starts recording after the message returns back to itself to prevent time leading of the initiator module. Each module further sends a roundtrip message to itself to measure the network latency at the beginning of each recording session. The round trip times can be seen in Table 2. Thus, we can assume that the average delay or desynchronization is within an acceptable range. Additionally, the synchronicity can be improved by taking the individual delays into account and shifting the timestamps after recording in the post-processing step. This is not done in the raw data.

# 3. Results

In the following results, the validation of the induction via questionnaires and via subjective feedback, as the data annotations are presented.

In total, three subjects were excluded from the analysis: two subjects from the first sample because of missing bipolar data (ID-04) and an absent logger data (ID-40) as well as one subject from the second sample because of missing sequences due to a technical error (Under and Frustration for ID-90). Because ID-90 represents the second measurement of a participant from the second sample, the first and third measurement of that subject (ID-80 and ID-100) were also excluded from the analysis. Consequently, the final datasetMAC contains 95 recordings from 57 subjects, presented for the following groups and subgroups:

- Group A includes 38 subjects from the first sample who consists of 38 recordings.
- Group B includes 19 subjects from the second sample who underwent different measurements. It consists of 57 recording sessions.
- Group C includes three subgroups: Group B1, Group B2, and Group B3 consisting of 19 recording sessions each representing the first, second and third measurement time of the 19 subjects, respectively.

While both groups underwent exactly the same experiment, they slightly differ in one modality acquisition: The EMG group of A includes muscle trapezius activity measurements (thus, without facial electrodes, which allows a better analysis of features from the video data). As Group B, the EMG data include activity measurements of three muscles: muscle trapezius, muscle musculator and muscle cygmaticus. In the results, Group A, Group B, and Group C are separately analyzed and presented.

The three questionnaires TeIQ-SF, ERQ, and TIPI from all the participants prior to the experiment are evaluated for Group A, Group B1, Group B2, and Group B3. For all questionnaires items, the possible score range varies between 1 (minimum) and 7 (maximum).

In Figure 6 the four dimensions of TeIQ-SF, consisting of Well-Being, Self-Control, and Sociability, are presented for the different groups. The mean values vary between 5.61 and 5.82 for the Well-Being, between 5.04 and 5.26 for the Self-Control factor, between 4.80 and 5.06 for the Emotionality factor and between 5.02 and 5.34 for the Sociability factor. The standard deviations (SD) range between 0.55 and 1.28 for all groups and factors. The first three factors of Well-Being, Self-Control and Emotionality have a small decreasing tendency within Group B1, Group B2 and Group B3, with the highest value obtained for Group B1. Only for Sociability the mean value of Group B3 slightly increased to the value of Group B2. Nevertheless, in total the mean values of all factors present a homogenous distribution within all the groups, showing mean deviations and, thus, stable results.

## 3.3. Validation via Subjective Feedback

Following, the evaluations of the participants are presented for Group A, Group B1, Group B2, and Group B3. They include the SAM Ratings and the evaluation of the dataset. The evaluation of subjective feedback is necessary to provide ground truth and validation of the dataset. The Free Speech data are not analyzed here but are part of the dataset.

## 3.3.1. SAM Ratings

The SAM Ratings were collected from every subject after each sequence. With the help of the three dimensions, Valence, Arousal, and Dominance, the induction of the different sequence levels are evaluated. First, the evaluation of the ratings of Group A is presented, then, the ratings of Group B are separately analyzed (Group B1, Group B2, and Group B3). Finally, repeated measures ANOVA and post-hoc corrections were performed to examine the significance of the variations.

In Figure 8, the SAM Ratings for Group A are presented. The highest valence values are found for Easy (7.32) and Interest (6.92) and Overload (6.84), while the lowest values are found for Overload (5.13) and Frustration (5.68). On the other sequences, the highest Arousal was perceived for these two later sequences, Overload (5.18) and Frustration (4.37), while the lowest Arousal was registered for Underload (2.11) and Easy (2.39). As the Dominance values, the highest mean values were also obtained for these two sequences, Overload and Easy (7.03 each), while the lowest values were registered for Overload (3.66) and Frustration (4.26).

![](img/601340ca744b738277eef45ff127492a_279_0.png)

Figure 8. SAM Ratings of Group A for all sequences with mean Valence, Arousal, and Dominance.

Figures 9–11 illustrate the SAM Ratings for the first (Group B1), second (Group B2), and third (Group B3) measurement time of Group B, respectively. Additionally, here the mean SAM Ratings values are consistent with each other for all measurement times showing temporal stability in the subjective evaluation. Additionally, compared to the rating results of the one-measurement group (Group A) illustrated in Figure 8, the mean Valence, Arousal, and Dominance values show similar rating trends.

In summary, the SAM Ratings show overall stable course with highest Valence, lowest Arousal, and highest Dominance values for the Easy and Underload sequences with lowest Valence, highest Arousal and lowest Dominance values for the Overload and Frustration sequences.
