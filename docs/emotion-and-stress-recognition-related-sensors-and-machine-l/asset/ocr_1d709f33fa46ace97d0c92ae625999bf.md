Smart shoes, in particular insoles, have been used in a variety of scenarios, such as to analyse gait [22], identify poses [23], calculate walking speeds [24], determine the ground surface [25] and recognising foot tapping gestures for an interaction control [26]. These smart shoes provide an unobtrusive way of collecting data. However, insole-based tracking has not yet been introduced to stress.

Motivated by the fact that feet and legs carry essential information about a person’s stress level [12,27] and the widespread availability of shoes, we propose PressStress. Our prototype encapsulates a smart shoe system that incorporates a pressure-sensitive insole based on force-sensitive resistor (FSR) technology and an inertial measurement unit (IMU). We drive a machine learning approach to detect acute stress situations in sitting postures, as sedentary office work. To scientifically validate this, we followed the research design process [28] by Constructing Validity—Study 1 developing a machine learning model based on the data of 23 participants, evidencing Empirical Replicability—Study 2 with 11 participants showing distinguishability between stressed and relaxed conditions and finally, for External Validity—Study 3 showing the generability in terms of robustness of our model for office workers during a typical 8 work day with 10 participants. In summary, we continue:

+   * a novel way to discriminate acute stress and relaxation by distinct foot movements and posture characteristics.
* ten mathematical features to train machine learning models,
* and design implications for applications in ubiquitous computing.





The most common approach in stress sensing is interpreting physiological responses, such as EDA [29], heart rate [30] (HR), HRV [31], pupil dilation [32], skin temperature [33,34] (ST) or EEG [8,25]. In addition, muscle activity, as microvations [9] and muscle tension [10,36], are affected by stress. The Autonomous Nervous System (SNS) of the Autonomous Nervous System uncommonly controls these vital signs and are considered reliable sources [37]. Prior work demonstrated EDA as literally related to and widely used in the context of stress sensing [38–40]. More specifically, EDA is used to measure stress in applications, such as measuring the call of after skin agents [39] and the discrimination of stress from cognitive load [40]. In addition, many laboratory and field studies, EDA is considered one of the gold-standard methods to sense stress [41]. As the SNS controls EDA, it is regarded as a reliable physiological sign for acute stress [42].

Furthermore, prior work revealed that stress related to cognitive load has an impact on HRV [6,8], in particular it has been observed during high intensity tasks [44]. Electrocardiography (ECG) and Photoplethysmogram (PPG) [46,47] are the earliest technologies used to measure HR and HRV. High power consumption and consumer price rates are the major draw backs of these technologies. Meanwhile, various studies, such as BioWatch [48], SensClass [49], SeismToTracker [50], investigated smart devices capable of state-of-the-art technologies [51], it is susceptible to unwanted motion artifacts and thus not reliable in resting states, such as sleeping, standing or sitting. In summary, wearable sensing using wristbands, neck clips, and headbands are unaffected by thickness and skin sensor.

Another option is contact-free sensing of physiological data [52], such as thermal cameras [53,54] or Doppler radar [55]. As cameras are typically expensive, webcams are a low-cost alternative to measuring HR and HRV from the human face [17,18]. Inspired by previous studies, COGCAM [16] measured cognitive stress with digital cameras, placed 3m away from the user.
