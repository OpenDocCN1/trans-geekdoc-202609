Hess et al. measured the intensity of emotional facial expression for the emotions of happiness, sadness, anger, and disgust, by two men and women [83]. The expressions depicted had six levels of intensity. The accuracy achieved was 85.3% (anger), 79.3% (disgust), 97.9% (sadness), and 91.8% (happiness). The method included clustering and pre-processing the color into high-quality grayscale images. This study was conducted using a set of 5 pre-defined intensity of expressions of 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%. The authors concluded that the study providing evidence that the intensity of emotion varied linearly with the plasticity of expressions.

Biele et al. performed an experiment where the intensity of happiness and anger were more intense than the extreme happy emotion. Anger was noted to be more intense than the static images (animations) [23]. Also, the results for dynamic images were higher than the static images. Gender differences also had an impact on the results, e.g., a difference in intensity was observed for anger in both males and among females. They used the 2 (Subject Sex) - 2 (Actor Sex) - 2 (Emotion) - 2 (Stilli Dynamics) ANOVA method for finding the intensity of emotions. A need for a new methodology for measuring the intensity of emotions to have a better insight into how men and women processed different emotional information was stressed upon this work.

Another work performed the experiment by measuring the intensity of the emotions [84]. The basis of analysis entirely relied on the fact that facial expressions conveyed the state of mind. Researchers have argued that neuromotor programs of facial expression patterns might be able to serve as the building blocks of emotion communication [84–87].

The interaction pattern was observed behaviorally for emotion intensity ratings, neutrally for functional resonance imaging activation in the amygdala (roughly almond-shaped mass of gray matter inside each cerebral hemisphere, involved with the experiencing of emotions), as well as fusiform and medial prefrontal cortex. However, this behavior was observed only for mid-intensity emotions.

Delannoy's experiments were based on the image-based approach for the three ranks of intensities, low-mid-high [88]. They performed one-vs SVMs for training classifiers. They used only 68 images from 10 subjects from the CK dataset and used ten-fold crossvalidation. However, the drawback in their approach was that multiclass classifier assume that the rankings of intensities were independent of each other. Hence the relation between labels was not well employed for performance enhancement. Furthermore, with the classification of expressions in three intensity ranges, the AU must be extracted from the image sequence as a feature representation.

Several researchers performed experiments to conduct more accurate emotion intensity estimation results [78,89–91]. The authors employed the training data with known intensity labels for these works. These intensity labels were categorized into two sections, discrete representations [88,89], and continuous value representations [92]. Once the labels were employed, the intensity degree was predicted and validated more accurately. The drawback of these approaches was that multiple or an image sequence were required to estimate the intensity of emotion and and. Also, sufficient images were required for estimation in a sequence-based approach.

Littleword et al. performed by conducting estimation of testing intensity for facial expressions using SVMs [93]. The authors used to the SVM hyperplanes to estimate the rankings of emotion intensities. Chang et al. performed a manifold learning approach for recognizing facial expressions and used the distances of the manifold to determine the intensity of the emotions [94]. However, the distances boundaries in the feature space may not necessarily reflect neutral/strong as an expression and, concluded inaccurate results.

Rudovic et al. performed a method using intrinsic technique of multidimensional fusion could. An ordinal manifold method developed the topology. The topology was used for the (H-CORF) Hidden Conditional Ordinal Random Field. Later, it was used for dynamic regression [95]. This was done to constrain H-CORF parameters to lie on the ordinal manifold. The resulting model aligned synchronous recognition and intensity estimation of facial expressions of emotions. This method was used for both posed and spontaneous expressions. They also tested their model on databases such as BU-DFE, CK, and CK+.
