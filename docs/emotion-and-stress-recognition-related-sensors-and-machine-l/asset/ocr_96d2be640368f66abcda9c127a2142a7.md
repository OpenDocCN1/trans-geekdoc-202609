# 1.2. Sense of Presence

In addition to the objective features of the set-up, the experience of users in virtual environments can be measured by the concept of presence, understand subjective feeling of "being-there" [28]. A high degree of presence creates in the user sensation of physical presence and the illusion of interacting and reacting as if (s)he sure that one is not actually in the real world [29]. In the 2000s, the strong illusion of being in a place, spite of not actually there, was called "place illusion" (Pl), to avoid confusion that might be caused by the multiple meanings of the word "presence". More, as Pl relates to how the world is perceived, and the correlation of movements and concomitant changes in the images that form perception, "plausibility illusion" (Pl) relates to what is perceived, in a correlation of events not directly caused by the participant [13]. Pl is determined by the extent to which a system produces events directly relate to the participant, and the overall credibility of the scenario being depicted in comparison with viewer expectations, for example, when an experimental participant is provoked into giving a quick, natural and automatic reply to a question posed by an.

Although presence plays a role in VR experiences, there is limited understanding of what affect presence in virtual environments. However, there is consensus that experception and interactivity factors affect presence. Some have shown that levels of interactivity and immersion, which are directly related to the experimental set-up, provoke particular emotions [30–32]. As the interactor factors, which are defined by the content displayed, participants will perceive higher presence if they feel emotionally affected; for previous studies have found a strong correlation between arousal and presence [33]. Recent research has also analysed presence in specific contexts and suggested that, for example, in social environments, it is enhanced when the VR elicit genuine cognitive, emotional and behavioural responses, and when participants create their own narratives about events [34]. On the other hand, presence increases when users experience physical problems, such as cybersecurity [35].

# 1.3. Virtual Reality in Human Behaviour

VR is, thus as a proposed tool to simulate complex, real situations and environments, offering researchers unprecedented opportunities to investigate human behaviour in closely devices designed in laboratory conditions [33]. There are now many researchers in the field, who have published many studies, so strong, interdisciplinary community exists [2].

Education and training is one field where VR has much used. Freina and Ott [36] show that VR can offer good travel-time experiences, for example, students can experience different historical periods. It can address physical inaccessibility, for example, students can explore the solar system in the first person. It can circumnavigate ethical problems, for example, students can "perform" surgery.
