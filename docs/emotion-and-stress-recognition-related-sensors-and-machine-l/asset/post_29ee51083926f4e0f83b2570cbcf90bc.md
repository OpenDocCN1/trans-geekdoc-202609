Typical feature extraction methods include wavelet transform [44], spectral power features [45], higher order crossings [46], short-time Fourier transform [47], asymmetry index [48] and/or statistical features [49], e.g., mean, standard deviation, variance, quadratic mean, skewness, and overpower. Finally, a classification method is used to discriminate an emotional state from the features. Support Vector Machines (SVM) [47,48,50,51], nearest neighbour classification [45,50], Naive Bayes or Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) [52] are examples of existent list of methods that were applied in this context.

Most EEG-based emotion recognition uses Russell's two dimensional bipolar emotional model to label and represent emotional states, which is based on activation and arousal [53]. This representation relies on the fact that these two variables account for the major proportion of variance in affect scales. In such models, each emotion is found as a combination of values for valence and arousal, feeling meaningfully around the perimeter of the space. The valence represents whether the emotion corresponds to a positive or a negative feeling; and arousal refers to the level of excitement. The valence/arousal representation was extended to a 3D space in [54], by also considering whether the subject feels in control of the situation (dominance).

The intensive work in emotion recognition has been supported by the existence of a number of public databases. The first large database is DEAP, which is presented in [33]. DEAP contains EEG and physiological scales of 32 people who were recorded as each watched 40-minute clips of music videos. These were stored with the levels of arousal, valence/like, dominance, and familiarity reported by the subjects. The database also contains frontal face video for 22 of the participants. Methods and results are presented for single-trial classification of arousal, valence, and like/like ratings using modalities of the EEG, physiological scales, and numerical content analysis. EEG signals were recorded by a BiosemiActive system. Despite its recent publication, it has been used extensively in the affect recognition field, with many different methods (e.g., [55-57]).

Another large database is presented in [34]. In this case, the repository contains data for 27 people, video data was provided for all participants, from six different cameras. The database also contains eye gaze information, as well as other physiological scales (including EEG). Data are stored with the emotional state reported by the subject, both using emotional keywords and on a scale of arousal, valence and dominance. EEG signals were recorded using AgCl electrodes placed according to the international 10-20 system (32 channels).

A more recent dataset of similar characteristics with regard to the EEG data has been provided in [35]. Under DREAMER contains EEG data from 23 participants who watched 18 music videos. The main difference with the previous two databases referred to the type of equipment that was used. In DREAMER, 128 Hz EEG signals were recorded using an Emotic POC, a device that offers a considerably better precision than the Biosemi Active Two. It summarizes the statistics of these three databases.

Major problematic issues that hindered the development of practical applications use EEG signals are related to time, resolution, and complexity of setting up experimental protocols that remain real-world activities [36]. This has motivated a work that focuses on mobile/low cost devices ([55,58,59]). Although these devices may be less accurate at the signal acquisition phase, they may offer a comparative performance at detecting emotional changes in the subject. This has led others to develop their own datasets to validate their results in specific contexts using low cost devices [55,59]. However, these datasets are not made public and are hence unusable in other research works.

The resulting features were used to plot a 2-D (two dimensional) map after a transformation using t-Distributed Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) [60]. t-SNE is an unsupervised dimensionality reduction method that is well suited for the visualization of high-dimensional data. t-SNE is capable of capturing and preserving much of the topological structure of high-dimensional data, while also revealing global structure such as the presence of clusters at several scales [60]. We reduced the data to two dimensions, so that we could easily display and analyze it using a scatterplot.

Figure 1 shows the result produced by t-SNE on the three databases used. We represent each subject using a different color, to observe that EEG samples from the same subject are topologically close to each other.

Although it seems that the topological structure of the maps presented in Figure 1 is not the best for the construction of inter-subject models, other previous works have obtained positive results when applying subject-independent models. For example, the effect recognition reported in [35] refer to accuracy of 0.62 in valence and arousal, using a SVM with a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel. However, they used an imbalanced dataset, with a proportion of 56–44% in arousal and 61–39% in valence. It is possible that the positive accuracy reported is in part due to this fact, rather than to the existence of emotion-evoked specific EEG patterns that are represented by multiple subjects.

For the purpose of this work, we performed feature extraction. First, we calculated Power Spectral Density (PSD) using Welch's method with a Hamming window. The spectral power was measured over $\theta$ (4–8 Hz), $\alpha$ (8–12 Hz), $\beta$ (12–30 Hz) and $\gamma$ (>30 Hz) from all electrodes. In addition, we computed the spectral power between the left and right hemisphere to measure possible asymmetry.
