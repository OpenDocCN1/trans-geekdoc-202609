On the other hand, the level of interactivity of 3D scenarios offer can be very useful in research, since they display much more realistic and interactive environments, facilitating decision-making and the analysis of dynamics. Taking some examples, Takac et al. [137] analysed the anxiety felt by speakers when faced by large audiences, Lin et al. [113] analysed the stress felt by individuals in a building on fire and Kisser et al. [130] analysed arousal in an exposure to high height.

Immersive VR can be a powerful tool to analyse human behaviour in controlled laboratory environments, but we do not know the level of VR validity needed to allow the extrapolation to the result of the insights gained in terms of physiological and behavioural responses. Indeed, 83% of the papers did not present any validity, and only 3 provided a direct comparison between the VR scene and the physical environment. Gorini et al. [109] analysed anxiety through HRV and DEA with virtual and real world, Higuera-Trujillo et al. [64] analysed pleasantness through DEA responses in a 3D, 360° real retail store, and physical machine. Morales et al. [165] analysed and valence oscillations with HRV and EEG in VR. Other research analysed the influence of [121] and other VR features. Thus, VR validity is still one topic that needs to be more actively addressed. Understanding and isolating the intrinsic dynamics of VR will be key in future years for the analysed of insights obtained using HMDs.

Finally, the results suggest that VR will play a central role in the affective computing field. The research has increased its complexity and maturity during the last two decades, and it is likely to continue growing the next years. First, future research should extend the analysis of the physiological dynamics as emotion in VR, which could allow a level of understanding as high as we have today using 2D pictures. Subsequently, VR might open up many research opportunities that would be beneficial to use with non-immersive systems. In particular, the inclusion of the dominance dimension, which is very closely related to the users' control of the environment, and impacts on sense of security. Moreover, the physical dimension is a crucial factor in the understanding of the emotional dynamics of human beings. The future inclusion of responsive, realistic avatars will help increase the understanding of emotions evoked during situations, and the associated physiological processes, in controlled.

# 5. Conclusions

This work analysed the current state-of-the-art in implicit measure-based emotion recognition using HMDs, and by providing a systematic and aggregated analysis that can guide future research. After two decades of literature analysing emotions using HMDs in combination with implicit measures, mostly understood through the physiological arousal responses of the ANS, in recent years, an inflection point has been reached. The number of papers published is increasingly exponentially, and more emotions are being analysed, including valence-related matters, more complex signal processing procedures are increasingly performed, and machine-learning algorithms are being newly applied to develop automatic emotion recognition systems. The results suggest that VR might revolutionise emotion elicitation methods in laboratory environments in the next decade, and impact affective computing research, in many areas, opening new opportunities for the scientific community. However, more research is needed to the understanding of emotion dynamics in immersive VR and, its validity in direct comparisons between simulated and real environments.
