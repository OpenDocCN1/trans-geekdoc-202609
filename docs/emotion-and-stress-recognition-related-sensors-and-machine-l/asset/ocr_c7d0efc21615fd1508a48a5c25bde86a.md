```
$I(x, y) = \int p(x, y) \log \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)p(y)}dxdy,\quad (2)$
```

where $p(x)$ and $p(y)$ are the marginal probability density functions of $x$ and $y$, respectively, and $p(x, y)$ is their joint probability function. If $x$ and $y$ are random variables and are statistically independent [58], mRMR maximizes $I(x, y)$ between each characteristic xi and the target vector $y$, and minimizes the average mutual information $I(x, y)$ between two characteristics.

Model frameworks can categorize classification algorithms [56,57]. The model' categories may be (1) generative-discriminative, (2) static-dynamic, (3) stable-unstable, and (4) regular [102–104].

There are two selection approaches for classifiers that work better under conditions in emotion recognition [56]. The first identifies the best classifier for a given BCI device. The second selects the best classifier for a set of features.

For synchronous BCIs, dynamic classifiers and ensemble combinations have shown better performances than SVMs. For asynchronous BCIs, the authors in this field have not determined an optimal classifier. However, it is that dynamic classifiers perform better than static classifiers [56] because they handle the identification of mental processes.

From the second approach, discriminative classifiers have been found to perform better than generative classifiers, principally in the presence of noise or users. Dynamic classifiers like SVM generally handle high dimensionality in the features. If there is a small training set, simple techniques like LDA classifiers may yield satisfactory results [58].
