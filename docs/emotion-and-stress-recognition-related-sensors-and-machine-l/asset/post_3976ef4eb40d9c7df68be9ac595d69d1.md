## 6. Discussion and Limitations

Overall, the sensor benchmarking worked well, both from a standardized laboratory study and data acquisition viewpoint, as well as from the data analysis perspective. The high correlations between the cardiovascular parameters HR and IBI were as expected because these parameters are simple to measure through a range of methodologies (electrical, optical).

The high correlations between the other ECG-derived measurements were a little more surprising because (1) ECG is measured through a multi-channel electric-based system, which is a complex procedure; (2) the use of contact electrodes of the BioHarness sensor (in contrast to the sticky electrodes of high-quality sensors) may cause (and thus ECG is measured at a very high frequency (at least 200 Hz)), which is technologically challenging for low-cost wearables.

For GSR, our experiment resulted in lower, but still reasonable similarities, which may be caused by factors like different measurement methods (sticky electrodes vs. plate electrode), and different placement of the sensors (hand palm vs. wrist).

From a general point of view, it is known that low-cost wearable sensors tend to be prone to producing datasets that further reduce data quality—even though we checked the appropriate positioning of the sensors before we started the exercise.

A particular issue with RP 1–2: as the results show, the measurements for these participants indicate low correlations for all physiological parameters. This may be due to problems with the contact between the electrodes and the skin, which may have been compromised by the participants' physical characteristics.

A vital part of the analysis is the visualisation of different levels: First, on the individual level, the data from different sensors measuring the same physiological parameter at the same time provide a direct comparison between the two-time series of interest. This enables reaching the sensors' measuring behaviour. Second, on the collective level, the consolidation of global metrics across all participants provides useful insights into the participants' individual behaviour (physical constitution, individual baseline of skin conductance, etc.).

These complementary visualizations enable a flexible method of interpretation. For instance, it allows starting the interpretation on the individual level on a particular pair of physiological parameters (e.g., HR, GSR) and the corresponding exploratory plot as shown in Figure 7b, then rolling-up the R² matrix together with the cross-correlation (Figure 7a) and comparing the result between participants. Furthermore, it allows checking whether a particular pair of participants has a functional relationship and that relationship is stable among other participants using the MIC.
