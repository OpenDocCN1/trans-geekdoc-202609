For a long time, most emotion recognition studies have focused on using facial expressions, and for continuous monitoring purposes, these approaches may not be the most suitable, as they may suffer from practical issues, such as ambient light and noise. For camera-based facial privacy is also a concern. Alternatively, physiological signals, such as galvanic skin response (GSR), electrocardiogram (EEG), skin temperature (ST), and electroencephalogram (EEG), which occur continuously and are harder to conceal. As emotions are thought to be related to activity in brain areas that direct, motivate behavior, and determine the significance of what is going on with us, EEG, which is the signal from fluctuations in the brain that are generated at the level of cellular membranes [6], has especially of interest.

Emotion classification has been shown to achieve high accuracy [1,7–16]. In reality, these conventional channel EEG headsets are not suitable for continuous monitoring due to their size and setup difficulty. The EEG recording device used for emotion monitoring should be small, take little time to set up, and be comfortable to wear.

For requirements, an in-ear EEG which an EEG recording device introduced by Looney et al. [2012] could be of interest. The potential benefits of using an in-ear type include that it does not obstruct the visual fact. It is also robust, as it is generally fixed inside the ears. And as it is similar to devices people use, such as earphones and earplugs, it is unlikely to encounter sweat, and is also user-friendly for setup and maintenance. Unlike scalp EEG, which may require some experienced assistants to help, in-ear EEG also has drawbacks. An in-ear EEG has much fewer electrodes and a much smaller area than the scalp EEG. So, its application is more convenient than that of the scalp EEG.

Our work was aimed at building an in-ear EEG device and evaluating it in terms of signal quality classification compared to those methods (i.e., T7 and T8 from the international 10-20 system [18]). The international 10-20 system is an internationally recognized system for labelling scalp locations for EEG measurement. The T7 position is located above the left ear, while T8 is positioned above the right ear. The prospect of an in-ear usage for emotion classification was investigated by experiments.

Experimental results and analysis are presented in Section 4. Significant findings of the results are discussed in Section 5. The conclusions are presented in Section 6.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1. Scalp-Based EEG Emotion Classification

Scalp-based emotion classification by multi-channel EEG has been an active field of research [1,7–9]. A review of some works have been found in [7]. The majority of the works have focused on signal processing techniques to improve accuracy. For example, Koestra et al. [19] presented methods for single trial classification using both EEG and peripheral physiological signals. The power spectral density (PSD) of EEG signals was used as the primary feature. A support vector machine (SVM) classifier was used to classify two levels of valence and two levels of arousal. EEG analysis results, and maximum classification rates of 55.7% and 67.0% were obtained for arousal and 58.8% and 76.0% for valence. Huang et al. [20] employed an asymmetric spatial pattern (ASP) technique to extract features for an EEG-based emotion recognition algorithm. The system employed k-nearest neighbor (K-NN), naive Bayes (NB), and support vector machine (SVM) methods.

The average accuracy rates for valence and arousal were 66.05% and 82.46%, respectively. We note here that several studies [7,21–23] have targeted the PSD of EEG data as the input features and performed emotion classification by SVM. Other machine learning techniques, such as Bayes, K-NN, LDA, and ANN, have been applied in other studies [9,24–26].

Other areas for scalp-based EEG classification include those in [15,27], which look to develop wearable headband solutions. For monitoring purposes, these designs suffer in conditions such as a warm climate; it might be uncomfortable to wear a headband for a long duration due to sweating. Moreover, the sweat could affect the electrode impedance, resulting in noisy and inaccurate measurements.

### 2.2. In-Ear EEG Development

Originally, an in-ear EEG, which is an EEG recording introduced by Cooley et al. in 2012, was demonstrated to have wearable characteristics that could potentially fulfill monitoring requirements [28]. It is small and could work around the ears, and is similar to earbuds or hands-free devices. Since then, research has focused on areas such as materials; system design, especially in terms of practicality; and the verification of signal quality [17,27–29]. For example, Goverdosky et al. [30] suggested a new prototype called EEG that consists of a viscoelastic substrate foam inlay and conductive cloth electrodes to ensure compliance with the ear canal for motion artifacts. Kulkarni et al. [27] designed a soft and foldable device that can capture the EEG from different outer surfaces of the ear and the epidermal features with fractal layers. Recent work by Kappel et al. [31] developed an in-ear EEG with a earpiece, which required customized molding to fit individual ears. The prototype showed good signal quality and the potential for long-term EEG monitoring.

### 2.3. In-Ear EEG for Control

In the field of brain-computer interfaces, EEG signals created through muscle activity, such as eye blinks or other facial expressions, have been studied as means for controlling emotion. For in-interfaces, major works in the area include; Matthias et al. [32] reported an in-ear headset based on a haurosky EEG sensor. The prototype utilizes eye winking and ear wigging for explicit control of the function of a smartphone. Additionally, in 2017, Matthias et al. [33] placed multiple electrodes onto an earplug to detect 25 facial expressions and head gestures. Five gestures could be detected with accuracy above 90%, and 14 with accuracy above 50%. ## 3. Materials and Methods

In this work, to achieve the goal of realizing an in-ear EEG, we looked to find answers to these questions:

- (1) What type of in-ear EEG should be used (physically, design-wise, and engineering-wise)?
- (2) What kind of signal quality would we be getting?
- (3) How good is it specifically for classification?

For (1) we reviewed previous works and built some prototypes to evaluate their suitability. Once we decided upon the solution, we then moved to verify the quality of signals compared to standard measures to answer (2). It is important to do this before the main experiment as the result should be relatively comparable. To achieve this, we used the negativity (NNM) to compare auditory response with ours. Finally, for emotion classification, we needed reference to benchmark our results, the DEAP dataset was used to calculate the accuracy. It results were then used as reference for comparing our own in-ear measurements. All of this is explained in more detail in the following sections.

### 3.1. In-Ear EEG Development

#### 3.1.1. Earpieces

Recent research on in-ear EEG devices were studied [17,30,31,37]. There are currently 2 types of in-ear EEG devices: one is personally customized, as illustrated in Figure 1, and the other is generic or non-customized. The first type is based on molds created from wax impressions, 3D scanning, CAD editing, 3D-printing, and a wiring process, respectively. This type of an in-ear device is robust as it fits completely to the owners' ear canal. However, it is relatively costly. Hence, this type of an in-ear EEG device was not considered in this study, as we would like a generic and low-cost device.
