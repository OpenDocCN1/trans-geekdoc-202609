## 1.5. Implicit Measures and the Neuroscience Approach

Traditional, most of human behaviour research has been based on a model of the human mind that assumes humans can think about and verbalise their thoughts, emotions and behaviours [66]. Therefore, classical psychological evaluations using self-assessment questionnaires and interviews quantify subjects' responses. However, explicit measures have been demonstrated to be subjective; stereotype-based expectations can lead to systematically biased behaviour, where others are motivated to be, or appear to, unbiased [67]. The terms used in questionnaires can also be differently interpreted by respondents, and the outcomes are subject to the biases of respondents possessing a wide knowledge of dispersions, which is not always the case [68].

Recent advances in neuroscience show that most of the processes that regulate emotions, attitudes and behaviours are not conscious. In contrast to explicit processes, humans cannot verbalise these implicit processes [69]. In recent years, growing interest has been "looking" inside the brain to solve problems that have not traditionally been addressed by neuroscience. Thus, neuroscience offers techniques to recognise implicit measurements not without conscious processes [70]. These developments have provoked the emergence in the last decades of a new field called neuroeconomics, which blends psychology, neuroscience and economics into models of decision-making, rewards, and uncertainties [71]. Neuroeconomics addresses human behaviour research, in particular the brain mechanisms involved in decision-making, from the point of view of cognitive neuroscience using implicit measures.

Several implicit measuring techniques have been proposed in recent years. Some examples of their applications in human behaviour research are:
- Heart rate variability (HRV) has been correlated with arousal changes in vehicle driving, detecting critical points on a route [72]
- Electrodermal activity (EDA) has been used to measure stress caused by cognitive load in the workplace [73]
- Electroencephalogram (EEG) has been used to assess engagement in audio-visual content [74]
- Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has been used to measure the brain activity of participants engaged in social vs. mechanical/analytic tasks [75]
- Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) has been used to measure decision-making processes in approach-avoidance theories [76]
- Eye-tracking (ET) has been used to measure subconscious brain processes that show correlations with information processing in risky decisions [77]
- Facial expression analysis (FEA) has been applied to detect emotional responses in e-learning environments [78]
- Speech emotion recognition (SER) has been used to detect depressive disorders [79]

## 1.6. Affective Computing and Emotion Recognition Systems

Affective computing, which analyzes human emotions using implicit measures, has developed into an important field of study in the last decades. Introduced by Rosalind Picard in 1997, it proposed the automatic quantification and recognition of human emotions as an interdisciplinary field based on psychology, computer science, biomedical engineering and artificial intelligence [1]. The automatic recognition of human emotion states using implicit measures can be transversally applied to all human behaviour and complex implicit responses. In particular, it can be applied to neurocognitive research as they share the scientific approach of using implicit measures, and to the important relationship that has existed between emotions and decision-making [71]. Emotion recognition models can be divided into three types: emotional modelling, emotion classification and emotion elicitation.

The emotional modelling approach can be divided into discrete and dimensional. Discrete models characterise the emotion system as a set of basic emotions, which include anger, disgust, fear, joy, sadness and surprise, and the complex emotions that result from them [91]. On the other hand, dimensional models propose that emotional responses can be modelled in a multidimensional space where each dimension represents a fundamental property common to all emotions. The most commonly used theory is the circumplex model of affect (CMA), which proposes a three-dimensional space consisting of: valence, arousal, which is the intensity of the emotion in activation, from low to high, and dominance, which ranges from feelings of total control or influence on environments and surroundings to the opposite extremes of feeling helpless and powerless.

Affective computing uses techniques and machine-learning algorithms to classify emotions automatically. Many have been used, such as voice, face, neuroscience and physiological [93]. It is noteworthy that one of the main emotion classification methods uses variables associated with central nervous system (CNS) and autonomic nervous system (ANS) dynamics [93]. First, human emotional processing and perception involve cerebral cortex activity, which allows the automatic classification of emotions using the CNS. EEG is one of the techniques most used in this context [94]. Second, many emotion recognition systems have used the ANS to analyse the changes in cardiovascular dynamics provoked by mood changes, and machine-learning algorithms, such as support vector machines, linear discriminant analysis, K-nearest neighbour and neural networks, have achieved high levels of accuracy in inferring subjects' emotional states [96].

Finally, emotion elicitation is the ability to reliably elicit affective states. This elicitation is a critical factor in the development of systems that can detect, interpret and adapt to human affect [97]. The methods that elicit emotions in laboratories can be divided into two groups, active and passive. Active methods directly influence stimuli, including behavioural manipulation, social interaction [99] and dyadic interaction [100]. Passive methods usually present external stimuli such as images, sound or video. To use these, the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) is among the databases used as an elicitation tool in emotion recognition methodologies [95]. This includes over a thousand depictions of people, objects and standards, standardised on the basis of valence and arousal [97]. As to audio, the International Affective Digitalised Sound System (IADS) database is the most commonly applied in studies where we use sound to elicit emotions [101].
