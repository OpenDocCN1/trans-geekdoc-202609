# 3.4. Data Annotation

In addition to the basic annotation, from the experimental design and application log files, the dataset is enhanced by various semi-automatic generated labels. The basic annotation contains the exact timing information at millisecond of the beginning and end of all sequences: Timestamps when each search item was presented, and when a subject presented the solution, whether the question was correct or wrong and all information of the given subjective feedback. These mostly technical annotations do not necessarily contain emotional information, although they can give hints on where the probability of emotional reactions rises, in case of timeouts or wrong answers, or during maximum loads.

The semi-automatic labels are generated by our data driven affective learning approach, as presented in [62]. The basic assumption of this approach is sparseness of emotions in audio and video modalities. We figured that in HCI scenarios, users mostly tend to have emotions only in a few situations, or show them only in sparsity [64]. This is due to the assumption that most of the recorded data represent neutral emotional content. Based on this assumption we train density estimation models, such as One Class SVM, SVDD, or GMM on the whole dataset (ignoring the underlying structural experiment) and then compare each feature vector against this neutral background. If a specific feature has a high density compared to the background, the probability of having an emotional instance increases. As such, less-fitting points are presented to experts, which rate the points towards the emotional content. After having the first points labeled (emotion or neutral), these labels are used to build the background iteratively, until most of the outlier data points are labeled. Details of this learning-based process can be found in the cited papers. The main conclusion of our affective learning is the dramatic reduction of annotation effort in case of affective datasets like the one presented here. In most cases, only 10% of a naturalistic HCI dataset has to be annotated in order to achieve the same classification results as the classifier using the full dataset. We further had to manually label nine participants in order to evaluate our affective learning approach. This method-facto manual labels are also part of the dataset.

Additionally, we provide some further created labels regarding the body pose information. As described in [65], we annotated body poses of the skeleton by distance measures of the Kinect sensor. Static poses include onions and offsets of: arms crossed, hands behind back, hands on hips, legs crossed, and in step position. Dynamic poses include: sideways movements away from body, facial touch, and quick movement of feet.
