
He et al. [5] developed a ten-layer CNN to detect cognitive stress based on short-term ECGs. Here, spectrum information was used to extract consecutive ECG R-peaks for use as instead of raw ECG data. This study also used a sliding window method to increase the number of data points. Their results showed that the proposed CNN achieved a lower error rate than conventional machine learning models. In addition, the authors found that meaningful information related to stress lie in the 0.4–20 Hz range by visualizing the activation maps of multiple layers. This demonstrates that deep learning approaches can benefit from having data-driven features that are not captured by conventional machine learning approaches.

## 3. Methods

We recruited 18 subjects (8 females and 10 males, 24.6 ± 4.6) from Pohang University of Science and Technology (POSTECH), South Korea, via open recruitment. The study subjects were selected based on the following criteria: (1) they had no cardiovascular or mental problems and (2) they had not undertake exercise before the day of the test and (3) they had not caffeinated beverages on the day of the test. This study was approved by the POSTECH Ethics Committee (IRB-2019-E001).

The experiments were conducted parallel normally, unexpected technical problems (namely a subject's carelessness and an unexpected Windows OS update) occurred during two experiments, meaning that these two subjects' data were not captured fully. Thus, we used the datasets from the remaining 16 subjects.

## 3.2. Experiments

Each experiment set took about 1 h, and comprised two stages: (1) an initial relaxation stage (colored with light green), before the experiment began (about 15–20 min) and (2) the main relaxation stage (about 45 min). These are described in more detail in Figure 1.

![](img/601340ca744b738277eef45ff127492a_222_0.png)

> Figure 1. An experiment process. There are two stages for the experiment: an initial relaxation stage (colored with light green), and a relaxation stage (indicated by gray lines). The regular experiment stage can be segmented into 5 min of relax tasks (colored with dark green) and stress tasks (colored with orange). From the start to end of the regular experiment, subjects' physiological signals were captured by a wearable device. At the end of each short relax or a stress task, a mental stress level assessment was carried (with an orange arrow).

During the initial relaxation stage, each subject was asked to wear a wearable device that collected ECG and RESP data and to completely relax, eliminating any excitement or nervousness regarding the experiment. In addition, we explain our protocol to prevent any mistakes by the subject. During the main experiment stage, the subjects alternately experienced simulated relaxing states (called RELAX tasks in Figure 1) and stressful states. During the relaxations, to sit in a chair in a comfortable position without any mental activity. The first relaxation task aims to build a psychological baseline and remove unwanted excitement before the regular experiment stage. Similarly, the other relaxation tasks aim to remove stress after a stressful task and prepare the next task by setting the psychological baseline. This design improves the reliability of the experiment's results [5]. During the stressful tasks, the subject was provided with one of two types of stressors: (1) a math task, namely requiring the subject to solve a series of subtraction problems via mental arithmetic, or (2) a Stroop task, a task where the subject was asked to respond with the color of a given word and image. Because all subjects were Korean, the words were presented in the Korean language. These are typical tasks that have been used to induce stress in studies [12,15,17].

The tasks also varied in difficulty, based on the results of a previous study by Cho et al. [15]. For example, an easy task might be required to subtract 1 from a four-digit number, within 7.5 s, while a hard math task might involve repeatedly subtracting a two-digit number, rather than 1, from a four-digit number with the same time limit. Likewise, easy Stroop tasks involved the same color and meaning, while these were mismatched for stress. In either case, the time limit for Stroop problem was 1.5 s. Appropriate sound feedback was also provided to indicate or not the answer was correct, encouraging the subject to pay attention to the task and adding additional stress.

The subjects were presented with four stress-inducing tasks (two with difficulties) in random order. Although a previous study [15] used a fixed order, we did not choose to do this, for reasons: (1) a fixed order could also bias the stress level, and (2) a random order would better replicate stress-inducing situations. All of the relaxation and stress tasks lasted for 5 min. At the end of each task, the subject (not at all) was asked to evaluate how mentally he or she felt, based on a visual scale (VAS) [1] from 0 to 10 (extreme stress). For example, if a subject is relaxed or stressed, the VAS score will be close to zero or 10, respectively. In this study, the use of VAS to measure the average effects (e.g., induce stress or relieve stress) of each task on the 16 subjects was used during experimental stage to minimize unexpected mental stimuli. When the experimental stage was complete, the subject took off the wearable device to complete the experiment.

### 3.2.2. Experimental Setup

A BioH module 30 (Zephyr Technology, Annapolis, MD, USA) was used to collect the subjects' ECG and RESP data. This wearable device is compact and can be tightened with a strap, making it a good choice to minimize disturbance during the experiment. Making the strap too tight could induce unnecessary stress or pain, so we asked the subjects not to tighten it so much it was uncomfortable to wear.

The experiments were conducted on a laptop computer (with a 2.8 GHz Core i7 processor (Santa Clara, CA, USA) and 16 GB of RAM) in a closed room. During each experiment, the subject was alone in the room, as shown in Figure 2. On the laptop, a graphical user interface was installed, which we developed with MATLAB R2016a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). This was designed to be as simple as possible so not to confuse the subjects.

### 3.2.3. Preprocessing

After running the experiment a total of 16 times, we collected datasets, consisting of ECG and RESP, and stress level. During the preprocessing, the ECG signal was first filtered by a 2000th-order bandpass filter, and a second by a 3000th-order finite response filter with 5-62 Hz bandwidth [12,17]. The ECG and RESP segments' start and end times were all synchronized. Here, there was only one data point of overlap between one segment and the next. Then, we excluded the first clip from each segment owing to the initialization time needed for each task. Finally, we labeled each segment with its binary class (stressed or unstressed) according to the task type. We obtained 720 segments.

## 3.3. Machine Learning Approaches

To compare our deep learning approach with conventional machine learning approaches, we developed several machine learning models for use as benchmarks. We selected ECG and RESP features that have been used in many previous studies [11–19].

We extracted handcrafted features from the ECG data, including four time-domain features and seven frequency-domain features (Table 1). As time-domain features, we extracted the HR (mean), standard deviation of the Normal-Normal (NN) interval, mean square of successive difference of R-peak to R-peak (RR) intervals, and the percentage of adjacent intervals that were greater than 50 ms (msns). As frequency-domain features, we extracted the NN powers in the following ranges: 0.00–0.04 Hz (VLF), 0.04–0.15 Hz (LF), 0.15–0.4 Hz (HF), and 0.4–14 Hz (FF). In addition, we included the ratios of LF to HF (LF/HF), LF to LF+HF (LF), and HF to LF+HF (nHF).

We also extracted a RESP features: three time-domain and five frequency-domain features (Table 1). As time-domain features, we used the square root of the mean squared RESP (RMS), interquartile range (IQR), and mean difference between adjacent elements of each RESP segment (MDA). As frequency-domain features, we used the powers in the 0.00–1.00 Hz (LF1), 1.00–2.00 Hz (LF2), 2.00–3.00 Hz (HF1), 3.00–4.00 Hz (HF2) ranges, as well as the LF1 + LF2.
