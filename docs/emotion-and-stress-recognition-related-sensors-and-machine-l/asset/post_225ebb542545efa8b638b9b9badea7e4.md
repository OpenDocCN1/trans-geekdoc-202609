
## 2.3.3. Respiration Baseline and Results' Summary

Following the subjective feedback, a baseline phase consisting of a breathing exercise to level off the physiological reactions related to that particular sequence is conducted by the subjects. Additionally, here, the users are guided by the system speech to first breathe deeply, then hold their breath for a few seconds, and finally exhale. The exercise was repeated three times subsequently. Finally, after the baseline phase, the system informs the user speech about his performance during the last sequence and the related results achieved, including the earned money, are presented on the screen.

## 2.4. Technical Implementation

The further developments of the generic paradigm and software implementation of the interaction scheme and experimental structure for the induction of various cognitive, stress, and affective states are realized using programming and integrated within the Seminar platform [24]. The workflow of the experiment including the structure, the content of the different sequences as well as the subjective feedback and baseline sections are defined in an external task file which can be imported at the beginning of the experiment. Within a task, the course setting of these sequences can be defined individually for every task and subject, allowing a high flexibility and an easy-to-handle workflow setup. The user system interaction mechanism (mouse, speech, or both) for every part within the experiment is predefined in this file. This also includes the text (spoken and written) given by the system. The task describes the content of events of the experiment and is consistent for all the participants, except for the second sample who underwent three measurements at three different times. For this, the content of speech output given by the system is slightly modified for the second and third measurements using alternative synonyms. The intention here to keep the interaction as natural as possible by preventing a repetition of the same words every time.

During the visual search, the user is instructed to give his answer by speech command. To recognize the speech command, our experimental implementation includes an integrated automated speech recognition algorithm. If well trained, speech recognition works properly in order to ensure smooth interaction between the user and the system, a “Wizard of Oz” (WOZ) scenario was also implemented and used to support the integrated automated speech recognition algorithm. This was especially useful when the automated recognition fails for instance because language disparity of subjects that strongly diverge from the normal language on which the recognition was trained. In the WOZ scenario, the experiment was observed on an external monitor by the experimenter, who controlled and adjusted the (correct) log of the answer, if necessary.

Finally, the behavior of the subjects and all their conducted actions as well the whole course of the experiment are triggered after the events. As a result, for every individual subject, a .log file is generated after every experiment including the course details of the experiment and can be used for the later processing and analysis of the signal.

## 2.5. Multimodal Sensors for Data Acquisition

In order to collect high quality data for a wide kind of multimodal analysis there are two important issues regarding the technical data acquisition. First, a wide set of different modalities with a maximum of data quality in each sensor needs to be ensured. Second, the synchronization between all sensors and the user interface components has to be as congruent as possible. The sensors used here are connected to the participant and sensors mounted to the environment. To ensure a high mobility of the participant, and therefore, less influence on the participant’s natural behavior, wireless sensors were used.

In particular, they include a small theatre stereo headset microphone with a frequency range of 20 to 20 000 Hz, sampled at 48 kHz, transmitted via digital radio and a gtec gMOBIllab+ Bluetooth amplifier for biophysical sensors. The bioamplifier was equipped with sensors for electromyography (EMG), electrocardiography (ECG), sensor condition level (SCL), respiration, and body temperature at a sampling rate of 256 Hz. To ensure accurate records free of motion artifacts, the signals from the sensors underwent an online monitor check adapted for our experiment using Simulink/softcheck. This online quality check was conducted during an initial record at rest in sitting position and to the first sequence of the experiment.

A stationary mounted frontal webcam with HD resolution of 1920 × 1080 at 30 frames per second was used. Further a Kinect v2 also was mounted in the front. The Kinect includes a Full RGB color video stream (1080 @ 30 Hz), an infrared (IR) video stream (512 × 424 @ 30 Hz), a depth stream (512 × 424 @ 30 Hz), a directed audio beam by microphone and pose estimation stream using skeleton information containing 25 joints. Kinect and primary webcam were placed on top of the interaction screen in front of the scenery looking towards the participants' face. Finally, a second webcam with a resolution of 1280 × 720 @ 30 fps was placed in the rear of the experimental setting in order to monitor the scenery overview and the atmosphere sounds. 3 shows the views from the frontal and rear cameras and the acquired depth information.

Summarized, we recorded 16 sensor modalities, including four video streams (front/rear/Kinect RGB/Kinect IR), three audio streams (helmet/directed/atmosphere), seven biophysical streams (3 × EMG/ECG/SCL/respiration/temperature), depth, and pose stream. Further, several information streams extracted from an application log file, described here, were also recorded. After recording, all data were post-processed in order to provide a high quality towards technical and quality issues. As visualization tool we used ATLAS [60,1] to present (and playback) the recorded data to the experts. Only sessions which passed all technical and manual quality checks below the final dataset of 100 (40 × 1 + 20 + 3 sessions). These are described in Section 3. In addition to the annotation from the log file and experimental design structures, some additional labels are created by a semi-automatic learning procedure as described in the Annotation (Section 3.4) of this.

Figure 4 shows an overview of the collected data of a single session displayed in the visualization tool ATLAS. The timescale is at minute, so structure of experimental phases be seen in the upper annotation line. It is not possible to record this massive amount of data on a single PC, as we developed a modular network-based infrastructure called MARS (Multimodal Activity Recognition and Recording System). This contains a specific recording mode on which the one hand controls each specific sensor according to its API. This can include preparation and initialization commands, trigger and timing control, data format transformations, and disk/write control of the streams. On the other hand, each module accomplishes the defined network commands and synchronization protocols. The modules are mostly written in C#, but due to the inter-module communication over network, there is no technical limitation to a specific programming language, operating system or hardware type. Depending on the sensors, hard and software requirements, in most cases more than one sensor can be grouped on a PC without influencing other.

In addition to the sensor modules, the user interface (UI) and WOZ module were encapsulated in such a network to control and monitor their behavior in the same synchronous manner. Finally, a logging module was established acting like a sensor, not recording physical data, but recording the whole sensor behavior. This includes about all participants and WOZ inputs, global information on the internal and external systems, information about the sensors attrs, any network communication. With this log file and the recorded sensor streams it is possible to reconstruct the whole experimental procedure in detail up to a virtual playback without a real participant. Therefore, for the development of real time capable online recognition systems.

Finally, each recorded had a network monitoring module, measuring the current network latency to synchronous recording. Due to the usage of "of the shelf" sensors like the Kinect sensor and webcams, which do not possess trigger input capabilities, and the multi-pc environment, we are not able to ensure synchronicity on a nanosecond level, like highly-specialized, expensive, hardware-triggered setups. Here, our setup is more flexible and a deal more realistic towards user implementations on custom hardware and smart devices. The recorded emotions and mental states occur typically in a longer range and all multimodal recognition approaches typically use time windows from 50 ms up to several seconds. Thus, inter-modality delays from under one millisecond are acceptable. To ensure this, each involved PC was directly attached to a separate recording sub network containing just one switch.
