
## 3.1.1. Setup

The setup was done as follows;

- 1. Preparation of experiment areas: The camera should be set. Empatica E4 should be ready.
- 2. Interviewers should keep eye contact with the participant. Their gestures and facial expressions should be neutral.
- 3. The participant is informed about the procedure and signs the consent form.
- 4. The participant wears the smart band (Empatica E4).
- 5. The participant is asked to turn off /her phone in order to eliminate distraction.

## 3.1.2. Pre-Stress Measures

Before the experiment, the following procedure was applied;

- 1. The participant filled out the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) with 14 questions.
- 2. The participant was told to stay in the waiting area and reading materials such as magazines with emotionally-neutral contents (home and garden, car magazines) were presented to the participant for this period.
- 3. The participant filled out the PSS-5 (ambulatory PSS) form. This questionnaire was first created by Cohen et al. [36] and used for measuring the perceived stress in ambulatory settings in [37] (see Figure 1).

## 3.1.3. The TSST

Our implementation of the TSST is described as follows:

- 1. The participant was directed to the interview area.
- 2. TSST speech preparation period: the following text was read to the participant: "This is the speech preparation portion of the task; you are expected to prepare a five-minute speech describing why you study [name of the degree that the participant studies/studied] and why you would be a good candidate for your ideal job. Your speech will be videotaped and reviewed by the psychologists who conduct the research. You have five minutes to prepare and your time begins now."
- 3. The participant prepared his/her speech. There should be a digital timer in the room set to five minutes. Interviewers should leave the room.
- 4. The following text was read at the end of the speech preparation period: "This is the speech portion of the task. You should speak for the entire five-minute time period. Your time begins now." Interviewers should start the recording of the camera.
- 5. TSST speech performance period. If the participant stopped during this period, interviewers interrupted him/her to stay alert for 20 s and prompted: "You still have time."
- 6. After the first of speech period, the participant was interrupted and asked to continue their speech in English by telling them: "Could you continue in English now, please?"
- 7. At the end of 2.5 minute, if the participant did not attempt to reply, interviewers prompted the participant to answer.
- 8. At the end of speech performance period, the communication between interviewers and the participant resumed. Interviewers reset the timer to 5 minute and read to the participant: "During the final five-minute part of this task, you will be asked to report your answers aloud Your time begins now. If the participant makes any mistake, the interviewer says 'That is incorrect, start over.'" (Figure 2)
- 9. Participant filled out PSS-5. (img/601340ca744b738277eef45ff127492a_49_0.png)

In order to alleviate the stress response, we applied a biofeedback intervention which was the built-in breathing application of Apple Watch. The procedure was applied as follows:

- 1. Participants were directed to a chair as a relaxing place.
- 2. Participants were given an Apple Watch to him/her at this stage, followed by breathing exercise for the remaining four minutes, sitting or lying as preferred.
- 3. Interviewers should leave the room after giving the Apple Watch.
- 4. At the end of five minute long recovery period, interviewers returned to the room, and the participant filled out PSS-5.

## 3.2. Daily Life Data Collection and Emotionalary Assessment

After the controlled experiments were finished, we gave Empatica E4 devices to all participants. They were told to wear Empatica E4 devices for twelve hours per day, between 9 a.m. and 9 p.m., for seven days. These days were not necessarily consecutive. We applied the EMA to collect information about the subject's stress level [39]. EMA involved the repeated sampling of subjects' current behaviors and experiences in real time [39]. EMA aims to minimize bias, maximize validity, and allow the study of micro processes that influence behavior in real-world contexts. We implemented an online version of PSS-5 questionnaire. After each hour, we asked the participant to fill in the EMA. In order to make the collection of self-reports, we sent emails every days at the end of each hour when they were wearing the wristband. In order, the participants were reminded to fill in PSS-5 at 12 p.m., 3 p.m., and 9 p.m. every days. The EMA was delivered to the participants via a survey app. The app was available on desktop and mobile browsers. The link to the EMA was delivered through email. Each email was sent to remind participants to fill the questionnaire with PSS-14 and for ambulatory assessments. In total, we collected 10h of physiological data and 38 EMAs (60 EMAs in total), in the lab.

The methodology used in this study was approved by the Institutional Review Board for Human Subjects of Bojack University Approval Number 2018/16. Prior to the data acquisition, each participant received a consent form that explained the experimental procedure and its benefits and implications to both society and the subject. The procedure was also explained verbally to the subject. All of the data were stored anonymously.

## 3.3. Stress Recognition Framework

In order to propose an unobtrusive stress wristband for everyday use, we used Empatica E4 [40] comfortable wristband, which has a variety of sensors such as the three-dimensional accelerometer (32 Hz), the continuous heart rate monitoring unit (4 Hz), and the EDA sensors (4 Hz). The major difference between the wristbands and contact sensors used in hospital settings is the vulnerability to motion artifacts due to their design and attachment. A daily life suitable and comfortable stress detection system should consider these artifacts. Thus, it should have a preprocessing unit that detects and removes the artifacts from contact and motion. In daily life, HRV and EDA can change due to high-intensity activity in short periods. Therefore a single sensor-based system can fail. Hence, a stress recognition system suitable for daily life should be multi-modal in terms of the collected data. We used robust preprocessing and feature extraction modules from our previous work [18] for this purpose (see Figure 3).

### 3.1.1 Preprocessing

The physiological signals coming from different sensors were segmented into non-overlapping time windows. We selected the window size as two minutes since it was reported that the duration of stress stimulation and recovery was approximately five minutes and the segment sizes could capture such processes [41]. IBI (Interbeat Interval) and EDA signals were sent to the detection units. Therefore, the response time of our system was approximately two minutes.

The artifact detection unit for the heart rate signal applied a threshold between successive R intervals. The threshold was set as [42]. After the removal of artifacts, they were replaced with cubic spline interpolation, as applied in Kubidis [43]. This interpolation method was selected since it achieved better results [18]. Windows containing more than 10% of RR intervals were removed [15].

For EDA artifact detection unit, we used the toolbox by Taylor et al. [44]. It uses the SVM classifier and detects artifacts in the EDA signal with 95% accuracy by analyzing skin temperature and conductance signals. A batch processing feature removed the detected artifacts. The resulting signal was transferred to the EDA feature unit.

### 3.3.2 Feature Extraction

To create a multi-modal stress recognition framework, we extracted state-of-the-art features from the EDA and RR intervals to create a feature vector. In this section, we describe the feature extraction methodology for each of the physiological signals. We decomposed the EDA signal into phasic and tonic components using convex optimization-based EDAconv tool [45]. EDA also cleans noise in the EDA signal. We extracted features from both the phasic and tonic components of the EDA signal. Percentiles are very handy for exploring the distribution using various EDA graphs. The following EDA features were selected from the literature [13,26]:

- Mean value
- Standard deviation
- Number of peaks
- Number of strong peaks
- Twentieth percentile
- Eighth percentile
- Quartile deviation

We extracted 13 heart rate variability (HRV) and frequency domain features from RR intervals. These features were commonly used in the previous works [11,26,43,47]. In order to compute frequency features, we re-sampled RR intervals at 4 Hz [48] and applied fast Fourier transform (TFT). The computed HRV features are shown below:

- Mean value of the inter-beat (RR) intervals
- Standard deviation of the inter-beat interval
- Root mean square of the successive difference of the RR intervals
- Percentage of the successive RR intervals varying more than 50 ms from the previous interval
- Total number of RR intervals divided by the height of the histogram of all RR intervals measured on a scale with bins of 1/128 s
- Triangular interpolation of RR interval histogram
- Power in the low-frequency band (0.04-0.15 Hz)
