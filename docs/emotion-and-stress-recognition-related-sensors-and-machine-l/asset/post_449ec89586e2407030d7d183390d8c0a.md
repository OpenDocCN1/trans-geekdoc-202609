## 1.2. Sense of Presence

In addition to the objective features of the set-up, the experience of users in virtual environments can be measured by the concept of presence, understand subjective feeling of "being-there" [28]. A high degree of presence creates in the user sensation of physical presence and the illusion of interacting and reacting as if (s)he sure that one is not actually in the real world [29]. In the 2000s, the strong illusion of being in a place, spite of not actually there, was called "place illusion" (PI), to avoid confusion that might be caused by the multiple meanings of the word "presence". More, as Pl relates to how the world is perceived, and the correlation of movements and concomitant changes in the images that form perception, "plausibility illusion" (Pl) relates to what is perceived, in a correlation of events not directly caused by the participant [13]. Pl is determined by the extent to which a system produces events directly relate to the participant, and the overall credibility of the scenario being depicted in comparison with viewer expectations, for example, when an experimental participant is provoked into giving a quick, natural and automatic reply to a question posed by an.

Although presence plays a role in VR experiences, there is limited understanding of what affect presence in virtual environments. However, there is consensus that experception and interactivity factors affect presence. Some have shown that levels of interactivity and immersion, which are directly related to the experimental set-up, provoke particular emotions [30–32]. As the interactor factors, which are defined by the content displayed, participants will perceive higher presence if they feel emotionally affected; for previous studies have found a strong correlation between arousal and presence [33]. Recent research has also analysed presence in specific contexts and suggested that, for example, in social environments, it is enhanced when the VR elicit genuine cognitive, emotional and behavioural responses, and when participants create their own narratives about events [34]. On the other hand, presence increases when users experience physical problems, such as cybersecurity [35].

## 1.3. Virtual Reality in Human Behaviour

VR is, thus as a proposed tool to simulate complex, real situations and environments, offering researchers unprecedented opportunities to investigate human behaviour in closely devices designed in laboratory conditions [33]. There are now many researchers in the field, who have published many studies, so strong, interdisciplinary community exists [2].

Education and training is one field where VR has much used. Freina and Ott [36] show that VR can offer good travel-time experiences, for example, students can experience different historical periods. It can address physical inaccessibility, for example, students can explore the solar system in the first person. It can circumnavigate ethical problems, for example, students can "perform" surgery. Surgical training is one of the most analysed research topics. Interventional surgery lacked satisfactory training methods before the advent of VR, except learning on real points [37]. Bhag, Liou and Chang [38] analysed improvements in training. These authors suggested that cost-effective 3D VR significantly improved students learning motivation and provided a positive impact on their live-firing achievement scores. In addition, besides enhancements in cost-effectiveness, VR offers a safe training environment, as evidenced by the extensive research into driving and flight simulators [39,40]. Moreover, de-Juan-Ripoll et al [41] proposed that VR is an invaluable tool for assessing risk-taking profiles and in trained skills, due to its transferability to real-world results.

Seneal researchers have also demonstrated the effectiveness of VR in therapeutic applications. It offers some distinct advantages over standard therapies, including precision control over the degree of exposure to the therapeutic scenario, the possibility of tailoring scenarios to individual patients’ needs and the capacity to provide therapies that might be otherwise possible [42]. Taking some examples, using VR they have analysed the improvement in training in social skills for persons with mental and behavioural disorders, such as phobias [43], schizophrenia [44] and autism [45]. Lönen, Noel and Alcaniz [46] showed that VR-based telerehabilitation interventions, the reconquisition of motor skills associated with balance, as well as when in-clinic interventions (both complemented with conventional therapy programs). Moreover, it has been proposed as a key tool for the training of neurodevelopmental disorders [47].

In addition, VR has been applied to many fields, such as architecture and marketing. In architecture, VR has been used as a framework within to test the overall validity of proposed plans and architectural designs, generates alternatives and conceptualise learning, and the design process itself [48]. In marketing, it has been used in the analysis of consumer behaviour in laboratory-controlled environments [49] and as to help developing engaging consumers experiences [50].

One of the most important points in human behaviour is emotions, due to the central role they play in many background processes, such as decision-making, creativity, memory and social interaction [51]. Given the presence that VR provokes users, it has been suggested as a powerful means of evoking emotions in laboratory environments [8]. Subsequently, many other studies showed that VR can evoke emotions, such as anxiety and relaxation [52], positive value in children taking exercise [53], natural environments, such as parks [54], and different modes in social environments featuring avatars [55].

Finally, it is crucial to point out that the usefulness of simulation in human behaviour research has been analysed through validity, which is the capacity to evoke a response from the user in a simulated environment that might be evoked by a physical environment [56]. Thus, there is a need to perform direct comparison between virtual and real environments. Some comparisons have studied the validity of virtual environments by assessing psychological responses [57] and cognitive performance [58]. However there have been few analyses of physiological and behavioural responses [59,60]. Hedydrian et al analysed user performance in office-related activities, for example, reading and identifying objects, and found that the participants performed similarly in an immersive virtual setting and in a benchmark physical environment for all of the measured tasks [61]. Chamilothi, Weinold, and Andersen compared subjective perceptions of daylight visits, and identified no significant differences between the real and virtual environments [62]. Kimura et al analysed orientation-task performance, where participants in a VR room showed less facility, suggesting that caution be used when interpreting the nuances of spatial cue in virtual environments [63].
