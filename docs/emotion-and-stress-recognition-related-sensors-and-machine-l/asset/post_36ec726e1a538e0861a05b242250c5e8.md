These neurological changes lead to physiological changes in heart rate (HR), skin conductance (RESP), and pupil diameter [2] that can be accurately measured by conventional biomedical instruments. Unfortunately, conventional instruments for measuring physiological signals are not optimal for continuous use in the workplace owing to their bulky size and awkward cables. The recent advancement of wearable technology has made it practical to measure various physiological signals with minimal disturbances, leading to increased interest in continuous stress detection on physiological signals.

Similar to the importance of the developments in monitoring devices, algorithms to analyze the data and recognize the occurrences of stress is also crucial. Several machine learning models have been proposed to recognize physiological signals [8–12]. Although these have demonstrated the feasibility of recognizing stress, they have one serious limitation, namely that learning approaches require us to extract well-defined, handcrafted features and find the best way to combine them, with very challenging tasks [13]. Furthermore, because the dependence on such handcrafted features means they cannot find new stress-related features, it can limit their generalization performance. Overcoming this limitation will require a breakthrough.

Recently, deep learning approaches have made great strides in processing and natural language processing [14]. This is because they not only extract features from data, but also learn high-level features from low-level ones owing their hierarchical structure, something that simple learning models cannot do. In particular, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and long-term memory networks (LSTMs) have led to success in numerous fields. Due to these advantages, attempts have also been made to use this approach to recognize [5,12]. However these have only considered one type of physiological signal. Because a single channel cannot capture all possible responses to stress, they may degrade their generalization performance. Conversely, the performance can be improved well as the size of individual physiological characteristics be collected using multiple physiological signals. It is essential to study the validity and feasibility of deep learning approaches based on multiple physiological signals.

Our goal is to propose an end-to-end deep neural network based on combining two types of physiological signals, namely electrocardiogram (ECG) and RESP data, which have been proposed as meaningful stress-related signals [10]. In addition, we compare the proposed network with conventional machine learning models and visualize the results to see the activation patterns produced by the ECG and RESP signals.

## 2 Related Works

Numerous studies have proposed machine learning approaches for recognizing mental stress based on various types of physiological signals [8,9,11,16]. Of these signals, ECGs and photoplethysmograms (PPGs) have been used to extract handcrafted features to heart activity, such as the HR and HR variability (HRV). In addition to these, other physiological signals have been investigated, such as RESP, electrodermal activity (EDA), galvanic skin response (GSR), pupil diameter, acceleration, electroencephalograms, electromyograms (EMGs), and electrocardiograms.

Then, with collected physiological signals, developing machine learning models requires the following steps:
1. preprocess and de-noise the data with a digital noise filter;
2. extract well-defined features from the multiple physiological signals and the best feature set;
3. use these features to train a machine learning model; and
4. evaluate the model on a test dataset.

Srimparaka et al. [8] considered stated workplace's stress was considered to replicate workplace stress and data were collected. Then, a support vector machine (SVM) was trained and evaluated with either well-defined features or combinations of features. The model was able to recognize stress with greater than 90% accuracy, leading the authors to conclude that HR, HRV, and GSR features in the time and frequency domains were sufficient to accurately detect stress.

In addition to workplace stress, recognizing stress during driving has also been studied. Here, stress is considered to be a factor that can cause aggressive driving and reduced concentration [16]. In [14], the authors developed two machine learning models, SVM and K-nearest neighbors (KNN) approach, to identify three distinct stress levels (low, medium, and high). Using Stress Recognition in Automobile Drivers (DRIVERDB)[10] in PHYSIonet, they collected multiple physiological signals including foot GSR, GSR, EMGs, ECGs, and RESP, then extracted well-defined features. By finding the feature that minimized the error rates, the SVM achieved 99% accuracy with a 5-minute window. Their analysis found selecting the right model, preprocessing steps, and feature set all helped to maximize its generalization performance.

Betti et al. [11] proposed a wearable physiological sensor for monitoring stress. They conducted Maastricht Acute Stress Tests and collected multiple physiological signals, including ECGs, and EEGs. After training, the proposed SVM achieved 86.0% accuracy and found correlations between the handcrafted features and the cortisol level, which is regarded as a biomarker of stress. By finding these correlations, the study validated the feasibility of monitoring stress with the proposed wearable sensor.

Although deep learning are heavily used in the image processing and natural language processing fields, a few studies have used them to detect or recognize stress [5,12,15], no study has applied this approach to analyzing multiple signals. Researchers have developed deep neural networks using the following methods:
1. process the physiological signals with a digital noise filter;
2. design a unique deep neural network based on domain knowledge;
3. train the network; and
4. evaluate it on a test dataset.

Cho et al. [15] proposed a promising approach to recognizing stress with a thermal imaging camera. The collected thermal images of people breathing were preprocessed to create spectrum sequences, and then a CNN was used to extract features from them. To increase the number of data points, a sliding window method was used to augment the data. The proposed CNN achieved greater than 80% accuracy on average in classifying the images as stressed or unstressed. Their main contribution is that they were the first to use stress sequences from thermal images as input.

Hwang et al. [12] proposed the Deep ECG for recognizing stress based on short-term ECGs (10 s). The authors proposed a 1D-CNN with optimized filter size and pooling length that used domain knowledge of ECGQRST waveforms. The proposed model achieved better performance than conventional machine learning methods. Visualizing the process showed that it detected patterns around ECG waves, meaning that it could automatically extract ECG waveform characteristics. Their network achieved around 80% accuracy on average in classifying the data as stressed or unstressed for their two experiments. Their main contribution is showing that optimized networks based on domain knowledge can provide better performance than conventional machine learning approaches and deep neural networks that are designed without domain knowledge.
