# # StressFoot: Uncovering the Potential of the Foot for
Acute Stress in Sitting Posture

Stress is a naturally occurring response and identifiable by several body signs. We propose a new way to discriminate acute stress and relaxation, using movement and posture characteristics of the foot. Based on data collected from 23 participants tasks that induced stress and relaxation, we developed several machine learning models to construct the validity of our method. We tested these in another study with 11 additional participants. The results demonstrated replicability with an overall accuracy of 87%. To demonstrate external validity, we conducted a field study with 10 participants, performing their usual office tasks over a working day. The results showed substantial robustness. We describe ten significant features in detail to enable easy replication of our models.

Stress has a direct impact on our well-being [1]. Although stress is often perceived negatively, it can have positive effects. For instance, acute stress aims to mentally and physically prepare our body to accomplish a demanding task. In contrast, episodic acute stress can cause a variety of negative symptoms, such as sleep disorders, headache, stomach pain and exhaustion. A elevated stress level may result in chronic stress [2], which can lead to severe health conditions, such as depression, hypertension and cardiovascular diseases [3]. Therefore, it is imperative to identify stressful situations to prevent them.

Previous studies have demonstrated the capability of identifying stress based on physiological measures [4], such as electrodermal activity (EDA), heart rate variability (HRV), brainwaves via electroencephalography (EEG) [8–9], muscle tension [10], facial expressions [11] and body language [12], as well as self-report [14,15]. Although these methods provide reliable indicators, there are drawbacks. For stress sensing, physiological parameters are necessary to detect movements. The sensor device also needs to be instrumented on the user's body, resulting in low comfort. An alternative method is contact free sensing, such as using cameras [16–18]. These systems suffer from varying lighting conditions, line of sight, and potentially privacy concerns.

Manual approaches, such as self-reporting, are prone to issues such as scalability and subjective biases. Other studies explore stress detection based on smartphone usage [19–21] by correlating screen-time with daytime, and using sensors show the capability of detecting stress over long periods of time. Identifying and predicting acute stress in the short-term may also be possible, still problematic [21].
