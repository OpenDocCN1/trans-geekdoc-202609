The type of behavioural measures, such as eye-tracking, gait patterns and navigation, might be a very powerful approach where VR can contribute to affective computing research, as they provide high levels of interactivity with the simulated stimuli. This might open a new field where emotional states can be sensed through behavioural measures in interactive, real situations.

The current trend is that the use of machine-learning algorithms to classify the emotions analysed is only 11.90% of the studies, that is, 71.21%. Since the early 2000s, physiological signals, in combination with HMDs, were first applied to emotion analyses, and all studies used testing and/or correlations to provide insights into the ANS oscillations during different affective states, except Reference [125], using EEG. Although the classical statistical techniques employed provided useful insights, they have some limitations: (i) testing analyses differences between two populations based on means and deviations, but it is difficult to analyse the effect of the combination of several features in datasets with large sets of variables, and (ii) they do not take account of non-linear relations. These limitations are being overcome by the use of machine-learning algorithms, as they can assess emotions through the development of algorithms in classification research, automatic feature selection to extract complex patterns inside data, and for non-linear kernels [143]. Marin-Morales et al. [105] presented the first emotion recognition system using SVM in combination with a rate of HRV features (time, frequency and non-linear dimensions) and EEG (with a mean phase coherence) in emotional rooms, achieving a recognition rate of 75% in arousal and 71.21% in valence. Marin-Morales et al. [105] developed an emotion recognition system in a realistic 3D virtual museum, using SVM in combination with HRV and EEG, with rates of 75% and 71.08% of recognition in arousal and valence, respectively. Grant et al. [139] presented an arousal-emotion recognition model with subjects playing a VR racing game. This procedure collected physiological responses, that is, the use of HRV, EMG and EEG. [140] analyzed the performance of a set of methods, including decision tree (DT), KNN, SVM, and NN, which adapted their model based on the level of fear, recognition in VR in a 3D approach game. The results showed recognition levels ranging from 42.5% to 89.5%. Therefore, the development of emotion recognition models in immersive VR is an open, fast-growing sub-field, which is moving from the classical statistical testing to supervised machine learning.

As the tools employed, Figure 6 shows the evolution of the HMDs used in measure-based emotion research. Among the first-generation VR HMDs of the 2000s was VFX3D, which offers a resolution of 380 x 337 pixels. In the 2010s, the Emagin 280 introduced the resolution of premium HMDs, offering 800 x 640 and a field of view, followed by Oculus Rift DK, which increased the resolution to 1080 x 960, in particular, the FOV to 90°. Finally, in the late 2010s, the HTC Vive offered an increase to 1600 x 1400 pixels, and democratised VR with their competitive price. These innovations in HMD performance are aligned with the exponential growth of the number of people who have used HMDs in emotion recognition research (Figure 2), and future HMDs, that might have 4K resolution for each user, could boost the use of VR to create realistic results in controlled laboratory environments.

The most used overall was the 3D environment (85.71%); 360° panoramas were in 16.67% of them. This is probably due to the fact that 3D environments provide a high level of interactivity, as 360° panoramas do not allow changes in view. However, both formats can be useful, on the day of the experiment. The 360° panorama set-ups can be very effective for updating controlled affective computing methodologies, in particular, when presenting users with a series of non-affective stimuli, such as CAPS [95] and LADS [144], but increasing degree of presence based on immersion level [30]. However, there is still a need to develop large datasets of validated immersive stimuli that cover a wide range of emotions, which could be used as general benchmarks to analyze physiological and behavioural dynamics in immersive VR. 360° approach offers a good solution to this, that, for interaction, for example, navigation, provokes uncontrolled variations during the emotional experience. The first dataset of stimuli published was by Marin-Morales et al. [105], which included 4 scenarios that recreated all quadrants of the CMA. On the other hand, the level of interactivity of 3D scenarios offer can be very useful in research, since they display much more realistic and interactive environments, facilitating decision-making and the analysis of dynamics. Taking some examples, Takac et al. [137] analysed the anxiety felt by speakers when faced by large audiences, Lin et al. [113] analysed the stress felt by individuals in a building on fire and Kisser et al. [130] analysed arousal in an exposure to high height.

Immersive VR can be a powerful tool to analyse human behaviour in controlled laboratory environments, but we do not know the level of VR validity needed to allow the extrapolation to the result of the insights gained in terms of physiological and behavioural responses. Indeed, 83% of the papers did not present any validity, and only 3 provided a direct comparison between the VR scene and the physical environment. Gorini et al. [109] analysed anxiety through HRV and DEA with virtual and real world, Higuera-Trujillo et al. [64] analysed pleasantness through DEA responses in a 3D, 360° real retail store, and physical machine. Morales et al. [165] analysed arousal and valence oscillations with HRV and EEG in VR. Other research analysed the influence of [121] and other VR features. Thus, VR validity is still one topic that needs to be more actively addressed. Understanding and isolating the intrinsic dynamics of VR will be key in future years for the analysis of insights obtained using HMDs.

Finally, the results suggest that VR will play a central role in the affective computing field. The research has increased its complexity and maturity during the last two decades, and it is likely to continue growing the next years. First, future research should extend the analysis of the physiological dynamics as emotion in VR, which could allow a level of understanding as high as we have today using 2D pictures. Subsequently, VR might open up many research opportunities that would be beneficial to use with non-immersive systems. In particular, the inclusion of the dominance dimension, which is very closely related to the users' control of the environment, and impacts on sense of security. Moreover, the physical dimension is a crucial factor in the understanding of the emotional dynamics of human beings. The future inclusion of responsive, realistic avatars will help increase the understanding of emotions evoked during situations, and the associated physiological processes, in controlled environments.

## 5. Conclusions

This work analysed the current state-of-the-art in implicit measure-based emotion recognition using HMDs, and by providing a systematic and aggregated analysis that can guide future research. After two decades of literature analysing emotions using HMDs in combination with implicit measures, mostly understood through the physiological arousal responses of the ANS, in recent years, an inflection point has been reached. The number of papers published is increasing exponentially, and more emotions are being analysed, including valence-related matters, more complex signal processing procedures are increasingly performed, and machine-learning algorithms are being newly applied to develop automatic emotion recognition systems. The results suggest that VR might revolutionise emotion elicitation methods in laboratory environments in the next decade, and impact affective computing research, in many areas, opening new opportunities for the scientific community. However, more research is needed for the understanding of emotion dynamics in immersive VR and its validity in direct comparisons between simulated and real environments.
