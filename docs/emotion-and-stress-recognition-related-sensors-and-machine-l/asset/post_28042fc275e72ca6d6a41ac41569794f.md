Individuals can raise their cognitive effort to adapt to increasing difficulties until mental capacities are reached. Above this limit, performance decreases, increasing increase in errors, emergence of stress, and negative effects [2]. An adequate level of cognitive load for an individual is desirable, in order to perform a task in an optimal manner. Results from our transnational study show the existence of a biological basis for success in human-computer interaction [4]. Therefore, in the context of HCI, knowledge about cognitive load is essential in order to intelligently match the level and nature of the interaction in such systems. Universal content generation for distant learning and adaptive learning systems [5], practical training sessions [6], monitoring pilots [7], and truck drivers [8], usability testing and evaluation of user-interface and mobile applications [9], digital assistance providing personalized services for stress reduction and health risk prevention strategies [10] are some relevant fields of applications.

Estimation of cognitive load can be achieved via various measuring approaches, subjective measures, performance measures, physiological measures, and behavioral measures [11–15]. Traditional simple measures are based on subjective ratings, asking the users to perform a self-assessment of their mental state. These measures lack objectivity and are not reliable for computational recognition. They are generally used as ground truths in experiments, however with the disadvantage of being acquired after the fact. Performance measures are measured in parallel, but are sensitive to load variations. Physiological and behavioral measures are non-intensive methods which provide a more reliable and direct access to cognitive load in an objective way. Cognitive recognition using multimodal sensors has the potential to enhance the robustness and accuracy compared estimation from single modality. Unlike subjective measures prevalent in psychological research, cognitive load estimation is reliable for advanced computational techniques. Further, real-life investigation requires the implementation of mobile measures “in-the-world”. [16]. Despite all technological advancements, the measuring devices and sensors are reliable and sensitive to body movements, and are also low cost, and easy to use.

Various datasets were specifically collected for the study of cognitive load. Most of the studies are based on statistical approaches or functional resonance imaging (fMRI) [17,18], alternative methods including physiological [19,20], text [21,22], speech [23,24], brain [25,26], and pupil change [27,28], are used to detect cognitive load. The relationship between cognitive and writing behavior was measured using the CLix (Cognitive Load via Text), CLSk (Cognitive Load Sketching) and CLDag Digits datasets [29]. The datasets are composed of writing samples of 20 students under three cognitive load levels, induced from a writing task experiment. Speech-based cognitive load is supported by the Cognitive Load with Speech and Electroencephalography (CSE) dataset [30]. It includes records of 26 speeches for the determination of a speaker’s cognitive load based on acoustic features. Matzys et al. developed an experiment to induce cognitive load based on a concurrent visual search for the investigation of the impact of cognitive load on the Garner effect. The effect of visual presentation was also investigated for the detection of cognitive load by eye movement signals and for this purpose designed an experiment to induce cognitive load [31]. In last paper, Grant et al. summarize research activities and issue related to multimodal cognitive load recognition in the real world. They examine the use of various electroencephalography (EEG) features, eye analytics, linguistic features, skin conductance, facial activities and writing behavior. An extended version of the report is their book “Robust multimodal cognitive load measurement” presenting all the related issues in details [29].

As for the induction of emotional states, many studies focusing on basic emotions in discrete (i.e., fear, anger, joy, sadness, surprise or disgust) or dimensional (i.e., valence, arousal, dominance) models. These emotional states are especially induced using standardized pictures [32,33] for instance from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) [34] or relying on audiovisual stimuli [35] as movie clips [36,37] or music clips. Emotional states can also be induced by game scenarios asking the user to perform a task [39]. This elicitation method is especially useful for the induction of HCI relevant emotional states as Frustration and Interest [40]. These states are relevant in designing efficient and easy-to-use interactive systems, in interactive, educational and social practices [42], or in therapeutic settings providing tailored feedback to reduce frustration states [43].

Taylor et al conducted a study to induce Frustration based on the inclusion of latency between the user's touch and the reaction of the breakout engine [44]. A more recent study on Frustration is given by Aslam et al examining the effects of any factor on HCI elements of Frustration and disappointment [45]. For the induction of frustration, the authors asked the subjects to fill in a registration form, which fails twice based on intended systems, before it succeeds in the third time. Additionally, Lisner et al designed an experiment for the induction of six emotions including classified results physiological via wearable computers and human-robot interaction, Liu et al provided a comparative study of machine learning methods using physiological signals for the recognition of five different emotions including Frustration [47].

In their article “Interest–the curious emotion”, Silva focuses on the role of Interest in learning and motivates its creative resources in cultivating knowledge and expertise, and [48]. Ree et al present a concept of Interest in three ways: as a basic emotion, and as an emotion schema [49]. They explain the importance of Interest in educational settings as a mean to motivate high-quality engagement that leads to positive learning outcomes and as a motivational and cognitive resource that leads to better performance. According to Ellsworth, Interest is related to the universality of a positive state which may also lead to curiosity and hope, while lack of control can reduce results in Frustration. The recognition of Frustration and the system reaction can reduce resignation [50]. Thus, in a HCI context, providing excitement through an appropriate degree of urgency might increase Interest, while providing a level of controllability, preventing implicit systems can reduce frustration. The recognition of turning it into a Interest state are critical aspects for avoiding negative consequences and valuable for enhancing positive interaction effects.

Despite many studies investigating emotional and cognitive states, particularly Overload, Underload, Frustration, and Interest, the measurement still poses many challenging issues especially with respect to multimodal, mobile and temporal acquisition. Additionally, requiring the validation of the experimental design, most of the studies limit their to one subjective modality. Further, previous studies restrict their induction to either cognitive or emotional elicitation and relate both into one single dataset. In this paper we focus on these issues and present a dataset for affective computing research, based on systematic induction of cognitive load (Overload, Underload) and emotional states related to HCI (Interest, Frustration) as well as a neutral and transient state (1) designed and acquired in a mobile HCI setting, (2) based on multimodal sensor data, (3) involving temporal acquisition via three different modalities. Combining these challenging issues related to mobile, multimodal, temporal, and validated acquisition into one dataset for both cognitive and emotional states are the main contributions of this work.
