
There are three possible approaches to normalization. The first is to record reference conditions on the subject. The values obtained were normalized by subtracting the reference value, then dividing by the reference value (or subtracting the reference value), and then dividing by that same value. The second approach also requires reference conditions. Those values are included in the feature vector, which will have two characteristics that make up the “baseline matrix”. The third approach normalizes the data separately by obtaining a specific range, for example, between -1 and 1. This method applied to each feature independently ensures that all characteristics have the same range [38,29].

The effect of normalization and its influence on the process of emotion recognition is not evident. However, some studies show that normalization allows the characteristics to be generalized so that they can be used in cross-subject emotion recognition. Tangentially, data normalization helps machine learning algorithms’ efficiency due to faster convergence.

### 2.2. Preprocessing

EEG signals preprocessing includes signal cleaning and enhancement. EEG signals are weak and contaminated with noise from internal and external sources. Thus, these processes are essential to avoid contamination that could affect posterior positioning. The body might produce electrical impulses through blinking, eye or muscular movement, or heartbeats that blend with EEG signals. It should be carefully considered whether these artifacts should be removed because they have relevant emotional information and could improve emotion recognition algorithms’ performance. If filters are used, it is necessary to use them carefully to avoid diverting signals.

The three commonly used filter types in EEG are (1) low-frequency filters, (2) high-frequency filters (commonly used by electrical engineers as low-pass and high-pass filters), and (3) notch filters. The first two are used to filter frequencies between 1 and 60 Hz.

For EEG processing, filters such as Butterworth, Chebyshev, or inverse Chebyshev, are preferred [39]. Each of these filters has features that need to be addressed. A Butterworth filter has a flat response in the passband and a sharp transition zone. The Chebyshev filter has a ripple in the passband and a sharp transition, so it is monotonic on the stopband. The inverse Chebyshev has a flat response in the stopband and a sharp transition, and it has a ripple in the stopband. A Butterworth zero-phase filter should be used to prevent a phase shift because this filter goes forward and backward over the signal to solve this problem.

Another preprocessing objective is to clean the noise that may surround low-frequency signals generated by an external source, such as power line interference [40]. Notch filters are used to stop the passage of a specific frequency rather than a range. This filter is designed to eliminate frequencies originated from electrical networks, and it typically ranges from 50 to 60 Hz depending on the electrical signal’s frequency in the specific country.

All of these are used for artifact removal in EEG signals. However, as noted, care must be taken when using filters. Generally, filters could distort the EEG signal’s waveform and structure in the time domain. Hence, filtering should be kept to a minimum to avoid loss of EEG signal information.

Nevertheless, preprocessing helps to separate different signals and sources. Table 3 shows methods used for preprocessing EEG signals which are mentioned in the literature from 2015 to 2020. Common Average Reference (CAR) is right for noise reduction. SL is applied for spatial filtering to improve the signal’s spatial resolution. ### 2.3. Feature Extraction

Once signals are noisy, the BCI needs to extract essential features, which will be fed to the classifier. Time-domain features can be computed in the domain of (1) time, (2) frequency, (3) time-frequency, or (4) space, as shown in Table 4 [33,38,39]. This table presents the most popular techniques used for feature extraction, advantages, and limitations.

Time-domain features include the event-related potential (ERP), Hjorth features, and higher-order (HOC) [58–60], independent component analysis (ICA), principal component analysis (PCA), and Higuchi’s fractal dimensions (FD) as a measure of signal complexity and self-similarity in this domain. There are also statistical measures, such as power, mean, standard deviation, variance, skewness, kurtosis, and band energy. The latter evaluates signal randomness [61].

Among frequency-domain methods, the most popular is the fast Fourier transform (FFT). Auto-regressive (AR) modeling is an alternative to Fourier-based methods for computing the spectrum of a signal [62,63].

The time-frequency domain varies in time and frequency, which are very descriptive of the neural activities. For instance, wavelet transform (WT) and wavelet packet decomposition (WPD) are used [62].

The spatial information provided in the characterization of EEG signals’ characteristics is also considered in a broader approach. For this dimension, signals are referenced to digitally linked ears (DLE) values, which are calculated in terms of the left and right electrodes follows:

$$V_{DLE} = \frac{1}{2}(V_{A1} + V_{A2}),$$

where $V_{A1}$ and $V_{A2}$ are the reference voltages on the left and right ear. Thus, EEG data is broken down, considering each electrode. Consequently, each channel contains spatial information of the location pertinent to its source.

For spatial computation, the Laplacian (SL) algorithm reduces volume effects dramatically. SL also improves EEG spatial resolution by reducing the distortion produced by volume conduction and reference electrodes [47].
