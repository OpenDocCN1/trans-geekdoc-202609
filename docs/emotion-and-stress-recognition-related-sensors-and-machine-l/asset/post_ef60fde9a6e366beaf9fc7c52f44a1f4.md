# How Laboratory Experiments in the Wild Can Be Exploited for Monitoring Stress: A Bridge Between Laboratory and Daily Life

Yekta Can*, Dilamura Gokay, Dilrubra Reyyan Kılıç, Deniz Ekiz, Niaz Chalabianoo and Cem Esoy

Computer Engineering Department, Bogazici University, Bebek, 34342 Istanbul, Turkey; dilat.gokay@boun.tr (D.G.), dilrubra@boun.tr (D.R.K.), deniz.ekiziz@boun.tr (D.E.); niaz.chalabianoo@boun.tr (N.C.); eros@boun.tr (C.E.)

+ * Correspondence: yekta.can@boun.tr

Received: 3 January 2020; Accepted: 1 February 2020; Published: 4 February 2020

**Abstract:** Stress leads to poor well-being, and has effects on life quality and health. Society may have significant benefits from daily life stress detection system using unobtrusive wearable devices physiological signals. However, the performance of these systems is not accurate when used in unrestricted daily life compared to the systems tested in real-life and laboratory conditions. To test our stress level detection system that processes physiological signals, features and applies machine learning classifications, we used a laboratory experiment and ecological momentary assessment data collection with smartwatches in daily life. We investigated the effect of different labeling techniques and different training and testing environments. In the laboratory environments, we had controlled situations, and we could validate the perceived stress from self-reports. When machine learning models were developed in the laboratory instead of training them with the data coming from daily life, the accuracy of the system when tested in daily life improved significantly. The subjectivity effect coming from the self-reports in daily life be eliminated. Our system obtained high stress level detection accuracy results compared to most of the previous daily life studies.

**Keywords:** smart band; stress recognition; physiological signal processing; machine learning

Stress, an ever-growing issue in modern societies, has become an inseparable part of people's daily-lived continuously. Increasingly increasing workload, tight deadlines, and the resulting time pressure to control the increasing stress levels. Stress is an organism's reaction mechanism to stress. In a stressful state, certain control systems in the human body, as the autonomic nervous system (ANS), act unconsciously to control the responses by regulating some bodily functions. This mechanism has been constantly improved throughout human evolution, to create prompt reactions in life-threatening situations [1]. Stress symptoms can be measured and observed in numerous ways. The sympathetic nervous system (SENS) kicks the stress reaction, which will appear in the form of psychological, physiological, and behavioral interactions [1]. The bidirectional effects of the impact on the body and vice versa are also the major hallmarks of the autonomic nervous system (ANS), which has evolved in a way to have a direct role in human life and survival [2]. The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is divided into the sympathetic nervous system (SENS) and the parasympathetic nervous system (PSNS). Most of the studies investigating the effects of chronic psychological stress on the human body have concluded that the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems become over and under activated, while individual under psychological stress. The resulting abnormal activities of the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems cause physical, behavioral, and affective irregularities. In order to regulate the physiological arousal states, a balance of activity is expected between the sympathetic and parasympathetic subclasses of the autonomic nervous system (ANS). It is feasible to evaluate the autonomic nervous system (ANS) function through non-invasive physiological phenomena like the electrodermal (EDA) and heart rate variability (HRV). For example, the high frequency (HF) component of the HRV, which is one of the frequency domain characteristics of the PNS's activity. In contrast, low frequency (LF) reflects the activity of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) [3–5]. The final goal concerning all of the stress detection research is to find ways to notify the user about their stress levels and help them to manage it the social, economic, and health-related consequences.

Traditional approaches for measuring stress are taken either by a psychologist interviewing the subject to fill in particular questionnaires designed explicitly for self-reporting. Such interviews require the constant presence of a trained specialist. Requesting the subjects to fill out lists of questionnaires and stress reports are the most widely adopted as standard. However, these methods are cumbersome and entirely manual. There are also other concerns and drawbacks regarding the interviews, stress-reports, and questionnaires due to the way that different subjects commonly behave. These reports are based on or influenced by personal feelings, tastes, or opinions, they are highly subjective. As example, some people tend to respond to questions in a manner that may be viewed by others as exaggerated [6]. Gender differences also play a role in how men and women report and express their stress levels under stressors [7]. In contradiction to that, stress-reports are the closest to the ground. Any potential stress detection framework for daily life will be developed using a mechanism that trims the need for intervention from a psychologist. Research has emerged utilizing reliable and proven indicators such as sympathetic nervous system activity, skin temperature (ST), electrodermal activity (EDA), and electrocardiogram (EC). The purpose of adopting ML methods for raw psychophysiological data is to create meaningful features. The raw data are collected in this process and transformed into information-containing features. Some of those features would be well while assigning labels. Stress questionnaires and daily momentary assessments (EMAs). The primary purpose of the whole process is to train machine learning algorithms to diagnose different types of behavioral and emotional states by using EMAs, known context, and test the system using records of any feature recorded in the future. Furthermore, as the process for almost all machine learning systems is the same, analysis of these signals by frequency and intensity is experienced by the subjects. Preliminary studies of stress detection devices sold in laboratory environments, realized that the stress level experienced in the laboratory is different from daily life [10]. However, the problems encountered in this new environment were more complicated and intricate. Achieving precise annotations and identification of perceived stress in the wild is a difficult task due to high diversity in human psychological evaluation and lack of direct observation over subject activities. Furthermore, unrestricted movements of subjects in the wild may cause artifacts in the signal data. Lastly, since medical-grade devices with cables, electrodes and boards cannot be used in daily life due to their obtrusive nature, more pervasive and comfortable devices should be used. However, alternatives as smart bands and smartwatches have lower data quality than those proposed for laboratory environments. Smartwatches and smart bands are unobtrusive, easy without specific actions, and are more suitable for daily life. They are adopted by consumers and easily available on the market. Most of them are equipped with photopleysmography (PPG) sensors [12]. Solution is also easily applicable to consumers.

The given complexity of data acquisition performed in the wild adds to the advantages of in-lab data; consequently, the question arises as whether it is possible to combine these systems and use high accuracy for the sake of the latter. One of the questions that stands out is the feasibility of these two systems to achieve even better results. In this study, we provide a framework by which it is possible to design a stress detection mechanism that utilizes both methods. The data collected in a laboratory can be used for long-term performance evaluation of the same system in daily life scenarios.

Our work contributes to state-of-the-art in four different aspects:

- Developing laboratory-based methods to improve the performance of daily life stress detection systems;
- Comparing the performance of daily life, and hybrid laboratory-daily life models;
- Collection of smartwatch-based EDA and HRV data coming from the laboratory and daily life (14 participants, 103 h of physiological data with 388 emotional momentary assessments (EMAs)), with self-reports and context information;
- Investigating the effect of using context and self-report labels when we train the model on system accuracy in different environments.

These contributions provide insights for researchers to improve the performance of daily life stress detection systems.

## 2. Related Work

Most of the automatic stress detection studies in the literature were conducted either in the laboratory environments or restricted daily life environments. We examine the studies in the five different classes. The first class develops a laboratory model with context labels and tests in the same environment. Since the stress levels (i.e. the context participants) are known at any time in laboratory experiments, they could be used as the ground truth labels for machine learning (ML) models, and we called this type laboratory-to-known context (LLKC) models. The second type uses collected self-report labels in laboratory environments and tests the created model in the same environment. This type (LLSR) models. The third type uses self-report questionnaires in the wild and testing the model in the same environment. We named this model daily-to-daily self-report (DDSR) models. Since we could not monitor the everyday life of participants and get the ground truth all the time, a known context does not exist in daily life environments.
