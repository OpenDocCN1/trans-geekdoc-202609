DTW aligns two series using the shortest path in a distance matrix, the path with the minimal global warping distance [25,26], finding the most representative distance of the overall difference [20]. However, comprehensive comparison of representation methods and distance measures of time series reveals inconsistencies and contradictions in the observations reported in individual studies [23]. An important consequence is that experimental results can be generated without critically reviewing the assumptions for a particular research context and design. As concluded in [23], “there is no clear evidence that one similarity measure exists that is superior to others in the literature of terms. While some similarity measures are more effective on certain data sets, they are generally inferior on other data sets (29). The DTW out follows Euclidean distance in a variety of studies [27]. Other types of measures are “Edit measures” and “Threshold measures”. The former includes, for instance, Long Common Sub-Sequence LCS, Edit on Real sequence Edit and Edit with Distance Penalty Edit The latter includes Tightness of Lower Bounds LB. The accuracy of the aforementioned types is close to the accuracy of DTW, but is much simpler [23,28]. We concluded to use DTW to assess the similarity of the physiological time series.

To assess the geometric shape of a curve or segment, other distance measures, such as the Frechet distance [29], can be used [30–32]. “The Frechet distance is typically explained as the relationship between a person and a dog and a dog walking along the two curves and trying to keep the leash as short as possible. The maximum length the leash reaches is the value of the Frechet distance [33] (p 7). We use the Frechet distance to assess the geometric similarity of time series sensor measurements of the same physiological parameter (e.g., GSR) on the same participant but with different sensors.



The correlation of time series has been investigated for decades, in diverse levels. Here, our focus is on time series correlation [34]; first, the correlation of equal-type physiological parameters measured by different sensors at the same time in order to quantify differences between low-cost and uncalibrated sensors versus high-end and calibrated laboratory sensor equipment; however, the correlation between physiological parameters of different types, for instance, IBI and GSR, to potentially identify hidden relations.

According to [34], the Pearson’s correlation coefficient is the most robust metric measuring the similarity in physiological time series—where robustness is unintended to small values. However, Pearson’s is highly sensitive to outliers and considers only linear relations. Spearman’s correlation coefficient (rho)—is the same—based on the rank of values rather than the values themselves; it measures monotonicity rather than linearity. Using Spearman’s rho to measure the strength of the associations between two variables leaves room for interpretation [35].

The human cardiovascular system and the autonomic nervous system are non-linear interactions between different physiological parameter. To explore possible underlying nonlinear interactions, here, use the Maximum Information Coefficient (MIC) [36,37]. Several studies show the possibility of gaining new insights into such non-linear interactions when applying the MIC, for instance, in the interactions between neural and respiratory dynamics [38].

Further, one method to assess the temporal lag (or lead) between pairs of time series is the cross-correlation function in the time domain [39]. For getting meaningful cross-correlation results, the time series needs to be stationary, i.e., having a constant mean and variance. Time series stationarity can be tested, for instance, the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test [40]. Unless the time series is stationary, it needs to be differenced and tested for stationarity.
