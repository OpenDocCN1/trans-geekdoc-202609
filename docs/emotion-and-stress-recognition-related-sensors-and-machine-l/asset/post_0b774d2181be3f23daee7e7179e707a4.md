
With the recent development of mobile sensors for ECG recording, HRV analysis and stress studies have been actively carried out. However, due to the inherent limitations of HRV analysis, which requires sufficient data to observe the variability, the longer the time window of the ECG record for an HRV analysis the more accurate statistical characteristics will be. In other, it is difficult to perform an HRV analysis with short-term ECG measurements. Some research has demonstrated the limited window required for an HRV analysis. Carr et al. [7] recommended at least a 5 min ECG series be used. To do so, computational algorithms such as the Pan-Tompkins algorithm [8] can be used. These algorithms require various preprocessing steps, such as filtering and QRS detection. Similarly, the classical HRV approach requires preprocessing steps with a limited window.

To recognize stress states, previous studies [9–11] have reported classical HRV analysis using machine learning algorithms, such as the support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), adaptive boosting (AB), and logistic regression (LR) methods, along with the ECG and other physiological signals. Other physiological signals include skin conductance (SC) [9,11], respiration [9,11], and skin temperature [11].

Table 1 shows a summary of previous studies which used conventional machine learning algorithms, as well as DNNs. The column "Window length" refers to the duration of the ECG measurement used to extract the HRV parameters. The column "Performance" indicates the accuracy of the classifier shown in Table 1. Most studies used more than one classifier, but only those that gave the best results. The study [11] used different stressors to the other studies [10], including the stress color word test (SCWT), mental arithmetic (MA), and counting tasks. They simulated an experiment where ECG was recorded on two different days. Castella et al. [10] designed an experiment where the participants were undergoing a university examination, and the next day was after a vacation. These studies (which were performed in a laboratory-controlled environment) showed an accuracy of 85%, outside of the accuracy reached only 80% [10]. These results might indicate the limitations of the stressors used in the laboratory environment and of the conventional machine learning methods. A conventional machine learning method based on HRV features involves not only the preprocessing of the ECG signals, but also feature selection among the HRV parameters.

As the deep neural network (DNN) approach has recently demonstrated outperforming pattern recognition accuracy [5], some [12,13,16–21] have utilized DNNs for biomedical engineering applications, including heart arrhythmia classification, medical image classification and enhancement, stress detection, and other medical diagnoses. U-net [21], which consists of a convolutional neural network (CNN), forms an autoencoder skip-connection between the encoder and decoder. This architecture could be used in medical imaging applications, including augmentation, classification, and detection. Hann et al. [16] achieved a performance in arrhythmia detection using deep convolutional neural network comparable to that of cardiologists.

Huang et al. [12] proposed the DeepCNN method, which detected stress using an ultra-short (10 s) ECG. They used raw ECG signals for the inputs of the DNN without extracting the HRV parameters. A model was configured with one CNN and two long short-term memory (LSTM) [22] models. They suggested selecting the hyperparameter values for the convolution filter and pooling window width, respectively of 0.6 and 0.8 s. Both are capable of covering the QRS-complex of HRV and pooling window width. They designed an experiment for inducing mental and physical stresses there were 20 and 30 participants, separated into two. In the two cases, the method [12] reached 87.3% and 73.96% accuracy. Saed et al. [13] suggested a multitask convolutional neural network (MT-CNN). Raw physiological signals with the heart rate (HR) derived from an ECG and SC were fed to the MT-CNN to detect stress. There was no mention of how long the duration of the ECG was.

## 2. Material and Methods

### 2.1. Subjects and Data Acquisition

PhysNet [2] offers access to a large number of records. It consists of various physiological records, ECG, electrocardiography, and respiration signals, which were recorded under various conditions. Healey et al. [14] tried to monitor real-world stress during driving. Among the recorded physiological records, we chose the ECG, which was sampled at 49 Hz. Modified load II electrodes were used to measure the ECG. Sixteen participant's records were uploaded to PhysNet. We excluded 2 subject's data, which did not contain a marker for when the participants changed the driving region to highway or city. Finally, we selected 14 subject's records for our use in study. All the selected records included approximately 50-85 min of ECG during driving and resting. Due to differences in traffic conditions, the total duration of experiments differed by subject. The participants were asked to make a rest for 15 min before and after driving.
