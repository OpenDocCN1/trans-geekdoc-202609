Early experimental practices in the laboratory were the first building blocks of this research field. These preliminary studies provided researchers with the the idea of choosing the most proper devices, features, and machine learning algorithms that can be used later in everyday life settings. In a trade-off between unobtrusiveness and accuracy chose the types of devices with different sensing technologies based on the requirements of their study. Several researchers demonstrated that wearable devices with PPG sensors added more comfort and convenience to ECG devices for HRV measurements [28–30]. Nevertheless, single-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) devices are becoming more popular, easy to use, and commercially available over time. In a recent study by Blicit, single-lead ECG was used to detect the autonomic nervous response by monitoring the heart rate and HRV features [31]. In [14], Zubrait used sensors to detect the stress values of the test subjects. In their designed scheme, EDA and the accelerometer were used together to enhance the detection accuracy. Their experiments resulted 91% accuracy for two-class stress-relaxed classification. Castaldo et administered in a laboratory environment to investigate the effects of ultra-short HRV (heart rate variability) with a two minute duration on stress detection [15]. By using the support vector machine (SVM) to HRV classification, they scored 70% accuracy on their binary classification problem. Although 70% accuracy seems low compared to other studies in the same year, they speculated that this could be due the stress induction methods used and ultra-short HRV features. Tivatakis et al. used HRV features from ECG signals and facial features to recognize negative emotions in the laboratory environment. Subjects were exposed to the International Affective Picture System (IAPs) sets of disturbing images for four minutes. After using the SVM classifier on the extracted features, classification for the negative emotion detection was 83% [21]. All of the in-implemented classes mentioned above used only one sensor, either EDA or ECG, in this proposed stress detection mechanism. Other students practiced multimodality by employing multiple sensors to achieve even higher accuracy. In a recent stress detection and classification research, Akaman et al. [19] used blood pressure, heart rate, and respiration rates of the participants.

In other cases, researchers sought to mitigate the trade-off between accuracy and unobtrusiveness and conducted their studies in the lab or in using unobtrusive wearable devices. The device of type (i.e., whether to use medical-grade obstructive devices with glasses, electrodes, or not) is not important because it determines the applicability of the system to daily life, which is the main and final goal of all the stress detection studies. In some of the studies, the data were collected in the wild and tested in these environments [22–25](DDSR model in the taxonomy). Ciman et al. [22] detected stress using smartphone usage behaviors. In the controlled laboratory environment, by developing an Android app with search and tagging tasks, participants' lap, swipe, scroll, and touch gestures were used to induce stress the participants. They obtained approximately 86% stress detection accuracy using SVM, KNN, and physical activity of this, the light values of the screen, and the events related to the phone screen were recorded in the wild experiment. The classification accuracy was about 70% with the same classifiers. Vijdounaite et al. [24] used mobile phone usage data and implemented MPM (Maximum Posterior Regression) and HMM (Hidden Markov Model) for stress.

Mishra et al. [25] detected stress by analyzing heart signals captured by Moto 60. The data were collected from 23 subjects for three days. They also added activity to physiological features to increase the accuracy. Random forest (RF) was used for the classification of stressed and relaxed individuals. They increased their F1 score from 0.50 to 0.76. Gjorski et al. used a continuous stress detection scheme, which consisted of a base detection using the activity recognizer for identifying the contextual information and a real-life stress detection mechanism that utilized the result from the laboratory for real-life stress detection. While training the model, they used only the context label in the laboratory environment (LDKC-type of model). They did not collect self-reports during these tests. The classification accuracy of their stress detection system built using context information in our taxonomy were increased to 98% by adding the activity type. Li et al. [27] built their model in the lab, verified performance in the lab with different participants, and later verified it test with new subjects. They used mental tasks to induce stress and seven-point Likert self-reports as labels in the laboratory (LDSR model). They evaluated their system performance using the Elastic-net regression technique. The correlation results of predicted labels and true self-reports were 0.72 and 0.56 respectively. The performance of daily life systems is lower than studies conducted in environments due to the mentioned issues such as low-quality physiological signals and the difficulty of collecting truth [33,34] (see Table 1).

# 3. Methodology

3.1. Laboratory Data Collection
We collected laboratory data from the participants during a psychological experiment using our implementation of Trial Stress Test (TST) [35]. The social test has been proven to be an effective way of stress after the baseline condition we created at the beginning of the experiment. This experiment was conducted on 14 participants who were university students between 20 and 25. There were nine male and female participants.

The experiment, which took approximately 1 hour, had the following steps:

1. Setup
2. Pre-stress measurements (baseline)
3. The TST (Trier Social Stress Test) (inducing stress)
4. Post-stress recovery measurements (recovery)

The communication language between interviewers and the participants was Turkish. Please note that the mother of all participants was Turkish. In addition to that, they knew English as a foreign language. This circumstance affected stress induction.
