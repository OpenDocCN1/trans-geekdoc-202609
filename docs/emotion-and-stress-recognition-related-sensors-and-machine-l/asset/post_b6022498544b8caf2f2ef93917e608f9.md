
# Previous Works

Previous works focused on the acquisition of data from a single user, i.e., where researchers train the network on a specific user's face. This approach allows user to train the network with precision on his/her face, reaching advanced recognition performance [1,4]. Our work presents a general approach using a multi-user dataset, containing images of users who were different ranging age, cultural background, gender and appearance. To that end, we used advanced methods of deep learning, transfer learning and continuous learning.

Multidisciplinary studies at the basis of our work in artificial intelligence (AI) stressed the importance of computer science for automated real-life tasks for assistive technologies [13,14]. Among them, label detection and lip-ripping [6] constitute our main background point [15].

# Problem Description and Proposed Solution

Our study exploits the high precision of CNNs by processing images to recognise emotional states using the most recent advances in affective computing. Affective computing is a novel approach in artificial intelligence, for the first time in 2003 by Rosalind Picard [16], becoming one of the most trending multidisciplinary studies. Affective computing, involving the collaboration of several disciplines, e.g., psychology, physiology, liberal studies, computer science and robotics, recently stressed the importance of emotion and recognition of affective mental states, e.g., emotions, moods and personality traits. Notwithstanding the interest in this new research, most of the available research focuses on sentiment analysis or pure theoretical models (e.g., in psychology and social sciences) and product-based applications [17], for marketing purposes. We prefer to focus on self-aid [18], health management and communication for supporting and understanding humans in real-life problems for any demanding task (e.g., due to disabilities [19], psychological states in emergencies or particular environments) with automated detectors and assistive assistants with machine intelligence capabilities [20].

In our connected world, individuals already use and produce an overwhelming amount of heterogeneous data in a manageable and personalised subset of datasets. Data can be directly used for emotion recognition (ER), e.g., for photos and text shared on social networks, or can contain elements inherently inferred for emotional intensity, such as physiological data carried by wearables-including sleep patterns, continuous heart-rate and movement tracking, emotions expressed by [21] and data from users experiencing exciting games [22]. Sentiment analysis relates only to recognising the positivity, negativity or neutrality of sentiments, moods and emotions, the process of emotion recognition is still an understudied field, implying the recognition of emotions of an emotional model. Various models have been proposed to recognize ER is in a application domain tasks from the widely recognised model of Ekan, which we chose for our study. Recent research updates [23] can practise Ekan's primary emotional states, such as happiness, sadness, anger, disgust and neutral on text [24] and physiological cues such as heart rate, conductance, gestures, facial expression and sound, which can be managed with a multidimensional approach [11] and compared.

Among all approaches, facial recognition is still predominant. Under that point of view, we decided to focus on the mouth as a facial element, always visible in any facial expression, even that some cultures underestimate the mouth's expressiveness more than others. Others from South America, as Ekan himself underlined in his first experiments on facial emotions labelling [9], rely more on the upper part of the face. The new issue is that, in general, when comparing such original results with ours in AI, the neutral emotion seems most misunderstood by humans and artificial agents; it tends to be labelled as anger or sadness. The fact that the same emotion is also the most mistaken by automatic mouth-based recognisers in the same classes of errors confirms that the automatic agent can recognise images correctly, based on human labels.

In this work, we tested the technique on multi-user datasets, to find solutions to the following research questions:

- With how much precision is it possible to recognise facial emotions solely from the mouth?
- Is the proposed technique capable of recognising emotions if trained on a generalised set of facial images?

In a user-centred implementation, the software supports personalised emotional feedback for each user: personal traits, such as scars or flaws, and individual variations in emotional feeling and expression, helping the training to precise recognition. The system can practise different users because it was trained on a comprehensive dataset, including images varying in ethnicity, age and gender.

In order to obtain optimised results, the ambient light setting should not require a proper setup.

- Robustness. The algorithm must be able to operate in the presence of low-quality data (e.g., low resolution or bad light conditions);
- Scalability. The user's position should not be necessarily fixed in front of the camera, in order to avoid constraining the person. Therefore, the software should be able to recognise the user despite the shot's point of view;
- Luminosity. Luminosity is an important issue because the variation of light harshly influences the recognition capabilities of a CNN-based system. Available datasets usually provide photos shot before a lighting setup. On the contrary, a sufficient number of training samples of each considered lighting (e.g., natural light, artificial bulbs and low-light conditions) should be provided for an efficient categorisation.

Our implementation of mouth-based ER explores transfer learning (i.e., knowledge transfer), which uses general-purpose neural networks pre-trained on extensive datasets including shapes, fine-tuned on the classification domain (i.e., mouth-based emotion recognition). Our application can be easily adapted for continuous learning, in a domain where said method is useful, and appropriate data serve as feedback to track the weights of the neural network and execute the training. Such a method should allow enhancing the precision of the emotion recognition in real-time situations, while the final weights of a previous training can be used in a subsequent time. The collateral effect of such a technique is the requirement of more exceptional computational capabilities and a semi-supervised system to stop and roll back in cases of glaring errors or overfitting in a specific environment, e.g., a particular camera resolution or light situation in a specific place or with time constraints. Continuous learning can continuously adapt and enhance the network's accuracy, leading the go to a more reliable system, and for a prolonged time.

# 3. The Framework Implementation

The proposed implementation has been developed using the Python programming language and the Keras framework [25]. The tests were carried out using the Google Colab platform, with the Python code was developed and executed, exploiting the powerfulness of the Keras libraries. Data analysis and processing have been developed with a chain of operations. The implemented procedure can be described with the following steps:

1. Raw dataset import. In the first phase, the dataset (at the beginning, a raw dataset) is composed of RGB images and is imported into the system.
2. Raw dataset cleaning. All the faces in the photos of the dataset are manually scanned, and the images representing fake emotions (e.g., simulated by professional actors) are discarded.
3. Data generation. From each image of the raw dataset, the portion representing the mouth of the subject is automatically extracted.
4. Training with data augmentation. At every step of the training, the system reads a partition of images from the dataset. At each reading, the system performs data augmentation [26] using random values of rotation degree within a given range. The model used for image analysis is exploited using transfer learning.
5. Model generation. At the end of the training, the structure and weights of the neurons that achieved the best performance in training are saved.
6. Results Evaluation. To evaluate the quality of the model, we analyse the accuracy of ER on the validation set.

The extraction from images of the raw dataset was carried out using as a pre-trained neural network the shape_predictor_68_face_landmarks CNN [2,8,27], which produced in output 68 landmarks, detected for each image. The shape predictor was pre-trained on the ib300-W dataset. The landmarks are expressed as a series of coordinates identifying elements of a face, e.g., the positions of the mouth, eyes and cheekbones. We used these to identify the area of the mouth and cropped the image (i.e., cutting out the part related to the mouth). All images of the mouth were transformed in size to 299 x 299 pixels.

In Figure 1, an example of mouth detection, cropping and resizing is shown. On the left side, an image taken from the dataset is visible on the right, the corresponding extracted mouth. At the end of the process, the mouth images were stored in a directory structure with the labelled emotion; a final inspection of the mouth images dataset shows that the mouth is always correct, whether it is open or not. We used data augmentation techniques [26] to increase the robustness of the neural network, by randomly transforming the images, i.e., with a flip and a random rotation within the [-4,+4] degree range.
