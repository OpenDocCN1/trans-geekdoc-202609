## 3.5.5. Multi-Layer Perceptron

In a multi-layer perceptron structure, transfer functions are used for mapping inputs to the output. These functions use sigmoid function, rectified unit linear, and hyperbolic tangent. The classifier uses back-propagation to classify instances. Multi-layer perceptrons are trained by minimizing the squared error of the network output, essentially treating it as an estimate of the class probability, which is given by the following equation,

```
$E=\frac{1}{2}(y-f(x)^2),$
```

where $f(x)$ is the network prediction obtained from the output unit and $y$ is the instance class.

## 4. Results and Discussion

### 4.1. Dataset

A total of 33 participants related to the education volunteered for this study. The participants reported no history of injury and were not using any medications that could have affected their brain activity at the time of experiment. Among these 33 healthy participants, 20 were male and 13 were females (60.6% male and 39.4 female). The participant's ages ranged from 18 to 40 years $(\mu=23.5, SD=5.48)$. In line with the Helsinki Declaration [37] and the departmental guidelines, all participants of the study were briefed about the research goals. In addition, a signed informed consent was obtained from each participant. This study was approved by the Directorate of Advanced Studies and Research at the University of Engineering and Technology.

### 4.2. Performance Parameters

The parameters used in this study include average accuracy, Kappa statistic, F-measure, mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean absolute error (RMAE). The F-measure is calculated by considering the precision and recall values. A value less than zero shows that the classification level is perfect classification. The Kappa statistic values between 0 and 1, means perfect classification. For stress classification, the generalization performance of the proposed system was tested using cross validation to avoid over- and under-fitting as well as make the proposed system adopts to unseen data. A 10-fold cross validation technique was used the training data was randomly divided into ten equal parts (nine for train and one part for test) and the process was repeated 10 times. During the process, every instance was used for testing at a time and the remaining instances were used for training of the classifier.

### 4.3. Stress and Control

The scores acquired from participants using the PSS questionnaire are shown in Figure 4. The green and red bars represent the PSS scores of participants belonging to control and stress groups respectively. The yellow bars indicate the PSS scores of participants not considered in the stress or the control group. Overall, for the PSS scores we have $(\mu,\sigma)=(20.4\pm6.14)$. A participant with a PSS score below 17.73 was considered to be in control group, whereas a participant with a PSS score higher than 23.47 was categorized in the stress group. These values were calculated using the threshold criteria defined in Equation (6). 12 participants were put into the stress group (red bars) and 9 into the control group (green bars).

In expert (hybrid) evaluation, the psychology expert considered both PSS scores and the symptoms obtained from the interview method. The expert interviewed each participant for an average duration of 15 minutes. Out of the 33 participants, 10 were assigned to the stress group and 10 were assigned to the control group. The details about each participant regarding gender, age, PSS score, the label assigned by using PSS score, and the label assigned by expert is in Table 2. There were fifteen differences in the assigned labels between those using PSS scores and the expert (hybrid) evaluation. The experimental results show that expert (hybrid) labeling helps in the classification of long-term stress. It is important to note that in a majority of the cases regarding label mismatch (13 out of 15), the PSS score ranges between 17 and 25, which covers the neutral range. We hypothesize that the expert (hybrid) labeling is better suited for the classification task, we have used these labels as ground truth.

### 4.4. Feature Selection Using t-test

We used a two-sided Student's t-test with a significance level of 0.05 and results are shown in Table 3 for different oscillations. For the t-test, the degree of freedom was 9 and the null hypothesis was tested for stress and control groups. It is evident that at a confidence level of 0.95, none of the extracted features were found statistically significant in the stress and control group when PSS-labeling was used for the reference standard. It is also that beta and gamma waves from AF3 were significant features in the stress and control group, labels acquired by experiment evaluation were used as a reference standard. Additional features, namely frontal (α1) and temporal (P1–P5) beta features, and alpha symmetry (α2), were also used (see Equations (1–5)). Results of t-test over these features in stress and control groups are presented in Table 4. It can be seen that alpha symmetry is statistically significant between the stress and control group using experiment-based labeling. Significant features, namely beta (AF3), gamma (AF3), and alpha symmetry were selected for long-term stress classification based on results of t-test. A p-value of 0.03 and 0.04 for these features showed statistical significance. The p-value of alpha symmetry from frontal and temporal channels was 0.005, indicating the statistical significance of alpha symmetry from both temporal and frontal regions.

The box plots are presented in Figure 5, where the first row features acquired by PSS labeling include alpha, beta, and gamma respectively. The second row shows the same features acquired through experiment evaluation. A comparatively box plot shows the features are in agreement with the median values. A taller box plot suggests different distribution within methods. From box plots (Figure 5b,c,d) it is observed that there is no difference in the beta and relative gamma features to differentiate stress and control groups for expert- and PSS-based labels. However, Figure 5d candidates are good features as they appear to differentiate the stress and control group. In Figure 5a, the alpha symmetry for the stressed group does not have a longer whisker, which shows alpha symmetry is not varied along the negative quantile, while in Figure 5d, the stressed group had varied alpha symmetry as shown by the lower and upper whiskers. Also, in Figure 5d the median is comparatively at the center of the distribution. This suggests that alpha symmetry is a good candidate to be used in the stress classification task.
