
# Emotion Assessment Using Feature Fusion Classification Based on Physiological Data: Are We There Yet?

**Abstract:** Emotion recognition based on physiological data classification has been a topic of growing interest for more than a decade. However, there is a lack of systematic analysis in literature regarding the selection of classifiers, sensor features, and range of expected accuracy, just a few limitations. In this work we evaluate emotion in terms of low/high arousal and valence classification through Supervised Learning (SL), Decision Fusion (DF) and Feature Fusion (FF) using multimodal physiological data, Electrocardiogram (ECG), Electrodermal Activity (EDA), Res (RIF), or Blood Pulse Volume (BVP). The main contribution of our work is a systematic study across five public datasets commonly in the Emotion Recognition (ER) state-of-the-art, namely: (1) Classification performance of ER marking across the datasets (in the arousal/valence space); (2) Summarising the ranges of classification accuracy reported across the literature; (3) Comparing the results for different classifiers, sensor modalities and feature set combinations for ER accuracy and f1-score; (4) Exploration of an extended feature set for each modality; (5) Systematic analysis of multimodal classification in DF and FF approaches. The experimental results showed that FF is the most competitive technique in results to the state-of-the-art for the selected datasets.

**Keywords:** emotion recognition; physiological signals; machine learning; signal processing

Emotion is an integral part of human behaviour, exerting a powerful influence in mechanisms such as perception, attention, decision making and learning. Indeed, when humans tend to notice and remember commonplace events that those ones evoke of joy, sorrow, pleasure, or pain [1]. Therefore, understanding emotional states is crucial to understand human behaviour, cognition and decision making. The computer science field dedicated to the study of emotions is Affective Computing, which modern potential applications include, (1) automated driver assistance–e.g., through an alert system monitoring for drowsiness or unhealthy states potentially hindering driving; (2) healthcare–e.g., through wellness monitoring applications identifying causes of stress, anxiety, depression or chronic diseases; (3) adaptive learning–e.g., through a teaching application to adjust the content delivery rate and iterations according to the user enthusiasm and frustration level; (4) recommendation systems—e.g. assisting and personalised content according to the user preferences as perceived by your response.

Emotions are communicated via external (facial or body expressions (alterations in heart rate (HR), respiration rate, perspiration, and others) and internal body expressions. These manifestations occur naturally and unconsciously, and their septic modulation can be used to influence the subjects' current emotional state. In a acquired systematic setting, it could be possible to influence the subjects' mood for the following day and their health condition.

External physical manifestations (e.g., facial expressions) are easily carried through a camera; however, present they low reliability which depend heavily on the user environment (if the subject is alone or in a group setting), or cultural background (if the subject grew in a society promoting the externalisation or internalisation of emotion), and can be faked or manipulated according to the subject goals, compromising the assessment of the true emotional state [2]. On the other hand, for internal physical manifestations, these constraints are less prominent, as the subject has little control over his body states. Alterations in physiological signals are not easily controlled by the subject, these entitle a more authentic insight into the subjective emotional experience.

Given these considerations, our work aims to perform a comprehensive study on emotion recognition using physiological data, such as Electrocardiogram (ECG), Electrodermal Activity (EDA), Respiration (RESP), Blood Pulse Volume (BVP). This choice of modalities is due to three factors: (1) Data can be extracted easily from portable, wearable technology, rather than more intuitive sensors (e.g., Electroencephalography (EEG), or Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fIRS)); (2) Widely reported in the literature state-of-the-art; (3) We use two major techniques: Feature Fusion (FF) versus Decision Fusion (DF) on a feature-based representation. Furthermore, in our model, the user's emotional response is assessed on the two-dimensional space: Valence (meaning unpleasant or pleasant) and Arousal (meaning emotional intensity).

The remaining of this paper is organised as follows: Section 2 presents a literature review on ER, with special emphasis on articles that describe the datasets used. Section 3 describes the overall machine learning pipeline of the proposed methods. Section 4 evaluates our methodology in public datasets. Finally, in Section 5, the main conclusions of work are presented.

## State of the Art

In literature, human emotion processing is generally described using two models: deconstructing emotion into basic/primary (derived from innate behaviour) and complex/secondary (derived from cognitive processes) [3,4]. On the other hand, the second model quantifies emotions into continuous dimensions. A popular model, proposed by Lang [5], uses valence (unpleasant-pleasant level) versus arousal (activation level) two-dimensional model [6], which we adopt in this work. Concerning affect, it is generally performed through film [6], virtual reality [7], music [8], or stressful environments [6], with no commonly established norm which is the optimal methodology for ER.

Regarding the automated recognition of emotional states, it is usually performed based on two methodologies: Machine Learning (ML) techniques [12–14]; Deep learning approaches [15–17]. Due to the limited size of datasets, most of the work focuses on traditional ML, in particular Supervised Learning (SL), such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) [18–20], k-Nearest Neighbour (KNN), Decision Trees (DT) [24,25], and others [26], namely Electroencephalography (EEG) [28–30], Electrocardiography (ECG) [31–33], Electrodermal Activity (EDA) [34–36], Respiration (RESP) [24], Blood Volume Pulse (BVP) [25–35] and Temperature (TEMP) [26]. Multi-modal approaches have prevailed; however, there is no clear evidence of which feature combinations and physiological signals are the most relevant. The literature shows that classification performance improves with the simultaneous exploitation of modalities [28,10,37], and that modality fusion can be performed at two levels: FF [24,38,39] and DF [8,5,7,40,41]. In the former, features are extracted from each modality and later concatenated to a single feature vector, to be used as input for the ML model. The other hand, in DF, from each modality, a feature is extracted to form a classification prediction through a voting system. With k modalities, k classifiers will be created to yield a final result. Both methodologies are found in the state-of-the-art [42], but it is considered the best to use in the ER using multimodal physiological data from non-intrusive wearable technology.

Detailed information on the state-of-the-art in a more generalized perspective; we review the surveys [21,11–47] and references therein, where a comprehensive review of the literature for working on ER with physiological signals can be found, highlighting the main achievements, challenges, take-home messages, and possible futures. The present work extends the state-of-the-art of ER through:
1. Classification performance in the analysis of the arousal/valence space, of ER for five publicly datasets that cover multiple elicitations;
2. Summarising the rates of classification accuracy across the literature for the evaluated datasets;
3. Characterising the results for diverse classifiers, modality and feature combinations for ER using classification accuracy and F1-score as evaluation metrics (the later being commonly reported as important to evaluation classification bias);
4. Exploration of an extended feature set for modality analysis, and relevance through feature selection;
5. Systematic analysis of multimodal classification in DF and FF approaches, with superior or comparable results to those reported in the state-of-the-art for the selected datasets.

To evaluate the classification accuracy in ER from physiological signals, we adopted the two Dimensional Valence/Arousal space. As previously mentioned, the ECG, RESP, EDA, and BVP signals are used, and we compare FF and DF techniques in a feature space based framework. The forthcoming sub-sections, a more detailed description of each approach is presented.

As previously mentioned, when working with multi-modal approaches the exploitation of the different signals can be performed using different techniques. We start by testing the FF technique. In FF, the features are independently extracted from each sensor modality (in our case ECG, BVP, EDA, and RESP) and concatenated afterwards to form a single, global, feature vector (57 features for EDA, 372 for BVP, and 487 for RESP), implemented and detailed in the BioSPPy software library https://github.com/PIA-Group/BioSPPy. Additionally, we applied sequential feature selection (SFFS) in order to preserve the most informative features, and save time and computational power of the machine learning algorithm applied in the next step. All the presented methods were implemented in Python and made available as open source software https://github.com/PIA-Group/BioSPPy.
