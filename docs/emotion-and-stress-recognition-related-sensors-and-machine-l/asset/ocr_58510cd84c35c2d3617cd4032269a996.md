Overall, these methods can solve the FER tasks in laboratory settings, where emotion images are produced in a controlled manner. However, with the introduction of relatively large real-world databases from emotion recognition competitions such as FER2013 [31] and emotion recognition in the wild [32–36], FER have a big transition from laboratory-controlled settings to challenging real-world scenarios. Traditional methods have many difficulties in solving intra-class similarity and inter-class differences, and are reported to be incapable of addressing the great diversity of factors unrelated to facial tasks.

Recently, the development of deep learning and the increase in the number of powerful CNN architectures [37] in the computer vision field imply using deep learning methods when building FER. For example, the winner of the 2013 International Conference on Machine Learning [41] combined a CNN with a support vector machine (SVM) to distinguish emotions. However, despite the impressive feature learning ability of deep learning, difficulties remain when applying to FER such as training data, the inter-class similarity, the inter-class differences, and the imbalance of emotion classes appearing in existing real-world facial databases. This work is taking advantage of deep models to extract robust facial features and translate them to recognize facial faces. Studies in FER have suffered from the lack of data for training deep CNN models, which may have resulted in overfitting. Over this problem task, transfer learning has been widely used for facial recognition tasks. In fact, the use of auxiliary data can help FER models to obtain a high capacity without overfitting, the weights of the CNN are pre-trained on additional data from other relative tasks (e.g., object detection and facial recognition) before being fine-tuned using suitable datasets. Clearly, applying transfer learning to FER has consistently achieved better results, compared to directly training the recognition on a small-scale FER dataset. Some dataset can be used as auxiliary data, such as ImageNet [41] for object recognition and VGGFace from the Visual Geometry Group [42] for the face recognition. In the work of Ly et al.
