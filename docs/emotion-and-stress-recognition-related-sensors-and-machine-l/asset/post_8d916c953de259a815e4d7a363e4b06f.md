
## 1. Introduction

Child-Robot Interaction (CRI) is a subfield of Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) [1], which is defined as the interaction between humans and robotic systems. Inside the several possibilities of HRI and CRI, socially assistive robots are used as a therapy-tool for children with Autism [2,3]. One feature that could improve this interaction is the ability of recognizing emotions, which can be used to provide a better CRI. For instance, children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) tend to lack the ability of emotion display, the robots should rely on involuntary biological measurements, such as skin topography [4–6].

A face is the region of the body that has a high response to emotions, and the facial thermal print may be linked to the child emotion. Thus, this feature can be a useful parameter to be applied in a CRI, since this biological signal is not voluntary and not mutable [7]. Due to this feature, researchers are focused on face detection and evaluation of emotion expressions in affective computing [8–10]. Moreover, it is more comfortable and unobtrusive technique to evaluate emotions, since no sensor touching is needed as in electroencephalography and electrograph [31,12].

A conventional system for face detection is composed of the three main stages: face and facial detection, detection of various spatial and temporal features, and emotion classification [10]. Then, the first stage for face detection over an input image, and frequently locate facial features (such as eyes, nose, and mouth) of landmarks, is a crucial task and still a challenge. In accurately detect emotions, it is necessary to use geometric or affective features methods [10,13–15], being latter the most popular, due to its superior performance [16]. On the other hand, Facial Landmarks (FL) should be used to locate salient points of facial regions, such as the end of the eyes, and the mouth [10,16].

Many students have debated dividing the face into specific regions for facial feature extraction can improve the performance during emotion recognition [17–29]. However, this may be affected by proper alignment method. Other works have been learning [30,31] feature extraction from specific regions have been proposed to locate these facial regions with higher contribution for emotion recognition. Nevertheless, these approaches are difficult to be extended as a generic system, due to the fact that positions and sizes of facial patches vary according to the training data.

It is worth considering that using thermal cameras for emotion recognition have shown promising results, but low-cost thermal cameras present a poor resolution, making it difficult to accurately detect facial regions by employing conventional methods as the Viola-Jones algorithm, widely used on images [15,32].

Then, we proposed a low-cost system for synchronous capture of face and thermal cameras may increase the accuracy of features for specific facial regions of interest (ROIs) over faces, and consequently improve the feature extraction, increasing the emotion detection. This way, we consider an alternative-approach, which is to firstly apply Viola-Jones algorithm on the visual image to detect ROIs, and then transferring it to its corresponding thermal image, including as a last stage for a ROI location correction based on error probability, taking into account manual annotations of a trained expert over a reference frame.

The goal of this work is to propose a system to detect facial regions for five emotions (disgust, fear, happiness, and sadness) in typically developing children during an interaction with a social robot (as an affective stimulus). In this study, our low-cost camera system allows obtaining of regions in the visual image using the Viola-Jones algorithm as a first stage, and then, transferring these ROIs to the corresponding thermal camera frame through a homography map. As the main novelty, we have introduced a new way to accurately improve the ROI locations after applying both Viola-Jones and homography transform. This computes the error probability to automatically find that ROI over thermal images, which is better placed to manual annotations of a trained expert. This ROI of highest probability (with lowest location error) is later used to relocate ROIs, improving the overall accuracy. Then, appearance features can be extracted, in order to increase the emotion discrimination by our proposed recognition system. Similarly, this method may be extended to other studies to accurately locate ROI over facial thermal images, which are physiologically relevant, as described in [17,21], allowing to understand phenomena linked to emotions, and others. As a result of this work, our system is capable of detecting ROIs on the child's face, Additionally, methods for feature extraction and dimensionality reduction applied using Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA). As another highlight, a set of visual and thermal images is acquired in an atypical context with children. For our knowledge, this type of approach has not been explored.

Many research to recognize facial emotion by contact-free strategies have proposed automatic methods for both face and ROI detection over visual and thermal images, as constructing an effective face representation from images is a crucial step for successful action analysis, in order to recognize facial emotions. In this field, there is the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), which is a taxonomy of facial expressions to facilitate human annotation of facial behaviour [9,14,33]. For instance, a total of 32 facial muscle movements, termed Action Units (AUs), and 14 other descriptors to mention possible actions are specified, which are used by automatic methods to locate facial landmarks and ROIs. These regions are used based on geometric [9,10,15] and appearance features to discriminate emotions [9,14]. Here, we review of the state-of-the art focuses on appearance features on the face, which are generally computed by dividing the face region into regular grid (holistic representation). Appearance features can be encoded to encode low-level or high-level information. For example, low-level information can be encoded through low-level histograms that are computationally simple and ideal for real-time applications.

In the authors use the Haar classifier for face detection, which is widely applied, due to its high accuracy and real time performance [32]. They extracted appearance features from the global face region by using Local Binary Pattern (LBP) that take care of min changes of facial expression for different emotions [9,34], followed by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction, to improve the speed of computation in real time during six emotions (anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, and surprise).
