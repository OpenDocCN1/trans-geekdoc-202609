# Enhancing Mouth-Based Emotion Recognition Using Transfer Learning

Valentina Franzoni 1*, Giulio Biondi 2, Damiano Perri 2 and Osvaldo Gervasi 1*

- 1 Department of Mathematics and Computer Science, University of Perugia, 06123 Perugia, Italy;
- 2 Department of Mathematics and Computer Science, University of Florence, 50121 Florence, Italy;
- giulio-biondi@unifi.it (G.B); damiano.perri@unifi.it (D.P);
- * Correspondence: Valentina.franzoni@fmi.uni.it (V.F.).

Received: 16 July 2020; Accepted: 10 September 2020; Published: 13 September 2020

**Abstract:** This work considers the first study on mouth-based emotion recognition while adopting a transfer learning approach. Transfer learning results are paramount for mouth-based emotion recognition, because most include emotional expressions simulated by actors, instead of adopting real-world categorisation. Using transfer learning, we can use fewer training data than training a whole network from scratch, and thus more efficiently fine-tune the network with emotional data and improve the convolutional network's performance accuracy in the desired domain. The proposed approach aims at improving emotion recognition dynamically, taking into account not new scenarios but also modified situations to the initial training phase, because the image of the mouth can be available even when the face is visible only in an unfavourable perspective. Practical applications include automated supervision of elderly patients in a healthcare management environment, and potentially applications supporting users with speech difficulties. This achievement builds upon previous work on mouth-based emotion recognition using deep-learning, and has the further benefit of being tested and compared to a set of other networks using an extensive dataset for face-based emotion recognition, well known in the literature. The accuracy of mouth-based emotion recognition was also compared to the corresponding full-face emotion recognition; we found that the loss in accuracy is compensated by consistent performance in the visual emotion recognition domain. We can, therefore, state that our method proves the importance of mouth detection in the complex process of emotion recognition.

**Keywords:** transfer learning; convolutional neural networks; emotion recognition

Visual emotion recognition (ER) has been widely studied as one of the first affective computing techniques, based on visual features of the face, to combine features of the eyes, mouth and facial features at the same time. Different approaches to visual recognition obtained different grades of classifications for different visual recognition techniques [1–3]. Recently, studies using only the mouth for facial emotion recognition promising results, while still not gaining the proper recognition among state-of-the-art. Such works used convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to detect basic emotions from ubiquitous and ubiquitous devices, e.g., smartphone or computer cameras, to produce textual, audio or visual feedback for listeners, or digital outputs to support other services, mainly for healthcare systems [4]. A neural network can obtain an excellent result with a relatively small dataset of images trained on a single individual, e.g., to detect particular states needing immediate medical intervention, or changes over time indicating an degenerative health condition.

Our analysis focused on emotion classification with the mouth, to study how the mouth is involved in emotional expression and can provide accuracy compared to full-face recognition. We analysed the position and curve of lips using CNNs. Recently, some of our preliminary works [1-4] analysed favourable results regarding emotion analysis using the mouth, and this is our first attempt to recap and complete our full study on mouth-based emotion recognition with extensive datasets. All our previous works used convolutional networks to reach their goals on the topic, using a self-collected dataset. In-depth work has been primarily done on emotions (i.e., joy, disgust and neutral) [5], which disgust is the least used in the literature.

After proving that this architecture can be itself an element for emotion recognition, in our work, we focus on the mouth as a unique element for facial-expression-based emotion recognition using advanced deep learning techniques. The goal of this approach was to enhance the mouth-based approach to emotion recognition in order to generalise the experimentation on multiple-user dataset. That is, advanced deep learning techniques have been implemented, i.e., knowledge transfer with elements of continuous learning. Such techniques are particularly suited to being applied in the healthcare environment.

For instance, connecting this architecture to appropriate services can help users convey emotions in an automated way, e.g., providing augmented stimuli to users affected by autism or other conditions involving social relating where user experiences difficulties recognising emotions expressed by other people. Another example may be a system to recognise severe conditions and call a human assistant for intervention, e.g., for hospitalised patients feeling pain or needing psychological support. Such applications provide feedback from healthcare personnel to exploit continuous learning.

We tested the most neural networks [1] for face recognition and mouth recognition, e.g., lip reading [6], with previous training on widely used datasets of emotions [7]. Knowledge transfer allowed our networks to be pre-trained on low-level features, e.g., edges, corners, colour distribution.

In the layers of the CNN, we carried out ad-hoc training to detect human face recognition of emotional classes. The work concluded the experiments by testing and comparing several CNNs on a widely-used dataset [8] of faces labelled with emotions from the Ekman model. In the preliminary work, for the final dataset we produced a filtered version, wherein photos showing simulated emotions, i.e., non-spontaneous expressions, have been removed.

The main problem in facial emotion recognition is the lack of proper datasets of images for training. Most of the available datasets take into consideration only the Ekman model [9] or its subsets [5], discarding more complex and complete models, like Plutchik [10], or models based on emotional affective [11]. Our dataset contains non-genuine expressions (e.g., simulated by professional actors) rather than spontaneous and natural forms of facial expression. Since deep learning can extract features not recognisable by humans, items related to non-real emotions make it hard to train a neural network effectively for emotion recognition. For these reasons, our work focused on a subset of the Ekman model, augmented with the neural expression as a state of control for recognition results on emotions [4], by investing a fair amount of effort into selecting proper images from the available datasets, both from internet sources and self-produced material.
