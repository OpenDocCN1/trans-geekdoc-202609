The first relaxation task aims to build a psychological baseline and remove unwanted excitement before the regular experiment stage. Similarly, the other relaxation tasks aim to remove stress after a stressful task and prepare the next task by setting the psychological baseline. This design improves the reliability of the experiment's results [5]. During the stressful tasks, the subject was provided with one of two types of stressors: (1) a math task, namely requiring the subject to solve a series of subtraction problems via mental arithmetic, or (2) a Stroop task, a task where the subject was asked to respond with the color of a given word and image. Because all subjects were Korean, the words were presented in the Korean language. These are typical tasks that have been used to induce stress in studies [12,15,17].

The tasks also varied in difficulty, based on the results of a previous study by Cho et al. [15]. For example, an easy task might be required to subtract 1 from a four-digit number, within 7.5 s, while a hard math task might involve repeatedly subtracting a two-digit number, rather than 1, from a four-digit number with the same time limit. Likewise, easy Stroop tasks involved the same color and meaning, while these were mismatched for stress. In either case, the time limit for Stroop problem was 1.5 s. Appropriate sound feedback was also provided to indicate or not the answer was correct, encouraging the subject to pay attention to the task and adding additional stress.

The subjects were presented with four stress-inducing tasks (two with difficulties) in random order. Although a previous study [15] used a fixed order, we did not choose to do this, for reasons: (1) a fixed order could also bias the stress level, and (2) a random order would better replicate stress-inducing situations. All of the relaxation and stress tasks lasted for 5 min. At the end of each task, the subject (not at all) was asked to evaluate how mentally he or she felt, based on a visual scale (VAS) [1] from 0 to 10 (extreme stress). For example, if a subject is relaxed or stressed, the VAS score will be close to zero or 10, respectively. In this study, the use of VAS to measure the average effects (e.g., induce stress or relieve stress) of each task on the 16 subjects was used during experimental stage to minimize unexpected mental stimuli. When the experimental stage was complete, the subject took off the wearable device to complete the experiment.

# 3.2.2. Experimental Setup

A BioH module 30 (Zephyr Technology, Annapolis, MD, USA) was used to collect the subjects' ECG and RESP data. This wearable device is compact and can be tightened with a strap, making it a good choice to minimize disturbance during the experiment. Making the strap too tight could induce unnecessary stress or pain, so we asked the subjects not to tighten it so much it was uncomfortable to wear.

The experiments were conducted on a laptop computer (with a 2.8 GHz Core i7 processor (Santa Clara, CA, USA) and 16 GB of RAM) in a closed room. During each experiment, the subject was alone in the room, as shown in Figure 2. On the laptop, a graphical user interface was installed, which we developed with MATLAB R2016a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). This was designed to be as simple as possible so not to confuse the subjects.
