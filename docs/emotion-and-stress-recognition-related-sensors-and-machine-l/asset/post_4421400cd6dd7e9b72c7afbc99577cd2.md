
The individual needs to attend to tasks away from their typical working desk. Our method poses no such issues.

## 6.2. Applications

### 6.2.1. Professional Environment

In our studies, the recruited participants mainly performed cashier-aided tasks in an office environment while sitting in posture. Other occupations, such as cashiers, emergency/non emergency centre workers, also perform their tasks while sitting in posture for prolonged periods. In addition, the working environment of these occupations are similar to an office environment. Moreover, our method may work for these occupations as well. In such a scenario, stress sensing could be used to improve mental health. However, further studies need to identify the viability of foot-based stress monitoring for such occupations. In addition, remove stress monitoring of adults under home care is another potential application. Simple accelerometer can be embedded to a sock and thus monitor both stress and activity level using a single sensor. However, to accomplish this further studies with better classifications stated in literature require [98,99].

### 6.2.2. The Quantified Self

Enriching self-tracking with a stress detection is another application. In this community, stress is being triangulated with several data of wearable sensors [100]. A simple feature could substantially increase accuracy. Moreover, using an available wearable, like a shoe, can address users who prefer not to wear additional garment accessories.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Work

### 6.3.1. Quantifying Multiple Stress Levels

Both labs have only investigated stress concentration without relaxation aiming to identify different levels of stress. However, the results of study 3 indicates that the frequency of demonstrating stress related postures could reveal the extent of stress. However, it requires further research to infer on levels of stress could be based on the frequency of stress-related foot movements and posture characteristics.

### 6.3.2. Stress Detection in Sitting Posture and Other Activities

In our society, sitting is the most common posture demonstrated during both the working week and weekends [101], which our findings also confirm (see Table 3). Therefore, proposed models would practically work for a majority of the time, whenever an individual is doing some sort of intellectual work seated. Detecting stress in other postures extends the scope of our current research. Identifying stressful situations when performing regular activities, such as walking, running and turning are considered future work. For this, we would first need to identify the current posture and activity, as by using an insole [24,25] or IMU [92,94,102], as shown in the literature.

### 6.3.3. Accuracy Boost with Personalised Models

We aimed to develop a generalised model of working across different users. We observed the accuracy of our generalised models decreases for features, such as Median and Dominant Frequency. This is not because all users demonstrate an elevated foot tapping/shaking when stressed. In addition, we observed that individuals may have slightly different foot posture and motion characteristics depending on the BMI and other personal habits. Relying on a personalised model will further boost accuracy, particularly for features like foot-tapping. Further, a Neural Network approach can be advantageous, particularly when building a personalised model with a high volume of data gathered in the wild.

## 6.4. Improved Hardware

In our early prototype, the IMU (in conjunction with the control unit) was attached to the ankle. Our long-term goal is to integrate all these components into a smart footwear. This may change the orientation of the IMU. Thus, the main signal could differ from another axis, resulting in another analysis to provide higher sharpness paramount.

## 7. Conclusions

We presented StressFoot, a pair of smart shoes that can sense stress unobtrviously by using a pressure sensitive and an IMU. Our prototype is capable of identifying stress and relaxation while sitting, as performing tasks. We identified four characteristics, which reflect pressure distributions, posture variations and foot tapping. On these features, we evaluated several machine learning models with 23 participants using a leave-one-out and validated this as a method to detect stress with an accuracy of ~85%. With 11 additional participants, we demonstrated the repeatability of our model with a similar overall accuracy of ~87%. Finally, to provide evidence validity, we conducted a field study with 10 participants, and the robustness of our models in an actual effect setting. The outcome was that the computed stress level with the self-reported stress level with a coefficient of r = 0.79. By drawing attention to the user's mental stress condition, such a system may already be able to contribute to an improvement in overall mental well-being in the future.
