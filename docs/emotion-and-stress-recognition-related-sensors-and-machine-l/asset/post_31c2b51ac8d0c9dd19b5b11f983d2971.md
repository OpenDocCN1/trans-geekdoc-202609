
## Emotion Recognition in Immersive Virtual Reality: From Statics to Affective Computing

**Javier Marin-Morales**, \*, **Carmen Llinares**, **Jaime Guiqueras** and **Mariano Alcaiz**

*Institución de Investigación e Innovación en Bioingeniería, Universitat Politècnica de Valencia, 46022 Valencia, Spain; clinear@mnp.upv.es (C.L.); jaiguip@i3b.upv.es (J.G.); malcaniz@i3b.upv.es (M.A)
\* Correspondence: jamarnol@i3b.upv.es*

**Received:** 23 July 2020; **8 September 2020**; **Published:** 10 September 2020

**Abstract:** Emotions play a critical role in our daily lives, so the understanding and recognition of emotions are crucial for human health. Affective computing research has mostly used non-immersive two-dimensional (2D) images or videos to elicit emotional states. However, immersive virtual reality allowing researchers to simulate environments in controlled laboratory conditions with high levels of presence and interactivity, is more popular in emotion research. Its synergy with implicit measures and machine-learning techniques has the potential to impact transversely in many research areas, opening new opportunities for the scientific community. This paper presents a systematic review of the emotion recognition research undertaken with physiological and behavioural measures using head-mounted displays as elicitation devices. The results highlight the evolution of the field, gave a clear perspective using aggregated analysis, reveal the current issues and provide guidelines for future research.

**Keywords:** affective computing; emotion recognition; emotion elicitation; virtual reality; head-mounted display; machine learning

Emotions play an essential role in decision-making, learning and a variety of other that affect both human physiological and psychological status [1]. Therefore, understanding and recognising emotions are very important aspects of human behaviour. To study human emotions, affective states need to be evoked in laboratory environments, using elicitation methods such as audio, video and, recently, virtual reality (VR). VR has experienced an increase in popularity in recent years in scientific and commercial contexts [2]. Its applications include training, education, health and marketing. This is based on the development of a new generation of low-cost headsets which demanded global purchases of head-mounted displays (HMDs). Nonetheless, VR has been used in research since the 1990s [4]. The scientific interest in VR is due to the fact that it provides simulated experiences that create the sensation of being in the real world [5]. In particular, environmental simulations are representations of physical environments that allow researchers to analyse to concepts [6]. They are especially important when they depict be physically presented. VR makes it possible to study these scenarios under controlled environments [7]. VR allows the time- and cost-effective isolation and modification of variables, unaffected in real space [8].

The set-ups that display VR simulations have been progressively integrated into studies as the relevant technologies that have evolved. Each consists of a combination of three objective features, formats, display devices and user interfaces.

The format describes the structure of the immersion displayed. The most common are two-dimensional (2D) multimedia and three-dimensional (3D) environments, and the main difference between them is their levels of interactivity [9]. 2D multimedia, including 360° panoramic images and videos, provide non-interactive visual representations. The validity of this format has extensively explored [10]. Moreover, the latest advances in computer-generated images simulate light, texture and atmospheric conditions to such degree of photorealism that it is possible to produce a virtual image indistinguishable, the naked eye, from a photograph of a real-world scene [11]. This format allows to test static computer-generated environments, with many variations, cheaply and quickly in a laboratory. On the other hand, 3D environments generate interactive representations which allow changes in the user's point of view, navigation and even interaction with objects and people [12]. Developing realistic 3D environments is more time consuming than developing 360° computer-generated photographs, and their level of realism is limited by the power of the hardware. Moreover, the processing capacity of GPUs (graphics processing) is increasing every year, which will enhance the performance capacity of 3D environments. Moreover, the facilitation of real-tasks, is a key aspect in the application of virtual reality [2].

The display devices are the technological equipment used to visualise the formats. They are classified according to the level of immersion they provide, which is the sensorimotor contingencies that they support. They are related to the actions which subjects carry out in the perception; for example, when they shift the position of their head, and they move down and under to meet an object. For example, turning the head, bending forward) supported by a system set of virtual actions, each carrying in terms of perception within the virtual environment [13]. Since immersion is objective, one system is more immersive than another if it is superior in at least one characteristic while others remain equal. Non-immersive systems use a screen, such as a desktop PC, to display environments [14]. Semi-immersive systems, such as the automatic virtual environment (CAVE), or the motion-based systems, use large projections to display environments on walls, enveloping the viewer [15, 16]. These displays provide a stereo image of an environment, with a perspective projection linked to the position of the observer's head. Immersive devices, such as HDMDs, are fully-immersive systems using the stereoscopic view, which responds to the user's movements.

There are three categories of immersion: non-immersive, semi-immersive, and fully-immersive.

The user interfaces, which are exclusive to 3D environments which allow this level of interaction, are the functional connections between the user and the VR environment which allow him or her to interact with objects and navigation [20]. Regarding interaction with objects, tasks include: selection (that is, identifying an object or subset of objects), positioning (that is changing the user's 3D position), rotation (that is, an object's 3D orientation). In terms of navigation methods in 3D environments, virtual locomotion has been analysed [22], and can be classified as physical or artificial. Regarding the physical, there are room-based methods, such as real-walking, which allow the user to walk freely inside a limited physical space. These are normally used with HDMDs, and position and orientation are determined by the position of the user's head. The most natural of these methods, however, is limited by the restricted area [23]. In addition, there is walking-in-a pseudo-naturalistic metaphor where the user performs a virtual locomotion to navigate, for example, by moving his/her hands as if he/she was walking, or by performing footstep-like movements, while remaining stationary.
