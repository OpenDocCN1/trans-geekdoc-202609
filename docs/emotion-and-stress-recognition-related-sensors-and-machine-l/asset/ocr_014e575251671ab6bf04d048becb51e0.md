# Previous Works

Previous works focused on the acquisition of data from a single user, i.e., where researchers train the network on a specific user's face. This approach allows user to train the network with precision on his/her face, reaching advanced recognition performance [1,4]. Our work presents a general approach using a multi-user dataset, containing images of users who were different ranging age, cultural background, gender and appearance. To that end, we used advanced methods of deep learning, transfer learning and continuous learning.

Multidisciplinary studies at the basis of our work in artificial intelligence (AI) stressed the importance of computer science for automated real-life tasks for assistive technologies [13,14]. Among them, label detection and lip-ripping [6] constitute our main background point [15].

One of the most promising advances of recent years for AI-assisted health is the opportunity to develop mobile applications on widely-spread devices [4], e.g., smartphones, tablets and smartwatches, to support disabled users.

# Problem Description and Proposed Solution

Our study exploits the high precision of CNNs by processing images to recognise emotional states using the most recent advances in affective computing. Affective computing is a novel approach in artificial intelligence, for the first time in 2003 by Rosalind Picard [16], becoming one of the most trending multidisciplinary studies. Affective computing, involving the collaboration of several disciplines, e.g., psychology, physiology, liberal studies, computer science and robotics, recently stressed the importance of emotion and recognition of affective mental states, e.g., emotions, moods and personality traits. Notwithstanding the interest in this new research, most of the available research focuses on sentiment analysis or pure theoretical models (e.g., in psychology and social sciences) and product-based applications [17], for marketing purposes. We prefer to focus on self-aid [18], health management and communication for supporting and understanding humans in real-life problems for any demanding task (e.g., due to disabilities [19], psychological states in emergencies or particular environments) with automated detectors and assistive assistants with machine intelligence capabilities [20].

In our soconnected world, individuals already use and produce an overwhelming amount of heterogeneous data in a manageable and personalised subset of datasets. Data can be directly used for emotion recognition (ER), e.g., for photos and text shared on social networks, or can contain elements inherently infer for emotional intensity, such as physiological data carried by wearables-including sleep patterns, continuous heart-rate and movement tracking, emotions expressed by [21] and data from users experiencing exciting games [22]. Sentiment analysis relates only to recognising the positivity, negativity or neutrality of sentiments, moods and emotions, the process of emotion recognition is still an understudied field, implying the recognition of emotions of an emotional model. Various models have been proposed to recognize ER is in a application domain tasks from the widely recognised model of Ekan, which we chose for our study. Recent research updates [23] can practise Ekan's primary emotional states, such as happiness, sadness, anger, disgust and neutral on text [24] and physiological cues such as heart rate, conductance, gestures, facial expression and sound, which can be managed with a multidimensional approach [11] and compared.

Among all approaches, facial recognition is still predominant. Under that point of view, we decided to focus on the mouth as a facial element, always visible in any facial expression, even that some cultures underestimate the mouth's expressiveness more than others. Others from South America, as Ekan himself underlined in his first experiments on facial emotions labelling [9], rely more on the upper part of the face.
