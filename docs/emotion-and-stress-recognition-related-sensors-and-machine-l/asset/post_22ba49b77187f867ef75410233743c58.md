DTW aligns two series using the shortest path in a distance matrix, the path with the minimal global warping distance [25,26], finding the most representative distance of the overall difference [20]. However, comprehensive comparison of representation methods and distance measures of time series reveals inconsistencies and contradictions in the observations reported in individual studies [23]. An important consequence is that experimental results can be generated without critically reviewing the assumptions for a particular research context and design. As concluded in [23], “there is no clear evidence that one similarity measure exists that is superior to others in the literature of terms. The maximum length the leash reaches is the value of the Frechet distance [33] (p 7). We use the Frechet distance to assess the geometric similarity of time series sensor measurements of the same physiological parameter (e.g., GSR) on the same participant but with different sensors.

The correlation of time series has been investigated for decades, in diverse levels. Here, our focus is on time series correlation [34]; first, the correlation of equal-type physiological parameters measured by different sensors at the same time in order to quantify differences between low-cost and uncalibrated sensors versus high-end and calibrated laboratory sensor equipment; however, the correlation between physiological parameters of different types, for instance, IBI and GSR, to potentially identify hidden relations.

According to [34], the Pearson’s correlation coefficient is the most robust metric measuring the similarity in physiological time series—where robustness is unintended to small values. However, Pearson’s is highly sensitive to outliers and considers only linear relations. Spearman’s correlation coefficient (rho)—is the same—based on the rank of values rather than the values themselves; it measures monotonicity rather than linearity. Using Spearman’s rho to measure the strength of the associations between two variables leaves room for interpretation [35].

The human cardiovascular system and the autonomic nervous system are non-linear interactions between different physiological parameter. To explore possible underlying nonlinear interactions, here, use the Maximum Information Coefficient (MIC) [36,37]. Several studies show the possibility of gaining new insights into such non-linear interactions when applying the MIC, for instance, in the interactions between neural and respiratory dynamics [38].

Further, one method to assess the temporal lag (or lead) between pairs of time series is the cross-correlation function in the time domain [39]. For getting meaningful cross-correlation results, the time series needs to be stationary, i.e., having a constant mean and variance. Time series stationarity can be tested, for instance, the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test [40]. Unless the time series is stationary, it needs to be differenced and tested for stationarity.

## 3. Physiological Parameter of Interest and Sensors used for Benchmarking

Here, we describe the physiological parameters we investigated, and the sensors used to measure them. We investigated three sensors and four physiological parameters (Table 1):

- HR: heart rate, i.e., heartbeat frequency, unit: beats per minute
- IBI: inter intervals, also known as the RR interval, the time between two R-peaks in the ECG's QRS complex, unit: milliseconds
- ECG: electrocardiogram, i.e., electrical activity of the skin, unit: volt
- GSR: galvanic skin response, i.e., the level of electric conductance of the skin, unit: microSiemens [µS]

The VariPort (http://www.bisigma.com) is a small, lightweight, and highly flexible recording system that is used for multi-channel physiological recordings in laboratory and ambulatory studies. The standard version of the device can record up to 16 signals from pre-amplifiers (e.g., electromyography, electrocardiography, electro activity, or respiration). The device has two-marker buttons that can be used to detect significant events occurring over time, resulting in an additional channel of data. We used these buttons to mark changes in the activity phases (for details refer to Section 4.1). Available sensors are either wire-connected to the device or are directly integrated into the device. The recorded data are stored on an SD card inside the device. The VariPort allows setting sampling rates for different channels, effectively reducing the required storage, especially in slowly changing signals (e.g., such as the skin conductance). For rapidly changing signals, such as ECG, sampling rates up to 1024 Hz can be set. Since the VariPort is the platform used for scientific studies at our Psychology Department, it is the gold standard for our benchmarking. In the remaining part of the paper, the VariPort sensor is called VP.

The Zephyr BioHarness 3 (https://www.zephyrany.com/) is a multivariable monitoring device with a chest belt that measures a wide variety of physiological parameters. The BH is a certified medical device (FDA Class II). Due to its design as a chest belt, the BioHarness 3 can measure ECG, RR intervals, respiration frequency, and other parameters such as 3D acceleration on a single sensor platform. Furthermore, parameters such as heart rate can be derived from the directly measured parameter, for instance, from ECG (HR within 0–240 BPM and an accuracy of ±1 BPM). The sampling rate for ECG is 250 Hz. The BH and the smartphone are connected wirelessly via Bluetooth. The raw data are accessible via a free SDK in binary format, and the device has extensively tested and validated in practical applications [41,42]. In the remaining part of the paper, the Zephyr BioHarness 3 sensor is called BH.

### 3.3. Empatica E4

The Empatica E4 (https://www.empatica.com/research/e4/) is a wrist band sensor that measures HR and GSR, as well as other parameters. The E4 is medically certified according to CE Medical 93/42/EEC Directive, class 2a, FCC. The sampling rate for GSR is 4Hz and for IBI 64Hz. According to Gradl et al. ## 4. Benchmark Method

Our study included 18 participants who were recruited via e-mail. The test group comprised nine females and nine males in an age range of 24 to 40 years. All test persons were physically in decent shape and did not suffer from any illness at the time of the study.

The study was carried out at the University of Salzburg's Department of Psychology. After the study leaders attached the sensors, the participants were instructed to sit on ergometer and follow the following routine:

- Resting phase: 5 min of seated resting on ergometer, not performing any physical activity; used as calibration phase for the measurements
- Cycling phase: 10 min of cycling at a constant 50 rpm; stepwise increase of physical load (5 steps of 2 min each, during which the resistance/power of the ergometer was increased from 35-65-100-133-165 W)
- Cool phase: 5 min of cool down, seated like in the resting phase

Participants were not told to interact with other people in the room, to focus on their task, and to perform any physical activity as instructed. This was observed by the study leaders. For each ‘run’, two persons were placed next to each other. Before commencing the actual exercise, we checked that sensors were well positioned according to the participants’ individual body shape. Additionally, we used surgical tape as necessary order to make sure that we received plausible measurements. 

The basic data acquisition is illustrated in Figure 1. Each sensor measures physiological parameter using different platforms, VioPort (VP), ZephyrBioHarness3 (BH), and Empatica E4 (E4). For each run, which records all exercises while their physiological parameters are measured, the raw sensor data are either stored in an SQLite database directly on the smartphone, or in a proprietary format on an SD card. The measurements from VP were exported to flat files using the Software ANSLAB [43]. In contrast, the “raw sensor data” serves as input for the pre-processing, which is necessary to prepare the data for further analyses. E-Diary App is herein primarily used for sensor collection and data management. During real-world field studies, the E-Diary App collects data such as GPS positions and contextual user feedback for ground-truth, thereby enabling the investigation of stress in a spatio-temporal manner [4,44].
