## 3.5. Experiment Setup

This experiment was designed to collect EEG data using in-ear EEG electrodes when subjects' emotions were stimulated by pictures, described in Section 3. The results would analyze the performance of in-ear EEG on emotion classification.

Twelve male and one female subjects from 20 to 30 years with an average age of 24, were recruited for emotion classification experiments. Before the experiment, the impedances of the in-ear EEG were re-measured for quality assurance. An in-ear EEG device was then inserted into either the right or left ear according to each subject's preference, whereas earphones were inserted into the ears. Earwax was cleaned with alcohol before the experiment started.

Unless the subjects preferred to put the in-ear EEG on the left, it was put on the right ear as the left ear shown to be better for listening to music [50]. The ground electrode was placed at forehead and the reference electrode was placed at cheek inferior to the ear. A small amount of saline was used as electrolyte gel. Forty trials were recorded per subject. IAPS and GAPED pictures were displayed to the subjects. The total number of pictures used for each emotion was suggested by IAPS and GAPED datasets.

Each picture was displayed for 30 s. Subjects were encouraged not to move during each picture viewing. Fifty seconds of black screen was displayed after each picture to neutralize subjects' emotions before the next picture was displayed. During the black screen subjects were free to move.

After eight pictures, subjects could have a small break and were free to move before they were ready for the next.

After the experiments were finished, the subjects were asked to evaluate their emotional response to each picture for emotion classification. This is because the emotional response to each picture may be different among subjects or from the IAPS and GAPED datasets.

Statistical analyses for any group comparison were performed using either t-tests or ANOVA, depending on the number of groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistics were performed using SPSS (IBM Corp., New York, USA).

### 4.2. DEAP Data Analysis

The emotion classification using T7 and T8 signals from DEAP dataset by SVM, as described in Section 2.4, was performed. From 32 subjects consisting of 40 trials per subject each were used for the classification. Ten-fold cross-validation was applied to suppress biases. In each classification, 36 trials were used as the training set and the other four were used for the test set. Ten different sets were trained and tested for each subject.

The accuracy achieved was 69.85 percent for valence and 76.7 percent for arousal. The overall accuracy was 58.12 percent.

Furthermore, the analysis of emotion classification using T7 or T8 was compared. The accuracy was 71.30% for valence, 76.67% for arousal, and 57.56% for four emotions (valence and arousal).

The T-test result from SPSS (IBM Corp., New York, USA) indicated that there was no statistically significant difference in classification between T7 and T8. The accuracy of T7 was 75.56 ± 15.19, the p-value was 0.955, indicating that there was no significance difference. 

The results show that T7 and T8 could be used as a single channel for valence, arousal, as the classification accuracy is comparable to the multichannel classification in [7].

### 4.3. In-Ear EEG Emotion Classification

Out of twenty-three subjects, 10 decided to put in-ear EEG on the left. The measurement of raw EEG data showed no statistically significant difference between EEG collected from left and right ear (p-value = 0.95).

In-ear EEG signals were recorded while subjects were watching pictures during experiment, described in Section 3.5. The EEG signal was filtered using a 4th order Butterworth filter to filter out noise at 50 Hz. The signal was separated into frequency bands: theta (4-8 Hz), beta (12-32 Hz). Statistical parameters by Picard et al. [48] were used for signal feature extraction.

Ten-fold cross-validation was used for classifying each subject's data. The SVM model described in Section 3.4 was used for classification. The signal processing and classification was performed using Matlab (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA).

Binary classification was done by SVM on valence (positive or negative) and arousal (high or low). The four emotion classification was performed using the valence and arousal classification results, mapped onto the simplified valence-arousal emotional model in Figure 5. For example, positive valence and high arousal was classified as happy. Hence the simplified emotions could be classified into four groups: positive valence/high arousal, positive valence/low arousal, negative valence/high arousal, negative valence/low arousal.
