
# In this book, we define ‘hardware-assisted security’ as the security/protection of hardware/IP core for DSP and multimedia applications in CE devices or information by a hardware/system of CE devices.

The term ‘security’ encapsulates a broad theme that covers many aspects including hardware security, protection, privacy, trustworthiness, and IP core protection and information security. The terms ‘security’ depicts the security of a system (such as CE device) that handles the data or information. In a CE-based structure, defense of its hardware and IP cores is very essential. Therefore, the practice of using secured IPs becomes significant. In this age of smart cities, growing CE hardware design complexity is outpacing the designer yield, thus, resulting into bigger undertakings. Additionally, the present group of CE designs is enormously reliant on global IP supply chains. To upsurge the design productivity and to reduce the design time, the usage of IP cores often supplied by a third-party vendor has turned into a vital and `de-facto` practice in the industry. It is estimated that top counterfeits could have an impact of $300B on the semiconductor market. However, increasing threats to security and swelling piracy concerns do affect the global supply chains. Thus, information security that covers the security of data, information, and multimedia which are handled all the time by CE devices becomes important ([Sengupta *et al.*, 2018](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib27); [Mohanty 2018a](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib10), [2018b](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib14)).

## 1.3 Intellectual property (IP) cores/hardware

### 1.3.1 Utility of IP cores in CE devices

The express intensification of silicon technology resulted into terrific upsurge in design complexity of CEs devices. In the realistic operation of multi-billion dollar foundries, more and more applications are unified on a single silicon die. Synchronously, demands of market have directed bigger design start-ups, reduced design time, and growing time-to-market burden. To defy these difficulties, IP cores, especially for complex DSP and multimedia applications, have become a pragmatic solution. An IP core once corroborated in a design with a specific process technology can be employed to other designs that use the identical process technology. In turn, this eradicates the necessity to embrace such a core in most of the system-level simulation testing, which in turn, decreases the intricacy of inclusive system design and verification. To provide a simple illustration, once a JPEG CODEC core or wavelet transformation core used in image compression is validated for a specific silicon process, it can be assimilated into SoC designs of other CE devices easily ([Sengupta, 2016](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib19)).

### 1.3.2 Why security and protection of hardware/IP cores?

Demands of sophisticated CE devices, including complex DSP/multimedia cores, have led to extensive usage of reusable IP cores. The swelling reuse of such complex IP cores inexorably uncovers them to perils of counterfeiting. Since an IP core can represent several years of design, R&D, and validation, a critical concern is how to safeguard this asset? and how can a counterfeit of a core be identified/prevented?

To the best of our knowledge, it is a fact that the privileges of the actual IP owner can be battered both deliberately and inadvertently. This can be conceivable by an abuse of the license treaty by deploying the IP core in numerous devices, when a solo license has been obtained. There can be a high enticement for a foundry or even to other IP-core makers to integrate a high-quality design into other system components where the actual licensing rule can prevents this. As the systems grow composite, there is a rising trend for IP core makers to sub-license other IP cores for including that in their designs, and once a design process has been finished, it is alluring to reuse it in other supplementary products that may not encompass the actual licensing. Another impending threat to IP cores is that of piracy, where by dishonest means or reverse engineering, direct stealing/copying of the IP for reuse without authorization are done. As a result of the IP theft, the attacker possibly may even claim the entitlement of the IP. Thus, a method of annulling dishonest claims of IP ownership is highly desirable. For the makers of the IP core or a whole-chip design, it is both problematic and costlier to demonstrate that their IP in the product has been employed unlawfully. In case of silicon chips, one of the realistic methods of identifying an IP core is to acquire an example of the product under test for sending to a professional laboratory for analysis. Furthermore, injection of any form from the third-party invasion in the hardware-design process may cultivate and result in serious security apprehension for CE hardware. Thus, safeguarding CE/IoT hardware can be realized as a protection of IP cores against dishonest entitlement of ownership. Another version of threat called ‘hardware Trojan’ is an act of intentionally injecting malicious logic into a design (such as IP core hardware) by an adversary in the vendor house, which will target detection/correction policies affecting security protocol. Both the hardware vulnerabilities, mentioned above, may occur in any of the design abstract levels. Pre-processing or post-processing style approach is defined based on the level at which security is employed, which is often driven by complexity of the DSP core. For example, in case of complex DSP/multimedia cores comprising gates in the order of 15K–50K, security approach during architectural (high-level) synthesis is more beneficial and fruitful ([Sengupta and Kundu, 2017](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib26)).

### 1.3.3 Traditional forms of IP protection not enough?

For the last several years, various methodologies for the protection of such cores through patents, copyrights, trademarking, etc., have been employed. Yet, these approaches are not established adequately to shield the worth of IP cores used in CEs devices. The primary reason is the design costs of implementation; in addition, liability of proof to exhibit infringement is also too expensive. Besides, when it comes to the protection of reusable IP cores in CE industry, most of the IP core vendors trust on a combination of communal conviction of their business collaborators and privacy during operation.
