
The design of a ring oscillator PUF is shown in [Figure 12.7](#Chapter12.html#c12_fig7) ([Suh and Devadas, 2007](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib69)). There are two different sets of ROs that are connected to two multiplexers “Mux1” and “Mux2.” The two multiplexers are connected to two counters which are connected to a comparator, which gives the final output. The multiplexers will select different combinations of ROs and feed the oscillations to the counters. The number of oscillations over a selected period of time are given to the input and clock of the comparator. The comparator compares the two input values and gives out “1” or “0.” The input to the multiplexers is the challenge input given to PUF and the output from the comparators is the response of the PUF.

Figure 12.7 Ring oscillator PUF

Due to the manufacturing variations in the IC, all the ROs will not oscillate at the same frequency. This causes the counters to give out different number of oscillations for a same period of time. When different inputs are selected by the multiplexers, different ROs will give distinct oscillation frequencies, which generate different bits at the output of the comparator. Thus the keys generated by the RO PUF will be unique. If there are `N` different ROs, only `N` × (`N` − 1)/2 different comparisons will be possible. There are some advantages to the implementation of ring oscillator PUFs. They are easier to build, evaluate, highly reliable, and relaxed layout and routing constraints in the design. There are some disadvantages also to the design. It is comparatively slow; to increase the number of bits, the chip area needs to be increase, which results in consumption of more power. In addition, aging is also one of the main issues that can affect the reliability of the ring oscillator PUF. Various factors like positive bias temperature instability (PBTI), negative bias temperature instability (NBTI), time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB), hot carrier injection (HCI), electro-migration (EM), and thermal cycling (TC) also affect PUF. Due to the aging effect, the change in the output oscillations of the ROs will affect the output bits of PUF module itself. This can become a major issue when implemented in the cryptographic applications. In a PMOS transistor, when a negative voltage is applied at gate-to-source terminal, an interface trap is generated at Si–SiO[2] interface. Over a period of time, this results in the change of transistor parameters like transconductance, drain current, and threshold voltage. This effect is called NBTI. The HCI occurs due to the switching activity of the transistor ([Mohanty, 2015](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib53)). NBTI and HCI will depend on various factors, including workload of the transistor and technology.

TDDB is occurred due to the rapid scaling of the device. The high operating voltages at the gate terminal of the transistor will lead to the structural degradation of the device, like the gate oxide and its interface. With the continuity, the dielectric layer will lose the property to insulate and as a result, a conduction path will form between the gate oxide and the substrate. This effect has been addressed and reduced significantly with the implementation of high-κ material. Other parameters that affect the performance of the device are the temperature and supply voltage. With the variations in the supply and temperature, the output oscillations of the ROs will change. When the temperature of the chip increases, the threshold voltage of the device is reduced. The increase in temperature can also decrease the drain saturation current and the carrier mobility. But the operating temperatures of the chip and the supply voltages can be regulated and maintained. If the temperature increases, it can be decreased using various methods. But the effects caused by aging cannot be reversed and have a permanent damage on the device and hence the chip itself. This affects the performance and reliability of the chip over time.

To avoid these, aging-resistance designs are introduced. [Figure 12.8](#Chapter12.html#c12_fig8) shows the design of an aging-resistant PUF ([Rahman *et al*., 2014](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib56)). The NBTI effect is decreased by introducing an NMOS transistor to connect two inverters in the RO as shown in the figure. The supply voltage ![image](OEBPS/images/c12-Eqn-in-12.gif)is connected to the ![image](OEBPS/images/c12-Eqn-in-13.gif)transistors which are activated using the ENABLE signal. These are introduced to avoid the logical “0” at the input of the inverter’s PMOS transistor. When the RO is not active, there is a chance that a “0” might be at the input of PMOS in the inverter and can cause power consumption. This design of PUF will reduce the NBTI effect in the off state. Various other designs of aging-resistant RO PUF designs were also introduced ([Chowdhury *et al*., 2017](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib10); Rahman *et al*., [2015](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib57), [2016](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib58)).
