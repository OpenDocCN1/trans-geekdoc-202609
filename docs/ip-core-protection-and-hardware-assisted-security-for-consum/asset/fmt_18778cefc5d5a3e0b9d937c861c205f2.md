
A typical HLS design flow accepts a DSP application in the form of C-code and converts it into a graphical representation called control data flow graph through parsing. Along with this application, HLS also accepts the technology library of modules based on which the design evaluation is performed. The technology library information is based on a particular technology scale and comprises details of silicon area, delay, power, etc., of a unit module. Once a control data flow graph (CDFG) is obtained after parsing the C-code, it then undergoes a series of transformations to convert it into an optimized form. These series of transformations can be compiler driven or Boolean function driven by nature. This optimized CDFG accepts HLS pragmas/directives to pass the information to the DSE system in HLS. The first step of DSE system is operation scheduled, which also accepts its own set of HLS pragmas/directives as well as scheduled configuration to assign time (cycle) stamp to each operation. Examples of directives at this step include chaining, multi-cycling, data pipelining, functional pipelining, loop unrolling, etc. This is followed by resource allocation and binding phase which also accepts its own set of directive for executing spatial constraint to functional resources. Binding enables sharing/reuse of functional hardware resources for multiple operations. Subsequently, register allocation is performed through its own set of HLS directive to obtain an optimal number of register sets required for implementing the application in hardware. Once these phases are over, then the determination of datapath is performed by analyzing the I/O connectivity, multiplexers, demultiplexers, etc., based on the datapath obtained, and the timing sequence for the generation of control path is determined. This yields cycle-by-cycle execution details of each hardware resource. Both the datapath and controller are described in hardware description language (HDL) and finally integrated to form the RTL system schematic/HDL description of the DSP/multimedia application. As the security algorithms/features of DSP IP cores are integrated during one or more HLS synthesis steps, hence understanding the HLS design framework is crucial. More details on HLS design process are available in [Sengupta and Mohanty (2016)](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib23).

## 1.5 Hardware-assisted media protection

The idea of ownership right, copyright, or IPR that a CE system can be subjected to is depicted in [Figure 1.9](#Chapter1.html#c01_fig9). As far as ownership right is concerned, the common factor of hardware, software, overall CE system, or media piracy is the loss of revenue of the actual or legal owner. But, these ownership issues/attacks are diverse, and correspondingly solutions to be deployed may differ. The hardware IP core protection aspects which are results of hardware piracy have been discussed in previous sections. Overall, system piracy or cloning is also a problem in the CE area, due to which multiple fake CE systems such as cameras, USB drives, hardware components for smart cars, fake batteries, are available in black market. These may be cheap, and user may think that he/she got a good deal, without having a knowledge that these would have lots of wrong implications. For example, a cloned/fake hardware may be providing a backdoor entry to the CE system that uses such hardware. Imagine, the case of a smart car, a hacker through this back door can get access to the smart car and take control of it. This can lead to fatal consequences, e.g., an attacker/hacker can create accidents. Similarly, a fake/cloned battery can be a safety hazard for the CE system as a bad battery can explode and catch fire, examples of all such several case scenarios are available in the Internet. Use of pirated software also can have potential backdoor for the hackers to take control over the system. Every CE system processes data, information, video, audio, and game in every second of its active state ([Mohanty, 2017a](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib11), [2017b](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib12)). It is estimated that the sensors, social networks, web pages, image and video applications, and mobile devices generate more than 2.5 quintillion bytes (i.e., 10^(18) bytes) data per day ([Mohanty *et al.*, 2016](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib15)). This will be further complicated as smart cities initiatives around the world take momentum in which IoT with billions of connective devices process data throughout the day and night. It is rightly said that `it is not oil rather it is data that is the new gold`. In terms of media piracy video, audio have impact on the entertainment industry, online streaming companies, and various-related industry in terms of revenue loss. In the chapter corresponding to this ownership matters, discussions will be presented on the hardware-assisted solutions for media protection. Both software-based and hardware-based solutions are possible, but hardware provides advantages in terms of real-time performance and protection right from the source-end to consumer-end.

## 1.6 Physical unclonable functions

It is a fact that most of the security, privacy, and IP rights solutions are based on some forms of encryption, scrambling, hashing, and watermarking. These mechanisms rely on some form of random number or pseudorandom number. It is a fact that a general-purpose processor, which is a deterministic machine, computes the next instruction based on a program counter that cannot generate true random numbers. Also, the keys random/pseudorandom numbers typically are stored in the memory or drives for possible use in future. Hacking a system is a matter of time only, and it is possible for a hacker to get access to it and consequently access to the system and information that it contains. Primitive PUF hardware generates a random number on the fly as shown in [Figure 1.10](#Chapter1.html#c01_fig10) and completely eliminate the need of storing keys on a hard-drive, thus making it secure ([Yanambaka *et al.*, 2017](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib30), [2018](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib31); [Joshi *et al.*, 2017](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib7)). A PUF relies on the variations at the physical level of the IC which is originated due to the nanoscale semiconductor manufacturing. Thus, PUF makes good use of the process variations instead of a designer trying to reduce the impact of the process variations on the IC. The output generated by a particular PUF is unique to it, and hence serves like a signature of it. The PUF chapter will discuss on topics such as PUF principles, various types of PUFs, and applications of PUFs.
