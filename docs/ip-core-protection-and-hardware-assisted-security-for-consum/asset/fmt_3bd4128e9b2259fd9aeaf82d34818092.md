#### 12.3.3 Randomness (uniformity)

For the output of a PUF to be random, the key should contain equal number of 1’s and 0’s. This ensures that the output of a PUF is random and secure to be deployed in an application. Apart from the HD, the randomness parameter validates the keys generated by the PUF. In some cases, it is also useful in determining the error rate in the output.

#### 12.3.4 Correlation (bit aliasing)

The correlation characteristics of a PUF are of two different types: (1) bit and (2) chip. There should be no correlation between any of the bits generated by the PUF module. If a different challenge is given to the PUF, the response should be different. And various chips on the same wafer should not affect the working of a PUF module and generate the same keys. In some cases, the manufacturing variations will be uniform across the wafer. In such cases, there is a chance that with the CRPs of one PUF chip, the other can be predicted.

#### 12.3.5 Power consumption

A PUF is designed to be deployed in devices like RFID tags which consume very low power. In an IoT environment, there is a chance that the devices are battery operated and need to work with low power consumption. PUF is also being deployed in various smart devices which require a low-power operation. Hence designing the PUF with low-power consumption is vital.

#### 12.3.6 Speed

There are various IoT environments depending on the place they are deployed and the application tasks they are supposed to perform. Based on the various tasks, there is a need that the environment has to send and receive the data in real time. In such cases, there is a need for security but the same cannot be a bottleneck to the processing speed or increase the transfer latency. Hence the speed with which the keys are generated is also going to play a major role in where the respective design is going to be deployed.

### 12.4 Classification of PUFs

PUFs can be classified based on the fabrication method and security strength as shown in [Figure 12.6 (](#Chapter12.html#c12_fig6)[Chang *et al*., 2017](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib5); [Joshi *et al*., 2017](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib26); [Škorić *et al*., 2005](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib66)). In a broad sense, there are two categories of PUFs. They are categorized based on the types of devices used to design the PUF and the other category is based on the level of security provided by the module itself. Further, each of the categories has different types of PUFs. Various researchers around the world have proposed different types of PUF: these are silicon-based, non-silicon-based, electronic, and non electronic. Based on the level of security provided, there are three types of PUFs, strong, controlled, and weak.

![image](OEBPS/images/PBCS0600-12-f06.jpg)

Figure 12.6 Different types of PUFs

#### 12.4.1 Device-based PUFs

Various devices are used by researchers to manufacture PUF. A PUF uses the manufacturing variability present in the devices to generate the outputs. Not all the devices in the world are ideal. Therefore, different varieties of devices can be implemented at the core of design to generate the PUF keys.

##### 12.4.1.1 Silicon-based PUFs

Almost all the ICs are made from silicon and other materials. This category of PUFs is made on the silicon and fabricated on the same die as part of the IC. Various designs of PUF are proposed by researchers around the world depending on the implementation and the area of deployment. Examples of this type of PUFs are SRAM ([Schrijen and Leest, 2012](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib65)), butterfly ([Kumar *et al*., 2008](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib33)), arbiter ([Lao and Parhi, 2011](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib34)), RO ([Rahman *et al*., 2014](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib56)), and lightweight secure ([Majzoobi *et al*., 2008](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib46)). These PUFs will be extracting the manufacturing variations on the IC which can generate the keys for cryptographic purposes.

##### 12.4.1.2 Non-silicon PUFs

PUFs that are not classified as silicon PUFs are referred as non-silicon PUFs. They are fabricated in silicon systems but require special fabrication techniques which are not part of a generic CMOS fabrication technology. The response is generated from the challenge set obtained from the random physical variation in the physical system rather than the ICs. Such types of PUFs are optical, acoustic, magnetic, radio frequency (RF), coating, etc.

#### 12.4.2 Security-based PUFs

Once a PUF is fabricated, the design itself cannot be modified. Hence, various designs of PUFs will generate different number of CRPs. The CRPs will decide where the particular design of PUF can be deployed and used. Based on the category, PUF is of three types: strong, controlled, and weak.

##### 12.4.2.1 Strong PUF

The strong PUF is the types of designs that will be able to generate more number of CRPs. The CRPs can also be not easily extracted from the device. A protection is provided through a complex input–output relations and an exponential number of CRPs. This type of PUF operates in the range of few MHz, so even a short duration of eavesdropping can collect many CRPs. However, all known strong PUFs are vulnerable to one or more types of attacks. This type of PUFs is frequently used for device authentication purposes. Some common example of circuits belonging to this class are arbiter, feed-forward arbiter, XOR arbiter, lightweight secure, and optical PUFs. This class of PUFs is also frequently referred to as super-high information content (SHIC) ([Lugli *et al*., 2013](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib39); [Ruhrmair *et al*., 2011](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib62)).

##### 12.4.2.2 Weak PUF

This category of PUFs will not be able to generate more number of CRPs. The least number of CRPs that can be generated is “1” in a worst-case scenario. Therefore, this design can be easily vulnerable to attacks. This is the reason that the design of this PUFs cannot be revealed publicly. These are mainly used to derive a secret key in cryptographic algorithms and are the least susceptible to modeling attacks. Some common examples of circuits belonging to this class are SRAM, coating, and butterfly PUFs. These are also known as physically obfuscated keys (POKs).

##### 12.4.2.3 Controlled PUFs

This class of PUFs has a strong PUF as an underlying core additionally protected by control logic. Since the challenges are preprocessed first before passing to the strong PUF and the responses are preprocessed before coming out of the strong PUF, the CRPs of the strong PUF are never accessed directly. This preprocessing of challenges and responses significantly improves the security of controlled PUFs. A controlled PUF ([Yu and Devadas, 2010](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib82)) is only accessed via an algorithm which is fabricated in the same chip as the PUF such that the PUF and the algorithm are inseparable. This implies that the actual PUF outputs are never given to the outside world directly and the outputs that are given to the outside world are the processed outputs from the algorithm.
