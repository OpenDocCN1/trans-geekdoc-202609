
## Sections 12.10 and 12.8

Sections 12.10 and 12.8 presented two different types of non-silicon material-based PUF designs. This section presents another type of non-silicon-based PUF designs, magnetic PUFs. There are various devices which use magnetic properties of materials for performing operations ([Gao *et al*., 2016](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib14)).

Spin transfer torque magnetic random access memory (STT-MRAM) devices are one of them. [Figure 12.25](#Chapter12.html#c12_fig25) shows the design of an STT-MRAM (Huai, [2010](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib22)). When current passes through the fixed layer, a spin-polarized current is produced. If the current that is produced is directed toward the free layer, the angular momentum can be transferred to the layer which can change the orientation of the magnet. When the two layers are parallel (P) to each other, the resistance is low. When the two layers are anti-parallel (AP) to each other, the resistance is high.

![image](OEBPS/images/PBCS0600-12-f25.jpg)

Figure 12.25 Spin transfer torque magnetic random access memory PUF

A magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ)-based PUF was proposed by [Marukame *et al*. (2014)](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib48) which can extract the fingerprint of the spin transfer-switching properties. The characterized STS characteristics were measured experimentally by [Marukame *et al*. (2014)](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib48) and a method to extract the PUF signature was also presented. It was also observed that different programming voltages for AP to P state and P to AP state transitions were required by the MTJ. Extrinsic properties such as the process damage and junction resistance lead to the MTJ. Due to these variations, an operation sequence for the extraction of bits from PUF-based 4×4 array was implemented. Initially, the reset voltage is applied to initialize them into a state of parallelism. Then the precalibrated voltage ![image](OEBPS/images/c12-Eqn-in-59.gif)is applied for inducing the switching with a probability of 50% for majority of the MTJs. The switching will result in a random distribution of the devices in an array in various states, AP and P. Signature extraction circuit will take care of the information resistance. After extracting the information, the entire array is set into P state. For increasing the reliability, the operation sequence is repeated for extraction of the bits.

[Figure 12.26](#Chapter12.html#c12_fig26) shows a phase change material (PCM) which is similar to the previous one and takes advantage of the magnetic properties for designing the PUF. A PCM has amorphous and crystalline natures in the PCM. The difference in the resistance offered by the two states is largely different. To change the material to amorphous state, a pulse with high amplitude and fast fall time is supplied to heater to initially melt it and then quench it rapidly. So a region of high resistive amorphous material is formed in the PCM. A long fall time moderate amplitude pulse is supplied to the cell for converting it into a crystalline state which will anneal the active region at a temperature between crystallization and melting temperature which forms a crystalline material in the cell which offers low resistance. [Figure 12.27](#Chapter12.html#c12_fig27) shows the structure of a PCM array. During the reading operation, the resistance of the cell is read by sending a small current to it. This pulse will not be disturbing the state of the cell ([Wong *et al*., 2010](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib75)). The resistance offered by the PCM cell can also be controlled by passing a small amount of current, a programming pulse with intermediate amplitude rise/fall time.

## 12.13 Practical implementation of PUF

Designing a PUF module can be half the problem but the implementation and deploying the same in an environment can pose additional challenges. Any design to be validated has to be implemented in a real-time scenario where it is subject to different real-time conditions. FPGA implementations of PUF are being explored by researchers for validation of the design and implementation of their proposed designs into different applications. Deploying the design on an FPGA opens up a lot of opportunities to the PUF design and adds more to the list of applications ([Chowdhury *et al*., 2017](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib10); [Ikezaki *et al*., 2017](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib23); [Johnson *et al*., 2015](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib25)).

The FPGAs are also evolving day-by-day and adding more features for better integration into the IoT world ([Johnson *et al*., 2015](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib25)). One such capability is the dynamic partial reconfiguration (DPR) or runtime partial reconfiguration (RPR). With these features of an FPGA, the circuit design that is on the FPGA can be configured partially during the runtime. This ability is called “Partial Reconfiguration.” The power consumption of the FPGA can be reduced by using the DPR which makes them ideal for use in IoT applications. But these DPR-enabled FPGAs are subjected to various attacks by the adversary which can get the entire network of devices into jeopardy. A protection technique that can be used for protecting the DPR-enabled FPGAs was proposed by [Johnson *et al*. (2015)](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib25). An XoR PUF was employed in the paper for securing the protocol. Because of the complex nature of the PUF that is deployed in the application, modeling an attack by the attacker will be difficult as there will be no access to the PUF responses. If that access is gained by the adversary, attacking the PUF with a modeling attack will become easy and successful in some cases.

There are also many different aging-resistant PUFs designs that are implemented on the FPGAs ([Chowdhury *et al*., 2017](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib10)). The aging effects on the FPGA are presented by [Maiti and Schaumont (2014)](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib43). It was also presented that a change in the location of the output response bits and the devices that are affected by the aging are also random. There are also many designs of PUF that are implemented based on SRAM on the FPGA that use the aging effects on the design to an advantage. An aging resistant RO PUF is proposed for reliable key generation in ([Rahman *et al*., 2016](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib58)). With the FPGA lacking the ability to change the circuit architecture, implementing this idea becomes more difficult.

## 12.14 PUF: case study applications

All the above sections discussed different designs of PUFs. This section discusses different applications of PUF in various domains. There are many applications and domains where PUF can be used and implemented. One such major issue that is becoming a problem is counterfeiting. [Figure 12.28](#Chapter12.html#c12_fig28) shows a big picture of counterfeiting in the case of an IC. When an IC is designed by a manufacturer, there can be many clones designed by various third parties to be sold in the market ([Chang *et al*., 2017](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib5); [Mohanty, 2015](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib53)). But this is a major issue economically and also in terms of security. The ICs that are created by the counterfeiting party can be malicious and might contain trojans. A PUF can be implemented in the IC design so that it can be used for authenticating the original device ([Yan *et al*., 2017](#Chapter12.html#c12_bib76)).
