
As this approach handles nested loop-based applications, screening of unrolling factors for all the loops is performed to discard the unfit unrolling factors of each respective loop. The input to the pre-processing algorithm is the maximum loop iteration count of each loop and the output is the screened list of unrolling factors for that respective loop. Compared to Sengupta *et al.* ([2017b](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib27)), in this approach, the screening algorithm needs to be executed multiple times corresponding to multiple loops. Thus the implementation complexity of this approach is higher than that in Sengupta *et al.* ([2017b](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib27)). Further, in this approach, similar to that of Sengupta *et al.* ([2017b](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib27)), a low-cost design solution is obtained through a PSO-based DSE process within the user-specified design constraints. However, the encoding of each particle in this approach is significantly different from that of Sengupta *et al.* ([2017b](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib27)) and can be represented as *P* = (*R*[*n*], *M*[*v*], UF[1], UF[2], …UF[*n*]), where UF[1] indicates the unrolling factor value for the outermost nested loop and UF[*n*] indicates the unrolling factor value for innermost nested loop. Moreover to achieve distinctness, this approach uses four vendor allocation modes to obtain low-cost Trojan secured design architecture for DSP cores. Here, the four different vendor allocation modes are *M*[*v*] = 00, 01, 10, and 11, where *M*[*v*] = 00 and 01 are the same as *M*[*v*] = 0 and 1, respectively (discussed earlier). The remaining of the vendor allocation modes are defined in the following.

The rules for vendor allocation mode at `M[v] = 10` are as follows:

*   In a particular unit (either `U^(OG)` or `U^(DP)`) all critical path operations must be allocated to the hardware of one vendor and all non-critical path operations must be allocated to the hardware of another vendor. For example, if all critical path operations of `U^(OG)` are allocated to the hardware of V1 then all non-critical path operations of `U^(OG)` must be allocated to the hardware of V2.

*   Similar operation of both the units must be allocated to distinctive vendors. For example, if all critical path operations of `U^(OG)` are allocated to the hardware of V1 then all critical path operations of `U^(DP)` must be allocated to the hardware of V2 and vice versa.

The rules for vendor allocation technique at `M[v] = 11` are as follows:

*   In a particular unit (either `U^(OG)` or `U^(DP)`) alternate vendor allocation is performed for the operations belonging to subsequent unrolled nested-loop iterations. For example, if all the operations of the first unrolled loop of `U^(OG)` are allocated to the hardware of V1 then the operations of the second unrolled loop of `U^(OG)` must be allocated to the hardware of V2, following which the operations of the third unrolled loop of `U^(OG)` must be allocated to hardware of V1, and so on.

*   Similar operation of both the units must be allocated to distinctive vendors. For example, if all the operations of the first unrolled loop of `U^(OG)` are allocated to the hardware of V1 then all the operations of first unrolled loop of `U^(DP)` must be allocated to the hardware of V2 and vice versa.

#### 3.4.2 Trojan security approach for combinational/sequential circuits

Code-coverage analysis is one of the promising Trojan-detection approaches that are available in the literature. Hu ([1997](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib9)) has identified the list of suspicious signals from the RTL design. In this process, if a signal remains stable (does not change its state) during coverage analysis, it is identified as a suspicious signal. The reason for selecting these types of signal as suspicious is that a Trojan usually does not change its states until triggered. Thereafter, uncovered parts of the design are activated by adding test vectors. However, this approach suffers from huge verification time.

Equivalence analysis on only suspicious signals (instead of all the available signals of a design) can detect Trojan logic in an efficient way ([Bushnell and Agrawal, 2013](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib5)). However, this approach also incurs some runtime overhead. Additionally, all suspicious signals may not always be a Trojan logic. Moreover, the number of suspicious signals will increase with the complexity of the inserted Trojan. Thus, achieving 100% code-coverage for all IP cores through this approach is difficult. Salmani *et al.* ([2009](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib19)) devised a Trojan-detection mechanism by inserting dummy flip-flops to increase the Trojan activity. Dummy flip-flops are inserted in such a way that they increase the transition probability of nets. Finally, they are capable of increasing Trojan activity while reducing Trojan activation time. Multiple Trojan secured methodologies for C-series benchmarks at lower design abstraction are also available in the literature ([Agrawal *et al.*, 2007](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib1); [Narasimhan *et al.*, 2010](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib13); [Jin and Makris, 2008](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib10); [Wang *et al.*, 2008](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib33)). The Trojan detection process by Agrawal *et al.* ([2007](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib1)) is performed on the basis of comparison of characteristics of the manufactured chip, such as path delay and power consumption with the expected value. In detecting Trojans, Narasimhan *et al.* ([2010](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib13)) rely on the fact that the hardware Trojan inserted into a design will change the design parameters of the circuit. This approach is capable of detecting Trojans in the presence of large process variation-induced noise. Wang *et al.* ([2008](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib33)) devised a multiple-supply transient-current integration approach to detect hardware Trojan. This approach is capable of isolating the Trojan through a localized current analysis approach. However, process variations for detection and isolation of very small size Trojan circuits (less than few gates) can be negative.
