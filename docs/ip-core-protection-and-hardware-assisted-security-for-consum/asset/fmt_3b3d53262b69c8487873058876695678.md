
On the other hand, structural obfuscation tries to hide the correct functionalities of a design through transformations, arbitrary assignment of logic components, inserting dummy wires, or irregular routing ([Vijayakumar *et al.*, 2017](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib44)). In other words, structural obfuscation changes the architecture of an IP core from its standard structure to an unobvious structure while preserving its functionality. Therefore, even if an adversary physically tampers or REs the IP core to obtain its design, it will be harder for him/her to discover its functionality by inspecting its structure. Structural obfuscation does not insert any additional component as key-gate into the design. Therefore, there is no such design overhead for structural obfuscation. In structural obfuscation technique, a design is secured until its functionality is hidden from an adversary. However, if the application is traced by the attacker, structural obfuscation cannot protect an IP core.

High-level transformations such as pipelining, interleaving, unfolding, folding, tree height transformation (THT), redundant operation elimination, and look-ahead transformations can be applied during architectural synthesis of a DSP IP core to achieve a trade-off between multiple design objectives such as area, power, and delay. However, these transformations can also be used as structural obfuscation techniques ([Sengupta and Roy, 2017](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib32); [Parhi, 2012](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib20)). These high-level transformations modify the structure of a DSP IP core significantly while keeping the actual functionality unchanged. These alterations result in multiple ambiguous architectures of the same DSP IP core. Authors in Lao and Parhi ([2015](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib14)) have performed structural obfuscation for DSP circuits through a single high-level transformation technique, i.e., folding (Note: Though authors in Lao and Parhi ([2015](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib14)) have performed both structural and functional obfuscation, we are only focusing on the structural obfuscation portion in this chapter). The folding transformation generates various meaningful modes which increase the complexity for an attacker to distinguish the correct functionalities within different modes. Additionally, different folding factors cause different equivalent circuits which lead to ambiguity for an attacker. According to Lao and Parhi ([2015](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib14)), it can be observed that due to folding transformation, circuits with different functionality may have the same structure, and circuits with same functionality may have dissimilar structure. Since folding factor has a crucial impact on the design cost, selecting an appropriate folding factor may lead to an optimized DSP circuit design. However, no optimal folding factor evaluation is presented in the aforesaid approach. Thus, it may lead to higher design overhead. Additionally, single high-level transformation-based ([Lao and Parhi, 2015](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib14)) structural obfuscation for DSP applications is less secured compared to a multistage high-level transformation-based structural obfuscation ([Sengupta *et al.*, 2017a](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib36)).

Sengupta *et al.* ([2017a](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib36)) performed a multiple compiler-driven high-level transformations-based structural obfuscation for DSP core/kernels during architectural synthesis. Unlike Lao and Parhi ([2015](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib14)), this approach is capable of obfuscating loop-based DSP applications also. Further, due to multiple high-level transformations, the obfuscated DSP IP core impacts gate-level structure significantly different than its original structure, thus enhancing the RE complexity. Additionally, this approach performs optimization to select a low-cost architecture that meets the design objectives such as latency, area, and all-inclusive design cost. Therefore, a compiler-driven, low-cost, multistage transformation-based structural obfuscation with stronger robustness is presented in Sengupta *et al.* ([2017a](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib36)). A detailed explanation of this approach is discussed in Section 7.3.

## 7.3 Compiler transformation-driven structural obfuscation

As mentioned earlier, due to the insertion of additional logic elements, the design as well as lack of framework to select low cost design architecture, overhead of logic obfuscation is significantly larger than the structural obfuscation in the context of DSP IP cores. Additionally, logic obfuscation techniques are vulnerable to multiple attack strategies such as key-sensitization ([Rajendran *et al.*, 2015](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib23)), Boolean SAT, and hill-climbing search-based attack ([Subramanyan *et al.*, 2015](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib38)). Further, the structural obfuscation presented in Lao and Parhi ([2015](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib14)) is less secured against the RE attacks due to its single-stage transformation-based obfuscation technique. The multistage structural obfuscation approach presented in Sengupta *et al.* ([2017a](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib36)) hides the functionality of a DSP application by transforming its design into an unobvious structure through several successive compiler-driven transformation techniques. Though compiler-driven transformation is a known concept for design optimization, to the best of authors’ knowledge, these transformation techniques have been never used before to perform structural obfuscation. These transformations increase the complexity for an adversary to discover its functionality. Five high-level transformation approaches, i.e., redundant operation elimination (ROE), logic transformation (LT), THT, loop unrolling (LU), and loop-invariant code motion (LICM) are used to obfuscate both loop and non-loop-based DSP IP cores ([Sengupta *et al.*, 2017a](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib36)). However, loop-based transformations (i.e., LU and LICM) are not applicable to non-loop-based DSP applications. The structurally obfuscated design is further explored using PSO-based design exploration (DSE) to obtain the low-cost design architecture ([Sengupta *et al.*, 2017b](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib37)). Lastly, a low-cost, structurally obscured DSP IP is created. The illustration of low-cost multistage-driven structural obfuscation process is shown in [Figure 7.4](#Chapter7.html#c07_fig4). Detailed explanation of each high-level transformation process used in Sengupta *et al.* ([2017a](#Chapter7.html#c07_bib36)) is explained in Section 7.3.
