
Non-functional identifiers in hardware metering are sub-classified into two types: (1) reproducible and (2) unclonable. Reproducible identifiers are a unique ID stored inside a chip package, die, or a chip memory. They can be in the form of indented serial number or digitally stored serial number. Its advantages are that it does not depend on randomness and are easy to track/identify. Disadvantages are that it is easy to clone/modify and easy to overproduce. On the other hand, unclonable non-functional identifiers can use extrinsic or intrinsic methods for metering. For example, intrinsic method may use IC leakage, power, timing, path signatures, etc., which are all unique due to process variations. This method does not need any additional logic and can be used as such on existing designs. On the contrary, extrinsically unclonable non-functional identifiers uses additional logic such as `PUF` which is a structure comprising ring of oscillators (`RO`) capable of generating random values due to process variations. `PUF`s are impacted by power supply noise, temperature, delay, etc.; therefore, the values will keep on changing constantly. The other class of metering method is functional identifier-based metering. This method links identifiers to chip’s internal functional details during the synthesis process. Some of the properties are:

*   Each chip’s function gets a unique signature;
*   Additional states added generate same output;
*   Function unchanged from input to output;
*   Internal transactions unique to each chip.

The above methods discussed so far in the context of hardware metering are all passive metering methods. There exists an active metering method which uses external-locking techniques to lock IC. Some of the properties of active metering methods are:

*   This technique tries to allow IP owner to have control over;
*   Uses key encryption to lock correct functionality of a chip;
*   At the gate level, `XOR` gates are placed on selected non-critical paths;
*   Requires that every chip be activated with an external key. Only IP owner can generate key ([Alkabani and Koushanfar, 2007](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib2); [Roy *et al.*, 2008](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib18)).

## (iv) IP core/hardware fingerprinting

This hardware protection mechanism is employed when the hardware threat anticipated is false claim of ownership by the IP seller/vendor or illegal redistribution of extra copies without the knowledge of the IP buyer. The threat model is unlawful redistribution/resell of IP core copies in the market by a dishonest IP seller ([Hong and Potkonjak, 1999](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib6)). Thus, IP core fingerprinting protects against the ownership abuse of IP buyer. Although fingerprinting protects against the aforesaid threats, it can only detect and not prevent the threats. In other words, fingerprinting approach is capable of providing passive forms of protection to an IP core. More details on this protection mechanism are presented in Chapter 5.

## (v) IP core/hardware obfuscation and encryption

This hardware protection mechanism is employed when the hardware threat anticipated is IP netlist theft/counterfeit/piracy through reverse engineering. There are two types of hardware obfuscation methodologies: (1) structural obfuscation and (2) functional obfuscation. In structural obfuscation, the goal is to enhance the reverse engineering complexity to make it more challenging for an attacker to discover the actual functionality of an IP core from its transformed structure. On the contrary, in functional obfuscation, the goal is to disable the IP core through ‘keys’ such that normal functionality of the obfuscated design can only be enabled by the successful application of a single pre-determined key or a series of covert keys at the input; otherwise, an IP core design will operate in a mode, which will exhibit incorrect functionality. Both these types of obfuscation methods aim to provide protection against IP infringement and Trojan insertion. Structural obfuscation is regarded as a ‘passive’ form of obfuscation as it does not affect the functionality of the IP core, while only making syntactic changes, i.e., modifying the circuit description in a soft form such that it becomes challenging for an attacker to understand the internal working of an IP core. A major shortcoming of the passive approach is that it does not change the black-box functionality of a design, and hence unable to avert potential usage of an IP as black-box in a design. However, since it does not add extra-locking circuitry to the IP core, the chances of design overhead is lower than active approaches which makes it an equally attractive choice in CE industry. More detail on this protection mechanism is presented in Chapters 7 and 8.

## (vi) Trojan security/detection of an IP core

Hardware Trojan in the context of IP cores is a malicious alteration of logic inside IP core that causes to change its functionality such that under rare event condition, the IP core produces wrong output. This hardware protection mechanism is employed to safeguard against hardware Trojan secretly hidden inside a 3PIP cores used in SoC integration. Due to globalization of IC design chain, involvement of third-party IP cores play a crucial role in the mass production of CE devices in stricter time to market pressure. Trojan security of DSP IP cores can be achieved through dual modular redundancy technique during system-on-chip integration running concurrently with an optimization framework. This enables detection of hardware Trojan that causes functional change. More detail on this protection mechanism is presented in Chapter 3.

## (vii) Physically unclonable functions

`PUF` is a ‘digital fingerprint’ that serves as a unique identity for the hardware. It acts as hardware security primitive due to its complexity and the unpredictability between its outputs and the applied inputs. `PUF`s are based on physical variations which occur naturally during semiconductor manufacturing and which make it possible to differentiate between otherwise identical hardware. Because of the random process variations, no two ICs even with same layout are identical. There are various types of `PUF`s, such as: (1) `Latch PUF`s where the output changes due to intrinsic property of the latch; (2) `SRAM PUF` where the initial power up values of the `SRAM` cells are random; (3) `Ring of oscillator (RO) delay-based PUF` where the path delay is unpredictable due to manufacturing variation; and (4) `Arbiter PUF` where the path delay is unpredictable due to physical randomness. In arbiter `PUF`, each challenge selects a unique pair of delay gates. Random process variation determines which path is faster ([Potkonjak and Goudar, 2014](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib17)). More detail on this protection mechanism is presented in Chapter 12.

## (viii) Hardware-based encryption

Another hardware protection mechanism for securing classified data used in CE device is hardware-based encryptions. Hardware-based encryption process can be used for securing data storage and transfer, as well as for DSP IP core protection in the context of CE devices. Advanced encryption standard (`AES`) ([Paar and Pelzl, 2009](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib4)) is the most robust encryption process that can be used as the hardware-based encryption of classified data as well as in DSP IP core protection from external attacks. More detail on this protection mechanism is presented in Chapter 10.

## (ix) Patent and copyright

A patent is an authorized privilege sanctioned by the government of a specific dominion which on submission safeguards the IP of the maker and thus can only be sold and used by the approval of the owner. More explicitly, the owner is given the constitutional privilege to thwart others from commercially abusing the invention. It is the owner’s right to report the case of any contravention under civil law ([Sengupta, 2015](#Chapter1.html#c01_bib20)).
