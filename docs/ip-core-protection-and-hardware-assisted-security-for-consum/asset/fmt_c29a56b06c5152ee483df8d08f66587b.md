
# The use of heterogeneous system-on-chip (SoC) architecture in modern consumer electronics (CE) devices

The use of heterogeneous system-on-chip (SoC) architecture in modern consumer electronics (CE) devices such as smartphones, gaming consoles, tablets, and digital cameras has become a common practice in the semiconductor industry. The in-house SoC designer or the system integrator mostly imports intellectual property (IP) cores from third-party IP vendors to minimize design time and to meet strict product delivery deadlines. These third-party IP (3PIP) cores can be DSPs/media processors, such as JPEG image compression-decompression (CODEC), finite impulse response (FIR) filters, infinite impulse response (IIR) filters, fast Fourier transformation (FFT) algorithms, Haar wavelet transformation (HWT) algorithms, and discrete cosine transformation (DCT) algorithms, or they can be micro IP cores/blocks, such as adders, multipliers, and comparators. However, these 3PIP cores have severe trust issues, because an untrustworthy IP vendor may insert malicious logic elements known as “hardware Trojans” into their IP cores to introduce malfunctioning/failure of the SoCs ([Tehranipoor and Koushanfar, 2010](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib32); [Liu *et al.*, 2014](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib12); Sengupta, [2016](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib20), [2017](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib21); [Chakraborty *et al.*, 2009](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib6); [Sengupta and Kundu, 2017](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib23)) (a thematic representation of the aforesaid statement during an SoC life cycle is shown in [Figure 3.1](#Chapter3.html#c03_fig1)). Moreover, a Trojan-infected IP core may leak confidential information by creating a backdoor in the design or may degrade the performance of the processor ([Bhunia *et al.*, 2013](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib4)). The typical trusted and untrusted phases in the life cycle of a SoC design are shown in [Figure 3.2](#Chapter3.html#c03_fig2).

Detection of a hardware Trojan is not always easy or straightforward, because an intelligent adversary always inserts malicious logic that will be activated under sporadic conditions ([Wolff *et al.*, 2008](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib34)) and/or exhibit its effect for a small duration to evade detection. Additionally, thorough analysis, test, and verification of a complete SoC is complex and time-consuming. The Trojan activation condition is termed the trigger condition and, once triggered, the effect of the Trojan is termed the payload ([Narasimhan *et al.*, 2010](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib13)). Logic testing ([Banga and Hsiao, 2009](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib2)) and side-channel analysis ([Narasimhan *et al.*, 2010](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib13); [Koushanfar and Mirhoseini, 2011](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib11)) are two commonly used techniques to detect hardware Trojans ([Narasimhan *et al.*, 2010](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib13); [Liu *et al.*, 2014](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib12)). In the first approach, functional or structural testing is performed ([Chakraborty *et al.*, 2009a](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib7); [Wolff *et al.*, 2008](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib34); [Banga and Hsiao, 2009](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib2)) to generate rare conditions, so that the effect of hardware Trojan logic gets reflected in the output. This type of detection approach is effective for ultra-small Trojans ([Narasimhan *et al.*, 2010](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib13)). In the second approach, detection of hardware Trojans is performed by measuring physical “side-channel” parameters such as delay ([Jin and Makris, 2008](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib10); [Rai and Lach, 2009](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib16)) or power signature ([Agrawal *et al.*, 2007](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib1); [Banga and Hsiao, 2008](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib3); [Rad *et al.*, 2010](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib15)). In this type of approach, detection of a hardware Trojan is possible without activating the Trojan logic and monitoring its effect on the output.

There are multiple classes of hardware Trojans which we discuss in Section 3.2.1; however, we mostly focus on how to provide security (detection) against a particular type of Trojan that changes the functionality or output of a DSP-based IP core. More specifically, we discuss the design process of a low-cost hardware Trojan secured datapath design for a DSP core through the dual modular redundant (DMR) schedule and vendor allocation processes ([Rajendran *et al.*, 2013](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib17); [Sengupta *et al.*, 2017b](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib27)). To obtain a low-cost Trojan secured design, a particle swarm optimization (PSO)-based design space exploration (DSE) (Sengupta and Sedaghat, [2011](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib24), [2013](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib25)) is performed for the DSP cores.

## 3.2 Types of hardware Trojans

This section discusses the different types of hardware Trojans, the advantages of handling this type of Trojan security for DSP cores at higher abstraction level, and details of the threat model of hardware Trojans.

### 3.2.1 Trojan features

We discuss here the major classification of Trojan-based hardware threats (shown in [Figure 3.3](#Chapter3.html#c03_fig3)) ([Chakraborty *et al.*, 2009](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib6); [Sinanoglu *et al.*, 2013](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib31); [Sengupta, 2017](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib21)). Digital hardware Trojans are classified into two types: trigger-based and payload-based. As mentioned earlier, triggering of a Trojan indicates activating it, which can be performed either through a combinational or a sequential mechanism. Similarly, the payload of a Trojan that indicates the effect of Trojan logic can change the functionality, degrade the performance, or leak secret information without changing the functionality.

![image](OEBPS/images/PBCS0600-03-f03.jpg)

Figure 3.3 Broad classification of digital hardware threats

**Combinational-triggering-based hardware Trojan:** This type of Trojan gets triggered under a very rare condition or event. Until that condition arises, the circuit works correctly. For example, as shown in [Figure 3.4](#Chapter3.html#c03_fig4), the original output (*Q*) will be same as the Trojan-infected output (*Q*^(\*)) for all input combinations of *A* and *B* except *A* = *B* = 0. In this particular case, the inserted Trojan logic triggers and the incorrect output in *Q*^(\*) will be generated as payload. Therefore, in this circuit, *A* = *B* = 0 is the triggering condition.

![image](OEBPS/images/PBCS0600-03-f04.jpg)

Figure 3.4 Combinational-triggering-based hardware Trojan under a rare event ([Chakraborty et al., 2009](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib6))

**Sequential-triggering-based hardware Trojan:** This type of Trojan gets triggered at a particular count of the counter. Once triggered, the circuit produces an incorrect output ([Chakraborty *et al.*, 2009](#Chapter3.html#c03_bib6)). For example, as shown in [Figure 3.5](#Chapter3.html#c03_fig5), there are *p* counters and all the counters execute synchronously using a clock signal. Once the counter reaches 2^(*p*)−1 count, the Trojan logic gets triggered and the incorrect output in *Q*^(\*) will be generated as payload instead of *Q*. As this type of Trojan gets triggered in a certain time period, it is also known as a “time-bomb hardware Trojan.”
