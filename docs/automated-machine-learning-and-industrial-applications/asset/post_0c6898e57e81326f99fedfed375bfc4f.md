### 13.1.1 Some of the Recent Publications and their Findings

Taojian Fan et al. [1] discussed the various 2D models and imaging currently in use in the biomedical industry as well as their potential shortfalls. They point out why it is vital to deploy drugs directly at the target site and also reviewed various 2D models currently in use.

Pandiraj Manickam et al. [2] researched on the current usage of the Internet of Medical Things(IoMT) in the industry and how, with the addition of AI to it, we can make it much more efficient. Their study found that AI helped improve the IoMT models as well as point of care (POC) devices.

LM Patrick et al. [3] leveraged AI techniques to improve data quality and better detect endogenous metabolites and diseases linked by applying it to the field of high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS).

Xiangzhou et al. [4] reviewed various recent practices, such as drug delivery at site development of biomembrane cloaked nano platforms derived from inherent host cells. Their study also highlighted potential paths for better vaccine development inspired by their diverse moieties.

Hua Ai et al. [5] demonstrated that thin films can be deposited on virtually any surface with the use of biomaterials up to a precision of 1 nm. JN Acosta et al. [6] developed techniques for low-cost utilization of multimodal datasets for healthcare. They also reviewed current industry practices and recent developments that make use of low-cost genome sequencing, more data from medical imaging, etc., to make a more robust model.

Gao et al. [7] worked on 2D biomedical imaging data and applied deep learning techniques, and one of their proposed model was a data-driven diagnostic model for hip dysplasia. They also developed a chest CT prediction model, which predicted the presence of the corona virus by comparing it to an NHL via a radiologist and their developed CT analysis software.

F. Qian et al. [8] utilized AI techniques for biogenesis and composition for extracellular vesicle (EV) cargo analysis. Ahmadi et al. [9] observed the reaction on horseradish peroxidase catalyzed by hydrogels. They used AI techniques to simulate and model the bio-fabrication process.

Tao Tu et al. [10] developed a medical large language model (LLM) Med PalM M, which can flexibly encode and interpret biomedical data. Their reports were preferred 40% more than the ones generated by radiologists showing real-world viability.

## 13.2 Current Day Usage of AI

The healthcare sector is currently utilizing artificial intelligence (AI) and related technologies, which are spreading throughout society and business. Numerous facets of patient care as well as administrative procedures used by payers, pharmaceutical companies, and healthcare providers may change as a result of these technologies.

AI has already been shown in numerous tests to be either as effective as humans or even more so in medical tasks like illness diagnosis. Radiologists can already identify potentially harmful cancers using algorithms, and they may also advise researchers on how to create cohorts for costly clinical studies. But before AI completely replaces people in medical processes, a number of years will pass. In this section, we take a look at some of the current day practices making use of AI to make it more efficient.

### 13.2.1 Deep Learning and Neural Networks

There is no lack of medical data. Each year, a hospital or clinic generates a vast amount of data from tests conducted on patients as well as forms filled and operations conducted. Now, if you consider a country or even just a city with the amount of data present, a model can be trained to accurately predict, for example, based on past data, which particular treatment would be more effective for a patient. This has potential to outperform human expertise since the model trained would have access to a much larger repository of data than any single hospital/clinic could have.

Neural networks have been used since the past 60 years for mapping inputs to outputs and make predictions for new data given to it. They function analogous to how the neurons work in the brain where we have different layers in our model with separate activation functions, which help process the data bit by bit until we get our desired output.

### 13.2.2 Natural Language Processing (NLP)

NLP focuses on interaction between humans and machines. It is the interface that enables smoother transition, enhances processing, and improves patient care. Some of its current usage in healthcare includes the following:

### 13.2.2.1 Clinical Documentation

NLP is used to convert spoken to written format and also helps in better storage and organization of our data. It helps in better categorization and standardization for electronic health data.

### 13.2.2.2 Disease Prediction

NLP can be deployed on a larger scale to conduct sentiment analysis and track the outbreak of any disease or epidemic beforehand. Analysis of social media, hospital records for strange cases, etc., can all be seen as potential uses of this.

### 13.2.2.3 Chatbots and Virtual Assistants

With the rise in use of chatbots virtually in all sectors, even in healthcare, it is much more useful to have users communicate with chatbots for faster 24/7 support, and the virtual assistants can help reduce demand on hospital staff, who can help oversee and step in if the bot flags someone having an emergency.

### 13.2.2.4 Report Analysis

Majority of the lab tests conducted convey a standard result of positive or negative and also whether the count of something, like platelets, for example, is low or not. With NLP, we can help speed this process up and directly provide the result of the analysis.
