These examples, taken from reality, are important to us as practicing machine learners, because they are symptomatic of a larger problem concerning how performance is reported in machine learning. To understand this problem, let again concentrate on a series of concrete examples, where the misleading nature of machine learning can be demonstrated.

# Criminal sentencing

The first example relates to the Compass algorithm, already discussed in Section 12.1. The algorithm is based on comprehensive data taken from offenders. It uses first component analysis (unsupervised learning) and logistic regression (supervised learning) to make predictions of whether a person will reoffend within two years. The performance was primarily measured using ROC (see Figure 4.13a for details of ROC curve) and the AUC of the resulting model, was typically slightly over 0.70 (Brennan et al. 2009).

To put this performance in context, we can compare it to a logistic regression model, with only two variables—age of defendant and number of prior convictions—trained to predict year recidivism rates for the Broward County data set collected by Julia Angwin and others Performing a 9/10 training/test split on this data, David Summer (2018) found an AUC of 0.73: to all practical purposes the same as the Compass algorithm. This regression model's coefficients implied that older defendants are less likely to be arrested for crimes, while those with more priors are more likely to be arrested.

This result calls into question both the problem of collecting data to put in an algorithm—the interviews added very little power over age and priors—and it contributed to the decision-making process—most judges are aware that age and priors play a role in whether a person will commit a crime in the future. A valid question is: what does the model actually do? In order to answer this and test much predictive power we need to have a sensitive benchmark to compare to.

One simple way is to see how humans perform on the same task. Dressel and Farid (2018) paid Mechanical Workers, all of whom were used in the USA, $1 to evaluate 500 defendant cases from the Probapus dataset (Dressel and Farid 2018). After each decision, the participants were asked, "Do you think this person will commit another crime within two years?", they answered "yes" or "no". On average, the participants were at a level comparable to the Compass algorithm—with an AUC of 0.7— suggesting very little advantage to the recommendation used.

These results imply that models should be used wisely in decision-making. In some cases, humans are prone to "seat of pants" judges that lead to incorrect decisions (Holinger et al. 2018). Instead, the message is about how we communicate performance. In the case of the Compass algorithm applied to the Probapus dataset, the performance is comparable to that of Mechanical Workers who were paid $1 to assess cases. Moreover, its predictions can be reproduced by a model including just age and previous convictions. For a sentencing application, it is sufficient to put it in production.

In other contexts advertising with human-level performance might be appropriate. For example, for a model used to suggest filters or products in online advertising, a performance level could would be deemed acceptable.
