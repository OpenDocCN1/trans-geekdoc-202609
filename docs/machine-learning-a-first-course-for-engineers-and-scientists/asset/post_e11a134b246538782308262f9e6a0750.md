Underfitting Overfitting

![](img/9c84a60f9e70ea0bf5c7c51ae709df3e_63_0.png)

**Figure 4.3:** Behavior of $\hat{E}_{train}$ and $\hat{E}_{new}$ for many supervised machine learning methods, as a function of model complexity. We have not made a formal definition of complexity, a rough proxy is the number of parameters that are learned from the data. The difference between the two curves is the generalization gap. The training error $\hat{E}_{train}$ decreases as the model complexity increases, whereas the new data error $\hat{E}_{new}$ typically has a U-shape. If the model is so complex that $\hat{E}_{new}$ is larger than it had with a less complex model, the term *overfit* is commonly used. Somewhat less is the term *underfitting* for the opposite situation. The level of model complexity gives the minimum $\hat{E}_{new}$ (at the dotted line) could be called a balanced fit. When we, for example, cross-validation to select hyperparameters (that is, tuning the model complexity), we are searching for a balanced fit.

The generalization gap is the difference between the expected performance on training data and the expected performance 'production' on new, previously unseen data. With the decomposition of $\hat{E}_{new}$ into

```
$\hat{E}_{new} = \hat{E}_{train} + generalization gap,$ (4.11)
```

we also have an opening for digging deeper and to understand what effects $\hat{E}_{new}$ has in practice. We will refer to (4.11) as the *training–generalization gap decomposition* of $\hat{E}_{new}$.

### What affects the generalization gap

The generalization gap depends on the method and the problem. Concerning the method, one can typically say that the more a method adapts to training data the larger the generalization gap. A theoretical framework for how a method adapts to training data is given by the so-called Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension. From the VC dimension framework, probabilistic bounds on the generalization gap can be derived, but those are unfortunately not conservative, and we will not pursue that approach further. Instead, if we use the term model complexity or *model flexibility* which we mean the ability of a method to adapt to patterns in the training data. A model with high complexity (such as a fully connected neural network, deep trees and k-NN with $k$ small) can describe complex input-output relationships, whereas a model with low complexity (such as logistic regression) is less flexible in what it can describe. For parametric models, the model complexity is related to the number of learnable parameters, but is also affected by regularization techniques. As we come back later, the idea of model complexity is an oversimplification and does not capture the full nature of various supervised machine learning methods, but it nevertheless carries some intuition.

Typically, higher model complexity implies a larger generalization gap. Furthermore, $\hat{E}_{train}$ decreases as the model complexity increases, whereas $\hat{E}_{new}$ typically attains a minimum for some intermediate model value: too small and too high model complexity raises $E_{new}$. This is illustrated in Figure 4.3. A too high model complexity, meaning that $E_{new}$ is higher than it is with a less complex model, is called overfitting. The other situation, when model complexity is low, sometimes called underfitting. In a consistent terminology, the point where $E_{new}$ attains minimum could be referred to as a balanced fit. Since the goal is to minimize $E_{new}$, we are interested in this sweet spot. We also illustrate this by Example 4.1.

Note that we discuss the usual behavior of $E_{train}$ and the generalization gap. We use the term ‘usual’ because there are so many supervised learning methods and problems that it is almost impossible to make any claim that is always true for all possible situations, and counterexamples also exist. One should also keep in mind that $E_{train}$ and $E_{new}$ are the average behavior when the model is trained and evaluated on (hypothetical) training data sets, see Example 4.1.

We see in Figure 4.5 that $k=2$ (right) adapts too much the data. With $k=70$ (left), on the other hand, the model is not enough to adapt to the noise, but possibly to be too inflexible to adapt well to the dotted line in Figure 4.4.

We can compute $E_{train}$ by counting the fraction of misclassified training data points in Figure 4.5. From left to right, we get $E_{train}=0.27, 0.24, 0.2$. Since this is a simulated example, we can also access $E_{new}$ (or estimate it numerically by simulating a test of data), and from left to right we get $E_{new}=0.26, 0.23, 0.33$. This resembles Figure 4.3, except for the fact that $E_{new}$ is smaller than $E_{train}$ for some values of $k$. However, to study $E_{new}$ we repeat this experiment 100 times:

The generalization gap (difference $E_{new}$ and $\hat{E}_{train}$) is positive and increases with model complexity, as $k=2$ suffers from overfitting, whereas $k=70$ is a case of underfitting.

We have so far concerned about the generalization gap and model complexity. Another very important aspect is the size of the training data, $n$. We can in general expect that the more data, the smaller the generalization gap. On the other hand, $\hat E_{train}$ typically increases as $n$ increases, since most models are unable to fit all data points if there are too many of them. A typical behavior of $\hat E_{train}$ and $E_{new}$ is sketched in Figure 4.6.

The generalization gap (difference between $E_{new}$ and $\hat E_{train}$) decreases, the same time as $n$ increases. However, the generalization gap is typically larger for a complex model when the training dataset is small.

## Reducing $E_{new}$ in practice

Our goal is to achieve a small error “in production”, that is, a small $E_{new}$. To achieve that, according to the decomposition $E_{new} = \hat E_{train} + generalization gap$, we need to have $\hat E_{train}$ as well as the generalization gap small. Let us draw some conclusions from what we have seen so far.

- The new error $E_{new}$ will not on average be smaller than the training error $\hat E_{train}$. Thus, if $\hat E_{train}$ is much bigger than $E_{new}$ you need for your model to be successful at the application hand, you do not need to waste time implementing cross-validation for estimating $E_{new}$. Instead, you should re-think the problem and method you are using.
- The generalization gap and $E_{new}$ typically decreases as $n$ increases. Thus, if possible, the size of the training data may help a lot with reducing $E_{new}$.

Making the model more flexible decreases $\hat E_{train}$, but often increases the generalization gap. Making the model less flexible, on the other hand, typically decreases the generalization gap but increases $\hat E_{train}$.
