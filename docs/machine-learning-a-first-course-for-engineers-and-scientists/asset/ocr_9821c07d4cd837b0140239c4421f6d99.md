# #9.4 Practical aspects of the Gaussian process

When using the Gaussian process as a method for supervised machine learning, there are a few important design choices left to the user. Like the methods presented in Chapter 8, the Gaussian process is a kernel method, and the choice of kernel is very important. Most kernels contain a few hyperparameters, also have to be chosen. That choice can be done by maximizing the marginal likelihood, which we will discuss now.

# #Kernel choice

Since the Gaussian process can be understood as the Bayesian version of kernel ridge regression, the Gaussian process also requires a positive semidefinite kernel. Any of the positive semidefinite kernels presented in Section 8.4 can therefore be used for Gaussian processes.

Among many kernels presented in this book, the Gaussian process could be the method where the exact choice of kernel has the biggest impact since the Gaussian posterior predictive $f(\mathbf{x},y)$ has a mean and a variance, of which both are heavily affected by the choice of kernel. It is important to make a good choice, and the discussion in Section 8.4 can also be instructive to compare different kernel choices in Figure 9.1, at least when working with one dimensional problems where visualization is possible. For example, as can be seen from Figure 9.11 the squared exponential and the Matérn kernel with $
u=5$ corresponds to drastically different assumptions about the smoothness of $f(x)$.

A positive semidefinite $\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')$ remains a positive semidefinite kernel when multiplied with a positive constant $c^2$. For the methods in Chapter 8 a scaling has no impact on what can be achieved by tuning the regularization parameter $\lambda$. However, for the Gaussian process it is important to also choose a constant $c^2$ since it becomes an important factor in the predicted variance.

In the end the kernel, with all its hyperparameters $c^2$ and $	au^2$, is a design choice left to the machine learning engineer. In the Bayesian perspective the kernel is an important part of the prior that implements crucial assumptions about the function $f$.

# #Hyperparameter tuning

Most kernels $\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')$ contain a few hyperparameters, such as $\ell$ and $\alpha$ and some parameter $\sigma^2$, in addition to the noise variance $\sigma^2$. Some of the hyperparameters are possible to set manually, for example if they have a natural interpretation, but most often hyperparameters are left as parameters for the user. We jointly refer to all those hyperparameters that need to be chosen as $\eta$, meaning that $\eta$ could be used for this purpose. Cross-validation but the Bayesian approach also comes to maximize the marginal likelihood $p(y)$ as a method for selecting hyperparameters in (9.4). To emphasize $\eta$ enters into the marginal likelihood, we will add the subscript $\eta$ to all terms that depends on it. From the construction of the Gaussian process, we have $p_\eta(y)=\mathcal{N}(y;0,K_\eta(X,X))$, and consequently

> $ln p_{\eta}(y)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^	op K_{\eta}(X,X)^{-1}\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{2}\ln \det(K_{\eta}(X,X))-\frac{n}{2}\log 2.$ (9.27)

In other words, the hyperparameters of the Gaussian process can be chosen by tuning the optimization problem of maximizing (9.27) with respect to $\eta$. If using this approach, solving the optimization problem can be seen as a part of learning the Gaussian process, which is illustrated in Figure 9.12.

When selecting hyperparameters $\eta$ of kernel, it is important to be aware that (9.27) as a function of the hyperparameters may have multiple local maxima, as we illustrate by Figure 9.13. It is important to carefully choose the initialization of the optimization procedure. The challenge with local maxima is not unique to the marginal likelihood approach, but can also be addressed through cross-validation to used hyperparameters.
