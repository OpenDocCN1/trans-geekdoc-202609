Consequently, the principal axes (columns of $Y$) are the same as the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. Furthermore, the eigenvalues are given by the squared singular values, normalized by $n$. The eigenvectors and eigenvalues of a covariance matrix can be said to denote its “geometry”. If we think about fitting a Gaussian distribution to the data and then draw a level curve of this will take the form of an ellipse. The shape of the ellipse is identified with the covariance matrix of the distribution. Specifically, the principal axes of the ellipse correspond to the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix (which are the same as the principal axes of the data). Furthermore, the variances of the data in the directions of the principal axes are given by the corresponding eigenvalues. The width of the ellipse along each principal axis is proportional to the standard deviation of the data along this direction, which corresponds to the singular values of the data. We illustrate this in the right panel of Figure 10.11.

As a final comment, we note that it often can be a good idea to standardize the data before applying PCA, in particular if the different variables $x_j, j=1, \dots, p$ have very different scales. Otherwise the principal directions can be heavily biased towards certain variables simply because they are expressed in a unit with a dominant scale. However, if the units and scales of the variables are meaningful for the problem at hand, it can also be argued that standardizing counters the purpose of PCA since the intention is to find the directions with maximum variance. Thus, what is most appropriate needs to be decided on a case-by-case basis.
