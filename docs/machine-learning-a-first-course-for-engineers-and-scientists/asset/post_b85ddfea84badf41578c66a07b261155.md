Angwin and his colleagues noted that the false positive rate for black defendants, 805/(805+990) = 44.8%, is almost double that of white defendants, 349/(349+1139) = 23.4%. This difference cannot be accounted for by simply overall reoffenders; as this is higher for black defendants (51.4% arrested for another offence within two years) when compared to white defendants (39.2%), the differences are smaller than those of false positive rates. In this basis, the model is clearly unfair in terms of true positive rate (recall). For black defendants this is 1369/(1369+532) = 72.0% vs 505/(505+349) = 52.2% for white. White defendants who go to commit crimes are more likely to be classified as lower risk.

In response to criticism the fairness of their method, the company Northpointe countered that in terms of performance (predictive positive value) was equal for both groups; 1369/(1369+806) = 63.0% for black defendants and 505/(505+349) = 59.1% for white. In this sense the model performs for both groups. Moreover, Northpointe argued that it is precision which is required, to be equal for different categories. Again this is the problem we highlighted above, but with serious repercussions for the policy they applied on: black people who won't reoff are more likely to be classified as high risk than white people.

Would be possible (in theory) to create a model that was fair in terms of false positives and precision? To answer this question consider the following confusion matrix.

| Category 1 | Negative y = -1 | Positive y = 1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Predicted negative ($\hat{y} = -1$) | $n_1 - f_1$ | $p_1 - t_1$ |
| Predicted positive ($\hat{y} = 1$) | $f_1$ | $t_1$ |

| Category 2 | Negative y = -1 | Positive y = 1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Predicted negative ($\hat{y} = -1$) | $n_2 - f_2$ | $p_2 - t_2$ |
| Predicted positive ($\hat{y} = 1$) | $f_2$ | $t_2$ |

Here, $n_i$ and $p_i$ are the number of individuals in the negative and positive classes, and $f_i$ and $t_i$ are the number of false and true positives respectively. The values of $n_i$ and $p_i$ are by the models controllers, they are constrained by outcomes in the results produced by the machine learning algorithm. For each category, we are constrained by a tradeoff between $f_i$ and $t_i$, i.e., as determined by the ROC for model 1. A similar constraint applies to category 2. We can't make our model arbitrarily.

However, we can (potentially using the ROC for each category as a guide) to tune $f_1$ and $f_2$ for both categories, independently of each other. In particular, can we argue that our model has the same false positive rate for both categories,

$$f_1 = \frac{f_2}{n_2}, \quad (12.1)$$

## Ethics in machine learning

In practice such a balance may be difficult to achieve, but our purpose is to show that limitations exist even when we control our model. Similarly, let's assume we can specify that the model has the same true positive rate (recall) for all categories.

$$t_1 = \frac{t_2}{p_2}, \quad (12.2)$$

Equal precision of the model for both categories is determined by $t_1/(t_1+f_1) = t_2/(t_2+f_2)$. Substituting (12.1) and (12.2) in this equality:

$$t_2 + \frac{f_2}{n_1} = \frac{t_2}{f_2}$$

which holds only if $f_1 = f_2 = 0$ or if

$$\frac{p_1}{n_1} = \frac{p_2}{n_2} \quad (12.3)$$

In words, Equation (12.3) implies that we can achieve equal precision when the classifier is on the positive and negative classes for both categories is equal. Both of these conditions are beyond our control as modelers. In particular, the number in each category is, as we stated, determined by the real world. Men and women suffer different medical conditions at rates; young and old people have different interests in systemic racism. These differences cannot be eliminated by model.

In general, our analysis shows that it is possible to achieve equality in precision, true positive and false rate. If we set our parameters so our model is fair for two of these classes, then we can satisfy the condition of (12.3) as a consequence of the third. Unless the positive and negative classes occur at the same rate for both then achieving all three is impossible. The result above has been refined by Kleinberg and colleagues, who include properties of the classifier, $f(x)$ in their derivation (Kleinberg et al. 2018).

Various methods have been suggested by researchers to achieve results as close as possible to all three criteria. We do not discuss them here, for one simple reason: We wish to emphasize that 'fairness' is not primarily a technical problem. The ethics through awareness empowers our responsibility to be aware of these limitations and explain them to clients and joint decision should be made to navigate the pitfalls.

## Criminal sentencing

The first example relates to the Compass algorithm, already discussed in Section 12.1. The algorithm is based on comprehensive data taken from offenders. It uses first component analysis (unsupervised learning) and logistic regression (supervised learning) to make predictions of whether a person will reoffend within two years. The performance was primarily measured using ROC (see Figure 4.13a for details of ROC curve) and the AUC of the resulting model, was typically slightly over 0.70 (Brennan et al. 2009).

To put this performance in context, we can compare it to a logistic regression model, with only two variables—age of defendant and number of prior convictions—trained to predict year recidivism rates for the Broward County data set. Angwin and others Performing a 9/10 training/test split on this data, Summer (2018) found an AUC of 0.73: to all practical purposes the same as the Compass algorithm. This regression model's coefficients implied that older defendants are less likely to be arrested, while those with more priors are more likely to be arrested. This result calls into question the algorithm added very little power over age and priors—and it contributed to the decision-making process.
