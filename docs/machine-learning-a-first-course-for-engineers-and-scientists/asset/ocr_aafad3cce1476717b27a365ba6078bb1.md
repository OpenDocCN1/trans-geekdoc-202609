The one-dimensional scale in Figure 4.8 and the notion of a bias-variance tradeoff is a simplified picture; decreased bias does not always increased variance, and vice versa. However, to contrast the decomposition of $E_{new}$ into training error and generalization gap, the bias and variance decomposition can show why $E_{new}$ decreases for different methods: sometimes, the superiority of one method over other can be attributed to either a lower bias or a lower variance.

A sample (and useless) way to increase the variance without decreasing the bias in linear regression, is to first alter the parameters using normal equations and then add zero-mean random noise to them. The error noise does not affect the bias, but increases the variance and leaves the average model $\hat{f}$ unchanged. This of training linear regression would be pointless in practice since it increases $E_{new}$, but it is a clear clear.) It illustrates the fact that increased variance does not automatically leads to decrease bias.

A much useful way of dealing with bias and variance is the meta-method called bagging, discussed in Chapter 7. It makes of several copies (an ensemble) of a base model, each trained on a slightly different version of the training dataset. By bagging averages over many base models, it reduces the variance, but the bias remains unchanged. By bagging instance the base model, the variance is decreased without significantly increasing the bias, in often resulting in a decrease in $E_{new}$.
