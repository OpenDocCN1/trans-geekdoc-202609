The two images are selected at random from those of people referred by a specialist for a scan. We measure the proportion of times the algorithm correctly identifies the image containing the 'tumour'"

Medical 2 "An algorithm is two input images, containing a cancerous tumour, not containing a cancerous tumour. The two images are selected randomly from people involved in a mass screening programme, where all measure the proportion of times the algorithm correctly identifies the image containing the 'tumour'"

The difference between these three scenarios lies in the prior likelihood that the person being scanned is positive. In Medical 1 it is likely to be relatively large, since the specialist ordered the scan because she suspected the patient might have a tumour. In Medical 2 the prior likelihood is low, since most people will not have a tumour. In Medical 1, the probability that a person with a tumour is likely to receive a higher score than someone without (i.e. AUC) is likely to be a good measure of performance, In Medical 2, the probability that a person with a tumour is likely to receive a higher score than someone without, is unless most people do not have a tumour. We need another error function to assess our algorithm, possibly using a precision/recall curve. In Medical 0, we need more information about the medical test before we assess performance. By clearly outlining our performance criteria and the data it is based on we can make sure that we use the correct measure of performance from the start of our machine learning task.

We have concentrated on AUC for two reasons: (i) it is a very popular way of measuring performance and (ii) it is a particularly striking example of how technical jargon gets in the way of a more complex, application based understanding. It is important to realise, though, that the same applies to all of the terminology used in this book, particular and machine learning. A quick glance at Table 4.1 reveals the confusing and esoteric terminology used to describe performance, of which hinders understanding and can create problems.

Instead of using this, when discussing false positives in the context of a mass screening for a medical condition, we would say "percentage of people who incorrectly called for a check-up" and when talking about false negatives we would say "percentage of the condition who were missed by the screening". This will allow us to easily discuss the relative costs of false positives and false negatives in one number anyway. Terms such as 'misclassification error' would be referred to as 'proportion of times that is incorrect', emphasizing that this measurement is limited because it does not differentiate between the condition and the tumour.

The ethical challenge here lies in honesty in communication. It is the responsibility of the data scientist to understand the domain they are working in and the error functions they use to that domain.
