# Explaining models in an understandable way

In Chapter 4 we defined AUC as the area under the curve plotting false positive rate against true positive rate. This is widely used performance measure in applications, and it is therefore important to think deeply about what it implies about our model. To help with this, we now give another, more intuitive, definition of AUC for four problem domains

**Medical** An algorithm is given two images, one containing a cancerous, one not containing a cancerous tumour. The two images are selected at random from those of people referred by a specialist for a case. AUC is the proportion of times the algorithm correctly identifies the image containing the tumour

**Personality** An algorithm is given two randomly chosen Facebook profiles and asked to predict which of the users is most neurotic (measured in a standardised questionnaire). AUC is the proportion of times it correctly identifies the most neurotic person.

**Goals** An algorithm is given input data of two randomly chosen shots from a season of football (soccer) and predicts whether the shot is a goal or not. AUC is the proportion of times it correctly identifies the goal.

**Sentencing** An algorithm is given demographic data of two convicted criminals, of whom one went on sentence for further crimes within the next two years. AUC is the proportion of times it identified the individual who was sentenced for further crimes.

In all four of these cases, and in general, AUC is equivalent to "the probability that a randomly chosen individual from the positive class has a higher score than a randomly chosen person from the negative class"

We prove this equivalence. To do this, we assume every member of our model. Machine learning methods can be used to produce a score, indicating whether the individual is more likely to belong to the positive class. For example, the function $g(x)$ in (3.36) produces a score for logistic regression. Usually, non-parametric machine learning methods, as $k$-nearest neighbours, dont no explicit score, but often have a parameter (e.g. $k$) which can be tuned in a way that matches. In what follows, we assume, for convenience, that the positive class typically has higher scores than the negative class.

We define a random variable $S_P$ which is the score produced by the model of a randomly chosen member of the positive class. We denote $F_P$ by the cumulative distribution of scores of the positive class, i.e.

```
$F_P(r) = p(S_P < r) = \int_{-\infty}^{r} f_P(s)ds,$$
```

where $f_P(r)$ is then the probability density of $S_P$. Likewise we define $S_N$ which is the score of a randomly chosen member of the negative class. We further denote $F_N$ by the cumulative distribution of scores of the negative class, i.e.

```
$F_N(r) = p(S_N < r) = \int_{-\infty}^{r} f_N(s)ds,$$
```

The true positive rate for a given threshold $r$ is given by $v(r) = 1 - F_P(r)$ and the false positive rate for a given threshold, is given by $u(r) = 1 - F_N(r)$. This is because all members with a score greater than $r$ are predicted to belong to the positive class.

We can use $v(r)$ and $u(r)$ to define

```
$AUC = \int_{0}^{1} v(r)u'(u)du,$$
```
