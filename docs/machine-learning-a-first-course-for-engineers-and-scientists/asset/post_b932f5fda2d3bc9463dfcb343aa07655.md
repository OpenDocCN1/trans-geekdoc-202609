
## 8 Nonlinear input transformations and kernels

The push-through matrix identity senses that $A(A^\top+I)^{-1} = (AA^\top+I)^{-1}A$ holds for any matrix $A$. By using it in (8.6), we can rewrite $\widetilde{Y}(\mathbf{x}_*)$

$$\widetilde{Y}(\mathbf{x}_*) = \frac{\mathbf{y}^\top\Phi(\mathbf{x})}{n} (\Phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\Phi(\mathbf{x}) + n\mathbf{I})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}_*) \quad (8.7)$$

It appears in (8.7) as if we compute $\phi(\mathbf{x}_*)$ without having to deal with any $d$-dimensional vectors or matrices, or if the matrix multiplication $\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^\top$ and $\Phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x})$ in (8.7) can be computed. Let us have a closer look at this:

$$\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^\top = \begin{bmatrix} \phi(x_1)^\top\phi(x_1) & \phi(x_1)^\top\phi(x_2) & \dots \\ \phi(x_2)^\top\phi(x_1) & \phi(x_2)^\top\phi(x_2) & \dots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ \phi(x_n)^\top\phi(x_1) & \phi(x_n)^\top\phi(x_2) & \dots \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \begin{bmatrix} \phi(x_1)^\top\phi(x_n) \\ \phi(x_2)^\top\phi(x_n) \\ \vdots \\ \phi(x_n)^\top\phi(x_n) \end{bmatrix} \quad (8.8)$$

Remember that $\phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}')$ is an inner product between the $d$-dimensional vectors $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ and $\phi(\mathbf{x}')$. The key insight is to note that the transformed inputs $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ enter into (8.7) only as inner products $\phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}')$, where each inner product is scalar. That is, if we are able to compute the inner product $\phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}')$ directly, without first computing the $d$-dimensional $\phi(\mathbf{x})$, we have reached our goal.

As a concrete illustration, let us simplify consider polynomials. With $p=1$, meaning $x$ is a scalar, and $x$ is a polynomial of order $(d=4)$ with the second and third term scaled by $\sqrt{3}$, we have

$$\phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}') = [ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \sqrt{3}x & \sqrt{3}x^2 & \sqrt{3}x^3 \end{bmatrix}^\top \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ \sqrt{3}x' \\ \sqrt{3}(x')^2 \\ \sqrt{3}(x')^3 \end{bmatrix} = 1 + 3xx' + 3x^2(x')^2 + x^3(x')^3 = (1+xx')^3 ] \quad (8.10)$$

It can be shown that if $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ is (a re-scaled) polynomial of order $d-1$, then $\phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}') = (1+xx')^d$. The point we make is that instead of first computing the $d$-dimensional vectors $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ and $\phi(\mathbf{x}')$ and thereafter computing their inner product, we could evaluate the expression $(1+xx')^d$ directly. With a second or third order polynomial this might not make much of a difference, but the computational saving in a situation where it is of interest to use $d$ in the hundreds or thousands.

The main point we are saying is that if we make the choice of $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ such that the inner product $\phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}')$ can be computed without computing $\phi(\mathbf{x})$, we let be possible to define inner products between infinite-dimensional vectors, there is nothing preventing us from learning.

The scaling $\sqrt{3}$ can be compensated with an inverse scaling of the second and third element in $\theta$.

## 8.2 Kernel ridge regression

A kernel $\kappa(x, x')$ (is in this book) takes two arguments $x$ and $x'$ from the same space, and returns a scalar. Throughout this book, we limit ourselves to kernels that are real-valued and symmetric, that is, $\kappa(x, x') = \kappa(x', x)$ for all $x$ and $x'$. Equation (8.10), for example, is such a kernel. And more generally the inner product of two nonlinear transformations is an kernel

$$\kappa(x, x') = \phi(x)^\top \phi(x'). \quad (8.11)$$

The important point at this stage is that $\phi(x)$ only appears in the linear regression model (8.7) via inner products, we do not have to design a $d$-dimensional vector $\phi(x)$ and derive its inner product. Instead, we can just choose a kernel $\kappa(x, x')$ directly which is the kernel trick.

- If $x$ enters as $\phi(x)^\top \phi(x')$ only, we can choose a kernel $\kappa(x, x')$ instead of choosing $\phi(x)$.

To be clear with what we mean in practice, we write (8.7) as (8.12):

$$\hat{y}(x^*) = y^\top (K(x^*, x^*) + n\lambda I)^{-1} K(x, x^*). \quad (8.12a)$$

$$K(X, X) = \begin{bmatrix} \kappa(x_1, x_1) & \kappa(x_1, x_2) & \dots & \kappa(x_1, x_n) \\ \kappa(x_2, x_1) & \kappa(x_2, x_2) & \dots & \kappa(x_2, x_n) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ \kappa(x_n, x_1) & \kappa(x_n, x_2) & \dots & \kappa(x_n, x_n) \end{bmatrix} \quad (8.12b)$$
