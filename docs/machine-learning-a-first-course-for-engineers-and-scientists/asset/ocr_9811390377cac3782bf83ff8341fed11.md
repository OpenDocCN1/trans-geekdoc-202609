To train a semantic segmentation model, the training data consist of a large number of images (inputs). For each such image there is a corresponding output image of the same size, where each pixel has labeled to belong to a certain class. The supervised machine learning problem amounts to using this data to find a mapping that is capable of taking a new unseen image and produce a corresponding output. Essentially, this is a type of classification problem, but all pixels need to be classified simultaneously while respecting the spatial dependencies across the image to result in a coherent segmentation.

The colored part of Figure 1.4 shows the prediction generated by such algorithm, where the aim is to classify each pixel as car (blue), traffic sign (yellow), pavement (purple), or tree (green). The best performing solutions for this task are today relying on cleverly crafted neural networks, see Chapter 6.

> Example 1.5: Estimating air pollution levels across London

Roughly 91% of the world's population lives in places where the air quality levels are worse than those recommended by the world health organization. Recent estimates indicate 4.2 million people die each year as a result of air pollution due to stroke, heart disease, chronic cancer and respiratory diseases.

A natural step in this problem is to develop technology to measure and aggregate information about the air pollution levels across time and space. Such information open for the development of machine learning models to better estimate and forecast air pollution, in turn allow for suitable interventions. The work we feature here sets out to do this for the city of London where more than 9000 people die every year as a result of air pollution.

Air quality sensors are now available—at relatively low cost. Combined with an increasing awareness of the problem, has been made by setting up sensors and many non-profit organizations and community groups to contribute the data available. More specifically, the data in this example comes from a sensor network of ground sensors providing readings of NO2 and satellite data at a spatial resolution of 7 km x 7 km. The resulting supervised learning problem is to build a model that can deliver forecasts of the air pollution level across time and space—a continuous variable that is a type of regression. The particularly challenging aspect here is that the measurements are reported at different spatial resolutions and at varying time scales.
