## Medical imaging

One of the most-spread uses of machine learning is in medical applications. There are several notable stories showing better detection of tumours in medical images, how hospitals are organised and improvement of treated patients (Volmer et al. 2020). At the same time, however, in the last three years, tens of thousands of papers published on medical applications of deep learning. How many of these articles actually contribute to improving medical diagnosis or over the methods that have previously been used?

One of measuring progress is to create more sophisticated machine learning methods (e.g. random forests, neural networks, and support vector machines) against simpler. Christodoulou et al. (2019) carried out a systematic review of 71 articles on medical diagnostic tests, using a logistic regression (as a baseline method) to order more complicated machine learning approaches. Their first finding was that, in the majority (48 out of 71 studies), there was potential bias in the validation procedures. This typically favoured the advanced learning methods. For

(2020) say "despite much promising research being undertaken, particularly in imaging, the literature lacks transparency, clarity to facilitate reproducibility, exploration for potential ethical concerns, and clear demonstrations of effectiveness." There have certainly been breakthroughs using machine learning in medical diagnosis, but the vast increase in publications have not, in many applications, led to significant improvements in model performance.

In general, it is common for researchers to see themselves as being in that free from commercial or outside pressures. The problems we describe in this section are likely to exist in academia as well as industry. Researchers in academia receive funding from a system which results in uncertain terms. In some cases, the reward systems are explicit. For example, machine learning progress is measured in performance or on predefined problem domain, challenging the development of methods. As awareness of fairness, our response to this challenge is on us engineers to become performance aware. We have to realise that most of our researchers emphasize the positive aspects of our results. We should deliberately fabricate about, for example, model validation— and doing so would be very unclear—but we might give the impression that our models have more generality than they actually have or that they are more robust than they actually are. We should instead use straightforward language, specifying the performance of our models, the limitations of the type of data that was tested on and how it compares to human performance. We should also follow Christodoulou et al. (2019) advice in making our approach is not biased in favour of any particular method.
