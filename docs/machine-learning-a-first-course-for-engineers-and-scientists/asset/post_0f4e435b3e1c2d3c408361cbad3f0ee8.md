
# User aspects of machine learning

With simpler model, like the ones in Chapter 2-3, it can come some de-degree possible for an engineer to inspect the learned model and explain the "reasoning" behind it for a non-expert. For the more complicated models it can be a harder task.

There are however methods on the research forefront, and the situation may look different in the future. A related topic is that of so-called adversarial examples, which essentially amounts to finding an input x' which is as close as possible to x but gives another prediction. In the image classification setting, it can be for example the problem of having a picture of a car being predicted as a dog by changing a few pixels.

# Worst case guarantees

In the view of this, a supervised machine learning model is good if it fits some $E_{now}$. It is, however, to remember that $E_{now}$ is a statistical claim, under the assumption that the training/or test data remains the quality that the model will find once it is put in production. And even if non-trivial assumption is satisfied, there are no claims about how badly the model will predict in the worst cases. This is indeed a shortcoming of supervised machine learning, and potentially also a show-stopper for some applications.

Simpler and more interpretable models, like logistic regression and trees for example, can be inspected manually in order to reduce the "worst case" that could happen. By looking at the leaf nodes in a regression tree, as an example, it is possible to give an interval to which all predictions belong. With more complicated models, like random forests and deep learning, it is very hard to give any worst case guarantees about how inaccurate the model can be in its predictions faced with some particular input. However, an extensive testing scheme might reveal some of the potential issues.

## 12 Ethics in machine learning

**by David Sumpter¹**

In this chapter we give three examples of ethical challenges that an apparently 'neutral' design choice in how we implement machine learning applications. These are all examples where the performance of a machine learning model leads to an unexpected consequence for its users or society. For each case study, we give concrete examples. In example, we will emphasize ethics through awareness, instead of attempting a technical solution to ethical dilemmas, we explain how they impact our machine learning engineers.

There are many other ethical issues arising from applications of machine learning than are covered in this chapter. These range from issues of medical privacy and social data collection on individuals (Pasquale 2015) and online advertising which, for example, identifies the most vulnerable people in society and targets them with online gambling, unnecessary health services and high interest loans (O'neil 2016); to the use of machine learning to develop weapons and oppressive technology (Russell et al. 2015). In addition to this, there is significant evidence in the tech industry (Alffrey and Twin 2017).

These issues are (in many cases more important to society than the issues we cover here) and the qualified scientist should become interested in the course. But they are largely beyond the scope of this book. Instead, here we look specifically at examples where the technical properties of the machine learning techniques we have learned so far become intertwined with ethics. It turns out that this subset of cases is substantial in size.

### 12.1 Fairness and error functions

At first sight, the choice of an error function (4.1) might appear an entirely technical issue, without any ethical ramifications. After all, the error function is to find out how well a model has performed on test. It should be given so that we can then control our methods as we want to. We might (naively) assume that a technical decision of this nature is neutral. To investigate how such an assumption plays out, let's look at an example.

### Fairness through awareness

There are other fairness calculations we can do. Imagine we are concerned with intrusive advertising, where people are shown adverts uninteresting for them. The probability of experiencing a recommendation that is uninteresting is the false positive rate, FP/(TP+FP). This is 100/(100+100)=1/4 for Non-Swedes and 350/(350+40)=7/8 for Swedes. Swedes receive almost twice as many recommendations than Non-Swedes. Now who is the Swedes we discriminated?

As a machine learning engineer, you should never tell that your algorithm is fair. You should explain how your model performs in aspects related to their conception of fairness. This insight is well captured by the work of Dwork and colleague's, ‘Fairness Towards Awareness’ (Dwork et al. 2012), which is recommended further reading. Fairness is about being aware of the decisions we make in design and in reporting the outcome of our model.

# Complete fairness is mathematically impossible
