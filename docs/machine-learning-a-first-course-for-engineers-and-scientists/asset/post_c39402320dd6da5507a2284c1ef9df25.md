## Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis is similar to an auto-encoder in the sense that the objective is to learn a low-dimensional representation $z \in \mathbb{R}^q$ of the data $x \in \mathbb{R}^p$, where $q < p$. This is done by projecting $x$ onto a $q$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^p$ by applying a linear transformation. Traditionally, the transformation is derived from the objective of obtaining as much information as possible, where information is measured in terms of variance. We will briefly discuss this via PCA below. However, an alternative approach is to consider PCA as an auto-encoder that is restricted to linear. That is, the encoder is a linear mapping that transforms $x$ into the latent representation $z$, the decoder is another linear mapping that tries to reconstruct $x$ from $z$, and both mappings are learned simultaneously by minimizing the reconstruction error with respect to the training data. We can create

$$z = W_{qp}x + b_{qp} \quad \text{and} \quad x = W_{px}z + b_{px} \quad (10.30)$$

for the decoder and offset vectors, respectively. The parameters of the model are the weight matrices and offset vectors, $\theta = \{W_e, b_e, W_d, b_d\}$. In light of Figure 10.10, this can be seen as a two-layer network with a bottleneck layer and linear activation functions. We show that the auto-encoder is also given by a linear transformation, and the reconstruction of $\hat{x}$

$$\hat{x} = W_dx + b_d \quad (10.31a)$$
$$\hat{x} = W_d(W_ex + b_e) + b_d \quad (10.31b)$$
$$\hat{x} = W_dW_ex + W_db_e + b_d \quad (10.31c)$$

To learn the parameters, we minimize the squared reconstruction error of the training data points $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n$

$$\hat{\theta} = \arg \min_{\theta} \sum_{i=1}^n ||x_i - (W_dex_i + W_db_i + b_d)||^2 \quad (10.32)$$

## 10.4 Representation learning and dimensionality reduction

Before proceeding, let us pause for a moment and consider this expression. The transformation of a data point $x$ is according to (10.31c) a linear transformation of $x$. However, this transformation depends on only the matrix $W_d$ and $b_d$. Consequently, there is no hope in uniquely identifying all parameters by (10.32). For instance, we can replace $W_d$ with $W_dT^T$, for any invertible $q \times q$ matrix $T$ and obtain an equivalent model. At best we can learn to learn the model $W_dW_e$ and $b_d$, but it is not possible to uniquely identifying $W_e, b_d$ from these expressions. Therefore, when performing PCA we wish to find one solution to (10.32), without necessarily characterizing all possible solutions. As we will see below, we will not just find any solution, but one that has a nice geometrical interpretation.

Based on this observation, we start by noting that there is redundancy in the "combined offset" vector $W_db+b_d$. Since $b_d$ is a free parameter, we can proceed without loss of generality $b_e = 0$. This means that the encoder mapping simplifies to $z = W_ex$. Next, plugging this into (10.32) it is possible to solve for $b_d$. Indeed, we follow a standard least squares argument, that the optimal value for $b_d$ is

$$b_d = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - W_dW_ex_i) = (I - W_dW_e)X$$

where $X = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$ and $x_i$ is the mean of training data. For notational brevity we define the centered data $x_{0,i} = x_i - \bar{x}$ for $i=1,\dots,n$ by subtracting the mean from each data point. The objective (10.32) this simplifies to-

$$\tilde{W}_e, \tilde{W}_d = \arg \min_{W_e, W_d} \sum_{i=1}^{n} ||x_{0,i} - W_dWex_{0,i}\|^2$$

We note that the role of offset vectors in the auto-encoder is to center the data around its mean. In practice, we handle this as a pre-processing step and center the data by subtracting the mean from each data point.

We can then focus on how to solve the problem (10.34) for the matrices $W_e$ and $W_d$. As have seen previously in this book, working with linear models it is often convenient to center the data vectors into matrices and make use of tools from matrix algebra. This is also true when deriving the PCA solution to (10.3). We define the matrices of centered data and reconstructions as

$$\mathbf{X}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} x_{0,1} \\ x_{0,2} \\ \vdots \\ x_{0,n} \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{\hat{X}}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{x}_{0,1} \\ \hat{x}_{0,2} \\ \vdots \\ \hat{x}_{0,n} \end{bmatrix} \quad (10.36)$$

respectively, matrices are of size $n \times p$. Here $\hat{x}_0 = W_dx_0$ is the reconstruction of the $i$th data point. With this notation we write

$$\tilde{W}_e, \tilde{W}_d = \arg \min_{W_e, W_d} \sum_{i=1}^{n} ||x_{0,i} - W_dWex_{0,i}\|^2 \quad (10.37)$$

where $||A||_F$ denotes the Frobenius norm of $A$. The role of offset vectors in the auto-encoder is to center the data around its mean.
