It is now all bells start to ring about equating with fairness. If we look at the false negatives (FN) for both, we see that there are twice as many false negatives as Non-Swedes as Swedes (100 vs.50, despite their being as many Swedes as Non-Swedes). This can be made easier by calculating the false negative rate (e.g. FN/(TP+FN)) (again see Table 4.1). This is 100/200=1/2 for many and 50/400=1/8 for Swedes. This new result can be put in context by noting that Swedes have a much greater tendency to be interested in the course (40 out of 1200 vs 20 out of 600). However, an interested Non-Swede is 4.5 times more likely to be recommended the course than an interested Swedish.
There are other fairness calculations we can do. Imagine we are concerned with intrusive advertising, where people are shown adverts uninteresting for them. The probability of experiencing a recommendation that is uninteresting is the false positive rate, FP/(TP+FP). This is 100/(100+100)=1/4 for Non-Swedes and 350/(350+40)=7/8 for Swedes. Swedes receive almost twice as many recommendations than Non-Swedes. Now who is the Swedes we discriminated?
This is a fictitious example, but it serves to illustrate the first point we want to make: there is no single metric for measuring fairness. In some cases fairness is perceived as misclassification, in others as false negative rate and in others as parity. It depends on the application. If the data above were used for a criminal sentencing application, ‘positives’ would be sentenced to longer jail terms, then problems with the false positive rate would have serious consequences for those sentenced. If it was for a medical test, where those not picked up by the test had a high probability of dying, then the false negative is most important for judging.
As a machine learning engineer, you should never tell that your algorithm is fair. You should explain how your model performs in aspects related to their conception of fairness. This insight is well captured by the work of Dwork and colleague's, ‘Fairness Towards Awareness’ (Dwork et al. 2012), which is recommended further reading. Fairness is about being aware of the decisions we make in design and in reporting the outcome of our model.

Race was not included in the model.
