In general, as like logistic regression, the training objective (3.51) is a nonlinear optimization
problem without a closed form solution. An important aspect of generalized linear models
is efficient numerical optimization algorithms exist for solving the maximum likelihood problem.
Specifically, under certain assumptions on the link function the problem becomes convex and Newton's
method is used to choose $\theta$. These are concepts that we will return to in Section 5.4
when we discuss numerical optimization in more detail.

# 3.5 Further reading

Compared to the thousand old $k$-NN idea (Chapter 2), the linear regression model with least squares
cost is much younger and "only" to be traced back over two hundred years. It was introduced
Adrien-Marie Legendre in his 1805 book Nouvelles méthodes pour la détermination des orbits des cometes (Methods for the determination of orbits of comets) as well as Carl Friedrich Gauss in his 1809 book Motus Corporum Coelumestium in Conicis Solis Ambientium (Theory
of the motion of heavily bodies moving about the sun in conic sections, that he claims
been used since 1795). Gauss also gave the interpretation of it as maximum likelihood solution
when the noise was assumed to have Gaussian distribution (rather than any distribution), although
the general maximum likelihood approach was much later by the work of Ronald Fisher (Fisher
1922). The history of logistic regression is as old as linear regression, and is described further by
ramer (2003). An in-depth account of generalized linear models is given in the classical textbook by
McCullagh and Nelder (2018).

# 3.A Derivation of the normal equations

The normal equations (3.13)

```
$$\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\hat{\theta} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}. $
```

can be derived from (3.12) (the scaling $\frac{1}{n}$ does not affect the minimizing argument)

```
$$\hat{\theta} = \arg \min_{\theta} ||\mathbf{X}\theta - \mathbf{y}||_2^2.$ $
```

in different ways. We will present one based on (matrix) calculus and one based on geometry and linear
algebra.

No matter (3.13) is derived, if $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is invertible, it (uniquely) gives

```
$$\hat{\theta} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}. $
```

If $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is not invertible, (3.13) has infinitely many solutions, which are all equal solutions
to the problem (3.12).
