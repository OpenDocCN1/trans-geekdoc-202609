## 9 The Bayesian approach and Gaussian processes

![](img/9c84a60f9e70ea0bf5c7c51ae709df3e_193_0.png)

**Figure 9.10:** 9.9 again, this time appended also with samples from $p(f(\mathbf{x}_*) \mid \mathbf{y})$.

### Drawing samples from a Gaussian process

When computing a predictive of $f(\mathbf{x}_*)$ for a single input point $\mathbf{x}_*$, the posterior predictive $p(f(\mathbf{x}_*) \mid \mathbf{y})$ captures all the information that the Gaussian process has about $f(\mathbf{x}_*)$. However, if we are interested in predicting $f(\mathbf{x}_*, \mathbf{x}_* + \delta)$, the Gaussian process contains more information than is present in the two posterior predictive distributions $p(f(\mathbf{x}_*) \mid \mathbf{y})$ and $p(f(\mathbf{x}_* + \delta) \mid \mathbf{y})$ separately. This is because the Gaussian process also contains information about the correlation between the function values $f(\mathbf{x}_)$ and $f(\mathbf{x}_* + \delta)$, and the pitfall is that $p(f(\mathbf{x}_*) \mid \mathbf{y})$ and $p(f(\mathbf{x}_* + \delta) \mid \mathbf{y})$ are only the marginal distributions of the joint distribution $p(f(\mathbf{x}_*), f(\mathbf{x}_* + \delta) \mid \mathbf{y})$.

If we are interested in computing predictions for a larger set of input values it can be cumbersome to grasp and visualize the high-dimensional posterior predictive distribution. An useful alternative can therefore be to visualize the Gaussian process posterior by samples from it. Technically this simply amounts to drawing a sample from the posterior predictive distribution, which we illustrate in Figure 9.10.

## 9.4 Practical aspects of the Gaussian process

When using the Gaussian process as a method for supervised machine learning, there are a few important design choices left to the user. Like the methods presented in Chapter 8, the Gaussian process is a kernel method, and the choice of kernel is very important. Most kernels contain a few hyperparameters, also have to be chosen. That choice can be done by maximizing the marginal likelihood, which we will discuss now.

### Kernel choice

Since the Gaussian process can be understood as the Bayesian version of kernel ridge regression, the Gaussian process also requires a positive semidefinite kernel. Any of the positive semidefinite kernels presented in Section 8.4 can therefore be used for Gaussian processes.

Among many kernels presented in this book, the Gaussian process could be the method where the exact choice of kernel has the biggest impact since the Gaussian posterior predictive $f(\mathbf{x},y)$ has a mean and a variance, of which both are heavily affected by the choice of kernel. It is important to make a good choice, and the discussion in Section 8.4 can also be instructive to compare different kernel choices in Figure 9.1, at least when working with one dimensional problems where visualization is possible. For example, as can be seen from Figure 9.11 the squared exponential and the Matérn kernel with $\nu=5$ corresponds to drastically different assumptions about the smoothness of $f(x)$.

A positive semidefinite $\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')$ remains a positive semidefinite kernel when multiplied with a positive constant $c^2$. For the Gaussian process it is important to choose $c^2$.

### Hyperparameter tuning

Most kernels contain a few hyperparameters, such as $\ell$ and $\sigma^2$, which can be set manually or by maximizing the marginal likelihood. In the Bayesian approach, the marginal likelihood is given by
$p(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{x}, \theta) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{0}, K_\theta + \sigma^2 I)$

In other words, the hyperparameters of the Gaussian process can be chosen by tuning the optimization problem. When using this approach, it is important to be aware that the marginal likelihood may have multiple local maxima, as we illustrate by Figure 9.12. The challenge with local maxima is not unique to the likelihood approach, but can also be addressed through cross-validation.
